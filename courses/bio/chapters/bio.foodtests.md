:::why
How do you know that a biscuit contains starch, that a drink has sugar in it, or that a protein has been digested? You test it. Food scientists, doctors and forensic labs all use the same idea: a reagent that gives a colour change with one type of molecule. These tests are a core part of the practical paper (Paper 3), where you must identify unknown solutions from your own results, and they appear again in the enzyme and digestion questions of the structured papers.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know what a reducing sugar and a non-reducing sugar are from [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs), why lipids do not dissolve in water from [Lipids and water](#bio.lipids), and what a peptide bond is from [Proteins](#bio.proteins). The enzyme chapter [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes) uses these tests to follow reactions.

Q: What does a hydrolysis reaction do to a glycosidic bond? (from [Biology from zero](#bio.f.basics))
A: It breaks the bond by adding a molecule of water, splitting a disaccharide or polysaccharide into smaller sugars.
Q: Which disaccharide is a non-reducing sugar, and which two monosaccharides does it contain? (from [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs))
A: Sucrose, made of glucose and fructose.
Q: Why does oil not dissolve in water? (from [Lipids and water](#bio.lipids))
A: Lipids are hydrophobic (non-polar), so they are not attracted to the polar water molecules and form separate droplets or a layer.

## How a food test works

Each test uses a **reagent**, a chemical that reacts with one type of molecule to give a change you can see. You do not need to know exactly what is in a food: you add the reagent to a small portion of the sample and look at the result.

- A **positive result** is the colour (or cloudiness) that shows the molecule is present.
- A **negative result** is what you see when it is absent: the reagent keeps its own starting colour, or the mixture stays clear.
- Always describe a result as **a change**: "the solution turned from blue to brick-red", not "it went red" or "it changed colour".

:::def Qualitative and semi-quantitative tests
A **qualitative** test shows only whether a substance is present or absent. A **semi-quantitative** test gives an approximate amount (for example "low", "medium" or "high", or an estimate read from a graph), because the result is judged by eye and compared with standards.
:::

Every test in this chapter detects a particular chemical feature, which is why the tests are specific:

| Test | Detects | The feature it reacts with |
|---|---|---|
| Benedict's | reducing sugars | a free carbonyl group that donates electrons |
| iodine | starch | the coiled shape of amylose |
| emulsion | lipids | hydrophobic molecules that dissolve in ethanol but not water |
| biuret | proteins | peptide bonds |

:::tip Use a control every time
Test a **negative control** (water in place of the sample) alongside your sample. It shows what "no change" looks like with your reagents, so you can tell a real colour change from a reagent that has simply aged. A **positive control** (a solution you know contains the molecule) shows that the test works.
:::

## Benedict's test for reducing sugars

**Reducing sugars** are sugars that can donate electrons to (reduce) another substance. All monosaccharides (glucose, fructose, galactose) and some disaccharides (maltose, lactose) are reducing sugars. The details are in [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs).

Benedict's solution is a clear **blue** alkaline solution that contains copper(II) sulfate. The blue colour is due to copper(II) ions. When it is heated with a reducing sugar, the sugar reduces the copper(II) ions to copper(I) ions, which form an insoluble red solid, copper(I) oxide. The more reducing sugar there is, the more copper(I) oxide forms, and the colour moves through a series.

:::lab Benedict's test for reducing sugars
**Aim**: find out whether a liquid sample contains a reducing sugar.

:::steps
1. Put 2 cm³ of the sample in a test tube. If the food is solid, crush it with a little water, let the solids settle (or filter) and use the liquid.
2. Add an **equal volume** (2 cm³) of Benedict's solution and mix.
3. Stand the tube in a **water bath** at about 80–95 °C (a beaker of hot water from a kettle or a heated bath) for a few minutes. Do not hold the tube in a flame.
4. Look at the colour after heating, and compare it with the ladder in the figure. Record the colour change and, if you can see it, the precipitate.
:::

**Positive result**: blue changes to green, yellow, orange or **brick-red precipitate**, depending on the concentration of reducing sugar. **Negative result**: the solution stays blue.

**What it shows**: a reducing sugar is present. It does not say which one: glucose, fructose, maltose and lactose all give the same result. So write "reducing sugar", not "glucose".

**Control**: water instead of sample (stays blue); glucose solution as a positive control.

**Sources of error**: the mixture was not heated long enough or was too cool, so a weak positive looked negative; the sample was so dilute that the change was tiny; a strongly coloured sample hid the colour change; dirty tubes.

**Safety**: Benedict's solution is a mild irritant. The main hazard is the hot water bath.

| Hazard | Risk | Precaution |
|---|---|---|
| hot water can scald | medium | use a test-tube holder, fill the bath carefully, keep the tube pointing away from faces |
| Benedict's solution irritates eyes and skin | low | wear eye protection, wash splashes with water |
:::

:::warn Do not skip the heating
Benedict's test only works when the mixture is **heated**. A mixture left at room temperature may stay blue even when sugar is present. Another common slip is to write that Benedict's tests for glucose, or that it "turns red with sucrose". It does not: sucrose is a non-reducing sugar and gives no change.
:::

## A semi-quantitative Benedict's test

A plain Benedict's test tells you whether a reducing sugar is present. Often you also need to know roughly **how much**. The test can be turned into a semi-quantitative one, if you **standardise** it: every tube is treated exactly alike, so the only thing that differs is the concentration of the sugar.

There are two common ways to read the result.

:::cols
:::col Colour standards
- Make a set of solutions of known concentration (the standards).
- Test them and the unknown under identical conditions.
- Compare the colour (and amount of precipitate) of the unknown with the standards. The closest match gives an estimate of its concentration.
:::
:::col Time to first colour change
- Heat all the tubes together and time how long each takes to show the **first** colour change from blue.
- A higher concentration gives a shorter time.
- Plot time against concentration for the standards and read the unknown's concentration from the graph.
:::
:::

:::lab A semi-quantitative Benedict's test
**Aim**: estimate the concentration of reducing sugar in a solution.

:::steps
1. Make a set of known glucose concentrations by **serial dilution** from a stock solution (see [Practical skills](#bio.practical) for the method). Keep one tube of water as the zero.
2. Put 2 cm³ of each standard, and of the unknown, into separate labelled test tubes.
3. Warm a water bath to a set temperature (for example 90 °C) and keep it there.
4. Add 2 cm³ of Benedict's solution to a tube, place it in the bath and **start the stopwatch at once**. Do each tube separately, or add all the Benedict's solution and place all the tubes in at the same moment.
5. Record the time of the first colour change from blue (or compare colours after a fixed time such as 5 minutes).
6. Plot a graph of time (y-axis) against concentration (x-axis) for the standards, draw a smooth curve, and read the concentration that matches the unknown's time.
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is the concentration of reducing sugar; the dependent variable is the time to the first colour change (or the colour after a fixed time). Standardise the volume of sample, the volume and the age and source of the Benedict's solution, the temperature of the bath, the size of tube, and the depth of the tube in the bath.

**Control**: water instead of sample, which should show no colour change in the time allowed.

**Limits**: judging "the first colour change" and matching colours are subjective; different people may disagree; the estimate is only valid inside the range of the standards.

**Improvements**: use more standards at smaller intervals; repeat and take means; have the same person judge every tube; or measure the result objectively. Two objective ways are to filter the mixture, dry and **weigh the precipitate** (more sugar gives a greater mass of copper(I) oxide), or to read the blue filtrate in a **colorimeter** (the more sugar, the less blue is left, so the absorbance falls; see [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes)).

**Safety**: as for the Benedict's test (hot water bath).
:::

:::eg A student made standards of glucose, heated 2 cm³ of each with 2 cm³ of Benedict's solution at 90 °C, and timed the first colour change. An unknown solution changed colour after 100 s. Estimate its concentration.
The times for the standards are in the table.

| glucose / % | time / s |
|---|---|
| 0.5 | 240 |
| 1.0 | 120 |
| 1.5 | 80 |
| 2.0 | 60 |
| 2.5 | 48 |

**Step 1: describe the pattern.** The time falls as the concentration rises: from 240 s at 0.5% to 48 s at 2.5%. Doubling the concentration from 0.5% to 1.0% halves the time, from 240 s to 120 s.

**Step 2: use the rate.** A shorter time means a faster change, so the rate is \(1/t\). For 1.0%:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{rate} &= \frac{1}{120\,\mathrm{s}} \\ &= 8.33 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

The rate is directly proportional to the concentration (it is \(8.33 \times 10^{-3}\) at 1.0% and \(1.67 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) at 2.0%).

**Step 3: read the graph.** Draw a horizontal line at 100 s on the plotted curve, then a vertical line down to the concentration axis. It reaches the axis at 1.2%.

**Step 4: check by calculation.** With \(t \times c = 120\) for every standard, \(c = 120 \div 100 = 1.2\%\).

The unknown contains about 1.2% reducing sugar. This is an estimate, because the first colour change is judged by eye. Repeating the unknown three times would show how much the result varies.
:::

:::eg You have 2.0% glucose solution. Describe how to make standards of 1.0%, 0.50%, 0.25% and 0.125% by serial dilution, and show that the last one is correct.
A serial dilution halves the concentration at each step, using the same volumes each time.

Label four tubes. Put 5 cm³ of water in each. Put 5 cm³ of the 2.0% stock into the first tube and mix: 5 cm³ of stock in a total of 10 cm³ gives half the concentration.

\[\begin{aligned} c_1 &= 2.0 \times \frac{5}{10} \\ &= 1.0\% \end{aligned}\]

Take 5 cm³ of the 1.0% solution into the second tube and mix: 0.50%. Take 5 cm³ of that into the third: 0.25%. Take 5 cm³ of that into the fourth: 0.125%. Remove 5 cm³ from the last tube, so all four tubes hold 5 cm³ and 5 cm³ of water is the zero standard.

\[\begin{aligned} c_4 &= 2.0 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^4 \\ &= 0.125\% \end{aligned}\]

Use a clean pipette or syringe for each solution, or work from the most dilute upwards, so that no carry-over changes the concentrations.
:::

:::eg Real context, diabetes: a urine sample is heated with Benedict's solution and turns orange. What can be concluded, and why is this not a reliable diagnosis on its own?
Urine from a healthy person contains little or no glucose. An orange colour shows that a reducing sugar is present, at a medium concentration. [B1]

Glucose in urine can be a sign of diabetes mellitus (see [Blood glucose, cell signalling and biosensors](#bio.glucose)). [B1]

But Benedict's solution gives a positive result for **any** reducing sugar, such as lactose or fructose, and for other reducing substances, so it does not prove that the sugar is glucose. [B1]

A clinic therefore uses test strips that contain the enzyme glucose oxidase, which is specific to glucose, and a blood test to confirm the result.
:::

## Testing for non-reducing sugars

Sucrose gives no change in Benedict's test, because the groups on its two rings that would reduce copper(II) ions are joined together in the glycosidic bond. But the bond can be broken by **hydrolysis**: boil sucrose with dilute acid and it splits into glucose and fructose, both of which are reducing sugars.

:::lab Test for a non-reducing sugar
**Aim**: find out whether a sample that gave a negative Benedict's test contains a non-reducing sugar such as sucrose.

:::steps
1. Test a portion of the sample with Benedict's solution, as before. It must stay **blue**: if it does not, a reducing sugar is present and this method cannot show a non-reducing sugar as well.
2. Take a **fresh** 2 cm³ portion of the sample and add 2 cm³ of dilute hydrochloric acid. Heat in a boiling water bath for a few minutes. The acid hydrolyses the glycosidic bonds, so any non-reducing sugar is split into reducing sugars.
3. Cool the tube. Add sodium hydrogencarbonate, a little at a time, until it stops fizzing, and check with pH paper that the mixture is **alkaline** (or at least neutral).
4. Add 2 cm³ of Benedict's solution and heat in the water bath as before.
:::

**Positive result**: a green to brick-red precipitate after the acid step, together with a blue result before it. This shows that a **non-reducing sugar** (for example sucrose) was present. **Negative result**: blue at both stages, so there was no reducing or non-reducing sugar.

**Why neutralise**: Benedict's solution must be alkaline to work. Acid left in the tube reacts with the alkali in the reagent, so the test fails even if reducing sugar has been released.

**Why start with a normal Benedict's test**: the acid step would also give a positive for a sample that already held a reducing sugar. Only a first result of "blue" followed by a positive result after acid points to a non-reducing sugar.

**Control**: repeat with water instead of the sample, and a sucrose solution as a positive control.

**Sources of error**: too little boiling time with acid (hydrolysis incomplete); acid not fully neutralised (a false negative); too much sodium hydrogencarbonate or a delay in testing.

**Safety**: dilute hydrochloric acid irritates the eyes and skin; sodium hydrogencarbonate fizzes (carbon dioxide), so add it slowly to avoid the mixture frothing over; a boiling water bath scalds. Risk: medium for the hot bath, low for the acid at this concentration. Wear eye protection and use a holder.
:::

:::warn Starch also gives reducing sugars with acid
Boiling starch with dilute acid also hydrolyses its glycosidic bonds, so an iodine-positive sample can give a positive result in the acid step. Test for starch with iodine as well, and do not call a sample "sucrose" unless the evidence supports it.
:::

:::eg Exam-style: describe how you would test a solution for a non-reducing sugar.
A full-mark answer gives five separate marking points in a logical order.

Test a sample with Benedict's solution first, heating in a water bath; it stays blue, so there is no reducing sugar. [B1]

Take a fresh sample and boil it with dilute hydrochloric acid, which hydrolyses the glycosidic bond in the non-reducing sugar. [B1]

Neutralise the acid by adding sodium hydrogencarbonate until it stops fizzing (check with pH paper). [B1]

Add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath. [B1]

A green to brick-red precipitate shows that a non-reducing sugar was present. [B1]

A weaker answer says "boil it with acid and then do Benedict's". It leaves out the first negative test, the neutralising step and the colour change, so it scores two marks at most.
:::

## Iodine test for starch

:::lab Iodine test for starch
**Aim**: find out whether a sample contains starch.

:::steps
1. Put a sample on a white tile or 2 cm³ of a liquid sample in a test tube. If the sample is a solid food, crush a small piece with water, or put the iodine on the food itself.
2. Add two or three drops of **iodine solution** (iodine dissolved in potassium iodide solution). Do not heat.
3. Look at the colour at once.
:::

**Positive result**: the iodine changes from **orange-brown to blue-black**. **Negative result**: it stays orange-brown (or yellow-brown, if the drops are diluted).

**Why**: starch is made of amylose and amylopectin. Amylose is a long unbranched chain of α-glucose units that coils into a **helix**. Iodine molecules fit inside the helix, and the complex they form absorbs light so that it appears blue-black. Glucose, maltose and sucrose are too small to form a helix. Cellulose is made of straight chains and does not coil, so it gives no colour.

**Control**: water in place of the sample; starch solution as a positive control.

**Safety**: iodine solution stains skin and clothes and can irritate the eyes. Risk: low. Wear eye protection.
:::

You will see the iodine test again in [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes), where the time for the blue-black colour to vanish measures the activity of amylase.

## Emulsion test for lipids

Lipids do not dissolve in water, so you cannot test them in a solution of water. The **emulsion test** dissolves them in ethanol, then adds water.

:::def Emulsion
A mixture of tiny droplets of one liquid dispersed in another liquid in which it does not dissolve. Droplets of lipid scatter light, so an emulsion looks cloudy and white.
:::

:::lab Emulsion test for lipids
**Aim**: find out whether a sample contains lipid (fat or oil).

:::steps
1. Put about 2 cm³ of the sample (a few drops of oil, or a crushed solid food) into a **dry** test tube.
2. Add about 2 cm³ of **ethanol**. Shake the tube thoroughly so that any lipid dissolves.
3. Let any solid settle, then pour only the ethanol into a second tube that contains about 2 cm³ of **water**.
4. Look at the mixture straight away.
:::

**Positive result**: a **cloudy white emulsion** forms. **Negative result**: the mixture stays clear.

**Why**: lipids are non-polar, so they dissolve in ethanol but not in water. When the solution is poured into water, the ethanol mixes with the water and the lipid is no longer dissolved. It comes out as tiny droplets that scatter light.

**Why the tube is dry**: water in the first tube would stop the lipid dissolving in the ethanol.

**Control**: water in place of the sample (stays clear), oil as a positive control.

| Hazard | Risk | Precaution |
|---|---|---|
| ethanol is flammable | medium | no naked flames in the room, small volumes, keep the bottle closed |
| ethanol irritates eyes | low | wear eye protection |
:::

## Biuret test for proteins

Proteins are chains of amino acids joined by **peptide bonds**, so the biuret test detects the bond itself rather than any particular protein.

:::lab Biuret test for proteins
**Aim**: find out whether a sample contains protein.

:::steps
1. Put 2 cm³ of the sample (a liquid, or a food crushed with water) in a test tube.
2. Add 2 cm³ of **biuret reagent**. If you are not given it ready made, add an equal volume of dilute sodium hydroxide (or potassium hydroxide) solution, mix, then add a few drops of dilute copper(II) sulfate solution.
3. Mix gently and leave for a minute or two. **Do not heat.**
4. Record the colour.
:::

**Positive result**: the solution changes from **blue to purple (lilac)**. **Negative result**: it stays blue.

**Why**: in alkaline solution, copper(II) ions form a coloured complex with the nitrogen atoms of peptide bonds. A protein has many peptide bonds. A single amino acid has none, so free amino acids give no colour change.

**Control**: water; a protein solution (such as egg white diluted with water) as a positive control.

**Sources of error**: a very dilute protein gives only a pale lilac, easy to miss against the blue; a coloured sample hides the change.

| Hazard | Risk | Precaution |
|---|---|---|
| sodium hydroxide is corrosive to eyes and skin | medium | wear eye protection, use dilute solution, wash splashes at once |
| copper(II) sulfate is harmful if swallowed | low | do not taste, wash hands afterwards |
:::

:::key The four tests in one table
| Test | Positive | Negative | Heat? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Benedict's (reducing sugar) | green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate | stays blue | yes |
| Benedict's after acid (non-reducing sugar) | green to brick-red after acid, blue before | blue at both stages | yes, with acid |
| Iodine (starch) | blue-black | stays orange-brown | no |
| Emulsion (lipid) | cloudy white emulsion | stays clear | no |
| Biuret (protein) | purple (lilac) | stays blue | no |
:::

:::exam Describe results in full
A mark needs the **starting** and **ending** colour, and the right word for the result: "blue to brick-red precipitate", "orange-brown to blue-black", "blue to purple". Vague words such as "it changed colour", "turned red" or "went dark" score nothing. State what the result **shows** as well: "a reducing sugar is present", not "glucose is present". For a negative result write "stays blue" or "no change".
:::

## Identifying unknown solutions

In Paper 3 you may be given several unlabelled solutions and asked to find out what each contains. The skill is to plan the tests, record the results clearly, and draw only the conclusions that the results allow.

:::steps
1. Label the tubes. Pour a small portion of each solution into separate tubes for each test: never test in the stock bottle.
2. Carry out every test on every solution, and add a water control.
3. Draw a table before you start, with the solutions in the left column and the tests across (or the tests down and the solutions across), and write the colour you see in each cell. Describe colours in words.
4. Work through the sugar tests in order: Benedict's first, then the acid step only on solutions that gave a negative result.
5. Write a conclusion for each solution that quotes the evidence ("P contains a reducing sugar because Benedict's gave a brick-red precipitate").
:::

:::eg Five unknown solutions, P to T, were each tested with Benedict's solution (before and after boiling with acid), iodine solution, the emulsion test and the biuret test. Identify what each contains.
First the sugar tests, then the other three.

| sample | Benedict's | after acid |
|---|---|---|
| P | brick-red | not done |
| Q | stays blue | brick-red |
| R | stays blue | stays blue |
| S | stays blue | stays blue |
| T | stays blue | stays blue |

| sample | iodine | emulsion | biuret |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | no change | clear | blue |
| Q | no change | clear | blue |
| R | blue-black | clear | blue |
| S | no change | cloudy | blue |
| T | no change | clear | purple |

**P** gave a brick-red precipitate in the first Benedict's test, so it contains a **reducing sugar** (a high concentration). Nothing else is present, because all the other tests are negative.

**Q** was blue with Benedict's but brick-red after acid, so it contains a **non-reducing sugar**, such as sucrose.

**R** gave blue-black with iodine, so it contains **starch**. It is negative for sugars, lipid and protein.

**S** is cloudy in the emulsion test, so it contains **lipid**.

**T** turned purple with biuret reagent, so it contains **protein**.

Each conclusion uses evidence from a specific result. P, Q, R, S and T each contain one main molecule here; if a solution had given two positive results, it would contain both.
:::

:::eg Plan an investigation: a student has a white powder that may contain starch, a reducing sugar, or both. How can the student find out?
Dissolve a small amount of the powder in water (or test it with iodine directly) and divide it into two portions. [B1]

To one portion add iodine solution: blue-black shows starch is present. [B1]

To the other add Benedict's solution and heat in a water bath: a colour change from blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate shows a reducing sugar is present. [B1]

If both tests are positive, the powder contains both. (Also credit: a water control; a positive control; the idea that each test is on a separate portion.)
:::

### Hazard and risk

Part of Paper 3 is to assess the hazards in an investigation, and you will see it again in the planning papers.

:::def Hazard
Something that can cause harm, such as a flammable liquid, a corrosive chemical or hot water.
:::

:::def Risk
How likely it is that a hazard causes harm, and how serious that harm would be. In Paper 3, risk is described as low, medium or high, and reduced by a precaution.
:::

In your answer, name the hazard, rate the risk and give a precaution that fits it. "Be careful" is not a precaution; "wear eye protection", "use a holder", "keep ethanol away from flames" are.

Q: State the colour change that shows starch is present when iodine solution is added, and name the part of the starch molecule that gives this result.
A: Orange-brown to blue-black. Amylose coils into a helix that traps iodine molecules.
Q: A student heats a sample with Benedict's solution and the colour changes from blue to green. What does this show?
A: A reducing sugar is present, at a very low concentration.
Q: Give two safety precautions when heating Benedict's solution with a sample.
A: Use a water bath (not a flame) and a test-tube holder; wear eye protection; point the tube away from faces.
Q: Why is the tube dry in the emulsion test, and why is ethanol added first?
A: The lipid will dissolve in ethanol but not in water, so water in the tube would stop it dissolving. The ethanol solution is then poured into water, where the lipid comes out as an emulsion.
Q: Why does the biuret test need no heating?
A: Copper(II) ions in alkaline solution react with peptide bonds at room temperature, giving the purple complex without any heat.
Q: A student boils sucrose with dilute hydrochloric acid and adds Benedict's solution at once. The mixture stays blue. Suggest why.
A: The acid was not neutralised. Benedict's solution only works in alkaline conditions, so no precipitate forms, even though the sucrose has been hydrolysed.
Q: Two glucose solutions of 1.0% and 0.25% are tested. Which will change colour first, and why?
A: The 1.0% solution, because more reducing sugar reduces the copper(II) ions faster, giving a shorter time.
Q: A student concludes that a juice contains glucose because it gave an orange precipitate with Benedict's solution. Comment on the conclusion.
A: Benedict's solution shows only that a reducing sugar is present. Fructose, maltose or lactose would give the same result.
Q: Blackcurrant juice is a very dark purple. Suggest why the Benedict's test is hard to read, and one way to deal with this.
A: The dark colour hides the blue-to-red change. Filter off the precipitate and look at it on the filter paper, or dilute the juice, or use glucose test strips.
Q: Starting from a 4.0% solution, how many 1 : 1 serial dilutions are needed to reach 0.25%? Show the working.
A: \(4.0 \to 2.0 \to 1.0 \to 0.50 \to 0.25\): four dilutions (\(4.0 \times (1/2)^4 = 0.25\)).

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A food scientist measured the reducing sugar in apple juice. Samples of known sugar concentration were heated with an excess of Benedict's solution, the precipitate was filtered off and the absorbance of the blue filtrate was read in a colorimeter with a red filter. The absorbance was 0.80 with no sugar and fell by 0.16 for every 10 g dm⁻³ of sugar, giving a straight line. The filtrate from the apple juice had an absorbance of 0.37. (a) Explain why the absorbance of the filtrate falls as the sugar concentration rises. (b) Calculate the concentration of reducing sugar in the apple juice, to 2 significant figures. (c) Explain why the precipitate must be removed before the reading is taken.
H: Reducing sugar removes copper(II) ions from the solution. What colour are copper(II) ions in solution?
H: Fall in absorbance \(= 0.80 - 0.37\). Divide by the fall per 1 g dm⁻³ (0.016).
A: (a) Reducing sugar reduces the blue copper(II) ions to copper(I) oxide, which forms the precipitate. [B1]
The more sugar, the fewer blue copper(II) ions are left in the filtrate, so it is less blue and absorbs less light. [B1]
(b) Fall in absorbance \(= 0.80 - 0.37 = 0.43\), and the line falls by \(0.16 \div 10 = 0.016\) per g dm⁻³ [M1]
\(0.43 \div 0.016\) [M1] \(= 27\,\mathrm{g\,dm^{-3}}\) (to 2 significant figures) [A1]
(c) The solid precipitate would scatter and absorb light, which would add to the absorbance and give a false reading. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A student followed the breakdown of starch by amylase at 35 °C. Every 2 minutes a sample was taken and tested with iodine solution and, separately, with Benedict's solution (heated).

| time / min | iodine | Benedict's |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | blue-black | stays blue |
| 2 | blue-black | green |
| 4 | orange-brown | yellow |
| 6 | orange-brown | orange |
| 8 | orange-brown | brick-red |

(a) Describe the changes in the two sets of results. (b) Explain the results. (c) State the result expected for a control with boiled amylase.
H: Starch is the substrate and maltose the product. Which test detects each?
H: The control is for the whole experiment: think about iodine and Benedict's at 8 minutes.
A: (a) The iodine test changes from blue-black to orange-brown, between 2 and 4 minutes. [B1]
The Benedict's test changes from blue through green, yellow and orange to brick-red between 0 and 8 minutes. [B1]
(b) Amylase hydrolyses starch (the substrate), so the starch is used up and the blue-black colour with iodine is lost. [B1]
The product, maltose, is a reducing sugar, so more of it gives a positive Benedict's test with more colour change as its concentration rises. [B1]
The brick-red precipitate at 8 minutes shows a high concentration of reducing sugar. [B1]
(c) Boiled amylase is denatured and cannot catalyse the reaction, so the iodine stays blue-black and Benedict's stays blue. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A sweet drink is labelled "sucrose only". A student heated it with Benedict's solution and got an orange precipitate. A: (a) Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar, so the sample should have stayed blue; a reducing sugar must also be present. [B1]

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ (#bio.proteins) The biuret test can be used to follow the digestion of protein. A protease was added to a protein solution at 35 °C, and samples were taken and tested with biuret reagent. The absorbance in a colorimeter (green filter) was 0.60 at 0 min, 0.44 at 10 min, 0.32 at 20 min and 0.24 at 30 min. (a) Explain why the absorbance falls. (b) Calculate the mean rate of fall in absorbance between 0 and 20 min. (c) After 30 minutes the absorbance is not zero. Suggest why.
H: What does the protease do to the peptide bonds?
H: A short chain of amino acids still has peptide bonds.
A: (a) The protease hydrolyses peptide bonds in the protein. [B1]
So there are fewer peptide bonds to form the coloured complex with copper(II) ions, and the solution is less purple, so it absorbs less light. [B1]
(b) \((0.60 - 0.32) \div 20\) [M1] \(= 0.014\) per minute [A1]
(c) The protein has been broken into short peptides, many of which still contain peptide bonds, so a purple colour remains until they are all hydrolysed to amino acids. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which result shows that a sample contains starch?
+ Iodine solution changes from orange-brown to blue-black.
- Benedict's solution changes from blue to brick-red.
- Biuret reagent changes from blue to purple.
- The sample forms a cloudy white emulsion in water.
> Starch coils into a helix that traps iodine, giving a blue-black colour. The brick-red precipitate is for reducing sugars, purple for protein, and a cloudy emulsion for lipids.
? A student heats sucrose solution with Benedict's solution. What is seen?
+ The solution stays blue.
- A brick-red precipitate forms.
- The solution turns purple.
- The solution turns blue-black.
> Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar, so it does not reduce copper(II) ions. The brick-red precipitate appears only if sucrose is first hydrolysed by acid and the acid is neutralised.
? Why is sodium hydrogencarbonate added in the test for a non-reducing sugar?
+ To neutralise the acid, because Benedict's solution works only in alkaline conditions.
- To hydrolyse the glycosidic bonds in the sugar.
- To make the solution blue.
- To oxidise the sugar to a brick-red precipitate.
> Acid does the hydrolysis. The alkali is added to neutralise the acid so that Benedict's solution can work.
? In the emulsion test, why is the lipid first dissolved in ethanol?
+ Lipids do not dissolve in water but do dissolve in ethanol.
- Ethanol hydrolyses the lipid.
- Ethanol makes the lipid turn white.
- Ethanol is needed to heat the lipid.
> The cloudy emulsion forms when the ethanol solution is poured into water, because the lipid is no longer dissolved.
? Which test needs the sample to be heated?
+ Benedict's test
- biuret test
- iodine test
- emulsion test
> Benedict's solution reacts with reducing sugars only when it is heated. The biuret, iodine and emulsion tests are done at room temperature.
? A sample gives a brick-red precipitate with Benedict's solution. Which conclusion is correct?
+ A reducing sugar is present.
- Glucose is present.
- Sucrose is present.
- A monosaccharide is present.
> Many sugars, including maltose and lactose (which are disaccharides), give a positive result, so the test shows only that a reducing sugar is present.
? A student wants to estimate the concentration of glucose in a solution using Benedict's solution. Which change would make the result less valid?
+ Using different volumes of Benedict's solution for the unknown and the standards
- Using a stopwatch to measure the time
- Using a water bath to keep the temperature constant
- Making the standards by serial dilution
> Only the concentration of sugar should differ between the tubes. Different volumes of reagent would change the time to the colour change for reasons unrelated to the sugar.
? A sample gives no colour change with Benedict's solution and no colour change with iodine solution, but a cloudy white emulsion in the emulsion test. Which conclusion is correct?
+ lipid is present, but no reducing sugar or starch
- starch and lipid are present
- protein and lipid are present
- reducing sugar and lipid are present
> Only the emulsion test is positive, so lipid is present and reducing sugar and starch are absent. No biuret test was done, so nothing can be said about protein.
? What does a blue-to-purple colour change with biuret reagent show?
+ peptide bonds, so a protein is present
- a reducing sugar is present
- amino acids are present
- starch is present
> The copper(II) ions react with peptide bonds. Free amino acids have no peptide bonds and give no change.

:::recap
- Benedict's (heat): blue to green, yellow, orange or brick-red precipitate = reducing sugar. More sugar, a shorter time and a redder colour.
- Semi-quantitative Benedict's: standardise volumes, reagent, temperature and time; compare with colour standards or time to first colour change; read the unknown from a graph. It is subjective, so repeat or use a colorimeter or weigh the precipitate.
- Non-reducing sugar: negative Benedict's, then boil a fresh sample with dilute hydrochloric acid, neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate, add Benedict's and heat; brick-red = non-reducing sugar.
- Iodine: orange-brown to blue-black = starch (amylose helix). Emulsion: ethanol then water; cloudy white = lipid. Biuret: blue to purple = protein (peptide bonds); no heating.
- Write colour changes with start and end colours, and say what the result shows (reducing sugar, not "glucose").
- Hazard = something that can cause harm; risk = low, medium or high; give a precaution.
:::
