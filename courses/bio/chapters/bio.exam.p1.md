:::why
Paper 1 is 40 marks of the AS and A Level, and every mark is won by choosing one letter. There is nothing to write, so the skills are different from the structured papers: reading the stem exactly, knowing the precise term, spotting the option that is almost right, and keeping to time. These skills can be trained. This guide shows the seven kinds of question, the traps the options are built from, and a method you can use on every question.
:::

## The paper at a glance

| | Paper 1 |
|---|---|
| Name | Multiple Choice |
| Time | 1 h 15 min |
| Marks | 40 |
| Questions | 40, each with four options A to D, all compulsory |
| Content | AS content, topics 1 to 11 |
| Weight | 31% of AS, 15.5% of A Level |
| Marks per question | 1 |
| Wrong answers | no mark is taken off |
| Calculator | allowed |

What the front page tells you (from the real June 2024 paper):
- "There are forty questions on this paper. Answer all questions."
- Each question has four possible answers, A, B, C and D. You record your choice in **soft pencil** on the multiple choice answer sheet.
- "Each correct answer will score one mark." No mark is taken off for a wrong answer.
- You may use a calculator. There is no data booklet or formula sheet, so every number you need is in the question or in your memory.
- You do rough working on the question paper itself, not on the answer sheet.

Paper 1 tests only AS content (topics 1 to 11), and it counts towards both the AS and the A Level. The marks are shared equally between knowledge and understanding (AO1) and handling, applying and evaluating information (AO2), so half of the marks reward what you know and half reward using it on a table, graph, diagram or unfamiliar example.

:::exam Never leave a blank
A wrong answer loses nothing, so a blank is a guaranteed zero and a guess is a chance of one mark. Before you hand in, check every question on the answer sheet has exactly one shaded lozenge. If you have eliminated two options, your guess is a 50% chance, not a 25% one.
:::

## Time: how long is a mark?

Seventy-five minutes for 40 questions is an average of \(75 \times 60 \div 40 = 112.5\) seconds, about 1 min 50 s, for each mark. The average hides a wide spread. A recall question takes 20 seconds; a calculation with a table can take three minutes. The way to use the spread is to make **two passes**.

| Pass | What you do | Time |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Go through in order. Answer at once if you are sure. If you are not sure after about 80 seconds, circle your best guess on the question paper, put a mark by the number and move on. | about 55 min (\(55 \times 60 \div 40 = 82.5\) s a question) |
| 2 | Return to the marked questions, hardest last. Try each again with a fresh mind. | about 15 min |
| Check | Count the shaded lozenges, then check that each letter you shaded is the one you circled. | about 5 min |

Fifty-five, fifteen and five add up to the full 75. The reason for two passes is simple: every question is worth one mark, so a hard question at number 7 is worth no more than an easy one at number 31. Never lose two minutes on one mark while ten easy marks are waiting.

## A method for every question

:::steps
1. **Read the stem and cover the options.** Put your hand or a ruler over A to D. The options are written to look right, so reading them first lets them steer your thinking.
2. **Answer the question in your head.** Say or write the answer in the margin ("peptide bond", "about 0.3", "SAN").
3. **Uncover the options and look for yours.** If it is there, check the other three quickly and choose it.
4. **If it is not there, eliminate.** Cross out every option that is false, reversed, or true but does not answer the question that was asked. Each option you cross out raises the chance of a lucky guess.
5. **Check the units and the exact words.** Look again at NOT, ONLY, ALWAYS, EXCEPT, "decreases", "per", "in", "between", and at units such as mm, µm and nm.
6. **Shade one lozenge.** Do it as you go, not at the end.
:::

:::warn Words that flip the answer
The most common wrong answers on Paper 1 come from skimming past a single word: **NOT**, **only**, **both**, **except**, **least**, **decrease**. Underline it on the paper when you read. "Which statement is NOT correct?" has three true statements and one false one. If you are hunting for a true statement you will choose a wrong answer and feel sure about it.
:::

## The seven kinds of question

Real Paper 1 questions fall into a few types. Each type below has an original worked example. The practice set later in this guide uses the same types on different content.

### 1 Recall

A question that asks for a fact, a term or a definition. These are the fastest marks, but the options are often terms that sound alike.

:::eg Which bond is formed between two amino acids when they join together by condensation?

**A** glycosidic

**B** phosphodiester

**C** peptide

**D** ester

**Cover the options first.** Amino acids join to make proteins and the link is a peptide bond. [B1] The other three are real bonds from other topics (carbohydrates, nucleic acids, lipids), put there because they are all condensation products too. The correct answer is **C**.
:::

### 2 "Which row is correct?"

A table of ticks and crosses (✓ and ✗) with four rows A to D. Only one row has every cell correct. **Judge each column on its own**: find the cell in a column that is wrong, and cross out every row with that cell.

:::eg Which row correctly describes the mitochondrion of an animal cell?

| | circular DNA | 80S ribosomes | double membrane |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| **B** | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **C** | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |
| **D** | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |

Take one column at a time. Mitochondria have **70S** ribosomes, not 80S, so the middle column must be ✗: row B is out. Mitochondria have small circular DNA, so the first column must be ✓: row C is out. They are bounded by two membranes, so the last column must be ✓: row D is out. Only row **A** is left. [B1]
:::

### 3 Data and graph reading

The stem gives a table or a graph. Read the headings and units before the numbers, and decide what the question really asks: a value, a trend, a rate, or a conclusion.

:::eg Pieces of potato were left in sucrose solutions of different concentrations. The table shows the change in mass of the pieces after one hour. Which sucrose concentration is closest to the concentration with the same water potential as the potato cells?

| sucrose / mol dm⁻³ | change in mass / % |
|---|---|
| 0.0 | +14 |
| 0.2 | +6 |
| 0.4 | −4 |
| 0.6 | −11 |
| 0.8 | −16 |

**A** 0.2

**B** 0.3

**C** 0.4

**D** 0.6

Where there is no change in mass there is no net movement of water, so the solution and the cells have the same water potential. The change in mass is +6% at 0.2 and −4% at 0.4, so it is 0% between them. Moving along the straight line between the two points, the zero is a fraction \(6 \div (6 + 4) = 0.6\) of the way from 0.2 to 0.4, at

\[\begin{aligned} 0.2 + 0.6 \times 0.2 &= 0.32\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}} \end{aligned}\]

This is nearest to 0.3, so the answer is **B**. [B1] A and C are the two concentrations on either side of the zero, which is where students who do not interpolate land.
:::

### 4 Calculation

Paper 1 calculations are one or two steps: magnification and actual size, percentage change, surface area to volume ratio, a mean, a ratio. Work in **one unit** before you divide, write your working beside the question, and then check that your answer has a sensible size.

:::eg A mitochondrion is 1.5 µm long. In an electron micrograph its image is 45 mm long. What is the magnification of the micrograph?

**A** ×30

**B** ×3000

**C** ×30 000

**D** ×300 000

Change the image length to micrometres first (1 mm = 1000 µm), then use \(M = I \div A\).

\[\begin{aligned} I &= 45\,\mathrm{mm} = 45 \times 1000 = 45\,000\,\mu\mathrm{m} \\ M &= \frac{I}{A} = \frac{45\,000}{1.5} = 30\,000 \end{aligned}\]

The answer is **C**. [B1] A forgets the conversion factor (\(45 \div 1.5 = 30\)), B converts by 100, and D multiplies by 10 too many. Each wrong option is a slip in the units, which is why you must write the conversion down.
:::

The drawing shows a nucleus (P, a large round body with a darker nucleolus), rough endoplasmic reticulum (Q, flattened sheets with ribosomes on them), a mitochondrion (R, an oval with inner folds called cristae) and a Golgi body (S, a stack of flattened sacs with vesicles pinching off). :::eg Which labelled structure modifies proteins and packages them into vesicles for secretion?

**A** P

**B** Q

**C** R

**D** S

Name the function before you look at the letters: modifying proteins and packaging them into vesicles is the job of the **Golgi body**. It is the stack of flattened sacs with vesicles beside it, which is **S**. [B1] Q is the tempting wrong answer: rough ER makes proteins and passes them on, but does not package them for secretion.

:::tip Recognising organelles in a micrograph
In an electron micrograph, look for **shape first**: a double membrane with inner folds is a mitochondrion; stacked flattened sacs are a Golgi body; membrane sheets studded with dots are rough ER; a large body with a double envelope and pores is a nucleus. Then check the size against any scale bar. Real micrographs are grey, so you cannot use colour, and a structure cut at an angle can look round instead of oval.
:::

### 6 Sequence

The options are four orders of the same four or five steps. Do not try to compare the four options at the same time. Work out the correct order yourself, using the first and last step to eliminate options, and then match.

:::eg The statements describe four stages in the production of antibodies after a pathogen enters the body.

**W** a B-lymphocyte divides and develops into plasma cells

**X** plasma cells secrete antibodies

**Y** a T-helper cell with a complementary receptor binds to an antigen presented on the surface of a phagocyte

**Z** a phagocyte engulfs the pathogen

Which sequence shows the correct order of these stages?

**A** Z → Y → W → X

**B** Y → Z → W → X

**C** Z → W → Y → X

**D** W → Y → Z → X

The pathogen has to be taken up first (**Z**) and the antibodies are released last (**X**). That removes B and D, which do not begin with Z, and no option now starts or ends with a wrong step. Between A and C, ask what a B-lymphocyte needs before it can divide: a T-helper cell must bind and activate it, so Y comes before W. The answer is **A**. [B1]
:::

### 7 "Which statement is NOT correct?"

Three statements are true and one is false. Decide true or false for each statement in turn and put a T or F beside it. Choose the F. If you find two Fs, you have misread one: go back to the stem.

:::eg Which statement about enzyme inhibitors is NOT correct?

**A** A competitive inhibitor has a shape similar to that of the substrate.

**B** A non-competitive inhibitor binds at a site other than the active site.

**C** The effect of a competitive inhibitor can be reduced by increasing the substrate concentration.

**D** A competitive inhibitor changes the shape of the active site so that the substrate no longer fits.

Mark each option T or F. A is true: the inhibitor fits the active site because its shape is like the substrate's. B is true: the non-competitive inhibitor binds elsewhere. C is true: more substrate competes better for the active sites. D is false: changing the shape of the active site is how a **non-competitive** inhibitor works, while a competitive one sits in the active site and changes nothing about its shape. The answer is **D**. [B1]
:::

## Ten traps and the chapter that cures each

Paper 1 options are written from the mistakes students really make. If you know the mistake, the wrong option looks wrong. The table gives ten of the most common ones from the AS course, and where to revise each.

| Trap (the wrong idea) | The correct idea | Revise |
|---|---|---|
| "Mitochondria make energy" | they release the energy of glucose to make ATP in respiration; energy cannot be made | [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells) |
| Hydrogen bonds hold the primary structure | peptide bonds hold it; hydrogen bonds hold the secondary structure and help the tertiary | [Proteins](#bio.proteins) |
| A hot enzyme is "killed" or "used up" | high temperature breaks bonds and **denatures** it; an enzyme is never used up | [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes) |
| A competitive inhibitor changes the shape of the active site | it fits the active site and blocks it; the non-competitive one changes the shape | [Enzyme kinetics, inhibitors and immobilised enzymes](#bio.enzymes2) |
| Water moves from "high concentration" of solute to "low" | water moves from a higher (less negative) to a lower (more negative) water potential | [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement) |
| A cell in G2 has 92 chromosomes | it has 46 chromosomes, each now made of two chromatids; the number of chromosomes only changes at anaphase | [Chromosomes and the mitotic cell cycle](#bio.mitosis) |
| Uracil pairs with thymine; DNA polymerase makes mRNA | uracil pairs with adenine in RNA; RNA polymerase makes mRNA | [Protein synthesis and gene mutation](#bio.synthesis) |
| Arteries carry oxygenated blood | arteries carry blood away from the heart; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood | [Blood vessels, blood and tissue fluid](#bio.mammaltransport) |
| The AVN is the pacemaker | the sinoatrial node is the pacemaker; the AVN delays the wave of excitation | [The heart and the cardiac cycle](#bio.heart) |
| Vaccines contain antibodies; antigens are the pathogens | vaccines contain antigens (or a weakened pathogen); antigens are molecules on the pathogen | [Antibodies and vaccination](#bio.antibodies) |

When you get a question wrong in practice, write the trap in this style in a notebook. Reading your own list of traps in the last week is the best revision for Paper 1.

## Ten practice questions

This set has the format of the real paper: one mark each, four options, one correct. Set a timer for **15 minutes** (the real average allowed is 18 min 45 s for ten questions, so this is a little faster, which helps in a real paper). Do not look at the answers until you have finished. The ten questions use the seven types: the cell drawing used above is needed for question 5.

:::try Practice set: ten questions
Q: [1 mark] A solution gave no colour change when heated with Benedict's reagent. A sample of the solution was boiled with dilute hydrochloric acid, neutralised with sodium hydrogencarbonate and heated with Benedict's reagent again. A brick-red precipitate formed. Which substance could the solution contain?

**A** fructose

**B** glucose

**C** maltose

**D** sucrose
A: **D**. Only a non-reducing sugar gives no colour with Benedict's reagent until its glycosidic bond has been hydrolysed. Sucrose is non-reducing, and acid hydrolysis breaks it into glucose and fructose, which are reducing sugars. [B1] Fructose, glucose and maltose are all reducing sugars, so they would give the colour in the first test.

Q: [1 mark] Which row correctly describes amylopectin?

| | α-glucose | 1,6 glycosidic bonds | in plant cell walls |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |
| **B** | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| **C** | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **D** | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |
A: **B**. Amylopectin is a polymer of α-glucose with 1,4 glycosidic bonds and branches at 1,6 bonds. [B1] It is a storage carbohydrate in plants, so it is not in the cell walls (cellulose is, and cellulose is made from β-glucose with no branches).

Q: [1 mark] A cuboid-shaped cell measures 2 µm by 4 µm by 5 µm. What is the surface area to volume ratio of the cell?

**A** 0.53 : 1

**B** 1.0 : 1

**C** 1.9 : 1

**D** 3.8 : 1
A: **C**. The surface area is \(2 \times (2 \times 4 + 2 \times 5 + 4 \times 5) = 2 \times 38 = 76\,\mathrm{\mu m^2}\) and the volume is \(2 \times 4 \times 5 = 40\,\mathrm{\mu m^3}\). The ratio is \(76 \div 40 = 1.9\), written 1.9 : 1. [B1] A is the ratio upside down (volume to surface area) and D counts every face twice.

Q: [1 mark] The length of a strip of plant tissue decreased from 50 mm to 44 mm in a concentrated sucrose solution. What was the percentage change in length?

**A** −12%

**B** −6.0%

**C** −13.6%

**D** +12%
A: **A**. The change is \(44 - 50 = -6\) mm, and the percentage change is \(-6 \div 50 \times 100 = -12\%\). [B1] C divides by the final length instead of the original, B gives the change in millimetres without dividing by the original length, and D loses the sign (the length decreased).

Q: [1 mark] This question uses the drawing of the animal cell given in the "Diagram identification" section above. Which two labelled structures are each surrounded by a double membrane?

**A** P and Q

**B** P and R

**C** Q and S

**D** R and S
A: **B**. The nucleus (P) is surrounded by a nuclear envelope, made of two membranes, and the mitochondrion (R) by two membranes, the inner one folded into cristae. [B1] Rough ER (Q) and the Golgi body (S) are made of a single membrane around a space.

Q: [1 mark] Water moves from the soil through the root to the xylem. Which sequence shows the correct order in which water passes through these tissues?

**A** cortex → endodermis → root hair cell → xylem vessel

**B** root hair cell → endodermis → cortex → xylem vessel

**C** endodermis → cortex → root hair cell → xylem vessel

**D** root hair cell → cortex → endodermis → xylem vessel
A: **D**. Water enters through the root hair cell, crosses the cortex, then passes through the endodermis (past the Casparian strip) and enters the xylem vessels. [B1] The root hair cell comes first, which removes A and C, and the xylem is last. The endodermis is the layer that surrounds the xylem, inside the cortex, so it comes after the cortex, which removes B.

Q: [1 mark] Which statement about the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) is NOT correct?

**A** It contains ribosomes made of RNA and protein.

**B** It has a protein capsid around its genetic material.

**C** It has RNA as its genetic material.

**D** It has a phospholipid envelope.
A: **A**. Viruses are non-cellular and have no ribosomes: they use those of the host cell to make their proteins. [B1] B, C and D are all true of HIV.

Q: [1 mark] The table shows the initial rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction at five substrate concentrations, with and without inhibitor X added at the same concentration each time.

| substrate / mmol dm⁻³ | rate without X / µmol min⁻¹ | rate with X / µmol min⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 12 | 6 |
| 20 | 20 | 10 |
| 40 | 28 | 14 |
| 80 | 33 | 16.5 |
| 160 | 35 | 17.5 |

Which conclusion is best supported by the data?

**A** X is a competitive inhibitor, because the rate rises as the substrate concentration rises.

**B** X is a competitive inhibitor, because it reduces the rate at low substrate concentrations.

**C** X is a non-competitive inhibitor, because the rate with X stays at about half the rate without X as the substrate concentration rises.

**D** X is a non-competitive inhibitor, because the rate with X is higher than the rate without X.
A: **C**. At every substrate concentration the rate with X is about half the rate without it, and it levels off at about half the maximum rate (17.5 compared with 35). [B1] More substrate does not overcome the inhibitor, which is what a non-competitive inhibitor does. A competitive inhibitor would reduce the rate at low concentrations but the two rates would become almost equal at a high concentration. A and B give a reason that would also be true of a competitive inhibitor, and D is false because the rate with X is lower.

Q: [1 mark] Which row is correct for a molecule of DNA?

| | uracil | deoxyribose | hydrogen bonds between strands |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ |
| **B** | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| **C** | ✗ | ✗ | ✓ |
| **D** | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
A: **A**. DNA has the base thymine, not uracil, so the first cell is ✗. It has deoxyribose as its sugar and hydrogen bonds between base pairs join the two strands. [B1]

Q: [1 mark] An antibiotic binds to 70S ribosomes and stops translation. Which cells or structures could the drug affect directly?

**A** bacterial ribosomes and the ribosomes in the cytoplasm of human cells

**B** bacterial ribosomes and the ribosomes in human mitochondria

**C** bacterial ribosomes and the ribosomes on the human rough endoplasmic reticulum

**D** bacterial ribosomes only
A: **B**. Bacteria have 70S ribosomes, and so do the mitochondria of eukaryotic cells (and chloroplasts), because they came from prokaryotes. Human cytoplasm and rough ER have larger 80S ribosomes, which the drug does not fit. [B1] D is the common assumption that antibiotics affect only bacteria; here the mitochondria also have 70S ribosomes.
:::

### How to mark yourself

Give one mark per correct answer. The set is out of 10, so 8 or more is a good result. Then, for each wrong answer, ask which of these three it was and write it in your notebook:
- **Knowledge**: you did not know the fact. Revise the chapter in the traps table.
- **Reading**: you knew it but misread the question (a missed NOT, a wrong unit, a wrong row). Underline the key words next time.
- **Technique**: you knew it and read it correctly but did not cover the options, so a wrong option pulled you away.

## Using real past papers

The best practice for Paper 1 is the real thing: the real papers show you the exact style, the length and the traps.

:::steps
1. **Do a whole paper in one sitting**, timed, at a quiet desk, with a calculator and a soft pencil. Do not look anything up.
2. **Mark it with the official mark scheme**, which is a list of 40 letters. Count the marks. Do not stop to read explanations yet.
3. **Make a log** for every wrong answer: the question number, the topic, the type (recall, row, data and so on), and why you lost the mark (knowledge, reading or technique).
4. **Revise the topics from the log**, using the chapter links in this guide, and write the correct explanation for each wrong answer in your own words.
5. **Return in a week and redo only the wrong questions.** If you now get them right and can say why, the fault is fixed.
:::

After four or five papers the log shows a pattern: usually two or three topics cause half the lost marks. Those topics, and not the ones you already know, deserve your revision time. Paper 1 covers topics 1 to 11, so after the AS chapters you can use any past paper, and you can set one as a mock under exam conditions in [Mock Paper 1](#bio.mock.p1) once you have finished the AS course.
