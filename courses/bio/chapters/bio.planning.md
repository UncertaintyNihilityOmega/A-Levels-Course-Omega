Q: Why is a buffer solution added in an enzyme investigation? (from [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes))
A: A buffer keeps the pH constant, so pH is a standardised variable and cannot affect the rate.
Q: How would you make 10 cm³ of a 0.4% solution from a 1.0% stock solution? (from [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical))
A: Mix 4 cm³ of the 1.0% solution with 6 cm³ of water (\(1.0 \times 4 \div 10 = 0.4\)).

A: The standard error shows how reliable the mean is; the standard deviation shows the spread of the data.

## What Paper 5 asks

Paper 5 (Planning, Analysis and Evaluation) lasts 1 hour 15 minutes and is marked out of 30. It is written, with no laboratory work, and has two or more questions (the June 2024 paper had two, of 19 and 11 marks). Planning is worth 14 to 16 of the marks and analysis, conclusions and evaluation the other 14 to 16 (the second kind is in [Analysis, conclusions and evaluation (Paper 5)](#bio.evaluation)). All the marks are for experimental skills (assessment objective AO3). The context may be outside the syllabus; anything you need to know about an unfamiliar organism, chemical or apparatus is given in the question, and the content can come from AS or A Level topics. You can use a calculator.

Marks are given one for each creditworthy detail, in the same way as for a structured question. "Add some water" gains nothing; "add 10 cm³ of distilled water using a syringe" may gain a mark. Your plan is judged by whether another student could carry it out and get results that test the hypothesis.

## A framework for any plan

Almost every planning question can be answered by working down the same list. Use it as a checklist, and keep the order of the question if it asks for sections.

:::steps
1. **Hypothesis and prediction**: what you expect, why, and (if asked) a sketch graph.
2. **Variables**: name the independent variable (IV) and the dependent variable (DV).
3. **Vary the IV**: at least five values, the range, and how you make each value (dilution, water bath, filter, distance).
4. **Measure the DV**: what you measure, with which apparatus, in which unit, and to what precision.
5. **Standardise**: each other key variable, how you keep it the same, and the value.
6. **Controls**: the control experiment that shows the DV changes because of the IV.
7. **Replicates**: how many, and what you do with the readings (mean, anomalies, spread).
8. **Method in order**: numbered steps that include quantities, timings and apparatus.
9. **Data processing**: table, graph, calculations (mean, rate, percentage) and statistical test.
10. **Risk assessment**: each hazard, how severe and how likely, and the precaution.
:::

The rest of the chapter takes the steps in turn.

## Hypothesis and prediction

:::def Hypothesis
A testable explanation of an observation, usually stating how the independent variable is thought to affect the dependent variable and the biological reason why.
:::

:::def Prediction
What you expect to see in the results if the hypothesis is correct. It is specific and can be shown on a graph or in numbers.
:::

A hypothesis includes a **reason**; a prediction does not. "Increasing the concentration of copper ions reduces root growth because copper ions inhibit enzymes in root cells" is a hypothesis. "Roots will be shorter at higher concentrations" is the prediction it makes. A mark scheme usually wants a prediction that gives the direction of the change, linked to a biological idea.

A **sketch graph** shows the prediction with the IV on the x-axis and the DV on the y-axis, both labelled with units. It needs no numbers on the axes, but the shape must match the prediction: a straight line, a curve that rises then levels off, a peak at an optimum, or a fall.

:::plot Sketch of the prediction: root length falls as the concentration of copper ions rises, and levels off at a low value.
--f "10+28/(1+(x/0.01)**1.5)" --x 0 0.115 --y 0 50 --noticks --nogrid --xlabel "concentration of copper ions" --ylabel "mean root length" --label "predicted root length" --legend tr --alt "A curve that starts high on the left and falls steeply as the concentration increases, then levels off at a low value."
:::

| relationship expected | shape of the sketch |
|---|---|
| rate proportional to the IV | straight line through the origin |
| rate rises, then limited by another factor | curve that rises, then flattens |
| optimum (temperature, pH) | rises to a peak, then falls |
| DV decreases as the IV increases | a falling line or curve |

:::eg Write a hypothesis, a prediction and a sketch graph for an investigation of the effect of the concentration of salt in the water on the percentage of lettuce seeds that germinate.
Hypothesis: a higher salt concentration lowers the water potential of the surrounding solution, so less water enters the seeds by osmosis and fewer seeds germinate. [B1]

Prediction: the percentage of seeds that germinate decreases as the salt concentration increases. [B1]

Sketch: salt concentration on the x-axis and percentage germination on the y-axis, both labelled with units; a curve that starts high and falls as the concentration rises. [B1]

A weaker answer says "I think more salt is bad for seeds". It has no biological reason, so it is a prediction only, and no graph axes are labelled.
:::

## Variables

:::def Independent variable
The variable that the investigator deliberately changes, for example the concentration of a solution or the temperature.
:::

:::def Dependent variable
The variable that is measured to see the effect of the change, for example the length of roots or the volume of oxygen produced.
:::

:::def Standardised variable
A variable, other than the independent variable, that could affect the dependent variable and is therefore kept the same in every treatment.
:::

:::def Control experiment
A treatment in which the independent variable is absent or at a baseline value, but everything else is the same, so that any change in the dependent variable can be attributed to the independent variable.
:::

A variable must be **precise**. "The amount of enzyme" does not say whether it is volume, mass or concentration; "the volume of 1.0% amylase solution, 2.0 cm³" does.

| vague (no mark) | precise (mark) |
|---|---|
| the amount of water | 10 cm³ of distilled water, measured with a syringe |
| the temperature | 25 °C, kept in a thermostatically controlled water bath and checked with a thermometer |
| the same seeds | seeds of one variety, from one packet, of similar mass |
| the time | 72 hours, measured with a timer |
| the light | a 20 W lamp at 30 cm, with a heat shield |

## Varying the independent variable

Choose **at least five values**, in equal steps or in a series, and say what they are. State the range and why: it should cover values below and above where the change is expected, include a baseline (often 0), and stay within what the organism or apparatus can tolerate.

- **Temperature**: thermostatically controlled water baths or incubators, for example 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C, checked with a thermometer.
- **Concentration**: proportional or serial dilution of a stock solution (below).
- **Light**: coloured filters, or the distance from a lamp.
- **pH**: buffer solutions of known pH.

Explain the range when you can. "The range 10 to 60 °C covers temperatures below and above the optimum of the enzyme, so the peak can be seen" earns a mark; "a range of temperatures" does not.

## Measuring the dependent variable

State what you measure, the apparatus, the unit and the precision. Prefer a **quantitative** measurement (a volume, mass, length, time or absorbance) to a qualitative one (a colour compared by eye). If a reaction is followed over time, say when you take the readings.

:::def Accuracy
How close a measured value is to the true value.
:::

:::def Precision
How close repeated measurements are to each other (the fineness of the measurement, such as the number of decimal places that an instrument allows).
:::

:::def Repeatability
Whether the same investigator, using the same method and apparatus, obtains similar results when the investigation is repeated.
:::

:::def Validity
Whether an investigation measures what it is supposed to measure, so that the results can be used to test the hypothesis. It needs measurements that are accurate and results that are repeatable, and only one variable (the IV) changing.
:::

To measure accurately, choose apparatus whose smallest division is small compared with the quantity measured. The **uncertainty** of a single reading is half the smallest division, and its size compared with the reading is the percentage uncertainty:

\[\text{percentage uncertainty} = \frac{\text{uncertainty}}{\text{reading}} \times 100\]

:::eg Gas collected in an experiment measured 20 cm³ in a measuring cylinder with 1 cm³ divisions, and 20.0 cm³ in a gas syringe with 0.2 cm³ divisions. Compare the percentage uncertainties.
Cylinder: the uncertainty is \(\pm 0.5\) cm³ (half of 1 cm³), so

\[\begin{aligned} \text{percentage uncertainty} &= \frac{0.5}{20} \times 100 \\ &= 2.5\% \end{aligned}\]

Syringe: the uncertainty is \(\pm 0.1\) cm³, so \(\dfrac{0.1}{20} \times 100 = 0.5\%\).

The syringe is five times more precise at this reading, so the measurements would be more accurate. A plan should name the better apparatus and give this reason.
:::

:::warn "Repeat to make it accurate"
Repeating readings does not make them more accurate. It shows whether they are **repeatable** and allows you to calculate a mean and spot an anomaly. Accuracy comes from using suitable apparatus and technique.
:::

## Standardising other variables

List the variables that could change the DV, and for each say **how** it is kept the same and **what value** you use. Keep to the key variables (those that really affect the dependent variable) and describe them precisely.

| variable | how it is standardised |
|---|---|
| temperature | water bath or incubator at 25 °C; thermometer check |
| volume of a solution | measured with a syringe or pipette, for example 10 cm³ |
| concentration of a reagent | the same stock solution used for every treatment |
| pH | buffer solution of the same pH |
| surface area of plant tissue | the same cork borer, discs cut to 5 mm thickness |
| number and size of organisms | 10 seeds of similar mass; same species, age and source |
| light intensity | same lamp at a fixed distance; a water-filled tank as a heat shield so the lamp does not warm the sample |
| time | a stopwatch, with the same time for every treatment |

## Volumes and concentrations

You may be asked to describe suitable volumes and concentrations. Concentrations are given as a percentage or in mol dm⁻³.

:::def Percentage concentration, % (w/v)
The mass of solute in grams dissolved in 100 cm³ of solution. A 5% (w/v) solution contains 5 g of solute in every 100 cm³.
:::

:::key Preparing a solution
- **% (w/v)**: mass needed (g) \(= \dfrac{\text{percentage} \times \text{volume (cm}^3)}{100}\).
- **mol dm⁻³**: moles \(= c \times V\) with \(V\) in dm³ (1 dm³ = 1000 cm³); mass \(= \text{moles} \times M\), where \(M\) is the molar mass (given in the question).
- Dissolve the solute in less water than the final volume, then make up to the final volume in a volumetric flask or measuring cylinder.
:::

:::eg (a) Calculate the mass of sodium chloride needed to make 250 cm³ of a 4.0% (w/v) solution. (b) Calculate the mass of glucose (\(M = 180\) g mol⁻¹) needed to make 100 cm³ of a 0.20 mol dm⁻³ solution.
(a) A 4.0% solution has 4.0 g in 100 cm³, so 250 cm³ needs \(4.0 \times 2.5 = 10\) g.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mass} &= \frac{4.0 \times 250}{100} \\ &= 10\,\mathrm{g} \end{aligned}\]

(b) Change the volume to dm³ first: \(100\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 0.100\,\mathrm{dm^3}\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{moles} &= 0.20 \times 0.100 \\ &= 0.020\,\mathrm{mol} \end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mass} &= 0.020 \times 180 \\ &= 3.6\,\mathrm{g} \end{aligned}\]
:::

### Making a range of concentrations

:::def Proportional dilution
Making solutions of different concentrations by mixing calculated volumes of a stock solution and water, so that the concentrations rise in equal steps (for example 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0).
:::

The volume of stock needed for a final volume \(V_f\) of concentration \(c_f\) from a stock of concentration \(c_s\) is

\[V_s = \frac{c_f \times V_f}{c_s}\]

and the volume of water is \(V_f - V_s\).

:::eg A student has a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ stock solution of sucrose and wants 20 cm³ of each of 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 and 0.8 mol dm⁻³. Prepare a dilution table.
For each concentration \(V_s = c_f \times 20 \div 1.0\), and the water is \(20 - V_s\).

| final concentration / mol dm⁻³ | stock / cm³ | water / cm³ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.2 | 4 | 16 |
| 0.4 | 8 | 12 |
| 0.6 | 12 | 8 |
| 0.8 | 16 | 4 |

For 0.6: \(V_s = 0.6 \times 20 \div 1.0 = 12\) cm³, so water \(= 20 - 12 = 8\) cm³. Use a separate syringe or pipette for the stock and for the water, and mix each tube.
:::

:::def Serial dilution
Making solutions of different concentrations in a series, in which each solution is made by diluting the previous one by the same factor (for example, 1 cm³ of solution + 9 cm³ of water for a tenfold dilution each time).
:::

A serial dilution is used when the concentrations need to cover several powers of ten, as with a poison, a hormone or bacteria. It is quick and uses small volumes, but any error in an early tube is carried into every later tube.

:::key Which dilution method?
- **Proportional**: equal steps in concentration (0.2, 0.4, 0.6 ...). Use when the effect is expected to change steadily over a narrow range.
- **Serial**: equal factors (1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001 ...). Use when you need a very wide range of concentrations.
:::

## Controls, replicates and the quality of results

### Controls

Say what the control is, what it shows and why it is needed. Common controls:

- **Zero of the IV**: distilled water instead of the solution being tested (treatment without the added factor).
- **Boiled or denatured enzyme (or boiled seeds, or dead tissue)**: shows that the change is caused by the living or active material and not by something else.
- **No organism, no enzyme or no substrate**: shows that the reagents alone do not cause the change.
- **A known standard** (positive control): shows that the method can detect the effect.

A control changes **one** thing. A "control" that differs in two ways from the experiment (a different volume and no enzyme) does not show which factor matters.

### Replicates and anomalies

Take **at least three replicates** (a fixed number of repeats of the whole treatment, using different individuals) at each value of the IV. Then:

- Calculate the **mean** at each value; replicates allow you to do this and reduce the effect of random variation.
- Identify any **anomalous result**, a value that does not fit the pattern or lies far from the other replicates. Check it for a cause, repeat it, and exclude it from the mean (stating that you did so) if there is a reason to.
- Assess the **spread** of the results using standard deviation, standard error or a 95% confidence interval (see [Statistics for biologists](#bio.stats)). A small SD means the replicates are close together, so the results are repeatable. Error bars on the graph show the standard error or the 95% confidence interval.

Together, accurate measurement and repeatable results allow you to judge the **validity** of the results: whether the experiment really tests the hypothesis.

## Writing the method in a logical sequence

The method is a numbered list that another student could follow. Each step says *what is done, with what, in what quantity, and for how long*. A good method:

1. prepares the treatments first (dilutions, water baths set up and allowed to reach temperature);
2. sets up each replicate in the same way and in the same order;
3. says when the stopwatch starts and when the readings are taken (for example "every 30 s for 5 minutes");
4. says how the readings are recorded and how the apparatus is used (for example "read the position of the meniscus at eye level");
5. includes the control, the replicates, and the clearing up.

## Processing the results

Say briefly what will be done with the readings.

- **Table**: the IV in the left column, units in the headings, readings for each replicate and the mean.
- **Calculations**: the mean, a rate (for example volume ÷ time, or \(1 \div\) time), a percentage change \(= \dfrac{\text{change}}{\text{original}} \times 100\), and SD or SE.
- **Graph**: the IV on the x-axis and the DV (or the mean DV) on the y-axis, with error bars if SE or 95% confidence intervals are used.
- **Statistical test**: the test suited to the data (a t-test to compare two means, a correlation test for two continuous variables, chi-squared for counts compared with an expected ratio) with the null hypothesis.

## Risk assessment

:::def Hazard
Something that has the potential to cause harm, for example a chemical, a hot object, a sharp tool or a micro-organism.
:::

:::def Risk
The chance that a hazard actually causes harm, taking into account how severe the harm would be, how likely it is to happen, and the conditions of use (concentration, quantity, time).
:::

A simple risk assessment names the **hazard**, says who or what could be harmed and **how severe** the harm would be, estimates how **likely** it is, and gives a specific **precaution**. The same chemical is a smaller risk in dilute solution than in concentrated solution, and "be careful" or "wear goggles" is not enough on its own.

| hazard | severity | probability | risk | precaution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ethanol (flammable) | fire, burns | low if there is no flame | medium | use an electrically heated water bath; no naked flames; keep the bottle capped |
| hot water bath at 60 °C | scalds | medium | medium | lift tubes with a holder; keep the bath away from the edge of the bench |
| dilute (0.1 mol dm⁻³) irritant solution | eye irritation | low | low | wear eye protection; wash splashes off with water |
| glass capillary tube or syringe | cuts | low | low | check for cracks; handle with care; sweep up broken glass with a brush |

## Three model plans

Each plan below is built the way a mark scheme is: one point, one mark. The first is written in full as continuous sentences; look at how many different decisions are made.

:::eg Plan an investigation of the effect of the concentration of copper(II) ions (a heavy metal) on the growth of the roots of cress seedlings.
Hypothesis: copper(II) ions inhibit enzymes and damage cells in the root, so the higher the concentration of copper(II) ions the shorter the roots will be after a fixed time. [B1]

The independent variable is the concentration of copper(II) sulfate solution; the dependent variable is the mean length of the roots. [B1]

Use six concentrations: 0 (distilled water), 0.000010, 0.00010, 0.0010, 0.010 and 0.10 mol dm⁻³. This covers a range from harmless to toxic. [B1]

Make the solutions by serial dilution from a 0.10 mol dm⁻³ stock: mix 1 cm³ of one solution with 9 cm³ of distilled water for each tenfold step. [B1]

Place a filter paper in each of 18 Petri dishes (three dishes for each concentration), add 10 cm³ of the solution to each dish with a syringe, and place 10 cress seeds on the paper. [B1]

Standardise the temperature by keeping all the dishes in one incubator at 25 °C in the dark. [B1]

Standardise the volume of solution (10 cm³), the number of seeds (10), and use seeds of one variety, from one packet, and similar in size. [B1]

The control is the dishes with distilled water (0 mol dm⁻³), to show how much the roots grow with no copper. [B1]

After 72 hours, measure the length of the root of every seedling with a ruler, to the nearest millimetre. [B1]

Calculate the mean root length and the standard error for each concentration, plot a graph with the concentration on the x-axis and the mean root length on the y-axis, with error bars, and compare each concentration with the control using a t-test. [B1]

Anomalous roots, for example from seeds that did not germinate, are identified and left out of the mean (with the reason given). [B1]

Risk assessment: copper(II) sulfate is harmful if swallowed, irritates the eyes and is toxic to aquatic life. The 0.10 mol dm⁻³ stock is a medium risk and the dilute solutions a low risk. Wear eye protection and gloves, wash hands afterwards, and put the waste in a labelled container, not down the sink. [B1]

(Also credit: a justified range on a log scale; percentage inhibition compared with the control; an incubator light cycle kept constant; using a graph with a logarithmic x-axis.)

A weaker answer says "put seeds in different amounts of copper and see which grows most". It names no values, no volume, no way to prepare or measure, and no control or risk, so it scores one or two marks.
:::

:::eg Plan an investigation of the effect of temperature on the rate of water uptake by a leafy shoot, using a potometer.
Hypothesis: a higher temperature increases the kinetic energy of water molecules so they evaporate and diffuse out of the leaves faster, and more water is pulled up the xylem, so the rate of uptake increases with temperature. [B1]

The independent variable is the air temperature around the shoot; the dependent variable is the rate of water uptake, measured as the distance moved by an air bubble in the potometer. [B1]

Use five temperatures: 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 °C, each set in a temperature-controlled cabinet (or incubator) and checked with a thermometer next to the leaves. [B1]

Cut the shoot under water at a slant, fit it into the potometer under water, and seal the joints with petroleum jelly so that they are airtight; check there are no air bubbles in the capillary tube. [B1]

Leave the apparatus in each cabinet for 10 minutes before taking readings, so that the shoot and the air are at the chosen temperature. [B1]

Introduce an air bubble, and measure the distance it moves along the capillary in 5 minutes with a ruler. Calculate the volume of water taken up as \(\pi r^2 \times\) distance, then divide by the time for the rate. [B1]

Use shoots of the same species, with the same number of leaves of similar total area. [B1]

Standardise light intensity (the same lamp at the same distance, with a heat shield so it does not warm the air), humidity and air movement. [B1]

Use three different shoots at each temperature and calculate the mean rate for each. [B1]

The control is a potometer with leaves coated in petroleum jelly (or a shoot with no leaves), which should show little uptake, so that the movement of the bubble depends on loss of water from the leaves. [B1]

Plot the mean rate (per cm² of leaf area) on the y-axis against temperature on the x-axis, calculate the standard deviation of the replicates, and use a correlation test (Spearman's rank). [B1]

Risk assessment: the glass capillary tube can break and cut; handle it carefully, check for cracks and sweep up any pieces with a brush. Keep the lamp and mains lead away from spilt water. The scalpel is sharp: cut away from the body on a tile. The risk is low. [B1]

(Also credit: shake the leaves dry before starting; allow the same time for all shoots; a bubble that reaches the end of the scale is reset using the reservoir.)

The potometer measures the uptake of water, which is a little more than the water lost by transpiration because some is used in photosynthesis and some keeps cells turgid. For a comparison between temperatures this does not matter.
:::

:::eg Plan an investigation of the effect of the wavelength of light on the rate of photosynthesis of algae immobilised in jelly balls. Hydrogencarbonate indicator solution changes from orange-red to purple as the carbon dioxide concentration falls.
Hypothesis: chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light most strongly and reflects green, so photosynthesis, which removes carbon dioxide, is fastest in red and blue light and slowest in green light. [B1]

The independent variable is the colour (wavelength) of the light; the dependent variable is the time taken for the indicator to change from orange-red to purple. [B1]

Use five colour filters: violet, blue, green, yellow and red, in front of the lamp. [B1]

The filters transmit different amounts of light, so measure the light intensity behind each filter with a light meter and move the lamp until all are equal (for example 50 lux). [B1]

Put 10 algal balls (same diameter, from one batch) and 10 cm³ of indicator in a clear bottle; start with all bottles at the same orange-red colour. [B1]

Place a tank of water between the lamp and the bottle as a heat shield and check with a thermometer that the temperature stays at 25 °C. [B1]

Use the same volume of indicator, the same number of balls and the same bottles, and darken the room so that only the filtered light reaches the bottle. [B1]

The control is a bottle with the same number of jelly balls without algae (or a bottle wrapped in foil), which should not change colour, so the colour change depends on photosynthesis by the algae. [B1]

Switch on the lamp, start the stopwatch, and record the time at which the indicator first matches a purple standard. [B1]

Do three bottles for each colour and calculate the mean time; the rate is \(1 \div\) time. [B1]

Plot the mean rate on the y-axis against the colour (wavelength) on the x-axis, find the standard deviation of the replicates, and compare the red and green means with a t-test. [B1]

Risk assessment: the lamp gets hot (burns) and mains electricity must be kept away from the water tank. Wash hands after handling algae. Handle the glass bottles carefully. The risk is low. [B1]

(Also credit: use a colorimeter to read the absorbance of the indicator after a fixed time; use a bench lamp with a cool LED.)
:::

:::eg Spot the error: planning a light intensity investigation. [3]
A student planned an investigation of the effect of light intensity on the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed. Part of the plan is shown.

Line 1: Place a lamp at 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm from a beaker of pondweed in water.
Line 2: Count the number of bubbles released in 2 minutes at each distance, and repeat three times.
Line 3: The temperature will be kept the same because all the readings are taken in the same room.

Line 1 gives five values of light intensity [B1]. Line 2 gives a measurement of the rate and repeats [B1]. **Line 3 is wrong: a lamp close to the beaker warms the water more than a lamp far away, so temperature changes with distance and is a confounding variable. The room temperature does not control the temperature of the water. That mark is lost [B0].**

Correct Line 3: "Put a tank of water between the lamp and the beaker as a heat shield, and check with a thermometer that the water stays at 25 °C." With that change the plan scores 3 marks.
:::

Q: An investigation tests the effect of nitrate concentration on the growth of duckweed (a floating plant). State the independent variable, the dependent variable and two variables to standardise, each with a value.
A: Independent: the concentration of nitrate in the water. Dependent: the number of fronds (or the mass of duckweed) after 7 days. Standardise, for example: temperature at 22 °C; volume of solution 100 cm³; the same number (5) of fronds at the start; the same lamp at 20 cm.
Q: Calculate the mass of sodium hydroxide needed to make 200 cm³ of a 5.0% (w/v) solution.
A: \(5.0 \times 200 \div 100 = 10\) g.
Q: Calculate the mass of sucrose (\(M = 342\) g mol⁻¹) needed to make 250 cm³ of a 0.40 mol dm⁻³ solution.
A: Moles \(= 0.40 \times 0.250 = 0.100\); mass \(= 0.100 \times 342 = 34.2\) g.
Q: You have a 1.5 mol dm⁻³ stock solution. Describe how to make 25 cm³ of a 0.30 mol dm⁻³ solution.
A: \(V_s = 0.30 \times 25 \div 1.5 = 5\) cm³ of stock. Mix 5 cm³ of stock with 20 cm³ of water.
Q: Describe how to make three solutions of 0.10, 0.010 and 0.0010 mol dm⁻³ from a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ solution by serial dilution.
A: Put 9 cm³ of water in each of three tubes. Add 1 cm³ of the 1.0 solution to tube 1 and mix (0.10). Transfer 1 cm³ from tube 1 to tube 2 and mix (0.010). Transfer 1 cm³ from tube 2 to tube 3 and mix (0.0010).
Q: Explain why each treatment in an investigation is repeated at least three times.
A: Repeats show whether the results are repeatable and allow a mean to be calculated, which reduces the effect of random variation. They also make an anomalous result easier to identify.
Q: A student wants to show that a plant extract slows the growth of bacteria on agar. Suggest a suitable control and explain its purpose.
A: Place a disc soaked in sterile water (instead of the extract) on the agar, with everything else the same. It shows that any clear zone is caused by the extract and not by the disc or the water.
Q: A balance reads 5.2 g, 5.2 g and 5.3 g when a standard mass of 5.00 g is weighed three times. Comment on the precision and accuracy.
A: The readings are close to each other, so they are precise. They are not close to the true value of 5.00 g, so they are not accurate.
Q: A student extracts pigment from beetroot with 95% ethanol warmed in a hot water bath. Give one hazard, rate the risk, and give a precaution.
A: Ethanol is flammable (fire, burns); the risk is medium (low probability but severe harm); use an electric water bath and keep naked flames away.
Q: "I will repeat the experiment to make it accurate." Explain what is wrong with this statement.
A: Repeating does not improve accuracy; accuracy comes from the apparatus and technique. Repeating shows repeatability and allows a mean to be calculated and anomalies to be found.

A: The independent variable is the concentration of the disinfectant and the dependent variable is the diameter of the clear zone around each disc. [B1]

A colorimeter can measure how cloudy a culture is. Plan an investigation. Include the way you would make a range of nitrate concentrations that covers four powers of ten.

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A student measures the volume of gas given off in 20 s by a yeast culture. In a measuring cylinder with 1 cm³ divisions she collects 12 cm³. She decides to use a gas syringe with 0.2 cm³ divisions. (a) Calculate the percentage uncertainty of the volume reading in each case. (b) The stopwatch is read to \(\pm 0.5\) s. Calculate the percentage uncertainty of the time and say which measurement limits the accuracy of the rate when the syringe is used. (c) Suggest one way to reduce this uncertainty.
H: The uncertainty of one reading is half the smallest division.
A: (a) Cylinder: \(0.5 \div 12 \times 100 = 4.2\%\) [M1 A1]; syringe: \(0.1 \div 12 \times 100 = 0.83\%\) [A1]
(b) Time: \(0.5 \div 20 \times 100 = 2.5\%\) [A1]
With the syringe, the time (2.5%) has a larger percentage uncertainty than the volume (0.83%), so the timing limits the accuracy of the rate. [B1]
(c) Collect gas for a longer time (for example 60 s) or use a timer with an automatic stop, so the same uncertainty is a smaller percentage. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which is the best description of a standardised variable?
+ A variable other than the independent variable that could affect the result and is kept the same in every treatment.
- The variable that the investigator changes on purpose.
- The variable that is measured to show the effect.
- The treatment that has no independent variable.
> The independent variable is changed, the dependent variable is measured and a control has the independent variable absent. A standardised variable is the other factor that must not change.
? A student makes 10 cm³ of each of five solutions from a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ stock. What volume of stock is needed for a 0.30 mol dm⁻³ solution?
+ 3.0 cm³
- 0.30 cm³
- 7.0 cm³
- 30 cm³
> \(V_s = 0.30 \times 10 \div 1.0 = 3.0\) cm³, with 7.0 cm³ of water. 7.0 cm³ is the volume of water, not stock.
? How many grams are in 500 cm³ of a 2.0% (w/v) solution?
+ 10 g
- 2.0 g
- 1.0 g
- 100 g
> 2.0% means 2.0 g in 100 cm³, so 500 cm³ contains \(2.0 \times 5 = 10\) g.
? A serial dilution starts with 1 cm³ of a 0.50 mol dm⁻³ solution added to 9 cm³ of water. What is the concentration of the first tube?
+ 0.050 mol dm⁻³
- 0.45 mol dm⁻³
- 0.50 mol dm⁻³
- 0.0050 mol dm⁻³
> The solution is diluted from 1 cm³ to 10 cm³, a tenfold dilution, so the concentration is \(0.50 \div 10 = 0.050\) mol dm⁻³.
? What does repeating each treatment three times and calculating a mean show or improve?
+ It shows repeatability and reduces the effect of random variation.
- It makes each measurement more accurate.
- It removes systematic errors.
- It makes the independent variable more precise.
> Repeats allow the investigator to see how close the readings are and to average out random variation. Accuracy comes from the apparatus, and a systematic error is repeated each time.
? Which is a suitable control for an investigation of the effect of a plant extract on the activity of an enzyme?
+ Water in place of the extract, with everything else the same.
- No enzyme and no extract.
- A different enzyme and the extract.
- Twice the volume of extract.
> The control has no extract but keeps every other factor the same. Changing two things (no enzyme and no extract) cannot show which factor matters.
? Which is the best precaution for working with a dilute irritant solution?
+ Wear eye protection and wash splashes off with water.
- Be careful.
- Avoid using it at all.
- Heat it to kill the hazard.
> A precaution must be a specific action that fits the hazard. "Be careful" is not specific, and heating does not remove the hazard.
? A graph shows a student's prediction as a line from the origin rising steadily. What does it predict?
+ The dependent variable is directly proportional to the independent variable.
- The dependent variable has an optimum value.
- The dependent variable levels off at a high value.
- The dependent variable does not depend on the independent variable.
> A straight line through the origin shows direct proportion. An optimum would show a peak; levelling off would show a limit.
:::

:::recap
- Plan in order: hypothesis and prediction, variables, vary the IV, measure the DV, standardise, control, replicates, method, data processing, risk.
- A hypothesis includes a biological reason; a prediction does not. A sketch graph has the IV on the x-axis, the DV on the y-axis and the right shape.
- Use at least five values of the IV, with a range that is justified. Name the apparatus and unit for the DV. Percentage uncertainty \(= \text{uncertainty} \div \text{reading} \times 100\).
- Standardise with a value and a method: water bath at 25 °C, 10 cm³ from a syringe, buffer of pH 7.
- % (w/v): grams per 100 cm³. mol dm⁻³: mass \(= c \times V \times M\). Proportional dilution: \(V_s = c_f V_f \div c_s\). Serial dilution: the same factor each time.
- A control changes one thing. Repeats show repeatability and give a mean; they do not make a measurement more accurate.
- Validity needs accurate measurement, repeatable results and controlled variables. Assess spread with SD, SE or 95% confidence intervals.
- A risk assessment names the hazard, its severity and probability, and a specific precaution.
:::
