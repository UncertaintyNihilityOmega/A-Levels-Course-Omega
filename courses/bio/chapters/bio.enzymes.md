## What an enzyme is

Inside a cell, thousands of different chemical reactions happen every second. At 37 °C most of them would be extremely slow on their own. Heating the cell to speed them up is not an option: high temperatures damage cells. Instead, cells use **catalysts** made of protein: enzymes.

:::def Catalyst
A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by the reaction.
:::

:::def Enzyme
A protein that acts as a biological catalyst. Enzymes are globular proteins, each with an active site that binds a specific substrate.
:::

An enzyme is a **globular protein** (see [Proteins](#bio.proteins)): one or more polypeptide chains folded into a roughly spherical shape. Hydrophilic R groups face outwards, so most enzymes are soluble in water. The tertiary structure, held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions between R groups, gives each enzyme a precise three-dimensional shape. A small part of that shape is the **active site**, where the reaction happens. Because the shape depends on the sequence of amino acids, each enzyme's job is set by its primary structure.

Enzymes are needed only in tiny amounts, because each molecule is unchanged at the end of the reaction and can be used again and again. Most enzyme names end in **-ase**, often added to the name of the substrate: maltase acts on maltose, lipase on lipids, protease on proteins.

### Inside or outside the cell

:::def Intracellular enzyme
An enzyme that catalyses reactions inside the cell that made it, for example catalase.
:::

:::def Extracellular enzyme
An enzyme that is secreted by a cell and catalyses reactions outside that cell, for example amylase in the mouth and small intestine.
:::

**Intracellular enzymes**

| Enzyme | Where it works | Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| catalase | liver and potato cells | hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen |
| DNA polymerase | nucleus | builds new DNA strands |

**Extracellular enzymes**

| Enzyme | Where it works | Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| salivary amylase | mouth | starch → maltose |
| pancreatic amylase | small intestine | starch → maltose |
| trypsin (a protease) | small intestine | proteins → shorter peptides |
| lysozyme | tears and saliva | breaks down bacterial cell walls |

Catalase protects cells. Hydrogen peroxide is a toxic by-product of some reactions in cells, and catalase breaks it down as fast as it forms:

\[\ce{2H2O2 -> 2H2O + O2}\]

Extracellular enzymes leave the cell by a route you met in [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells). The enzyme is made on ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum, folds inside the RER, travels in vesicles to the Golgi body, is packaged into secretory vesicles, and leaves when the vesicles fuse with the cell surface membrane.

:::key Enzymes in one box
- Enzymes are **globular proteins** that act as biological catalysts.
- **Intracellular** enzymes work inside the cell that made them; **extracellular** enzymes are secreted and work outside it.
- An enzyme is not used up: one molecule catalyses the same reaction many times.
:::

:::note Not all catalysts in cells are proteins
A few RNA molecules also catalyse reactions, but the syllabus treats enzymes as proteins, and so should your answers.
:::

## The active site and specificity

:::def Active site
The region of an enzyme molecule, formed by a small number of amino acids, where the substrate binds and the reaction is catalysed. Its shape is complementary to the shape of the substrate.
:::

:::def Substrate
The molecule (or molecules) that an enzyme acts on. The enzyme turns it into products.
:::

:::def Enzyme–substrate complex
The temporary structure formed when a substrate molecule is bound to the active site of an enzyme.
:::

Here is what happens in every enzyme-catalysed reaction, step by step.

:::steps
1. Enzyme and substrate molecules move randomly in solution and **collide**. Only a collision in which the substrate meets the active site, the right way round, can lead to a reaction.
2. The substrate binds to the active site because their shapes are **complementary**. It is held by temporary bonds (hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds) to R groups in the active site. This is the **enzyme–substrate complex**.
3. The reaction happens while the substrate is in the active site. The enzyme lowers the activation energy, so the reaction is fast.
4. The products are formed (an enzyme–product complex). Their shape no longer fits the active site, so they are released.
5. The enzyme is unchanged and its active site is free for another substrate molecule.
:::

In symbols, with E for enzyme, S for substrate and P for products:

\[\mathrm{E + S \rightarrow ES \rightarrow EP \rightarrow E + P}\]

:::def Specificity (of an enzyme)
An enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or one type of reaction, because only a substrate with a shape complementary to its active site can bind and form an enzyme–substrate complex.
:::

Specificity explains some facts you already know. Amylase hydrolyses the glycosidic bonds between α-glucose units in starch, but it cannot hydrolyse cellulose: in cellulose, β-glucose units are joined with every other unit turned over, so the region around each bond has a different shape that does not fit amylase's active site. Lactase hydrolyses lactose, but not sucrose or maltose.

:::warn "Same shape" loses the mark
The active site and the substrate are **complementary** in shape, like a hand and a glove, not "the same shape". Also write that the substrate binds to the **active site**, not just "to the enzyme".
:::

:::cols
:::col Lock-and-key
- Active site rigid, fixed shape.
- Exactly complementary to the substrate before it binds.
- Explains specificity simply.
- Cannot explain how binding strains the substrate, or why some enzymes accept a few similar substrates.
:::
:::col Induced fit
- Active site flexible.
- Close to complementary; becomes fully complementary as the substrate binds.
- Explains specificity too: only the right substrate can induce the right change.
- Explains how the enzyme strains bonds and lowers the activation energy; it is the model accepted today.
:::
:::

:::story Two models, sixty years apart
Emil Fischer suggested the lock-and-key idea in 1894, long before anyone could see the shape of a protein. In 1958 Daniel Koshland proposed induced fit, because some enzymes behaved as if their active sites changed shape. Later, images of enzyme structures showed active sites closing around their substrates, as induced fit predicts.
:::

## How enzymes lower the activation energy

A reaction can only start if the reacting molecules have enough energy to break or rearrange their bonds. That energy barrier is the activation energy (you meet the same idea in [Reaction kinetics](#chem.kinetics) in chemistry).

:::def Activation energy
The minimum energy that must be supplied to the reactants for a chemical reaction to take place.
:::

An enzyme lowers the activation energy by giving the reaction a different route, through the enzyme–substrate complex:
- **Breaking a substrate** (for example hydrolysis of starch): as the active site moulds around the substrate (induced fit), bonds in the substrate are strained and break more easily.
- **Joining substrates** (for example building DNA): the active site holds two substrate molecules close together and the right way round, so they react without needing high-energy collisions.
- **Charged R groups** in the active site attract or repel parts of the substrate, which also weakens particular bonds.

:::analogy A lower pass through the mountains
Think of the activation energy as a mountain between two valleys. Without an enzyme, only a few walkers have enough energy to climb over the summit. An enzyme opens a lower pass, so many more walkers cross in the same time. The two valleys stay at the same heights: the start and end of the journey do not change.
:::

:::key How an enzyme works (the marking points)
1. The substrate has a shape **complementary** to the **active site**.
2. Substrate and enzyme **collide**; the substrate **binds** to the active site, forming an **enzyme–substrate complex**.
3. (Induced fit) the active site **changes shape slightly** to fit the substrate closely, straining bonds.
4. The **activation energy is lowered**, so the reaction happens faster.
5. **Products** form, leave the active site, and the enzyme is **unchanged** and reused.
:::

:::eg Explain how an enzyme increases the rate of a reaction.
A full-mark answer gives four separate marking points, in order, each in mark-scheme language.

The substrate has a shape that is complementary to the enzyme's active site. [B1]

The substrate collides with and binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex. [B1]

The active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds (induced fit), which puts strain on bonds in the substrate. [B1]

This lowers the activation energy of the reaction, so more of the substrate molecules react per unit time; the products are released and the enzyme is reused. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the substrate fits the enzyme and the reaction is faster". It never names the active site, the enzyme–substrate complex or the activation energy, so it scores at most one mark.
:::

## Measuring the rate of an enzyme reaction

:::def Rate of reaction
The amount of product formed, or of substrate used up, per unit time, for example \(\mathrm{cm^3}\) of oxygen per second.
:::

There are two ways to follow an enzyme reaction:
- measure how fast a **product appears** (catalase: volume of oxygen), or
- measure how fast the **substrate disappears** (amylase: starch, detected with iodine).

### The progress curve and the initial rate

If you mix catalase with hydrogen peroxide and record the volume of oxygen collected over time, you get a **progress curve**. It is steep at the start, then gets less steep, then levels off.

:::plot Oxygen released by catalase over time. The gradient is greatest at the start; the tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate of 1.2 cm³ s⁻¹.
--f "48*(1-exp(-x/40))" --f "1.2*x+0*sqrt(46-x)" --label "oxygen collected" --label "tangent at t = 0" --x 0 140 --y 0 65 --xlabel "time / s" --ylabel "volume of oxygen / cm³" --pt "40,48,tangent reaches 48 cm³ at 40 s@e" --legend tr --alt "The volume of oxygen rises steeply at first, then more and more slowly, and levels off at 48 cm³ when all the hydrogen peroxide has been used. A straight tangent drawn at time zero passes through 48 cm³ at 40 s."
:::

Why does the curve level off? At the start the substrate concentration is highest, so enzyme–substrate collisions are most frequent and the most enzyme–substrate complexes form per second. As substrate is used up, collisions become less frequent and the rate falls. When all the substrate has been converted to product, the reaction stops and the curve is flat. The enzyme has not run out: the substrate has.

So the rate changes during the experiment. To compare experiments fairly, biologists use the **initial rate**: the rate at the very start, before the substrate concentration has fallen. You find it from the gradient of a **tangent** drawn to the curve at time zero.

:::key Finding the initial rate from a progress curve
1. Draw a straight tangent that touches the curve at \(t = 0\) and follows its initial slope.
2. Make a large triangle under the tangent (at least half the grid).
3. Initial rate \(= \dfrac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}\), with units, for example \(\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}\).
:::

:::eg Catalase from potato was mixed with hydrogen peroxide at 30 °C. Use the results to find the mean rate over the first 30 s and the initial rate.
| time / s | oxygen / cm³ |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 10 | 11 |
| 20 | 19 |
| 30 | 25 |
| 40 | 30 |
| 60 | 37 |
| 80 | 42 |
| 100 | 44 |
| 120 | 46 |

**Mean rate over the first 30 s**: divide the volume collected by the time taken.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mean rate} &= \frac{25\,\mathrm{cm^3}}{30\,\mathrm{s}} \\ &= 0.83\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

**Initial rate**: plot the graph and draw the tangent at \(t = 0\) (as in the graph above). The tangent passes through \((0, 0)\) and \((40\,\mathrm{s}, 48\,\mathrm{cm^3})\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{initial rate} &= \frac{48 - 0}{40 - 0} \\ &= 1.2\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

The mean rate over 30 s is lower than the initial rate, because the rate was already falling during those 30 s as hydrogen peroxide was used up. Between 60 s and 120 s the mean rate is only \((46 - 37) \div 60 = 0.15\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}\).
:::

:::lab Measuring the rate of product formation with catalase
**Aim**: follow the breakdown of hydrogen peroxide by catalase by measuring the volume of oxygen produced.

:::steps
1. Put a fixed volume (for example 10 cm³) of hydrogen peroxide solution of known concentration in a conical flask, and stand it in a water bath at a set temperature for 5 minutes.
2. Prepare the catalase source: a fixed volume of yeast suspension, or potato discs of the same size and number (a cork borer and ruler keep the surface area the same).
3. Add the catalase, put the bung in at once and start the stopwatch.
4. Read the volume of oxygen in the gas syringe every 10 s for 2 minutes (or collect the gas in an upturned measuring cylinder full of water).
5. Repeat at least three times and calculate mean volumes. Plot volume against time and find the initial rate from a tangent.
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is whatever you are investigating (for example hydrogen peroxide concentration); the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen (or the initial rate). Standardise the temperature (water bath), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, the amount of catalase (same volume of yeast suspension, or same number and size of potato discs), and the pH (add a buffer).

**Control**: repeat with boiled (denatured) yeast suspension. No oxygen should be collected, which shows that the oxygen comes from catalase activity.

**Typical results**: a progress curve like the graph above; a higher hydrogen peroxide concentration gives a steeper start and more oxygen in total.

**Sources of error**: some oxygen escapes before the bung is in; the syringe plunger can stick; potato discs vary in catalase content; the temperature of the flask drifts. Improvements: add the catalase from a small tube tipped over inside the closed flask, use a gas syringe rather than a cylinder, check the flask temperature with a thermometer, and repeat.

**Safety**: hydrogen peroxide is an irritant to the eyes and skin. Wear eye protection and wash splashes off with water. Risk: low at the dilute concentrations used.

A quick alternative: soak small discs of filter paper in catalase solution and drop them into hydrogen peroxide. Oxygen bubbles make each disc float up; the time to rise is shorter when the rate is higher.
:::

:::lab Measuring the rate of substrate disappearance with amylase
**Aim**: follow the hydrolysis of starch by amylase by testing samples with iodine at regular intervals.

:::steps
1. Put one drop of iodine solution in each well of a spotting tile.
2. Put fixed volumes of starch suspension and of amylase solution in separate tubes in a water bath for 5 minutes, so both reach the bath temperature.
3. Mix them and start the stopwatch at once.
4. Every 30 s, use a clean pipette (or glass rod) to put one drop of the mixture into the next well of iodine.
5. Record the time of the first well that stays orange-brown: starch has all been hydrolysed. This is the **end point**.
6. Repeat three times and calculate the mean time. The rate is \(\dfrac{1}{\text{time}}\), in \(\mathrm{s^{-1}}\).
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is, for example, amylase concentration or temperature; the dependent variable is the time to reach the end point. Standardise the volume and concentration of starch, the volume of amylase, the temperature, the pH (buffer) and the size of the drops.

**Control**: boiled amylase, or water instead of amylase. The iodine stays blue-black throughout, showing that starch is broken down only by active amylase.

**Typical results**: a higher amylase concentration gives a shorter time to the end point, so a higher rate.

**Sources of error**: judging when the blue-black colour has gone is subjective; the end point lies somewhere in the 30 s before the first orange-brown well, so short times are uncertain; carrying mixture over on a dirty pipette. Improvements: sample more often (every 10 s), compare with a colour standard (a well of iodine with water), or use a colorimeter.

**Safety**: iodine solution stains and irritates the eyes; enzyme solutions can cause allergic reactions. Wear eye protection and avoid skin contact. Risk: low.
:::

### Using a colorimeter

A colorimeter measures how much light a coloured solution absorbs. It turns a colour judgement ("fairly blue") into a number, so it is used for any enzyme reaction with a colour change.

:::def Colorimeter
An instrument that passes light of a chosen colour through a sample and measures how much is absorbed (absorbance) or how much passes through (transmission). The more coloured substance in the sample, the higher the absorbance.

:::key Using a colorimeter to follow an enzyme reaction
1. **Filter**: choose the filter of the colour the solution absorbs most, which is the complementary colour (a red filter for a blue or blue-black solution).
2. **Blank**: fill a cuvette with everything except the coloured substance (for starch tests, iodine solution in water) and set the reading to zero absorbance (100% transmission).
3. **Calibration curve**: measure the absorbance of solutions of known concentration, plot absorbance against concentration and draw the line of best fit.
4. **Samples**: take samples of the reaction mixture at set times, treat them in the same way (for example, add the same volume of iodine) and read the absorbance. Read each concentration from the calibration curve.
5. Keep the cuvettes clean and use the same cuvette, the same way round, so only the sample changes.
:::

## Temperature

Every enzyme has an **optimum temperature**, at which its rate of reaction is highest. For many human enzymes it is close to body temperature, about 37–40 °C.

:::plot A model enzyme: the rate rises with temperature up to the optimum, then falls steeply as the enzyme is denatured.
--f "7*2**(x/10)/(1+exp((x-45)/2.5))+0*sqrt(64-x)" --x 0 80 --y 0 130 --xlabel "temperature / °C" --ylabel "rate / arbitrary units" --label "rate of reaction" --pt "41,100,optimum@ne" --pt "50,26.7,denaturation@e" --legend tr --alt "The rate rises slowly from 7 units at 0 °C, roughly doubling for each 10 °C rise, to a peak of 100 units at the optimum near 41 °C. It then falls steeply and reaches almost zero by 60 °C."
:::

**Below the optimum**, raising the temperature increases the rate:
- Enzyme and substrate molecules gain **kinetic energy** and move faster.
- They **collide more frequently**, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per unit time.
- More collisions have energy above the activation energy.
- For many enzymes the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise in this range.

**Above the optimum**, the rate falls quickly:
- The enzyme molecules **vibrate** more strongly.
- **Hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds** (and hydrophobic interactions) that hold the tertiary structure **break**.
- The tertiary structure, and so the **shape of the active site, changes**.
- The substrate is no longer complementary to the active site, so **fewer enzyme–substrate complexes** form.
- The enzyme is **denatured**. For most enzymes this is permanent.

:::def Denaturation
A change in the tertiary (three-dimensional) structure of a protein, caused by breaking the bonds that hold it, so the protein no longer works. An enzyme's active site loses its shape and can no longer bind its substrate.
:::

**At low temperatures**, such as 0–5 °C, an enzyme is not denatured: it is only slow, because molecules move slowly and collide rarely. If it is warmed again, its activity returns. This is why food in a fridge spoils more slowly.

The optimum depends on where the enzyme normally works. Taq polymerase, from a bacterium that lives in hot springs, works best at about 72 °C. Its tertiary structure has more bonds holding it, so it is not denatured at temperatures that would destroy human enzymes. It is used in the polymerase chain reaction (see [Tools of genetic technology](#bio.genetech)).

:::warn Enzymes are not alive
Enzymes are molecules, so they cannot be "killed". Write "denatured", and explain it as bonds breaking and the active site changing shape. Also, high temperature breaks **hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds**, not peptide bonds: the primary structure stays the same.
:::

:::exam Describe, then explain
"Describe" means say what the data show: the trend, the turning point and figures with units ("the rate increases from 12 to 70 arbitrary units between 10 °C and 40 °C"). "Explain" means give the biological reasons (kinetic energy, collisions, enzyme–substrate complexes, bonds, active site). When a question says "describe and explain", do both, and keep them in the same order as the graph.
:::

## pH and buffer solutions

pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions: the lower the pH, the higher the concentration of \(\ce{H+}\) ions. Each enzyme works best at its own **optimum pH**, usually the pH of the place where it works.

:::plot Rate against pH for two enzymes: pepsin from the stomach has an optimum near pH 2, and salivary amylase near pH 7.
--f "100*exp(0-((x-2)/1.3)**2)+0*sqrt(5.5-x)" --f "100*exp(0-((x-7)/1.3)**2)+0*sqrt(x-3.5)+0*sqrt(10.5-x)" --label "pepsin (stomach protease)" --label "salivary amylase" --x 0 14 --y 0 135 --xlabel "pH" --ylabel "rate / % of maximum" --legend tr --alt "Two bell-shaped curves. Pepsin's rate peaks at 100% at pH 2 and is almost zero above pH 5. Salivary amylase peaks at 100% at pH 7 and is almost zero below pH 4 and above pH 10."
:::

Why does a change in pH matter? Many R groups in an enzyme carry a charge, such as –COO⁻ or –NH₃⁺, and that charge depends on the concentration of \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{OH-}\) ions around them.
- Away from the optimum, extra \(\ce{H+}\) ions (low pH) or \(\ce{OH-}\) ions (high pH) **change the charges on R groups**.
- **Ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds** between R groups are broken, so the **tertiary structure and the active site change shape**.
- Charged R groups in the active site that attract the substrate may lose or change their charge, so the substrate **cannot bind** as well.
- Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form, so the rate falls.

A small change from the optimum is usually reversible: return the pH and activity comes back. A large change denatures the enzyme permanently.

:::def Buffer solution
A solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added, so it keeps the pH of a reaction mixture constant at a known value.
:::

In a pH investigation, buffers do two jobs: they set each pH you want to test, and they keep it constant even though some reactions release or use up \(\ce{H+}\) ions (for buffers in chemistry, see [Acids and bases](#chem.acids)).

:::warn pH does not "heat" the enzyme
Extreme pH and high temperature both denature, but for different reasons. Temperature works through **vibration**; pH works through **ions changing the charges on R groups**. Give the right cause for the factor in the question.
:::

## Enzyme concentration

If there is plenty of substrate, more enzyme means more active sites, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per unit time. The initial rate is **directly proportional** to the enzyme concentration.

If the amount of substrate is limited, adding more and more enzyme eventually stops helping: there is not enough substrate to fill the extra active sites. **Substrate concentration** has become the limiting factor and the graph levels off.

:::plot Initial rate against enzyme concentration: a straight line when substrate is in excess; levelling off when the substrate runs short.
--f "20*x+0*sqrt(5.5-x)" --f "(20*x+60-abs(20*x-60))/2" --label "substrate in excess" --label "limited substrate" --x 0 7 --y 0 130 --xlabel "enzyme concentration / arbitrary units" --ylabel "initial rate / arbitrary units" --legend tr --alt "Both lines rise together in a straight line through the origin. The substrate-in-excess line keeps rising to 110 units at 5.5. The limited-substrate line becomes flat at 60 units from an enzyme concentration of 3."
:::

:::tip Name the limiting factor
On any rate graph: where the line is rising, the factor on the x-axis is limiting the rate. Where it is flat, something else is limiting it. Say what that is (for example "the substrate concentration") and why ("all the substrate molecules are already bound").
:::

## Substrate concentration

Now keep the enzyme concentration fixed and increase the substrate concentration.

:::plot Initial rate against substrate concentration for a fixed amount of enzyme. The rate rises steeply, then levels off at the maximum rate Vmax.
--f "100*x/(3+x)" --x 0 40 --y 0 130 --hline 100 --xlabel "substrate concentration / mmol dm⁻³" --ylabel "initial rate / arbitrary units" --label "initial rate" --pt "2,40,substrate limiting@se" --pt "30,90.9,enzyme limiting@s" --pt "38,100,Vmax@n" --legend tr --alt "The rate rises steeply and almost in a straight line at low substrate concentrations, then curves and levels off, approaching a dashed line at the maximum rate of 100 units."
:::

- **At low substrate concentration**, many active sites are empty. Adding substrate means more frequent collisions and more enzyme–substrate complexes, so the rate rises steeply. Substrate concentration is the limiting factor.
- **As the concentration increases further**, more and more active sites are occupied at any moment, so each extra substrate molecule adds less to the rate.
- **At high substrate concentration**, all the active sites are occupied all the time: the enzyme is **saturated**. The rate is at its maximum and cannot rise further. Enzyme concentration (the number of active sites) is now the limiting factor.

:::def Vmax (maximum rate of reaction)
The maximum initial rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction, reached when the substrate concentration is so high that all the active sites are occupied.
:::

Raising the enzyme concentration raises Vmax, because there are more active sites to fill. Vmax is used to work out a value that compares how well different enzymes bind their substrates: that is the next chapter, [Enzyme kinetics, inhibitors and immobilised enzymes](#bio.enzymes2).

:::warn Do not say "the enzyme is used up"
At the plateau the enzyme is still there and still working at full speed. The rate stops rising because every active site is already busy, not because the enzyme has run out.
:::

## Inhibitor concentration

:::def Inhibitor
A substance that binds to an enzyme and reduces the rate of the reaction it catalyses.
:::

Some inhibitors have a shape similar to the substrate and bind to the active site, competing with the substrate: they are **competitive inhibitors**. Others bind somewhere else on the enzyme and change the shape of the active site: they are **non-competitive inhibitors**. Both kinds, and how to tell them apart from data, are in [Enzyme kinetics, inhibitors and immobilised enzymes](#bio.enzymes2).

For this chapter you need the effect of **inhibitor concentration**. With the enzyme and substrate concentrations fixed:
- More inhibitor molecules bind to more enzyme molecules at any moment.
- So fewer active sites are available to the substrate, and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form per unit time.
- The rate falls as inhibitor concentration rises. It falls most steeply at first, then levels off towards a very low rate when nearly every enzyme molecule is inhibited.

:::eg Real context, biological washing powder: explain why a biological washing powder removes egg stains well at 40 °C, but works less well at 90 °C.
Biological washing powders contain enzymes, including proteases and lipases, produced by bacteria. Egg stains are mostly protein.

At 40 °C, proteases in the powder hydrolyse the peptide bonds in the egg proteins. [B1]

The products are shorter peptides and amino acids, which are soluble and wash away in the water. [B1]

At 90 °C, hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds in the protease break, so the shape of its active site changes: the enzyme is denatured. [B1]

The egg protein is no longer complementary to the active site, so no enzyme–substrate complexes form, and only the detergent in the powder cleans. [B1]

The same idea saves energy: because the enzymes work at low temperatures, clothes can be washed at 30–40 °C instead of in hot water.
:::

A: (a) Maltose (a reducing sugar) reduces blue copper(II) ions to a brick-red precipitate, which is filtered off. [B1]
More maltose leaves fewer copper(II) ions in the filtrate, so it is less blue and absorbs less light. [B1]
(b) Concentrations: \((0.90 - 0.66) \div 0.075 = 3.2\) and \((0.90 - 0.39) \div 0.075 = 6.8\,\mathrm{mmol\,dm^{-3}}\) [M1] [A1]
Rate \(= (6.8 - 3.2) \div 4 = 0.90\,\mathrm{mmol\,dm^{-3}\,min^{-1}}\) [A1]
(c) It gives a quantitative and objective reading, so it does not depend on judging colour. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.cells) ★★★ Cells in the pancreas make amylase and secrete it into the small intestine. (a) Outline how amylase is made and secreted by a pancreatic cell. (b) Amylase cannot hydrolyse cellulose. Explain why.
H: Start at the ribosomes and follow the protein through the cell to the cell surface membrane.
H: For (b), compare the glucose units and bonds in starch and cellulose, then use the word "complementary".
A: (a) Amylase is made on ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum, where amino acids are joined into a polypeptide. [B1]
The polypeptide folds into its tertiary structure in the RER and is carried in vesicles to the Golgi body. [B1]
The Golgi body modifies the protein and packages it into secretory vesicles. [B1]
The vesicles move to the cell surface membrane and fuse with it, releasing amylase outside the cell (exocytosis). [B1]
(b) Cellulose is made of β-glucose, with every other monomer turned over, so its glycosidic bonds and their surroundings have a different shape from those in starch. [B1]
Cellulose is not complementary to the active site of amylase, so it cannot bind and no enzyme–substrate complexes form. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] (#bio.proteins) ★★★ In one enzyme, an amino acid with a negatively charged R group in the active site normally forms an ionic bond with the substrate. A change in the gene replaces this amino acid with one that has a non-polar R group. (a) Suggest and explain the effect of this change on the activity of the enzyme. (b) A different change replaces an amino acid on the surface of the enzyme, far from the active site, with another amino acid with a similar R group. Suggest why the activity of the enzyme is not affected.
H: Which bond can the new R group no longer form?
H: For (b), what decides the shape of the active site?
A: (a) The non-polar R group cannot form an ionic bond with the substrate. [B1]
So the substrate binds less well to the active site, or the active site's shape is changed. [B1]
Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form, so the activity of the enzyme decreases. [B1]
(b) The new R group forms the same kinds of bond as the old one, so the tertiary structure is not changed. [B1]
The shape of the active site is unchanged, so the substrate is still complementary and binds as before. [B1]
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The temperature coefficient, \(Q_{10}\), compares rates 10 °C apart: \(Q_{10} = \dfrac{\text{rate at } (T + 10)\,°\mathrm{C}}{\text{rate at } T\,°\mathrm{C}}\). An enzyme had rates of 6.0, 12.6, 25.2, 37.8 and 18.9 arbitrary units at 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C. (a) Calculate \(Q_{10}\) between 10 °C and 20 °C, and between 30 °C and 40 °C. (b) Suggest why \(Q_{10}\) between 30 °C and 40 °C is lower than between 10 °C and 20 °C. (c) \(Q_{10}\) between 40 °C and 50 °C is 0.5. State what a value below 1 shows.
H: Divide the higher-temperature rate by the lower-temperature rate.
H: For (b), two effects happen at once as the temperature rises: one speeds the reaction up, the other slows it down.
A: (a) \(Q_{10} = 12.6 \div 6.0\) [M1] \(= 2.1\) between 10 °C and 20 °C [A1]
and \(37.8 \div 25.2 = 1.5\) between 30 °C and 40 °C. [A1]
(b) Between 30 °C and 40 °C some enzyme molecules start to denature, as hydrogen and ionic bonds break and active sites change shape. [B1]
So the increase in rate from more frequent collisions is partly cancelled by the loss of working active sites. [B1]
(c) The rate is lower at the higher temperature: denaturation now outweighs the effect of increased kinetic energy, so the temperature is above the optimum. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ In an investigation, a protease from a fish that lives in water at about 2 °C was compared with a protease from a mammal. At 5 °C the fish protease had a higher rate than the mammal protease. The fish protease had an optimum temperature of about 25 °C and was denatured above 35 °C; the mammal protease had an optimum near 40 °C. Scientists found that the fish protease has fewer hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds holding its tertiary structure. Use this information to suggest explanations for these results, and suggest one advantage of using such an enzyme in washing powder.
H: Fewer bonds means a more flexible molecule. How might flexibility help at low temperature, given the induced-fit model?
H: Fewer bonds also means less energy is needed to break them.
A: With fewer bonds, the fish protease is more flexible, so its active site can change shape around the substrate (induced fit) easily even when molecules have little kinetic energy. [B1]
So at 5 °C more enzyme–substrate complexes form and the activation energy is still lowered, giving a higher rate than the mammal enzyme. [B1]
Fewer bonds also means less energy is needed to break the bonds that hold the tertiary structure. [B1]
So the fish protease is denatured at a lower temperature (above 35 °C), and its optimum is lower than the mammal enzyme's. [B1]
In washing powder it would work at low wash temperatures, saving the energy needed to heat the water. [B1]
:::

:::quiz
? Which statement about the active site of an enzyme is correct?
+ Its shape is complementary to the shape of the substrate.
- Its shape is identical to the shape of the substrate.
- It is the part of the substrate that binds to the enzyme.
- It is formed by all the amino acids in the enzyme.
> The substrate and active site fit together like a hand in a glove: complementary, not identical. The active site is part of the enzyme and is formed by only a few of its amino acids.
? An enzyme is heated to 70 °C and denatured. Which bonds are broken?
+ hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds between R groups
- peptide bonds between amino acids
- glycosidic bonds in the enzyme
- covalent bonds in the substrate
> Denaturation changes the tertiary structure, which is held by hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and hydrophobic interactions. The peptide bonds of the primary structure are not broken, and enzymes contain no glycosidic bonds.
? How does an enzyme affect a reaction?
+ It lowers the activation energy but does not change the overall energy change.
- It supplies the activation energy to the substrate.
- It lowers both the activation energy and the overall energy change.
- It raises the temperature of the substrate molecules.
> The enzyme provides a route with a lower energy barrier. The energies of the substrate and the products, and so the overall energy change, are the same with or without it.
? On a graph of initial rate against substrate concentration, what limits the rate where the curve has levelled off?
+ the number of active sites (the enzyme concentration)
- the substrate concentration
- the activation energy
- the amount of product present
> At the plateau every active site is occupied. Adding more substrate cannot help, but adding more enzyme (more active sites) would raise the rate.
? An amylase reaction reached its end point in 250 s. What is the rate of reaction?
+ \(4.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
- \(4.0 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
- \(2.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
- \(250\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)
> Rate \(= 1/t = 1/250 = 0.0040 = 4.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). \(4.0 \times 10^{-2}\) is a decimal slip, \(2.5 \times 10^{-3}\) muddles the digits of 250 into the answer, and \(250\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) forgets to take the reciprocal.
? Why is a buffer solution used in an enzyme investigation?
+ It keeps the pH constant at a known value.
- It provides the hydrogen ions the enzyme needs to react.
- It stops the enzyme from being denatured at high temperatures.
- It acts as a second substrate for the enzyme.
> A buffer resists changes in pH, so pH can be set as the independent variable or kept as a standardised variable. It has no effect on denaturation by heat.
? Which observation supports the induced-fit hypothesis but not the lock-and-key hypothesis?
+ The shape of the active site changes as the substrate binds.
- Each enzyme catalyses only one type of reaction.
- The enzyme is unchanged at the end of the reaction.
- Boiling stops the enzyme working.
> Both models explain specificity and an unchanged enzyme, and both are consistent with denaturation. Only induced fit has an active site that changes shape.
? Samples from an amylase–starch mixture are mixed with iodine and read in a colorimeter. What happens to the absorbance as the reaction goes on?
+ It decreases, because less starch is left to form the blue-black colour.
- It increases, because more maltose is made.
- It stays the same, because iodine is not used up.
- It increases, because the enzyme becomes coloured.
> Absorbance measures the blue-black starch–iodine colour. As starch is hydrolysed, the colour fades and less light is absorbed. Maltose gives no colour with iodine.
? An enzyme is kept at 5 °C. What happens to its activity?
+ It is low, but it returns to normal when the enzyme is warmed.
- It is zero, because the enzyme is denatured.
- It is low, because the active site has changed shape permanently.
- It is higher than at 37 °C, because the enzyme is more stable.
> At low temperature molecules have little kinetic energy and collide rarely, so the rate is low. The bonds of the tertiary structure are not broken, so the enzyme is not denatured.
? Which enzyme is intracellular?
+ catalase in liver cells
- salivary amylase
- trypsin in the small intestine
- lysozyme in tears
> Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide inside the cells that make it. The other three are secreted and work outside the cells.

:::recap
- Enzymes are globular proteins that act as biological catalysts. Intracellular enzymes (catalase) work inside cells; extracellular enzymes (amylase, trypsin) are secreted.
- The active site has a shape complementary to the substrate. Substrate binds to form an enzyme–substrate complex; products are released and the enzyme is reused.
- Specificity: only a substrate complementary to the active site can bind.
- Lock-and-key: rigid active site. Induced fit: the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds, straining bonds; this is the accepted model.
- Enzymes lower the activation energy; they do not change the overall energy change.
- Rate = amount of product formed (or substrate used) per unit time. Initial rate = gradient of the tangent at \(t = 0\). From an end-point time, rate = \(1/t\).
- Catalase: measure oxygen volume (gas syringe). Amylase: sample into iodine until it stays orange-brown. Colorimeter: filter, blank, calibration curve, absorbance.
- Temperature: more kinetic energy, more collisions, more enzyme–substrate complexes up to the optimum; above it, hydrogen and ionic bonds break, the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured.
- pH: \(\ce{H+}\) or \(\ce{OH-}\) ions change the charges on R groups, breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds; buffers set and keep the pH.
- Enzyme concentration: rate proportional when substrate is in excess. Substrate concentration: rate rises to Vmax when all active sites are occupied. Inhibitor concentration: more inhibitor, lower rate.
:::
