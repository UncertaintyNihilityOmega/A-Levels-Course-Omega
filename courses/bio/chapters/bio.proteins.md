## Amino acids

Proteins are polymers. The monomers are **amino acids**. A protein contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and many contain a little sulfur as well. Every amino acid has the same four groups bonded to one central carbon atom.

:::def Amino acid
A small molecule with a central carbon atom bonded to an amine group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom and a variable side chain called the R group. Amino acids are the monomers of proteins.
:::

There are 20 different amino acids in proteins. They all have the same amine group, carboxyl group and hydrogen, and **differ only in the R group**. The R group can be a single hydrogen atom, a short chain, a ring, or a group that carries a charge. The R group is what decides how an amino acid behaves, and so how the protein folds.

| Amino acid | R group | What it is like |
|---|---|---|
| glycine | –H | smallest; fits into tight spaces |
| alanine | –CH₃ | non-polar, hydrophobic |
| serine | –CH₂OH | polar, hydrophilic (can form hydrogen bonds) |
| aspartic acid | –CH₂COOH | carries a negative charge (–COO⁻) |
| lysine | –(CH₂)₄NH₂ | carries a positive charge (–NH₃⁺) |
| cysteine | –CH₂SH | contains sulfur, can form a disulfide bond |

You do not need to learn these structures. You do need to know that **some R groups are hydrophobic, some are polar, some are charged, and cysteine contains sulfur**, because these four facts explain the four kinds of bond that hold a protein in shape. For more on the chemistry of amino acids, see [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen).

## The peptide bond

:::def Peptide bond
A covalent bond between the carbon of the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the nitrogen of the amine group of the next. It is formed in a condensation reaction, which removes a molecule of water.
:::

Two amino acids join when the –OH of the carboxyl group of one and an –H of the amine group of the other leave together as water. The carbon and nitrogen left behind join: that is the **peptide bond** (–CO–NH–). The product is a **dipeptide**. A third amino acid can join to the free end of the dipeptide, and so on.

:::def Polypeptide
A chain of many amino acids joined by peptide bonds. A protein consists of one or more polypeptides.
:::

The reverse reaction is hydrolysis: a peptide bond is broken by adding a molecule of water. This is what protease enzymes do when you digest protein. A polypeptide of \(n\) amino acids has \(n - 1\) peptide bonds, and \(n - 1\) molecules of water were removed when it was made. The same counting rule applied to sugar chains in [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs).

:::eg Draw the formation of a dipeptide from two amino acids, and name the bond formed.
Draw each amino acid with its amine group on the left and its carboxyl group on the right, with the R group below the central carbon.

Each amino acid is drawn with an amine group (H₂N), a central carbon with a hydrogen and an R group, and a carboxyl group (COOH). [B1]

The –OH of the carboxyl group of the first amino acid and an –H of the amine group of the second are removed. [B1]

A molecule of water is released: this is a condensation reaction. [B1]

The carbon of the first carboxyl group joins the nitrogen of the second amine group by a peptide bond (C–N, written –CO–NH–). [B1]

A weaker answer draws the two amino acids joined end to end but forgets the water, or labels the bond as a "glycosidic bond" or a "hydrogen bond".
:::

:::eg A polypeptide has 146 amino acids. State the number of peptide bonds and the number of molecules of water released when it was made. How many different sequences are possible for a polypeptide of three amino acids chosen from the 20 amino acids?
Use the \(n - 1\) rule, then count the choices at each position.

Peptide bonds \(= 146 - 1 = 145\), and 145 molecules of water were released. [B1]

At each of the three positions there are 20 possible amino acids, so the number of sequences is the product of the choices. [M1]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{sequences} &= 20 \times 20 \times 20 \\ &= 20^3 \\ &= 8000 \end{aligned}\]

There are 8000 different tripeptides. [A1]

The same idea gives the variety of proteins: a chain of only 100 amino acids has \(20^{100}\), about \(1.3 \times 10^{130}\), possible sequences, far more than the number of atoms in the universe. The order of amino acids, not the list, is what makes each protein unique.
:::

## Four levels of protein structure

A polypeptide chain does not stay as a straight string. It folds into a precise three-dimensional shape, and the shape is built up in four levels.

:::def Primary structure
The sequence (order) of amino acids in a polypeptide chain, joined by peptide bonds. It is determined by the gene that codes for the protein (a gene is a length of DNA that codes for one polypeptide).
:::

:::def Secondary structure
The regular folding of parts of a polypeptide chain into an α-helix or a β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of the peptide bonds along the chain. The R groups are not involved.
:::

:::def Tertiary structure
The precise, compact three-dimensional shape of a whole polypeptide, formed by further folding and held by interactions between the R groups of the amino acids.
:::

:::def Quaternary structure
The arrangement of two or more polypeptide chains (and sometimes non-protein groups) in one protein molecule. Not every protein has it: only those with more than one polypeptide.
:::

Read the figure from top to bottom. The **primary** structure is just the order. The **secondary** structure appears as the chain starts to fold: in an α-helix the chain coils like a spring, and in a β-pleated sheet it lies in zigzag strands side by side. Both are held by many hydrogen bonds between the backbone groups, one hydrogen bond between every few amino acids. The **tertiary** structure folds the whole chain into a ball or a rod, and the **quaternary** structure puts two or more chains together.

### What holds a protein in shape

The primary structure is held by strong covalent peptide bonds. Above that, a protein is held in shape by four kinds of interaction between the R groups of the amino acids (and, for secondary structure, hydrogen bonds between the backbone groups). The backbone is the repeating N–C–C chain of the polypeptide, without the R groups.

| Interaction | Between which groups | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydrophobic interactions** | non-polar (hydrophobic) R groups, which cluster together in the middle of the protein, away from water | weak each, but many |
| **Hydrogen bonds** | polar R groups, such as –OH, and groups with a δ+ hydrogen and a δ− oxygen or nitrogen | weak each, but many |
| **Ionic bonds** | oppositely charged R groups, such as –NH₃⁺ and –COO⁻ | stronger than hydrogen bonds |
| **Disulfide bonds** | the sulfur atoms of two cysteine R groups (–S–S–) | strongest: a covalent bond |

The same four interactions hold the chains together in a quaternary structure. Hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds are broken by heat and by changes in pH, so they are what change when a protein is **denatured** (denatured = the tertiary structure is changed so the protein stops working; see [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes)). Disulfide bonds are covalent bonds and are not broken by small changes in temperature or pH. Peptide bonds are not broken by denaturation either, so the primary structure is unchanged.

:::key Levels of structure and what holds them
- **Primary:** order of amino acids; **peptide bonds**.
- **Secondary:** α-helix or β-pleated sheet; **hydrogen bonds** between C=O and N–H of the backbone.
- **Tertiary:** 3D folding of one polypeptide; **R-group interactions**: hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds.
- **Quaternary:** two or more polypeptides together (same interactions).
- The primary structure decides all the others: a different sequence means different R groups, so different bonds and a different fold.
:::

:::warn Which bond holds which level?
The tertiary structure is **not** held by peptide bonds: those hold the primary structure. The secondary structure is held by hydrogen bonds between the **backbone** groups, **not** by R groups. And disulfide bonds form between the R groups of **cysteine** only, not between "any two amino acids". Mark schemes check each of these.
:::

:::eg Explain the meaning of the terms primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure of a protein.
Give each level its own marking point, with the bond that holds it.

Primary structure is the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide, held by peptide bonds. [B1]

Secondary structure is the folding of the chain into an α-helix or β-pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of the backbone. [B1]

Tertiary structure is the further folding of the whole polypeptide into its 3D shape, held by bonds between R groups (hydrogen, ionic and disulfide bonds and hydrophobic interactions). [B1]

Quaternary structure is the way that two or more polypeptide chains are arranged in one molecule. [B1]

A weaker answer repeats "folding" for every level with no bonds, or says that peptide bonds hold the tertiary structure.
:::

:::eg Real context: one amino acid can change the whole protein. In an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction by binding a molecule called the substrate at a small region called the active site), an amino acid on the surface of the folded protein with a polar R group is replaced by one with a non-polar R group. Suggest why this might change how the protein works.
Think of the chain: R group → bonds → folding → shape → function.

The new amino acid has a different R group, so the R group may no longer form the same hydrogen bonds or ionic bonds. [B1]

The tertiary structure may fold differently, because the bonds between R groups hold the shape. [B1]

If the shape changes, the shape of the active site (or of a binding site) may change, so the substrate is no longer complementary (no longer fits) and the protein works less well. [B1]

The effect depends on where the change is. A change far from the active site, to an R group of similar type, may do nothing. Some changes in a gene cause disease: in sickle cell anaemia, one hydrophilic amino acid in the β-globin chain is replaced by a hydrophobic one, and haemoglobin molecules stick together. The genetics is in [Genes, phenotype and gene control](#bio.genecontrol).
:::

## Globular and fibrous proteins

Proteins fall into two broad groups, by shape.

:::def Globular protein
A protein whose polypeptide chain(s) fold into a compact, roughly spherical shape. Globular proteins are generally soluble in water and have physiological roles, such as enzymes, haemoglobin and many hormones.
:::

:::def Fibrous protein
A protein whose polypeptide chains form long strands or sheets. Fibrous proteins are generally insoluble in water and have structural roles, such as collagen and keratin.
:::

| Feature | Globular | Fibrous |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | compact, rounded | long strands or fibres |
| Solubility in water | generally soluble | generally insoluble |
| R groups on the outside | hydrophilic | many hydrophobic (or the chains pack too tightly for water to enter) |
| Role | physiological: enzymes, haemoglobin, some hormones | structural: collagen, keratin |

**Why globular proteins are soluble.** When a globular protein folds, the **hydrophobic R groups point to the middle**, away from water, and the **hydrophilic R groups face outwards**. Water molecules are attracted to the hydrophilic surface and surround the protein, so it dissolves. That solubility lets enzymes and haemoglobin work in the watery cytoplasm and in blood plasma.

**Why fibrous proteins are insoluble.** Fibrous proteins have long strands with a repeating primary structure, packed tightly side by side and bonded together. Many of their surface R groups are hydrophobic, and the strands are held so tightly that water cannot get between them. Being insoluble, they are strong and stable, and they stay where they are built.

## Haemoglobin: a globular protein

Haemoglobin is the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs to the tissues. It is a standard example of a globular protein with **quaternary structure**.

:::def Haemoglobin
A globular protein with quaternary structure, made of four polypeptide chains (two α-globin and two β-globin), each with a haem group containing one iron ion. It carries oxygen in the blood.
:::

- **Four polypeptides.** Two **α-globin** chains and two **β-globin** chains fit together in a compact shape (the quaternary structure). Each chain is folded by its own tertiary structure and mostly coiled into α-helices.
- **Haem groups.** Each polypeptide holds a **haem group**. A haem group is not a protein: it is a non-protein group, built round a single iron ion, \(\ce{Fe^2+}\). Haemoglobin is therefore a protein with four non-protein groups attached.
- **Oxygen binds to iron.** One oxygen molecule \(\ce{O2}\) binds, reversibly, to the \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) in each haem. One haemoglobin molecule can carry **four** \(\ce{O2}\) molecules, eight oxygen atoms.

How the structure suits the function:
- **Soluble**: the hydrophilic R groups face outwards, so haemoglobin dissolves in the cytoplasm of red blood cells, packed in huge numbers.
- **Iron in the haem group** binds oxygen: without iron there is nothing to hold the oxygen. The binding is reversible: oxygen is picked up in the lungs, where there is a lot of it, and released in the tissues, where there is little.
- **Four chains** (and four haem groups) carry four oxygen molecules in each molecule, so each red blood cell carries a great deal of oxygen.
- **Quaternary structure** is needed for the shape to change as oxygen binds: when one oxygen molecule binds, the shape of the whole molecule alters and the next oxygen binds more easily (see [Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide](#bio.oxygen)).

:::warn Haem is not the protein, and iron is not "oxygen-carrying" by itself
Haem is the **non-protein group** and haemoglobin is the protein. Say "the haem group contains an iron ion (Fe²⁺) to which oxygen binds". A mark scheme gives a mark for "iron in haem" and another for "binds oxygen reversibly", not for "iron carries oxygen".
:::

:::eg Relate the structure of haemoglobin to its function.
Use structure → function pairs, one marking point for each pair.

Haemoglobin is a globular protein with hydrophilic R groups on the outside, so it is soluble and can be carried in the cytoplasm of red blood cells. [B1]

It is made of four polypeptide chains (two α-globin and two β-globin), so it has quaternary structure. [B1]

Each chain has a haem group, so one molecule has four haem groups. [B1]

Each haem group contains an Fe²⁺ ion that binds one oxygen molecule, so one haemoglobin molecule carries up to four oxygen molecules. [B1]

The binding is reversible, so haemoglobin picks up oxygen in the lungs and releases it in the tissues. [B1]

A weaker answer says "haemoglobin has iron to carry oxygen" without the haem group, the reversible binding or the number of oxygen molecules.
:::

## Collagen: a fibrous protein

Collagen is the most abundant protein in the human body. It is found in tendons, ligaments, skin, bone, cartilage and the walls of arteries. It must resist **pulling forces** (tension) without stretching or breaking.

:::def Collagen
A fibrous protein made of three polypeptide chains wound together into a triple helix. Collagen molecules are joined into fibrils and fibres. It has a structural role and very high tensile strength.
:::

1. **Three polypeptides** wind round each other into a **triple helix**, the collagen molecule. About every third amino acid is **glycine**, the smallest amino acid (its R group is a single hydrogen atom). Glycine can fit in the centre of the helix where the chains pack closely, so the helix is tight.
2. **Hydrogen bonds** form between the three chains and hold the triple helix together.
3. Collagen molecules lie side by side, and their ends are **staggered**, so that there is no weak line across the fibril where all the ends meet.
4. **Covalent cross-links** form between neighbouring molecules, tying them together.
5. Many molecules form a **fibril**, and fibrils bundle into **fibres**.

How the structure suits the function:
- The **tight triple helix** with hydrogen bonds between the chains is very strong when pulled: high **tensile strength**.
- **Cross-links** between molecules and **staggered ends** stop molecules sliding past one another or breaking along one line.
- Collagen is **not elastic**: it does not stretch, which is what a tendon needs when it transmits the pull of a muscle to a bone.
- It is **insoluble** (a fibrous protein), so it stays in place.

| Tissue | What collagen does |
|---|---|
| tendons | transmit force from muscle to bone without stretching |
| skin | gives strength and resists tearing |
| bone | collagen fibres reinforce bone and give it some flexibility |
| artery walls | resist the high pressure of the blood so the wall does not burst |

:::note Vitamin C and scurvy
Cells need vitamin C to make collagen with strong cross-links. Without it the collagen is weaker, which causes scurvy: bleeding gums, bruising and wounds that heal poorly.
:::

:::warn Collagen is strong, not stretchy
Collagen has high **tensile strength** but is **not elastic**. Do not write "collagen is elastic" or "stretchy". Say "strong when pulled" and "does not stretch". Also, its strength comes from hydrogen bonds between the three chains **and** covalent cross-links between molecules, not from a single bond type.
:::

### Haemoglobin and collagen compared

| Feature | Haemoglobin | Collagen |
|---|---|---|
| Type | globular | fibrous |
| Polypeptides | 4 (2 α, 2 β) | 3 (wound into a triple helix) |
| Shape | compact, roughly spherical | long rope-like molecule, in fibres |
| Solubility | soluble (hydrophilic R groups outside) | insoluble |
| Non-protein group | haem with Fe²⁺ | none |
| Special features | binds oxygen reversibly | glycine every third amino acid; cross-links |
| Function | carries oxygen | gives tensile strength to tissues |

:::eg Compare the structure and function of haemoglobin and collagen.
Both are proteins with more than one polypeptide, so start with a similarity. Six marking points.

Both are proteins made of polypeptide chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, and both have a quaternary structure of more than one polypeptide. [B1]

Haemoglobin is globular and collagen is fibrous. [B1]

Haemoglobin has four polypeptides (two α-globin and two β-globin) and collagen has three, wound into a triple helix. [B1]

Haemoglobin is soluble, because hydrophilic R groups are on the outside; collagen is insoluble. [B1]

Haemoglobin contains haem groups with Fe²⁺, which bind oxygen reversibly, so it carries oxygen; collagen has no haem group. [B1]

Collagen has cross-links between molecules and hydrogen bonds between chains, giving high tensile strength, so it has a structural role in tendons, skin and artery walls. [B1]

A weaker answer describes one protein and then the other with no matching feature. A comparison needs both proteins in each point.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: Name the four groups attached to the central carbon atom of an amino acid.
A: An amine group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom and an R group.
Q: State what makes one amino acid different from another, and how many different amino acids are found in proteins.
A: Only the R group differs. There are 20.
Q: A polypeptide has 250 amino acids. State the number of peptide bonds, and the number of water molecules released in its formation.
A: \(250 - 1 = 249\) peptide bonds and 249 water molecules.
Q: Name the type of reaction that forms a peptide bond and the type that breaks it.
A: Formed by condensation (water removed); broken by hydrolysis (water added).
Q: Name the bond that holds the primary structure of a protein, and one type of bond that holds the tertiary structure.
A: Primary: peptide bond (covalent). Tertiary: any one of hydrogen bond, ionic bond, disulfide bond, or hydrophobic interaction between R groups.
Q: Between which amino acids do disulfide bonds form? Why is a disulfide bond stronger than a hydrogen bond?
A: Between two cysteine amino acids (the sulfur atoms of their R groups). It is a covalent bond, whereas a hydrogen bond is a weak attraction.
Q: State what holds the α-helix of a secondary structure in shape.
A: Hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of the peptide bonds in the backbone.
Q: A protein is heated and its tertiary structure changes. State which bonds are broken and which are not.
A: Hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds (and hydrophobic interactions) are broken. Peptide bonds are not broken, so the primary structure is unchanged.
Q: Explain why most globular proteins are soluble in water.
A: Hydrophilic R groups are on the outside of the folded protein, attracted to water, and hydrophobic R groups are in the middle.
Q: State the metal ion in a haem group and the number of oxygen molecules that one haemoglobin molecule can carry.
A: Iron, \(\ce{Fe^2+}\). Four oxygen molecules (one per haem group).
Q: Explain why glycine occurs at about every third position in a collagen polypeptide.
A: Glycine has the smallest R group (a hydrogen atom), so it fits in the centre of the triple helix, where the three chains are packed tightly together.

:::

:::checklist What you can now do
- [ ] I can describe and draw the general structure of an amino acid, and the formation and breakage of a peptide bond.
- [ ] I can explain what primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary structure mean.
- [ ] I can describe the interactions that hold a protein in shape: hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds and disulfide bonds.
- [ ] I can state that globular proteins are generally soluble and have physiological roles, and fibrous proteins are generally insoluble and have structural roles.
- [ ] I can describe the structure of haemoglobin: two α-globin chains, two β-globin chains and four haem groups.
- [ ] I can relate the structure of haemoglobin to its function, including the role of the iron in haem.
- [ ] I can describe the structure of collagen, from triple helix to fibres.
- [ ] I can relate the structure of collagen molecules and fibres to their function.
:::

:::recap
- An amino acid has an amine group, a carboxyl group, H and an R group on one carbon. 20 amino acids differ only in R. Peptide bond: condensation, water removed; a polypeptide of \(n\) amino acids has \(n - 1\) peptide bonds.
- Primary: sequence (peptide bonds). Secondary: α-helix or β-pleated sheet (hydrogen bonds in the backbone). Tertiary: 3D folding (R-group interactions). Quaternary: more than one polypeptide.
- Bonds in tertiary structure: hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bonds (covalent, between cysteines, the strongest).
- Denaturation breaks hydrogen and ionic bonds, changing the tertiary structure; peptide bonds are not broken.
- Globular: compact, soluble (hydrophilic R groups outside), physiological roles. Fibrous: strands, insoluble, structural roles.
- Haemoglobin: 2 α-globin + 2 β-globin, 4 haem groups, each with Fe²⁺ that binds one O₂ reversibly: up to 4 O₂ per molecule.
- Collagen: three polypeptides in a triple helix, glycine every third amino acid, hydrogen bonds between chains, staggered molecules with covalent cross-links, fibrils then fibres: high tensile strength, not elastic.
:::
