## Chromosomes

A human cell contains about 2 m of DNA, packed into a nucleus only a few micrometres across. A cell can only manage this because the DNA is wound up tightly. When the cell is not dividing, the DNA is spread through the nucleus as thin threads of **chromatin**. Before the cell divides, the chromatin coils up and becomes short, thick **chromosomes** that can be moved around without tangling or breaking.

:::def Chromosome
A structure made of one very long DNA molecule wound round proteins. In a cell that is about to divide it is condensed, so that it can be seen with a light microscope.
:::

:::def Histone
A protein with a positive charge, around which the DNA molecule is wound. Histones also help to coil the DNA into a compact shape.
:::

DNA has a negative charge, because of its phosphate groups, and histones are positively charged, so they attract each other. The DNA is wound round groups of histone proteins like thread round a row of beads, and the "beads on a string" are then coiled again and again, which shortens and thickens the chromosome.

### Sister chromatids, centromere and telomeres

Before a cell divides, it makes an exact copy of every DNA molecule (the next chapter, [Nucleic acids and DNA replication](#bio.dna), explains how). After copying, each chromosome consists of **two identical sister chromatids**, held together at one point.

:::def Sister chromatids
The two identical copies of a chromosome that are made when its DNA is replicated. They are joined at the centromere until the cell divides.
:::

:::def Centromere
The region where two sister chromatids are joined. Spindle fibres attach to it so that the chromatids can be pulled apart.
:::

:::def Telomere
A region at each end of a chromosome, made of a short sequence of bases repeated many times. It is non-coding DNA, so it carries no genes, and it protects the genes at the end of the chromosome.
:::

:::key Chromosome structure: the five terms
- **DNA**: one long molecule in each chromosome (or in each chromatid, once copied).
- **Histone proteins**: the DNA is wound round them and coiled up to make the chromosome short and thick.
- **Sister chromatids**: the two identical copies of a replicated chromosome.
- **Centromere**: joins the sister chromatids and is where spindle fibres attach.
- **Telomeres**: the ends of the chromosome; repeated non-coding sequences.
:::

### Counting chromosomes and chromatids

The most common mistakes in this topic are counting errors. Learn this rule and every count follows from it.

:::key Counting rule
- A chromosome is **one DNA molecule** (with its proteins) when it has not been copied.
- After DNA replication a chromosome has **two chromatids** and contains **two DNA molecules**. It is still **one chromosome**.
- When the sister chromatids separate in anaphase, **each chromatid is now a chromosome**.
:::

A human body cell has 46 chromosomes. They come in 23 pairs, and the two chromosomes of a pair are the same length and carry the same genes in the same order (one came from each parent; see [Meiosis](#bio.meiosis)). In mitosis each chromosome behaves on its own and the pairs do not join up.

| stage | chromosomes | chromatids | DNA molecules |
|---|---|---|---|
| G1 (not yet copied) | 46 | 0 | 46 |
| G2 (copied) | 46 | 92 | 92 |
| metaphase | 46 | 92 | 92 |
| anaphase (whole cell) | 92 | 0 | 92 |
| each daughter cell | 46 | 0 | 46 |

:::warn 46 chromosomes in G2, not 92
A human cell in G2 has 92 chromatids but only **46 chromosomes**, because the two chromatids of one chromosome count as one chromosome. Say "46 chromosomes, each made of two chromatids".
:::

## Why cells divide: the importance of mitosis

:::def Mitosis
Nuclear division in which a parent nucleus produces two daughter nuclei that have the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and are genetically identical to it and to each other.
:::

"Genetically identical" means that the daughter nuclei have the same DNA base sequences, so the same genes. The only differences are rare mutations. Mitosis is therefore used whenever an organism needs more cells of the same kind, with the same instructions:

- **Growth of multicellular organisms.** A fertilised egg divides by mitosis to make an embryo, and the embryo grows into an adult by more mitosis. Every cell receives the same genes, so each can later become a specialised cell. In plants, new cells are made in growing regions such as the tips of roots and shoots.
- **Replacement of damaged or dead cells.** Cells wear out. Skin cells are lost from the surface all the time, and the cells that line your gut are replaced within days. New cells are made by mitosis.
- **Repair of tissues by cell replacement.** After a cut or a broken bone, cells next to the damage divide by mitosis to fill the gap.
- **Asexual reproduction.** One parent makes offspring by mitosis, so the offspring are genetically identical to the parent: clones. Examples are a yeast cell budding, a strawberry plant sending out runners and a *Hydra* growing a bud that breaks off. Asexual reproduction is quick and needs no partner, but the offspring show no genetic variation.

:::eg Explain the importance of mitosis in the repair of a cut in the skin.
Cells at the edge of the wound divide by mitosis to make new cells that replace the ones that were damaged or killed. [B1]

The DNA is replicated first, and the sister chromatids are separated, so each daughter cell receives a full set of chromosomes. [B1]

The daughter cells are genetically identical to the original cells, so they have the same genes and can become the same type of skin cell. [B1]

A weaker answer says "mitosis makes new cells". This is true of any cell division, and gains no mark unless it says what the new cells replace and that they are genetically identical.
:::

## The mitotic cell cycle

A cell does not spend its life dividing. Between divisions it grows and works, and it must make a second copy of everything before it can split in two. The repeated sequence of events from one division to the next is the **cell cycle**.

:::def Mitotic cell cycle
The sequence of events in a cell between one nuclear division and the next. It has three parts: interphase (G1, S and G2), mitosis (division of the nucleus) and cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
:::

:::cols
:::col Interphase
Everything between one division and the next. Not a "resting" phase: the cell is busy. It is usually the longest part of the cycle.
- **G1** (the first gap): the cell grows, makes proteins and new organelles and carries out its normal job.
- **S** (synthesis): the DNA is replicated. Each chromosome becomes two sister chromatids.
- **G2** (the second gap): the cell grows more, copies organelles, makes the proteins needed for mitosis (for example, the proteins of the spindle) and checks that the DNA has been copied. In animal cells the centrioles are copied during interphase.
:::
:::col Division
- **Mitosis**: the nucleus divides to make two nuclei with identical chromosomes. It has four stages: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase.
- **Cytokinesis**: the cytoplasm divides, and the cell surface membrane (and a cell wall, in plants) forms between the two nuclei. This makes two separate daughter cells.
:::
:::

DNA is replicated in S phase of interphase and **not** in prophase. By the time mitosis starts, every chromosome has already been copied. Mitosis only shares the copies out.

The amount of DNA in the cell shows this clearly. It stays constant in G1, doubles during S phase and stays doubled in G2 and until the chromatids are separated. It halves again when the cell divides in two.

## The stages of mitosis

Mitosis is one continuous process, but biologists divide it into four stages so that they can describe it. The names are the same in animal and plant cells. The photographs in textbooks show cells with their chromosomes stained, but the drawings below show the same features. The figures show an animal cell with four chromosomes: two long and two short. The two long ones are a pair and so are the two short ones.

Each stage is described by what happens to four things: the **chromosomes**, the **nuclear envelope**, the **spindle** and the **cell surface membrane**.

:::def Spindle
A structure made of protein fibres (microtubules) that stretches from the two poles of the cell. Spindle fibres attach to the centromeres of the chromosomes and move them. In animal cells the spindle forms from the centrioles; plant cells have no centrioles, and their spindle forms from the poles of the cell.
:::

**Prophase**
- The chromosomes condense: they coil up, shorten and thicken, so that they become visible as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere.
- The nucleolus disappears.
- In an animal cell the two pairs of centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and spindle fibres start to grow between them.
- Near the end of prophase the nuclear envelope breaks down into small pieces, so the chromosomes now lie free in the cytoplasm.

**Metaphase**
- The spindle is complete and the nuclear envelope has gone.
- The chromosomes line up across the middle of the cell (the equator).
- Each chromosome is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from **both** poles: one chromatid faces each pole.

**Anaphase**
- The centromeres divide, so the sister chromatids separate. Each is now a chromosome.
- The spindle fibres shorten, pulling the chromosomes to opposite poles, centromere first, so the arms trail behind in a V-shape. This movement uses energy from ATP.
- Now there are two identical sets of chromosomes, one at each pole. The cell has twice as many chromosomes as before, for a short time.

**Telophase**
- The chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil. They become long and thin again (chromatin).
- A nuclear envelope forms around each group of chromosomes and the nucleolus reappears. There are now two nuclei.
- The spindle fibres break down.
- The cell begins to divide. In an animal cell the cell surface membrane starts to be pulled inwards.

### Cytokinesis: animal and plant cells

Mitosis divides the nucleus. **Cytokinesis**, the division of the cytoplasm that forms two cells, usually starts in late anaphase or telophase and finishes after it. It is not part of mitosis.

A plant cell cannot be pinched in two, because it has a rigid cell wall. Instead, it builds a new wall from the inside outwards.

:::warn Four mistakes to avoid
- DNA is replicated in S phase, not in prophase.
- The chromosomes **line up** in metaphase and are **pulled apart** in anaphase. Do not write "the chromosomes are pulled apart in metaphase".
- Cytokinesis is not part of mitosis. It is the division of the cytoplasm after the nucleus has divided.
- A plant cell does not "pinch in". It builds a cell plate. And do not give plant cells centrioles.
:::

## Recognising the stages

In exams you identify a stage from a drawing or from a photomicrograph (a photograph taken through a microscope). Work through four questions, in this order:

:::steps
1. **Is there a nucleus with an envelope round it?** If yes, and the chromosomes are not visible as separate structures, the cell is in **interphase**. (A nucleolus is often visible.)
2. **Are the chromosomes condensed and visible, and scattered?** If the nuclear envelope has gone or is breaking down: **prophase**.
3. **Are the chromosomes in a line across the middle?** **Metaphase**.
4. **Are there two groups?** If the chromatids are V-shaped and moving apart: **anaphase**. If the groups are at the poles, have uncoiled, and a nuclear envelope (or cell plate) is forming between them: **telophase**.
:::

In a stained photomicrograph the chromosomes absorb the stain and look dark, while the cytoplasm is paler. Interphase nuclei are round and fairly uniform, with a darker nucleolus. In prophase you see dark threads or short rods. In metaphase the chromosomes make a dark line, in anaphase two parallel dark lines or two clumps, and in telophase two dark clumps with a new cell plate between them. Look at every cell on a slide before you decide: most cells are in interphase.

:::lab Looking at mitosis: a root tip squash
**Aim**: prepare a temporary slide of a root tip to see cells in different stages of mitosis, and to find the mitotic index.

The cells at the very tip of a root are dividing, because the tip is a growing region. Cells further up are growing longer and no longer dividing.

:::steps
1. Cut off the last 2–3 mm of a root tip (from a germinating onion, garlic or broad bean) and put it into warm (about 60 °C) 1 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid for 5 minutes. The acid breaks down the material that sticks the cells together (and stops mitosis), so the cells can be spread out.
2. Rinse the tip in water, and place it on a slide.
3. Add a few drops of a stain that binds DNA (acetic orcein or toluidine blue) and wait 2–5 minutes.
4. Break up the tip with a mounted needle and lower a cover slip over it with a needle, without trapping bubbles.
5. Cover the slide with filter paper and press down firmly on the cover slip with your thumb, without moving it sideways. This squashes the cells into a thin, single layer.
6. Look at the slide with the ×10 objective lens, find the area with small square cells in rows, then use ×40 to identify the stages and count the cells.
:::

**Variables and counting**: choose several fields of view, and in each count every cell and the number in each stage of mitosis. Count at least 100 cells in total, and preferably several hundred. A small count is unreliable, because it samples few cells.

**Typical results**: most cells (often over 80%) are in interphase. Of the cells in mitosis, prophase is usually the commonest and anaphase the rarest, because the stages take different lengths of time.

**Sources of error**: judging between neighbouring stages (the stages blend into one another); counting a cell twice; a thick or uneven squash with overlapping cells; counting only one part of the slide. Improvements: more fields of view, a second person counting the same fields, and a grid on the eyepiece to count each cell once.

**Safety**: 1 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is an irritant, so wear eye protection and wash off any splashes. Stains mark skin and clothes. Keep scalpels, razor blades and needles away from your fingers. Risk: low.
:::

## Mitotic index

If mitosis is common in a tissue, the tissue is growing or being repaired. A single number measures this.

:::formula Mitotic index
\[\text{mitotic index} = \frac{\text{number of cells in mitosis}}{\text{total number of cells}}\]
Give it as a decimal or multiply by 100 for a percentage. Count the cells in **all four stages**, and count the total number of cells **including those in interphase**.
:::

A high mitotic index means that many cells are dividing: for example, in a growing root tip or in a tumour. Mitosis happens in a fixed fraction of the time of each cycle, so **the fraction of cells in a stage equals the fraction of the cycle spent in that stage**. That lets you find how long each stage lasts when the length of the whole cycle is known.

:::key Time spent in a stage
\[\text{time in a stage} = \frac{\text{cells in that stage}}{\text{total cells}} \times \text{time for the whole cell cycle}\]
This works because the cells on a slide are at random points in their cycle, so the proportion of cells in a stage matches the proportion of the cycle that the stage takes.
:::

:::eg A student counted 500 cells in a root tip squash and noted the stage of each. In this root the whole cell cycle takes 20 h. Calculate the mitotic index and the time that a cell spends in each stage of mitosis.
The counts were: prophase 52, metaphase 12, anaphase 7, telophase 19 and interphase 410.

**Step 1: add the cells in mitosis.**

\[\begin{aligned} \text{cells in mitosis} &= 52 + 12 + 7 + 19 \\ &= 90 \end{aligned}\]

**Step 2: mitotic index.** The total number of cells is 500, and includes the interphase cells.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mitotic index} &= \frac{90}{500} \\ &= 0.18 \quad (18\%) \end{aligned}\]

**Step 3: time in each stage**, as (cells in the stage ÷ 500) × 20 h:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{prophase} &= \frac{52}{500} \times 20 = 2.08\,\mathrm{h} \\ \text{metaphase} &= \frac{12}{500} \times 20 = 0.48\,\mathrm{h} \\ \text{anaphase} &= \frac{7}{500} \times 20 = 0.28\,\mathrm{h} \\ \text{telophase} &= \frac{19}{500} \times 20 = 0.76\,\mathrm{h} \end{aligned}\]

Anaphase is the shortest stage (about 17 minutes) and prophase the longest (about 2 hours), which agrees with the counts. All of mitosis takes \(0.18 \times 20 = 3.6\,\mathrm{h}\), and interphase the other \(16.4\,\mathrm{h}\).
:::

## Telomeres

Every time a cell copies its DNA, the machinery that does the copying cannot reach the very end of the chromosome, so a short stretch at the end is not copied. The new DNA molecule is therefore slightly shorter than the one it was copied from. (The next chapter shows why: DNA polymerase works in only one direction.)

If this happened to genes, each division would delete part of a gene. **Telomeres** prevent this. They are made of a short sequence of bases repeated many times, and they carry no genes. Each replication removes a little of the telomere, not of the genes behind it.

:::key What telomeres do
1. The end of a chromosome is **not fully copied** at each DNA replication, so the chromosome gets shorter.
2. The telomere is **non-coding DNA**, so what is lost is part of the telomere, not genes.
3. Telomeres therefore **prevent the loss of genes** from the ends of chromosomes.
4. Telomeres get a little shorter at every division. When they are very short, the cell stops dividing, which limits the number of times a cell can divide.
:::

:::note Extra (not in the syllabus): telomerase
Stem cells and most cancer cells have an enzyme, telomerase, which adds repeats back to the ends of the chromosomes, so their telomeres do not shorten as quickly. This helps to explain why a tumour can keep dividing.
:::

## Stem cells

A tissue that loses cells all the time needs a supply of new ones. The cells that supply them are stem cells.

:::def Stem cell
An unspecialised cell that can divide by mitosis to make more stem cells and that can also differentiate (become specialised) into one or more types of specialised cell.
:::

When a stem cell divides, one daughter cell can stay a stem cell, which keeps the supply going, while the other differentiates into a cell that does a particular job, such as a red blood cell. So stem cells **replace** cells that have died and **repair** damaged tissue, and they do it by mitosis, so every new cell has the same genes as the stem cell.

- **Bone marrow** contains stem cells that divide to make all the types of blood cell, including red blood cells and white blood cells. A person has to replace millions of these cells every day.
- The **lower layer of the skin** contains stem cells that make new skin cells to replace those lost from the surface.
- In plants, the **cambium** and the tips of roots and shoots contain cells that stay unspecialised and divide.

Cells in an early embryo can make every type of cell in the body, but stem cells in an adult make only a few types, such as blood cells.

:::eg Real context, leukaemia: a patient with leukaemia has a bone marrow transplant. Explain why transplanted bone marrow can restore the patient's blood cells.
Bone marrow contains stem cells. [B1]

The stem cells divide by mitosis to make more stem cells, which keeps a supply of cells in the marrow. [B1]

Some of the daughter cells differentiate into red blood cells and white blood cells, which replace the patient's damaged cells. [B1]

(Before the transplant, the patient's own diseased marrow is destroyed, so that the donor's stem cells can take over.)
:::

## Uncontrolled division and tumours

The cell cycle is controlled. Genes make proteins that start the cycle when new cells are needed and proteins that stop it when they are not. The cell cycle also stops if the DNA is damaged.

A **mutation** is a change in the base sequence of a gene (see [Protein synthesis and gene mutation](#bio.synthesis)). If a mutation damages a gene that controls the cell cycle, the protein it makes may not work. The cell can then divide when it should not, or ignore signals to stop. Mutations are caused by chance errors when DNA is copied and by carcinogens such as ultraviolet light and chemicals in tobacco smoke.

:::steps
1. A mutation changes a gene that controls the cell cycle.
2. The cell no longer responds to the signals that tell it to stop dividing.
3. The cell divides by mitosis again and again, and so do its daughter cells. Division is **uncontrolled**.
4. The cells form a mass of cells: a **tumour**.
:::

:::def Tumour
A mass of cells that is formed when cells divide by mitosis in an uncontrolled way. 
:::

:::note Benign and malignant tumours
A **benign** tumour grows slowly, stays in one place (often with a capsule round it) and does not spread; it can still cause harm by pressing on organs. A **malignant** tumour (a cancer) grows faster and invades nearby tissues; its cells can break off, travel in the blood or lymph, and form secondary tumours elsewhere (**metastasis**).
:::

Cancer treatments use the fact that tumour cells divide rapidly. **Radiotherapy** damages the DNA of dividing cells. **Chemotherapy** uses drugs that block cell division. Some drugs stop the spindle forming, so that the chromosomes cannot be pulled apart in anaphase. Other drugs prevent DNA replication, so that cells cannot pass S phase. Cells that cannot complete the cycle die. Normal cells that divide often are also damaged, which explains the side effects: hair follicle cells, cells of the gut lining and bone marrow stem cells all divide rapidly, so patients may lose hair, feel sick and become more likely to get infections.

:::eg Real context, chemotherapy: a drug used to treat cancer stops spindle fibres from forming. Explain why the drug slows the growth of a tumour, and why it causes hair loss.
Without a spindle, the chromosomes cannot be pulled to the poles of the cell in anaphase. [B1]

The cells cannot complete mitosis or divide into two, so the cells do not increase in number and the cells that are stopped die. [B1]

Tumour cells divide rapidly, so many of them are affected by the drug. [B1]

Cells in hair follicles also divide rapidly by mitosis, so they are also affected, and the hair falls out. [B1]
:::

(a) State the number of nuclei in the cell and the number of chromosomes in each nucleus after mitosis. (b) State the DNA content of the cell, in units of the G1 DNA content. (c) The cell then starts a second cycle. State the DNA content of the cell and the total number of chromatids in G2 of this second cycle.
H: Mitosis divides the nucleus; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.
H: DNA is replicated again in the next S phase.
A: (a) Two nuclei, [B1] each with 46 chromosomes. [B1]
(b) 2 units, because the DNA was replicated once and the cell did not divide into two. [B1]
(c) 4 units, because the DNA of both nuclei is replicated again in S phase. [B1]
Total chromatids: 92 chromosomes, each with two chromatids, so 184 chromatids. [B1]
:::

:::quiz
? A human cell in G2 contains how many chromosomes and chromatids?
+ 46 chromosomes and 92 chromatids
- 92 chromosomes and 92 chromatids
- 46 chromosomes and 46 chromatids
- 92 chromosomes and 184 chromatids
> DNA is copied in S phase, so each of the 46 chromosomes has two sister chromatids: 92 chromatids. The chromatids count as chromosomes only after they separate in anaphase.
? During which stage of mitosis do the chromosomes line up on the equator of the cell?
+ metaphase
- prophase
- anaphase
- telophase
> The chromosomes line up in metaphase. They are pulled apart in anaphase, and uncoil in telophase.
? In which part of the cell cycle is DNA replicated?
+ S phase of interphase
- prophase of mitosis
- G2 of interphase
- cytokinesis
> The S in S phase stands for synthesis of DNA. By G2 the copying is finished, and mitosis only shares out the copies.
? What makes a cell plate during cytokinesis in a plant cell?
+ vesicles from the Golgi body fusing at the equator
- the cell surface membrane being pulled inwards
- the spindle fibres, which form a wall
- the nuclear envelope, which breaks up into pieces
> The Golgi vesicles fuse to form new membranes and carry the material for a new cell wall. Pulling the membrane in is the way an animal cell divides.
? What is the mitotic index of a sample of 180 cells in which 27 cells are in mitosis?
+ 0.15
- 0.18
- 6.7
- 0.85
> \(27 \div 180 = 0.15\). 0.18 divides by only the 153 interphase cells (27 ÷ 153 = 0.176), 6.7 turns the fraction upside down, and 0.85 is the fraction of cells **not** in mitosis.
? What is the function of the telomeres at the ends of a chromosome?
+ They are non-coding sequences, so the loss of DNA at each replication does not remove genes.
- They join the two sister chromatids together.
- They attach the chromosome to the spindle fibres.
- They code for the histone proteins that wind up the DNA.
> The centromere joins the chromatids and attaches to the spindle. Telomeres are repeated non-coding sequences that are shortened instead of genes.
? Which description of a stem cell is correct?
+ It is unspecialised, divides by mitosis and can differentiate into specialised cells.
- It is specialised and divides by meiosis to make gametes.
- It is unspecialised and cannot divide again.
- It is specialised and can change into any other cell type.
> Stem cells divide by mitosis to renew themselves and to produce cells that differentiate. Specialised cells cannot change into other types.
? What happens in anaphase?
+ The centromeres divide and the chromatids are pulled to opposite poles.
- The chromosomes condense and become visible.
- A nuclear envelope forms around each group of chromosomes.
- The DNA is copied so that each chromosome has two chromatids.
> Condensation is prophase, new envelopes form in telophase, and the DNA is copied in S phase.
? Which statement about benign and malignant tumours is correct?
+ Cells of a malignant tumour can spread to other parts of the body and form secondary tumours.
- A benign tumour is formed without uncontrolled mitosis.
- A malignant tumour grows more slowly than a benign tumour.
- Only a benign tumour is made of cells that have mutated.
> Both kinds are caused by uncontrolled mitosis after mutation. Only a malignant tumour invades tissue and spreads (metastasis).
? A student states that plant cells have centrioles that form the spindle. What is wrong?
+ Plant cells have no centrioles; their spindle forms from the poles of the cell.
- Plant cells have no spindle, because their cell wall holds the chromosomes in place.
- Only plant cells have centrioles; animal cells form the spindle from the nucleus.
- Plant cells have centrioles, but they take no part in mitosis.
> Animal cells form the spindle from centrioles, plant cells do not have them, but both cells have a spindle that moves the chromosomes.

:::recap
- A chromosome is one DNA molecule wound round histone proteins. After S phase it has two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere; telomeres (repeated non-coding sequences) cap the ends.
- Counting rule: a copied chromosome is still one chromosome (two chromatids, two DNA molecules). Separated chromatids become chromosomes in anaphase.
- Mitosis makes genetically identical daughter nuclei: growth, replacement of dead cells, tissue repair and asexual reproduction.
- Cell cycle: interphase (G1 growth, S DNA replication, G2 growth), mitosis, cytokinesis. DNA doubles in S phase and halves when the cell divides.
- Prophase: chromosomes condense, nuclear envelope breaks down, spindle forms. Metaphase: chromosomes line up on the equator. Anaphase: centromeres divide, chromatids pulled to the poles. Telophase: chromosomes uncoil, nuclear envelopes re-form.
- Cytokinesis: animal cells form a cleavage furrow; plant cells form a cell plate from Golgi vesicles. Plant cells have no centrioles.
- Mitotic index = cells in mitosis ÷ total cells. Time in a stage = (cells in stage ÷ total cells) × cycle time.
- Telomeres: the end of the chromosome is not fully copied, so telomeres shorten instead of genes.
- Stem cells are unspecialised, divide by mitosis and differentiate to replace and repair cells.
- A mutation in a gene that controls the cell cycle can cause uncontrolled mitosis and a tumour; malignant tumours spread (metastasis).
:::
