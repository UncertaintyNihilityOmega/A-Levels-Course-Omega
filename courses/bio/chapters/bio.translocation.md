## Sources and sinks

Plants make sugar in the leaves by photosynthesis. They use sugar everywhere: in roots, in new leaves, in flowers and fruits, and in storage organs. **Translocation** is the transport of organic substances in a plant, in the phloem, from where they are made or stored to where they are needed.

:::def Assimilates
Organic substances made by a plant from the products of photosynthesis, which are transported in the phloem, such as sucrose and amino acids.
:::

:::def Source
A part of a plant that **loads** assimilates into the phloem: it makes them (for example a photosynthesising leaf) or releases them from a store (for example a tuber that is breaking down starch).
:::

:::def Sink
A part of a plant that **removes** assimilates from the phloem, to use them or to store them, for example a root, a growing shoot tip, a developing fruit or a storage organ that is being filled.
:::

The phloem carries assimilates **dissolved in water**: the solution is called **phloem sap**. The main assimilates are **sucrose** and **amino acids**. Plants transport sucrose, not glucose, because sucrose is very soluble and chemically unreactive (it is a non-reducing sugar), so it is not used up on the way.

A plant organ can be a source at one time and a sink at another. A potato tuber is a sink in summer, when it takes in sucrose and stores it as starch. In spring, it is a source: it breaks the starch down and the sugar is sent to the new shoots, which are sinks until their leaves are big enough to be sources.

| Source | Sink |
|---|---|
| mature leaf in the light | root tip, growing shoot tip |
| a tuber or bulb in spring | a developing fruit or seed |
| a germinating seed's store | a tuber or bulb being filled in summer |

:::eg Classify each part of a plant in the list as a source or a sink at the time given, and give a reason: (a) a fully grown leaf in bright sunshine; (b) a developing strawberry; (c) a bulb in early spring, when its leaves are growing; (d) a root tip in the soil.
(a) Source, because it makes sucrose by photosynthesis. [B1]

(b) Sink, because it receives sucrose to build its tissues and to store sugar. [B1]

(c) Source, because it breaks down stored starch to sucrose and sends it to the growing leaves, which are sinks. [B1]

(d) Sink, because it uses sucrose for respiration and growth and has no photosynthesis. [B1]
:::

## Phloem tissue

[Plant tissues and water transport](#bio.planttransport) gave the structures. Here they are in terms of how they work.

| Cell | Structure | Why it matters for translocation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sieve tube element** | living, thin layer of cytoplasm; **no nucleus**, few organelles; **sieve plates** with pores at its ends | the sap can flow from one element to the next through the pores, with little to obstruct it |
| **Companion cell** | nucleus, dense cytoplasm, **many mitochondria**; **plasmodesmata** to the sieve tube element | its mitochondria supply **ATP** for loading sucrose; its nucleus controls both cells |

A sieve tube element has too few organelles to keep itself alive. It depends on its companion cell, to which it is joined by plasmodesmata.

## Loading sucrose into the phloem

Sucrose is made in the mesophyll cells. To reach the sieve tubes it moves to the companion cells, along the cell walls and cell to cell. Sieve tubes often have a higher sucrose concentration than the cells around them, so the sucrose has to be **loaded** against its concentration gradient. That needs energy.

:::def Cotransporter protein
A membrane protein that carries two different substances across a membrane together in the same direction. One of them moves down its concentration gradient, and that movement provides the energy to bring in the other against its gradient.
:::

:::steps
1. **Proton pumps** in the cell surface membrane of the companion cell use **ATP** (made by the cell's many mitochondria) to move **hydrogen ions (H⁺)** out of the companion cell into the cell wall space. This is **active transport**.
2. The concentration of H⁺ outside the cell is now **higher** than inside: there is a concentration gradient (and an electrical gradient) for H⁺.
3. H⁺ **diffuse back into the companion cell** down their gradient through **cotransporter proteins** in the membrane. Each cotransporter carries a **sucrose molecule** into the cell with the H⁺ at the same time.
4. In this way sucrose is brought into the companion cell **against its concentration gradient**, and its concentration inside the companion cell becomes high.
5. The sucrose passes from the companion cell into the sieve tube element **through the plasmodesmata**, down a concentration gradient.
6. The sucrose lowers the **water potential** of the sieve tube, so **water enters by osmosis** from the xylem and neighbouring cells. This builds up a **high hydrostatic pressure** in the sieve tube at the source.
:::

The loading of sucrose is the only step that needs metabolic energy: ATP is used by the proton pump. Sucrose itself is not pumped: it is carried in by the cotransporter, using the energy stored in the H⁺ gradient that the pump built.

:::warn The proton pump does not carry the sucrose
The proton pump moves **H⁺** only. Sucrose enters the companion cell through a **cotransporter** with H⁺, down the H⁺ gradient. If you write "sucrose is actively transported by the proton pump", you lose the mark. The accurate sentence is "H⁺ are pumped out using ATP; sucrose is carried in with H⁺ through a cotransporter protein".
:::

:::eg Explain how sucrose is loaded from the cell walls of the mesophyll into the sieve tube elements of a leaf.
A full-mark answer gives five points in the order of the process.

Proton pumps in the membrane of the companion cell use ATP to pump H⁺ out of the cell by active transport. [B1]

This makes a high concentration of H⁺ outside the cell, so there is a concentration gradient for H⁺ into the cell. [B1]

H⁺ diffuse back in through cotransporter proteins, which carry sucrose into the cell with them, against the concentration gradient of sucrose. [B1]

The sucrose passes from the companion cell into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata. [B1]

The sucrose lowers the water potential of the sieve tube, so water enters by osmosis and the hydrostatic pressure rises. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the companion cell uses active transport to move sucrose into the sieve tube". It does not mention H⁺, the cotransporter or osmosis, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## Mass flow

:::def Hydrostatic pressure
The pressure that a liquid exerts on its surroundings, here the push of the sap on the walls of the sieve tube element. It is high where more liquid is squeezed in.
:::

:::def Mass flow
The movement of a liquid and everything dissolved in it, together, from a region of higher hydrostatic pressure to a region of lower hydrostatic pressure.
:::

:::html

:::

- **At the source**, loading lowers the water potential of the sieve tube, so water enters by osmosis. The **hydrostatic pressure rises**.
- **At the sink**, sucrose leaves the sieve tube and is used (in respiration or for growth) or stored (as starch). The water potential of the sieve tube rises, so **water leaves** by osmosis and the hydrostatic pressure **falls**.
- So there is a **hydrostatic pressure gradient** from source to sink. The sap flows down this gradient from the source to the sink, as water flows along a pipe from a high-pressure pump to a low-pressure tap. This is **mass flow**. The sieve plates have pores, so the sap passes from one element to the next.
- Mass flow is not diffusion. Diffusion is the random movement of single kinds of molecules down their own concentration gradients, and it is far too slow over the distances in a plant. In mass flow all the sap, water and solutes together, moves in the same direction at a speed that can be about a metre per hour.

The direction depends on where the source and sink are, not on gravity. Sap flows up to a developing flower or shoot tip as well as down to the roots, and in different sieve tubes of the same stem the sap can flow in **opposite directions** at the same time.

:::analogy A hosepipe
Think of a sieve tube as a hosepipe. Loading at the source is the tap being turned on: pressure builds. Unloading at the sink is an open end: pressure is low. The water in the hose, with everything dissolved in it, flows from the tap to the open end because of the pressure difference, not because each water molecule diffuses.
:::

:::warn Two common mistakes
"Phloem flow is only downwards" is false: it goes from sources to sinks, in any direction. "Mass flow is diffusion of sucrose down a concentration gradient" is false: it is bulk flow down a **hydrostatic pressure gradient**, which is produced by osmosis.
:::

## Evidence for translocation in phloem

**Ringing (girdling).** A ring of bark, which includes the phloem but not the xylem, is removed from a stem.

:::

After some days the stem **swells above the ring**, and the sucrose concentration there is high, while it is low below the ring. The sucrose could not pass the ring because the phloem was removed. The leaves do not wilt, because the xylem is intact and water continues to rise. If the ring is wide the roots, which depend on sucrose from above, may die, so the whole plant dies.

**Aphid stylets.** An aphid pushes its needle-like mouthpart (stylet) into a sieve tube element. Sap flows into the aphid or, if the aphid is removed and its stylet is cut, **sap continues to ooze out of the stylet** for hours. This shows the sap is **under positive pressure** (high hydrostatic pressure), as mass flow requires, and the sap can be collected and analysed: it contains sucrose and amino acids, and the concentration of sucrose is higher near the source than near the sink.

**Radioactive tracers.** If a leaf is given carbon dioxide labelled with radioactive carbon-14, the plant makes radioactive sucrose, and the radioactivity moves out of the leaf in the phloem. A photographic film pressed against the stem (an autoradiograph) shows the radioactivity in the phloem and not in the xylem. The distance moved in a measured time gives the speed of flow.

:::eg A ring of bark was removed from the stem of a young bean plant. Forty-eight hours later the width of the stem and the sucrose concentration in the bark were measured above and below the ring and in an unringed plant at the same height.
| position | stem width / mm | sucrose in bark / mg g⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| ringed, above the ring | 6.4 | 38 |
| ringed, below the ring | 4.1 | 7 |
| unringed control | 4.2 | 21 |

Calculate the percentage increase in the width above the ring compared with the control, and explain the results.

**Percentage increase** in width: the difference is \(6.4 - 4.2 = 2.2\) mm.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{increase} &= \frac{6.4 - 4.2}{4.2} \times 100 \\ &= 52\% \end{aligned}\]

The width above the ring is 52% greater than the control. [M1] [A1]

**Explain.** The bark removed with the ring contained the phloem, so sucrose moving down the stem could not pass the ring. [B1] It built up above the ring (38 mg g⁻¹, compared with 21 mg g⁻¹ in the control), and the tissue above the ring swelled. [B1] Below the ring, little sucrose arrived (7 mg g⁻¹), so its concentration fell as it was used. This shows that sucrose is transported downwards in the phloem. [B1]
:::

:::eg A leaf was supplied with carbon dioxide containing carbon-14, and the radioactivity was found 0.84 m below the leaf in the stem after 40 minutes. Calculate the mean speed of translocation in m h⁻¹.
**Step 1: change the time to hours**, so that the units are metres per hour.

\[\begin{aligned} t &= \frac{40}{60} \\ &= 0.667\,\mathrm{h} \end{aligned}\]

[M1]

**Step 2: speed = distance ÷ time**, using the unrounded time.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{speed} &= \frac{0.84\,\mathrm{m}}{0.6667\,\mathrm{h}} \\ &= 1.3\,\mathrm{m\,h^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

The speed is 1.3 m h⁻¹ (1.26 unrounded). [A1]

At this speed sucrose would travel 30 m in less than a day. Diffusion over 30 m would take years, which is why translocation in the phloem needs mass flow.
:::

:::eg Real context, aphids as crop pests: an aphid feeds on a broad bean plant. Explain why the aphid is able to feed on phloem sap without sucking, and why it harms the plant.
The aphid's stylet enters a sieve tube element, where the sap is under high hydrostatic pressure, so the sap is forced up the stylet into the aphid. [B1]

Phloem sap contains a lot of sucrose and some amino acids, so the aphid takes in the sugar and amino acids that the plant would have sent to sinks. [B1]

The plant is harmed because the sucrose and amino acids that would have reached the growing shoots, fruits and roots are lost, so growth and yield fall. [B1]

Aphids can also spread plant viruses from one plant to another as they feed, and aphids excrete sugary honeydew, which fungi grow on. Farmers control aphids with insecticides, or with ladybirds and other predators (biological control).
:::

## Comparing xylem and phloem

| | Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|---|
| **Cells** | dead vessel elements (lignified) | living sieve tube elements and companion cells |
| **Substances carried** | water and mineral ions | sucrose and amino acids, dissolved in water |
| **Direction** | one direction only, **upwards** from the roots | from sources to sinks, so **up or down**, and in both directions in different tubes |
| **Driving force** | tension (transpiration pull) and cohesion | a hydrostatic pressure gradient, from loading by active transport at the source |
| **Energy** | no energy from the plant: powered by evaporation (the Sun) | metabolic energy (ATP) is needed to load sucrose into the sieve tubes |
| **Pores or end walls** | no end walls between elements | sieve plates with pores between elements |
| **Movement of water** | water moves **out** through the leaves | water moves **in** at the source by osmosis and out at the sink |

:::eg Compare the transport of substances in the xylem and the phloem.
Xylem carries water and mineral ions, and phloem carries sucrose and amino acids. [B1]

Xylem transports in one direction only, upwards, but phloem transports from sources to sinks, in either direction. [B1]

Xylem is dead tissue with no end walls, but phloem cells are living, with sieve plates and companion cells. [B1]

The movement in xylem is caused by transpiration pull and tension, and does not need metabolic energy from the plant. [B1]

The movement in phloem is by mass flow down a hydrostatic pressure gradient, and needs ATP for loading by the companion cells. [B1]

Notice the pattern: **a difference needs both sides**. "Xylem carries water" alone does not compare; "xylem carries water but phloem carries sucrose" does.
:::

:::exam Compare, describe and explain
"Compare" needs both sides of each point in the same sentence ("whereas", "but"). "Explain how sucrose is loaded" needs the sequence **ATP → H⁺ pumped out → H⁺ gradient → cotransporter brings in sucrose with H⁺ → plasmodesmata → water enters by osmosis**. In data questions on ringing, name the tissue that was removed (phloem) and the tissue that was left (xylem).
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State what is meant by a source and a sink, with one example of each.
A: A source loads assimilates into the phloem, for example a photosynthesising leaf. A sink removes assimilates from the phloem to use or store them, for example a growing root tip or a developing fruit.
Q: Name the two main assimilates transported in the phloem, and state what the plant dissolves them in.
A: Sucrose and amino acids, dissolved in water (the solution is called phloem sap).
Q: Explain why a plant transports sucrose and not glucose.
A: Sucrose is soluble but chemically unreactive (a non-reducing sugar), so it is not used up in reactions on the way to the sink.
Q: Radioactive carbon-14 sucrose moved 45 cm along a stem in 25 minutes. Calculate the speed in m h⁻¹.
A: \(0.45\,\mathrm{m} \div (25 \div 60)\,\mathrm{h} = 1.08\,\mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}\), which is 1.1 m h⁻¹.
Q: Describe two ways in which a companion cell is adapted to loading sucrose into a sieve tube element.
A: Many mitochondria, which make the ATP needed by the proton pumps. Plasmodesmata join it to the sieve tube element, so the sucrose can pass through. (Also credit: cotransporter proteins and proton pumps in its membrane.)
Q: Explain what is meant by a cotransporter protein.
A: A protein in a membrane that carries two substances in the same direction at the same time. H⁺ moves down its gradient and brings sucrose in against its own gradient.
Q: Explain why water enters the sieve tube at a source.
A: Sucrose is loaded into the sieve tube, so its water potential falls and becomes lower than that of the surrounding cells and the xylem. Water therefore enters by osmosis.
Q: Explain why the hydrostatic pressure is lower at a sink than at a source.
A: Sucrose is removed from the sieve tube at the sink, so the water potential rises, and water leaves by osmosis. The volume of liquid falls, so the pressure falls.
Q: A tree is ringed by removing the bark, including the phloem, in a band round its trunk. State two changes you would expect above the ring and explain them.
A: The trunk swells above the ring and the sucrose concentration above it rises. Sucrose travelling down the phloem cannot pass the gap where the phloem was removed, so it builds up.
Q: State two differences between mass flow in the phloem and diffusion.
A: Mass flow moves all the sap (water and solutes) together, in bulk, down a pressure gradient, and is fast; diffusion is the movement of one type of particle down its own concentration gradient, and is slow over long distances.
:::

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [3 marks] ★ Describe the structure of a sieve tube element and explain how it is suited to transporting sucrose.
A: The sieve tube element has no nucleus and few organelles, so there is little to obstruct the flow of sap. [B1]
Its end walls are sieve plates with pores, so the sap can pass from one element to the next. [B1]
It is a living cell with a thin lining of cytoplasm, joined to a companion cell by plasmodesmata. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ A scientist measured the sucrose concentration in the sap of a plant at different distances from a leaf.

| distance from leaf / cm | sucrose in sap / % |
|---|---|
| 10 | 14.2 |
| 30 | 12.9 |
| 50 | 11.1 |
| 70 | 9.6 |

(a) Describe the trend. (b) Calculate the percentage decrease in the concentration between 10 cm and 70 cm.
H: Quote figures with units.
H: Percentage decrease \(= (\text{old} - \text{new}) \div \text{old} \times 100\).
A: (a) The sucrose concentration decreases as the distance from the leaf increases, from 14.2% at 10 cm to 9.6% at 70 cm. [B1]
(b) \((14.2 - 9.6) \div 14.2 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 32\%\) [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain the role of the companion cell in the transport of sucrose in the phloem.
A: The companion cell has many mitochondria, which make ATP for the proton pumps. [B1]
Proton pumps use ATP to move H⁺ out of the cell, forming a gradient of H⁺. [B1]
H⁺ come back in through cotransporter proteins, which bring sucrose into the cell with them. [B1]
Sucrose passes through the plasmodesmata into the sieve tube element. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Explain how mass flow moves sucrose from a leaf to a root.
H: Say what happens to the water potential and the pressure at each end.
A: Loading at the leaf (source) lowers the water potential of the sieve tube, so water enters by osmosis. [B1]
This causes a high hydrostatic pressure at the source. [B1]
At the root (sink) sucrose is removed, so water potential is higher and water leaves by osmosis, giving a low hydrostatic pressure. [B1]
So there is a hydrostatic pressure gradient from source to sink. [B1]
The sap, with the sucrose dissolved in it, flows down the gradient through the sieve tubes. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ The table shows the effect of a ring of bark removed from the stems of two groups of seedlings. After 72 hours the dry mass of roots was measured.

| treatment | dry mass of roots / g |
|---|---|
| bark ring removed | 0.8 |
| unringed control | 2.1 |

(a) Calculate the dry mass of roots of the ringed seedlings as a percentage of the control. (b) Explain the difference. (c) Suggest why the leaves of the ringed seedlings did not wilt.
H: For (b), what could not reach the roots?
A: (a) \(0.8 \div 2.1 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 38\%\) [A1]
(b) The ring removed the phloem, so sucrose made in the leaves could not be transported to the roots. [B1] The roots could not get the sucrose they needed for respiration and growth, so they grew less. [B1]
(c) The xylem was not removed, so water could still travel up to the leaves. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Compare the transport of water in the xylem with the transport of sucrose in the phloem.
H: Use "whereas": one point about each tissue, in the same sentence.
A: Xylem carries water and mineral ions, whereas phloem carries sucrose and amino acids dissolved in water. [B1]
Xylem moves substances upwards only, whereas phloem moves them from source to sink, in either direction. [B1]
Xylem vessel elements are dead, whereas sieve tube elements and companion cells are living. [B1]
Movement in xylem is caused by transpiration pull (tension) and cohesion, whereas movement in phloem is by mass flow down a hydrostatic pressure gradient. [B1]
Xylem needs no metabolic energy, whereas phloem needs ATP for loading sucrose. [B1]
Xylem has no end walls, whereas phloem has sieve plates with pores. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.planttransport) ★★★ Sap oozes for several hours from a cut sieve tube in the stem of a castor oil seedling. (a) Explain why sap continues to flow from the cut sieve tube. (b) Suggest why the sucrose concentration of the sap is higher in a stem just below a mature leaf than in a stem just below a young leaf. (c) Explain why xylem sap would not ooze from a cut vessel in the same way.
H: In (c), is the xylem sap under positive pressure or under tension?
A: (a) The sap in the sieve tube is under high hydrostatic pressure, so it is pushed out through the cut end. [B1]
The pressure is caused by water entering by osmosis because of the sucrose loaded at the source. [B1]
(b) A mature leaf is a source and loads sucrose into the phloem, whereas a young leaf is a sink and receives sucrose. [B1]
So the sucrose concentration in the sap is higher near the mature leaf. [B1]
(c) The sap in xylem is under tension (negative pressure), not positive pressure. [B1]
So it would be pulled back into the plant rather than pushed out (a cut xylem vessel draws air in). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The table shows the rate of loading of sucrose into a leaf with different treatments.

| treatment | rate of loading / arbitrary units |
|---|---|
| none | 100 |
| a chemical that stops mitochondria making ATP | 18 |
| a chemical that makes the cell surface membrane leaky to H⁺ | 22 |

(a) Explain the results in terms of the loading mechanism. (b) Suggest why the chemical that stopped ATP production did not reduce the loading to zero straight away.
H: What did the ATP do, and what does a leaky membrane do to the H⁺ gradient?
A: (a) The first chemical stops ATP production, so the proton pumps cannot move H⁺ out of the companion cell. [B1]
So no H⁺ gradient is built up, and the cotransporter cannot bring sucrose in. [B1]
The second chemical lets H⁺ leak back in, so the H⁺ gradient cannot be maintained. [B1]
So less sucrose is brought in by the cotransporters, so loading falls. [B1]
(b) There is already an H⁺ gradient (and ATP) in the cell when the chemical is added, so loading goes on until it runs down. [B1]
Some ATP can still be made without the mitochondria (by anaerobic respiration in the cytoplasm), so a few pumps keep working. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A potato plant grows from a tuber in spring. Describe the movement of assimilates in the phloem in spring and in late summer. In each season, state which parts of the plant are sources and which are sinks, and explain how a sucrose concentration gradient is maintained between a leaf and a new tuber.
H: A tuber changes its role. What is the stored substance and what is it converted to?
H: Think about what happens to sucrose when it arrives in the tuber.
A: In spring the old tuber is a source: starch is broken down to sucrose, which is sent in the phloem to the growing shoots, which are sinks. [B1]
When the leaves are mature and photosynthesising they become the main sources. [B1]
In late summer the leaves are sources and the new tubers are sinks. [B1]
In the new tuber, sucrose is removed from the phloem and converted to starch, which is insoluble. [B1]
This keeps the sucrose concentration, and so the water potential gradient, favourable: the water potential of the phloem there stays high so water leaves and the pressure stays low. [B1]
Therefore there is a pressure gradient from leaf to tuber, and mass flow continues. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A scientist cooled a short section of a stem to 2 °C with a jacket round it and measured the speed at which labelled sucrose moved through that section. The speed fell sharply while the jacket was on and returned to its original value within an hour of removing it. (a) Suggest two reasons why cooling the stem section reduces the speed. (b) A student concludes that translocation needs energy from the living cells of the stem. Evaluate this conclusion.
H: Cooling affects the living cells (respiration and ATP) and also the sap itself.
H: Could the result be explained without any change in the energy supply?
A: (a) Cooling slows respiration in the companion cells of that section, so less ATP is made to keep the sieve tube elements working. [B1]
Cold sap is more viscous, so it flows more slowly down the same pressure gradient. [B1]
(b) The speed recovered after rewarming, so the cells were not killed and the effect is reversible, which fits a process that depends on living cells. [B1]
But the higher viscosity of cold sap would slow mass flow even if no energy were needed in the stem, so the result does not prove the conclusion. [B1]
A better test would treat only that section with a respiratory inhibitor at normal temperature and compare the speed with an untreated plant. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which describes a sink in a plant?
+ a developing fruit that receives sucrose from the phloem
- a leaf that makes sucrose in sunlight
- a xylem vessel carrying water to the leaves
- a stoma that lets water vapour out
> A sink removes assimilates from the phloem to use or store them. A photosynthesising leaf is a source, and xylem and stomata are part of the water pathway.
? What is moved out of a companion cell by a proton pump?
+ hydrogen ions, using ATP
- sucrose, using ATP
- sucrose, down its concentration gradient
- water, by osmosis
> The proton pump moves H⁺ out by active transport. Sucrose is brought in by a cotransporter with H⁺.
? What does a cotransporter protein in the companion cell membrane carry?
+ H⁺ and sucrose together, into the cell
- H⁺ out of the cell and sucrose in
- sucrose and ATP together, out of the cell
- water and sucrose into the cell
> H⁺ flows back into the cell down its gradient and carries sucrose in with it in the same direction.
? Why does water enter a sieve tube element at a source?
+ the sucrose in the sieve tube lowers its water potential
- the proton pump pumps water in
- sucrose is hydrolysed to glucose
- the sieve plate is partially permeable to water only
> Water moves by osmosis into the solution of lower water potential. The pump moves H⁺, not water.
? Mass flow in phloem is the movement of sap from
+ high hydrostatic pressure to low hydrostatic pressure
- low hydrostatic pressure to high hydrostatic pressure
- high sucrose concentration to low by diffusion only
- high temperature to low temperature
> Sap flows down a hydrostatic pressure gradient from source to sink. Diffusion of sucrose alone is far too slow.
? A ring of bark and phloem is removed from a stem. What is the result above the ring?
+ the stem swells and the sucrose concentration rises
- the stem shrinks and the sucrose concentration falls
- the leaves wilt as water cannot rise
- there is no change in the stem
> Sucrose cannot pass the gap where the phloem was, so it builds up above the ring. The xylem is intact, so water rises and the leaves do not wilt.
? What is a difference between xylem transport and phloem transport?
+ phloem transport needs ATP for loading, xylem transport does not
- xylem transport can be in either direction, phloem upwards only
- xylem vessels are living, sieve tubes are dead
- phloem transports water only, xylem transports sucrose only
> Phloem loading uses ATP. Xylem moves substances upwards only, in dead vessels, and carries water and mineral ions.
? Radioactive sucrose travels 54 cm along a stem in 30 minutes. What is the mean speed?
+ 1.08 m h⁻¹
- 1.8 m h⁻¹
- 0.27 m h⁻¹
- 108 m h⁻¹
> \(0.54\,\mathrm{m} \div 0.5\,\mathrm{h} = 1.08\,\mathrm{m\,h^{-1}}\). 1.8 is the speed in cm per minute (54 ÷ 30), not m h⁻¹. 0.27 divides by 2 instead of by 0.5, and 108 forgets to convert cm to m.
? Why does sap flow out of a cut sieve tube?
+ the sap in the sieve tube is under high hydrostatic pressure
- the sap in the sieve tube is under tension
- the companion cells pump it out using ATP
- sieve tubes are dead and empty
> Pressure in the sieve tube pushes the sap out. Xylem is the tissue under tension, and sieve tubes are living.

:::recap
- **Assimilates** (sucrose and amino acids) are transported dissolved in water in phloem sieve tubes from **sources** (leaves, stores being broken down) to **sinks** (roots, shoot tips, fruits, stores being filled).
- **Loading**: proton pumps in the companion cell use ATP to pump H⁺ out; H⁺ diffuses back in through **cotransporter proteins**, bringing sucrose with it; sucrose passes to the sieve tube through plasmodesmata.
- Sucrose lowers the water potential of the sieve tube, so **water enters by osmosis**: high **hydrostatic pressure** at the source. At the sink sucrose is removed, water leaves, and the pressure is low.
- **Mass flow** carries the sap from high to low hydrostatic pressure. It is bulk flow, not diffusion, and can run in either direction in different tubes.
- Evidence: ringing (swelling above the ring, xylem intact), aphid stylets (sap under pressure), radioactive tracers.
- Xylem: dead, upwards only, tension and cohesion, no energy from the plant. Phloem: living, source to sink, hydrostatic pressure, ATP for loading.
:::
