:::why
Papers 3 and 5 are different from every other paper: they test skills, not facts. Paper 3 asks you to do an experiment and use a microscope in a laboratory, and Paper 5 asks you to plan, analyse and evaluate an investigation on paper. Together they are 70 marks, every one of them for experimental skills (AO3), and any biology you need that you have not met is printed in the question. Students lose marks here for habits, not for gaps in knowledge, and habits can be fixed. This guide lists the skills, the habits that cost marks, and a way to practise them.
:::

## The two papers

| | Paper 3 | Paper 5 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Advanced Practical Skills | Planning, Analysis and Evaluation |
| Time | 2 h | 1 h 15 min |
| Marks | 40 | 30 |
| Questions | 2 or 3 | 2 or more |
| Style | laboratory practical, done by you | written, no laboratory work |
| Content | AS content and unfamiliar contexts | AS and A Level content; unfamiliar contexts with the information given |
| Weight | 23% of AS, 11.5% of A Level | 11.5% of A Level |
| Assessment objective | AO3 100% | AO3 100% |

On both papers you answer on the question paper, in black or dark blue pen, and use an HB pencil for diagrams and graphs. You may use a calculator, there is no formula sheet or data booklet, and the front page says: "You should show all your working and use appropriate units." Marks for each part are in brackets. A statistics formula or a table of critical values is printed in the question if you need it; degrees of freedom are never given.

:::tip The context can be unfamiliar
Both papers may use contexts outside the syllabus content. Do not panic when you meet an organism or a reagent you have never heard of: all the information you need is printed in the question. The marks reward what you do with it: the decisions, the measurements, the table, the graph and the evaluation.
:::

## Paper 3: the practical paper

### What it contains

The paper has two or three questions. In the real June 2024 paper there were two, worth 21 and 19 marks, and the second was the microscope and photomicrograph question. **Half the candidates start with the microscope work and half with the investigation**, so that the equipment is shared; the paper tells you which to do first. You must not dissect animal material. For the microscope you use a ×10 eyepiece and ×10 and ×40 objective lenses only, which give total magnifications of ×100 and ×400.

The marks are shared between three groups of skills.

| Skill | Marks |
|---|---|
| manipulation, measurement and observation | 15 to 17 |
| presentation of data and observations | 11 to 13 |
| analysis, conclusions and evaluation | 11 to 13 |

Full instructions are given, so the task is to follow them exactly, take careful readings and then show what you saw. More than half of the marks (presentation plus analysis, 22 to 26 of the 40) are for what you do with the results, which you can do well even if your practical went a little wrong.

### Time

Two hours for 40 marks is \(120 \div 40 = 3\) minutes a mark. For the real June 2024 paper, that is \(21 \times 3 = 63\) minutes for the 21-mark question and \(19 \times 3 = 57\) minutes for the 19-mark question. Do not spend longer than that on one question, because all the marks are worth the same.

:::steps
1. **Read the whole paper first (about 5 minutes).** Note which parts need timings, which need waiting, and what the apparatus is.
2. **Start any long timing at once.** If a water bath must reach 40 °C or a reaction must run for ten minutes, set it up first. Do other parts while you wait.
3. **Use waiting time well.** Draw a table, prepare your axes, answer questions that do not need the results, or draw the specimen.
4. **Record as you go.** Write each reading in your table straight away, never on scrap.
5. **Leave 5 minutes** to check units, headings, labels and the sum of what you were asked for.
:::

### Paper 3: the skills checklist

Each line below is a skill that earns marks. The right-hand column says where the course teaches it.

| Skill group | What you must be able to do | Learn it |
|---|---|---|
| Decisions | name the independent variable (IV) and dependent variable (DV); choose a range with at least five values and sensible intervals; say how to change the IV and measure the DV; replicates; controls; standardised variables | [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical) |
| Microscope | set up and focus; find and draw tissues and cells; make a temporary stained slide; calculate actual size from magnification; scale bars and graticules; estimate numbers by sampling | [Practical skills: microscopy and drawing (Paper 3)](#bio.practical2) |
| Hazards and risk | identify a hazard, rate the risk as low, medium or high, and give a precaution | [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical) |
| Drawings | plan diagrams (no cells, correct proportions) and cell drawings (shapes, wall thickness, only what you can see); sharp pencil, unbroken lines, most of the space, no shading | [Practical skills: microscopy and drawing (Paper 3)](#bio.practical2) |
| Tables | headings with units, IV on the left or above, no units in the body, consistent decimal places, significant figures that match the data | [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical) and [Maths skills for biology](#bio.maths) |
| Graphs | IV on x; labelled axes with units; a scale that uses most of the grid and is readable to half a square; crosses or dot-in-circle; best-fit line or curve, or ruled point to point; no extrapolation without a reason | [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical) |
| Interpreting | calculations; read graphs; estimate a concentration; identify unknowns by food tests; anomalies; patterns and trends | [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical) |
| Conclusions and evaluation | does the evidence support the hypothesis; predictions; systematic and random errors; improvements (better standardisation, more accurate measuring of the DV, smaller intervals, replicates and means); extensions | [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical) |

## Paper 5: planning, analysis and evaluation

### What it contains

Paper 5 is written and has no laboratory work. It has two or more questions: the June 2024 paper had two, worth 19 and 11 marks. About half the marks are for **planning** (14 to 16) and half for **analysis, conclusions and evaluation** (14 to 16). It can test AS and A Level content. The information about any unfamiliar organism, apparatus or data is in the question, as in Paper 3.

Time is \(75 \div 30 = 2.5\) minutes a mark. For the June 2024 question marks that gives \(19 \times 2.5 = 47.5\) minutes and \(11 \times 2.5 = 27.5\) minutes. A planning question is often long, so plan your answer in bullets in the margin first.

### Paper 5: the skills checklist

| Skill group | What you must be able to do | Learn it |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothesis and prediction | a prediction, in words or as a sketch graph, linked to a hypothesis; the IV, the DV and the key standardised variables | [Planning investigations (Paper 5)](#bio.planning) |
| Methods | how to vary the IV and measure the IV and DV accurately to a suitable precision; standardise; volumes and concentrations; serial or proportional dilution; controls; logical sequence; deal with anomalies; spread using SD, SE or 95% CI; validity (accuracy and repeatability); risk assessment (severity and probability) and precautions | [Planning investigations (Paper 5)](#bio.planning) |
| Data | tables and graphs; error bars; choose calculations (means, percentages, rates, percentage gain or loss); use SD or SE to judge significance; choose and justify a statistical test; state the null hypothesis; types of data (nominal, ordinal, continuous) | [Statistics for biologists](#bio.stats) and [Analysis, conclusions and evaluation (Paper 5)](#bio.evaluation) |
| Conclusions | main conclusions and key points; the extent to which the hypothesis is supported; strengths and weaknesses of the evidence; explanations; further predictions | [Analysis, conclusions and evaluation (Paper 5)](#bio.evaluation) |
| Evaluation | anomalies and explanations; replication; the range and intervals of the IV; whether the DV measurement is suitable; control of variables; validity and confidence; improvements | [Analysis, conclusions and evaluation (Paper 5)](#bio.evaluation) |

:::board Board difference you may meet
AQA A Level Biology (7402) assesses practical skills in its written papers, plus a separately reported practical endorsement, with no timed hands-on practical exam. Cambridge has a timed hands-on exam, Paper 3, so you need to practise handling equipment, a microscope and a stopwatch as well as writing about them.
:::

## The marks students lose most often

Mark schemes reward the same habits, and the same slips lose marks. Check this table against every practice answer.

| The slip | What to do instead | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Units in the body of a table** ("12 cm³", "30 °C") | put the unit once in the column heading ("volume / cm³") and write plain numbers underneath | [investigations](#bio.practical) |
| **Fewer than five values of the independent variable** | use at least five, evenly spaced, across a sensible range | [investigations](#bio.practical), [planning](#bio.planning) |
| **Vague standardised variables** ("same amount", "same temperature") | name the variable, give its value and say how it is measured or kept: "10 cm³ of enzyme, measured with a syringe"; "water bath at 30 °C" | [planning](#bio.planning) |
| **Shading in drawings** | use a sharp pencil and clean, unbroken lines, and show darker parts by labels | [microscopy and drawing](#bio.practical2) |
| **Joining the points wrongly** | a ruled best-fit line only if the points suggest a straight line, a smooth curve if they suggest a curve, and point-to-point lines if the question or the data call for them | [investigations](#bio.practical) |
| **"Human error" as the source of error** | say which step: for example, the end point of a colour change is hard to judge by eye, so a colorimeter or a colour standard would reduce the error | [evaluation](#bio.evaluation) |
| **Conclusions beyond the data** ("proves", "at all temperatures", predicting past the highest value) | say what the data show within the range tested, and whether they support the hypothesis | [evaluation](#bio.evaluation) |
| **Graph axes or scale** (IV on y, no units, a scale that uses a third of the grid or is hard to read) | IV on the x-axis, labelled with units, a scale that uses most of the grid, easy to read to half a square | [investigations](#bio.practical) |
| **Inconsistent decimal places or significant figures** | use the same number of decimal places down a column; give calculated answers to the same significant figures as the data, or one more | [Maths skills for biology](#bio.maths) |

:::exam Command lines in these papers
"Identify the independent variable": the one thing you changed. "State **how** you standardised …": the value and the measuring tool. "Suggest one source of error": a specific step and why it matters. "Describe the pattern": trend, figures with units, turning points. "Explain": the biology. For Paper 5, "evaluate" the plan or the data means strengths and weaknesses of the evidence, then how the investigation could be improved.
:::

## How to practise Paper 3 without a laboratory

You cannot do the apparatus work at home, but most Paper 3 marks are for what you do before and after it, and you can practise those.

:::steps
1. **Use real Paper 3 results.** Take a past paper and a mark scheme. Work only on the data parts: draw the table, calculate, plot the graph, describe and evaluate. Mark with the scheme, line by line.
2. **Draw from images.** Use good photomicrographs and prepared-slide images from your textbooks or online. Practise plan diagrams and cell drawings with a sharp pencil, in the size the paper gives, with ruled label lines. Compare with the drawing rules in [Practical skills: microscopy and drawing (Paper 3)](#bio.practical2).
3. **Calculate sizes** from drawings and scale bars until \(M = I \div A\) with unit conversion is automatic.
4. **Do any practical your teacher offers**, and write up the table, graph and evaluation for each as if it were an exam.
5. **Use safe kitchen biology.** For example, potato cylinders in salt or sugar solutions, with a kitchen balance and a ruler, give you a table, a graph, a standardisation list and an evaluation. Use only food-safe materials, and ask an adult.
6. **Time yourself.** Work out a three-minute-a-mark timetable for the question in front of you before you begin.
:::

## A Paper 5 planning question (8 marks)

Try writing the plan first, in 10 minutes, then compare it with the model. Each marking point earns one mark.

:::eg Plan an investigation of the effect of temperature on the permeability of the cell surface membranes of beetroot. A colorimeter and a thermometer are available.
The red pigment of beetroot is kept inside the cell vacuoles. It leaks out into the surrounding water when the membranes are damaged or more permeable. So the more pigment in the water, the higher the permeability.

**Hypothesis and prediction**: as temperature increases, the permeability of the membrane increases, because the proteins in the membrane denature and the phospholipid bilayer is disrupted, so more pigment leaks out and the absorbance of the surrounding solution is higher. [B1]

**Independent variable**: the temperature of the water bath. Use six temperatures, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 °C, which gives a wide range with 10 °C intervals. Measure each with a thermometer. [B1]

**Dependent variable**: the absorbance of the solution that surrounds the beetroot, measured in a colorimeter with a green filter (the colour that red pigment absorbs most), after a fixed time, with distilled water used to zero the colorimeter. [B1]

**Standardised variable 1**: cut the beetroot into cylinders with the same cork borer and cut each to 10 mm long with a ruler, so that every piece has the same surface area. Rinse all the pieces in distilled water until the water is clear to remove pigment from cut cells. [B1]

**Standardised variable 2**: place one cylinder in 10 cm³ of distilled water, measured with a measuring cylinder or syringe, in each tube, and leave each for the same time, 10 minutes. [B1]

**Replicates**: use three cylinders (three tubes) at each temperature and calculate the mean absorbance at each. Check for anomalous results and repeat any that are. [B1]

**Risk assessment**: the cork borer and scalpel can cut; cut on a tile, away from the hand, and carry the tubes in a rack. Water at 70 °C can scald; use a thermometer and tongs to handle the tubes and leave the water to cool before emptying. [B1]

**Results**: plot a graph of mean absorbance (y-axis) against temperature (x-axis) and look for the temperature at which the absorbance rises steeply. [B1]

(Also credit: control with beetroot in distilled water at room temperature; use the same colorimeter and cuvette each time; stir or mix the tubes the same way.)

A weaker plan says "put beetroot in water at different temperatures and see how red the water goes". It has no values, no measurements, no replicates and no way to measure the colour, so it scores one mark at most for the hypothesis. The model above scores each mark for a different decision.
:::

## A Paper 3 results question

The skills in Paper 3 that most students find easy to lose are the table, the graph and the description. This example shows all three.

:::eg A student added different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide to a suspension of yeast, which contains catalase, and collected the oxygen given off in 60 s. The volumes of oxygen were 8, 12, 14, 16 and 17 cm³ for hydrogen peroxide concentrations of 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10%. (a) Draw a results table that includes the rate of reaction. (b) Calculate the rate for 2%. (c) Describe how to plot the graph. (d) Describe the pattern shown by the results.
**(a) The table**: the independent variable is in the left column, every heading has a unit, there are no units in the body, and the rate has a consistent number of decimal places. [B1] [B1] [B1]

| hydrogen peroxide / % | volume of oxygen in 60 s / cm³ | rate / cm³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 8 | 0.13 |
| 4 | 12 | 0.20 |
| 6 | 14 | 0.23 |
| 8 | 16 | 0.27 |
| 10 | 17 | 0.28 |

**(b) The calculation**: rate is volume divided by time.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{rate} &= \frac{8}{60} \\ &= 0.13\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1] The volumes have one or two significant figures, so the answers are given to two significant figures.

**(c) The graph**: put hydrogen peroxide concentration (the independent variable) on the x-axis and rate on the y-axis, each labelled with its unit [B1]. Choose scales that use more than half of the grid in each direction and are easy to read, such as 1% to two small squares and 0.05 cm³ s⁻¹ to two small squares [B1]. Plot each point as a small cross, accurately, to half a small square [B1]. Draw a smooth best-fit curve through the middle of the points, because they suggest a curve and not a straight line, with a sharp pencil and no sketching [B1].

**(d) The pattern**: as the concentration rises from 2% to 10% the rate increases, from 0.13 to 0.28 cm³ s⁻¹ [B1]. The curve becomes less steep, so the rate rises quickly at first (0.13 to 0.20 between 2% and 4%) and slowly later (0.27 to 0.28 between 8% and 10%), showing the rate begins to level off [B1].
:::

:::plot The expected graph for the example: the rate rises with hydrogen peroxide concentration but more and more slowly (invented results). The curve is a smooth best-fit curve, not a zigzag.
--f "0.4*x/(4+x)+0*sqrt(x-2)+0*sqrt(10-x)" --x 0 12 --y 0 0.4 --ystep 0.1 --xlabel "hydrogen peroxide / %" --ylabel "rate / cm³ s⁻¹" --label "best-fit curve" --pt "2,0.1333,0.13@se" --pt "4,0.2,@se" --pt "6,0.2333,0.23@se" --pt "8,0.2667,@se" --pt "10,0.2833,0.28@se" --legend tr --alt "Five points rise from 0.13 cm cubed per second at 2 percent to 0.28 at 10 percent, with a smooth curve that gets flatter as the concentration rises."
:::

Notice what the answer did not do: it did not explain the biology of why the curve flattens. "Describe" is only for the pattern. Part (e) in the real paper might say "explain", and you would then say that the active sites of the catalase became saturated, so the substrate concentration was no longer limiting.

## Quick checks: fix the slip

:::try Fix the slip
Q: [2 marks] A student wrote "keep the temperature the same" as one standardised variable. Rewrite it so that it would earn a mark.
A: Name the value: "the temperature of the water bath was kept at 30 °C" [B1], and say how it was kept or measured: "checked with a thermometer, and hot or cold water added when needed" [B1].

Q: [2 marks] A student's table has the column heading "Time" and the entries "12 s", "18 s" and "27 s". Correct the table.
A: Put the unit in the heading: "time / s" [B1], and write the entries as numbers only: 12, 18, 27 [B1].

Q: [1 mark] A student gives "human error" as a source of error when timing how long a colour change takes. Give a better answer.
A: It is difficult to judge by eye exactly when the colour change finishes, so the times are not accurate; a colorimeter, or a colour standard to match, would reduce the error [B1].

Q: [2 marks] A student plots a graph showing the rate rising as concentration rises from 2% to 10%, and writes "this proves that the rate will keep increasing at 20%". Give two criticisms.
A: Data can support a hypothesis but not "prove" it [B1], and 20% is outside the range tested (extrapolation), so the rate may level off instead [B1].

Q: [1 mark] A student shades the nucleus in a cell drawing to show that it is darker. Explain what is wrong.
A: Drawings should have sharp, unbroken lines and no shading; the nucleus is shown by its outline and a label [B1].

Q: [2 marks] A student investigates the effect of pH using only pH 3, 7 and 11. Suggest two improvements to the range.
A: Use at least five values of pH [B1]. Use smaller intervals around the expected optimum, such as pH 6, 7 and 8, so that the optimum can be found more precisely [B1].
:::
