## Recombinant human proteins

:::def Recombinant human protein
A human protein made by a microorganism or cultured cell into which the human gene for that protein has been put, using genetic engineering.
:::

Many diseases are caused by a missing or faulty protein. A person who cannot make a protein can be treated by giving them the protein. In the past, the protein had to be taken from animals or from human donors. Now the human gene is put into bacteria, yeast or cultured animal cells (see [Tools of genetic technology](#bio.genetech)), which are grown in large fermenters, and the protein is purified.

### Insulin

In type 1 diabetes the beta cells of the pancreas do not make enough insulin (see [Blood glucose, cell signalling and biosensors](#bio.glucose)). Insulin used to be extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle. Their insulin differs slightly from human insulin in amino acid sequence. Today, human insulin is made by bacteria or yeast that carry the human insulin gene.

### Factor VIII

Haemophilia A is a sex-linked recessive condition (see [Monohybrid inheritance](#bio.inheritance)) in which blood does not clot properly because factor VIII, one of the proteins of the clotting cascade, is missing. People with haemophilia used to be treated with factor VIII extracted from donated human blood. Today, recombinant factor VIII is made by cultured animal cells (cells of a mammal are used because the protein needs to be processed after translation, as it is in human cells).

### Adenosine deaminase

Adenosine deaminase (ADA) is an enzyme found in all cells. A child who inherits two faulty alleles of the gene (the condition is recessive) has no working ADA. A toxic by-product of a pathway builds up and kills lymphocytes, so the child has almost no working B- and T-lymphocytes. This is one cause of **severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID)**: the child cannot fight infections and, without treatment, dies in infancy. One treatment is regular injections of ADA made as a recombinant protein (enzyme replacement).

### Why use recombinant proteins?

| Advantage | Explanation |
|---|---|
| the protein is identical to the human protein | it has the correct shape for its function and is not recognised as foreign, so it is much less likely to cause an immune (allergic) reaction than a protein from an animal |
| no risk of infection from donors | protein from donated blood or animal tissue can carry viruses or prions; a protein made by microorganisms in a fermenter does not |
| unlimited supply | fermenters can be scaled up, and the supply does not depend on the number of donors or animals |
| fewer ethical and religious objections | no animals are killed, and no animal products are used (for some patients, products from pigs or cattle are unacceptable) |
| consistent quality | every batch is the same, and the protein can be modified to work faster or longer |

Disadvantages to mention in a balanced answer: development is costly, so the medicine can be expensive; the protein must usually be injected, regularly and for life, because it treats the symptoms and does not correct the faulty gene; and some patients make antibodies against the protein.

:::eg Explain the advantages of using recombinant human insulin, made by genetically modified bacteria, rather than insulin extracted from the pancreas of pigs.
Four separate marking points, each with its reason.

The insulin has the same amino acid sequence as human insulin, so it has the correct shape to bind to human insulin receptors and works effectively. [B1]

It is not foreign to the body, so it is less likely to cause an immune (antibody) response or an allergic reaction. [B1]

Bacteria grow fast in fermenters, so large amounts can be made, and the supply does not depend on the number of animals. [B1]

No animals are killed, which avoids ethical or religious objections and the risk of transferring animal diseases. [B1]

(Also credit: the quality is consistent from batch to batch; the cost can be lower once the process has been developed.)

A weaker answer says "it is better because it is made by bacteria". It gives no reason linked to the structure of the protein, the immune response or the supply.
:::

## Genetic screening

:::def Genetic screening
Testing a person (or an embryo or fetus) for the presence of a particular allele, or a particular change in a chromosome, that causes or increases the risk of a disease.
:::

A small sample of DNA is taken (a cheek swab, blood sample, a few cells from an embryo or from a newborn baby's heel prick). The DNA is tested for a known allele, using the tools in [Tools of genetic technology](#bio.genetech): PCR copies the region, then the product is analysed using gel electrophoresis, a DNA probe or a microarray.

### BRCA1 and BRCA2

BRCA1 and BRCA2 are genes whose normal products help to repair damaged DNA and to control cell division, so they protect against cancer. Some alleles are faulty. A person who inherits one of these faulty alleles has a much higher **risk** of breast cancer and ovarian cancer than the average person. The risk is increased, not certain: some carriers never develop cancer.

Advantages of testing a person with a family history of breast cancer:

- A person who knows that she has a faulty allele can have more frequent screening (such as mammograms), so that a tumour is found early, when it is easier to treat.
- She can choose **preventive treatment**, such as the removal of the breasts or ovaries, or take medicines, to lower the risk.
- She can plan her life and her family, and relatives can be told, so they can be tested too.
- A person who does not have the faulty allele is spared unneeded surgery and worry.

:::eg Real context, BRCA1: a woman whose mother and sister both had breast cancer is tested and found to carry a faulty allele of BRCA1. Outline how the result may help her.
She now knows that she has a much higher risk of breast and ovarian cancer than the average person (the result gives a risk, not a certainty). [B1]

She can be screened more often, so that any cancer is found early, when treatment is more likely to succeed. [B1]

She can choose preventive surgery, or other treatment, to reduce the risk. [B1]

She can make decisions about having children, and tell relatives that they may carry the allele and could be tested. [B1]
:::

### Huntington's disease

Huntington's disease is caused by a **dominant** allele of the HTT gene (see [Genes, phenotype and gene control](#bio.genecontrol)). Symptoms usually begin in middle age and get worse, and there is no cure. A child of an affected parent who is heterozygous has a 50% chance of inheriting the allele. A test of DNA from a young adult shows whether the allele is present, years before the symptoms.

Advantages: a person who knows can make decisions about having children, can plan finances, career and care, and can join research; a person who tests negative is relieved of worry. Disadvantages and concerns are discussed below.

### Cystic fibrosis

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is caused by a **recessive** allele of the CFTR gene, which codes for a chloride ion channel in the cell surface membrane (see [Membrane structure and cell signalling](#bio.membranes)). The faulty channel makes mucus thick and sticky, so the lungs become infected and the pancreas is blocked. A person with CF has two copies of the faulty allele; a **carrier** has one copy and is healthy.

- **Carrier testing:** a couple can find out, before they have children, whether they both carry the allele. If so, each child has a 1 in 4 chance of having CF.
- **Testing of embryos:** a couple who are both carriers can have embryos made by IVF. One or two cells are removed from each embryo when it is a few days old and tested for the faulty allele. This is called **preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD)**. Only embryos without two copies of the allele are placed in the uterus.
- **Newborn screening:** a blood sample from the heel of a newborn baby is tested. If the baby has CF, treatment can start early, before lung damage occurs.

:::eg Two healthy parents, who are both carriers of the cystic fibrosis allele, have a child with CF. Use a genetic diagram to find the probability that their next child has CF, and the probability that their healthy child is a carrier.
Use F for the normal allele and f for the CF allele. Both parents are Ff. Their gametes are F or f.

| | F | f |
|---|---|---|
| **F** | FF | Ff |
| **f** | Ff | ff |

[M1]

One of the four equally likely offspring is ff, so the probability that the next child has CF is \(\tfrac14\), or 25%. [A1]

A healthy child is not ff, so is one of FF, Ff or Ff (three equally likely outcomes). Two of the three are carriers (Ff). [M1]

\[\text{probability that a healthy child is a carrier} = \tfrac{2}{3}\]

[A1]

A weaker answer says "one in two" because it counts the two Ff boxes out of four, but a healthy child can't be ff, so only the three other boxes count.
:::

When the command word is "discuss" you must also give the **disadvantages** (anxiety, insurance, privacy) and weigh them. See the practice at the end of this chapter.

## Gene therapy

:::def Gene therapy
Treating a genetic disease by putting a healthy copy of a gene into the cells of a patient, to replace the effect of a faulty allele.
:::

Gene therapy does not remove the faulty allele. It adds a working copy of the gene to the patient's cells, so that the cells make the missing protein. The gene is carried into the cells by a **vector**:

- a **virus** that has been modified so that it cannot cause disease (and, in some cases, cannot reproduce), carrying the human gene. Viruses are very good at entering cells;
- a **liposome**: a tiny sphere made of a phospholipid bilayer that holds the DNA inside and can fuse with the cell surface membrane to release it into the cell.

There are two ways to deliver the gene:

- **Ex vivo:** cells are removed from the patient, treated in the laboratory and returned (used for blood cells).
- **In vivo:** the vector is put into the patient's body, in or near the tissue to be treated (used for the eye).

:::def Somatic gene therapy
Gene therapy in which the gene is added to body (somatic) cells of the patient. The change affects only that person and is not passed on to children.
:::

:::def Germ-line gene therapy
Gene therapy in which the gene is added to gametes, zygotes or early embryos, so that every cell of the person who develops, including their gametes, has the change and it is passed on to future generations. It is not allowed in many countries.
:::

Medical gene therapy so far is **somatic**.

### Gene therapy for SCID

For the form of SCID caused by a lack of the enzyme ADA, the treatment is to give the lymphocytes a working ADA gene.

:::steps
1. **Collect** cells from the patient: T-lymphocytes or, better, stem cells from the bone marrow, which divide and give rise to all the blood cells.
2. **Add the gene:** a working ADA gene is put into the cells in the laboratory, using a virus as the vector.
3. **Check** that the cells take up the gene and make ADA.
4. **Return** the modified cells to the patient's blood.
5. The cells divide and make working ADA, so the patient makes functioning lymphocytes and the immune system recovers.
:::

Using stem cells is better than using mature T-lymphocytes because stem cells divide again and again, and the modified cells go on supplying new lymphocytes for years.

:::eg Outline how gene therapy can be used to treat severe combined immunodeficiency caused by a lack of adenosine deaminase (ADA).
Five marking points in sequence.

Bone marrow stem cells (or T-lymphocytes) are removed from the patient. [B1]

A working copy of the ADA gene is put into the cells in the laboratory, using a vector such as a modified virus. [B1]

The cells are grown and tested to check that they have taken up the gene and make ADA. [B1]

The modified cells are returned to the patient's blood. [B1]

The cells divide and make ADA, so the lymphocytes survive and the immune system can work. [B1]
:::

### Gene therapy for inherited eye diseases

In some inherited eye diseases, the light-sensitive cells of the retina die because a gene they need is faulty. In one treatment, a working copy of the gene, inside a harmless virus, is injected into the eye, close to the retina, so that the retinal cells take it up and make the missing protein.

:::eg Real context, the eye: explain why an inherited eye disease is a good target for gene therapy.
The eye is small, so only a small amount of the vector is needed. [B1]

The vector can be injected directly into the retina, so it stays in the eye and does not spread to other tissues. [B1]

The eye is protected from much of the immune system, so there is less risk of an immune response to the virus. [B1]

The retinal cells divide rarely, so the inserted gene is not lost when cells divide and the effect can last for years. [B1]
:::

### Problems with gene therapy

- The patient may have an **immune response** to the viral vector, which can be dangerous, and can destroy the modified cells.
- The effect may be **short-lived**: cells that divide may lose the gene, or the cells with the gene die, so the treatment may need repeating.
- The gene may be inserted in the **wrong place**, interrupting another gene. If the gene lands in a gene that controls cell division, this may lead to cancer. This happened in some trials of gene therapy for SCID, in which some children developed leukaemia.
- Getting the vector into the **right cells** in enough numbers is difficult, and a gene can't easily be put into every cell of the body.
- The treatment is **expensive**, and so is available to few patients.

:::eg Spot the error: outline gene therapy for an inherited disease. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: A healthy copy of the gene is put into the patient's cells using a vector, such as a modified virus.
Line 2: This changes the genes in every cell of the patient's body, so the disease is cured for ever.
Line 3: The treatment may need to be repeated, because the modified cells may die or lose the gene.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: gene therapy changes only the target cells (for example blood or retinal cells), not every cell, and the faulty allele is still there in all other cells. It is not a permanent cure, as Line 3 says. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct Line 2: "This gives the target cells a working gene so that they make the missing protein; it is somatic gene therapy, so the change is not passed on to the patient's children." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.
:::

## Social and ethical considerations

Scientists can do many things that societies debate. Exam questions ask you to **discuss** the social and ethical issues, so you must give points on both sides.

### Genetic screening

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| a person can make informed choices about having children, and the choices of treatment | a positive result for a disease with no cure causes anxiety and distress |
| early treatment or prevention (preventive surgery, early treatment of newborns with CF) | insurers or employers could refuse cover or jobs to people with a harmful allele (discrimination), which may stop people being tested |
| a negative result brings relief | the results also concern relatives (who may not wish to know), and must be kept private |
| embryos can be tested (PGD) so a family avoids the disease | selecting embryos or ending a pregnancy after prenatal testing is thought wrong by some people, who believe an embryo has the right to life; it may lead to choosing embryos for other characteristics |
| populations can be monitored and treatments planned | the tests are costly, and results are difficult to interpret (risk, not certainty) |

Consent matters: a person must understand the test and agree to it freely, and children generally should not be tested for diseases that start in adult life unless it brings a benefit.

### Gene therapy

| For | Against |
|---|---|
| it can treat or cure diseases that can't be treated by drugs | the risks: immune reaction, cancer, effect not lasting |
| a one-off treatment may be better than lifelong injections | it is very expensive, so only wealthy people or countries can afford it |
| it relieves suffering | **germ-line** changes would be passed to future generations who cannot consent, and could be used to "design" babies for features such as height or intelligence rather than to treat disease |
| somatic therapy affects only the patient | the long-term effects are not yet known |

:::eg Discuss the social and ethical issues of genetic screening for Huntington's disease.
This is a discussion. Balanced answers earn up to six marks: three for each side is a good plan.

A person who tests positive can make an informed choice about having children, as each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the allele. [B1]

They can plan for the future (finances, career, care), and a negative result removes uncertainty and worry. [B1]

Embryos can be tested during IVF (PGD) so that a couple can have children who will not inherit the allele. [B1]

However, there is no cure, so a positive result causes anxiety and distress, and some people prefer not to know. [B1]

Insurers or employers might discriminate against a person with a positive result, so people may not take the test. [B1]

The result also shows that a relative (a brother, sister or child) has a risk of carrying the allele, who may not want to know, so the information must be kept confidential; and selecting embryos is thought wrong by some people. [B1]

(Also credit: the test can't predict at what age symptoms begin; consent and counselling are needed.)

A weaker answer lists only benefits (or only objections), which cannot earn more than three or four marks.
:::

:::exam Build a discuss answer
Plan "discuss" answers as: benefits (with a reason each) → problems or objections (with a reason each) → a short judgement if the question asks for one. Use the words **consent**, **confidentiality**, **discrimination** and **informed choice**, which are the language of the mark scheme. Say "some people believe" for views, and keep facts and opinions apart.
:::

## Genetic engineering and food

The world population is growing, and farmland is limited, so more food has to come from the same land. Genetic engineering is used to improve the **quality** (more nutrients, longer shelf life) and the **productivity** (yield, growth rate, fewer losses) of farm animals and crops. In each case a gene from another species is transferred into the organism, using the tools in [Tools of genetic technology](#bio.genetech). The new organism is a **genetically modified (GM) organism**, or GMO.

### GM salmon

Farmed Atlantic salmon with an added **growth hormone gene** from another salmon species grow faster than ordinary salmon. The growth hormone gene is joined to a **promoter** from another fish, which keeps the gene active all year, whereas in normal salmon growth hormone is made mainly in the warmer months. So the GM salmon grow all year and reach market size sooner, using less food per kilogram. This increases productivity.

To reduce the danger to wild salmon if fish escape, GM salmon are farmed in tanks on land, which are contained, and the fish are all females that have been made sterile, so they can't breed with wild fish.

### Herbicide-resistant soybean

A **herbicide** kills weeds, which compete with the crop for light, water and minerals. Many herbicides also kill the crop. Soybean has been modified with a gene from a soil bacterium. The gene codes for a form of an enzyme that is not inhibited by the herbicide glyphosate, which normally blocks an enzyme the plants need to make some amino acids. So the herbicide can be sprayed on the whole field: it kills the weeds and the GM soybean survives. Benefits: the farmer can control weeds in one or two sprays after the crop has emerged, there is less competition, so the yield is higher, and less ploughing is needed, which reduces soil erosion.

### Insect-resistant (Bt) cotton

Cotton is attacked by the caterpillars of moths, such as bollworms. The bacterium *Bacillus thuringiensis* (Bt) makes a protein toxin that kills certain insects. A gene for the toxin has been transferred into cotton, so the plant's own cells make it. When a caterpillar eats the cotton, the toxin is activated in its gut and damages the gut lining, so it stops feeding and dies. Benefits: **fewer sprays of insecticide**, which saves money and reduces the harm to people and to other insects; and more of the crop survives.

The toxin affects only some groups of insects (those whose gut conditions activate it and have receptors for it), not all insects, and it does not harm humans, who lack those conditions.

:::eg Real context, Bt cotton: in a field trial (invented data), conventional cotton and Bt cotton were grown on similar land. Calculate the percentage reduction in the number of insecticide sprays and the percentage increase in yield, and explain why Bt cotton needs fewer sprays.
| | conventional cotton | Bt cotton |
|---|---|---|
| insecticide sprays per season | 8 | 2 |
| yield / kg per hectare | 1200 | 1500 |

\[\begin{aligned} \text{reduction in sprays} &= \frac{8 - 2}{8} \times 100 \\ &= 75\% \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{increase in yield} &= \frac{1500 - 1200}{1200} \times 100 \\ &= 25\% \end{aligned}\]

[A1]

The Bt cotton makes the toxin in its own cells, so caterpillars that feed on it are killed. [B1]

So fewer caterpillars survive to damage the crop, and the farmer needs to spray less often. [B1]
:::

:::warn Bt toxin does not kill "all insects"
Bt toxin is toxic to particular insects, mostly the caterpillars of moths and butterflies. It does not kill all insects, or harm mammals. Say "certain insects" and name the effect in the gut. And a GM crop is not "unsafe" as a fact: say "concerns include …", and name the concern.
:::

### Issues with GM crops and animals

| Benefits | Concerns |
|---|---|
| higher yields and lower losses help to feed more people (food security) | **gene flow:** pollen from GM crops can pass the gene to wild relatives or to non-GM crops |
| less insecticide or less ploughing may protect wildlife and soil | **resistance:** pests evolve resistance to Bt, and weeds evolve resistance to a herbicide, so the benefit is lost and more chemicals are used |
| crops can be made more nutritious | **non-target effects:** harm to other insects, and a loss of biodiversity if only a few weeds and insects are controlled |
| farmers' costs may fall | **cost and control:** GM seed is patented and costly, farmers can't always save seed, and a few companies control the supply; poorer farmers may not benefit |
| animals grow faster, so fewer resources are needed | safety testing and **labelling**: some people want long-term safety testing and the right to choose, and feel that it is unnatural to move genes between species; there are animal welfare concerns |

:::eg Discuss the use of herbicide-resistant soybean.
A balanced answer uses the benefits and the concerns.

The herbicide kills weeds but not the crop, so weeds can be controlled easily by spraying after the crop has emerged. [B1]

With fewer weeds there is less competition for light, water and minerals, so the yield is higher and more people can be fed. [B1]

Less ploughing is needed, which reduces soil erosion. [B1]

However, weeds can evolve resistance to the herbicide by natural selection, because resistant plants survive and reproduce, so the benefit is lost and more herbicide may be needed. [B1]

The gene might spread by pollen to wild relatives. [B1]

The seeds are patented and cost more, and the farmer can't save seed, so poorer farmers may not benefit. [B1]

(Also credit: concerns about safety testing and labelling; heavy use of herbicide may harm wild plants and the animals that feed on them.)
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State two advantages of using recombinant factor VIII to treat haemophilia, rather than factor VIII from donated blood.
A: There is no risk of passing on viruses (infections) from donors. The supply is larger and does not depend on donated blood.
Q: Explain how injections of recombinant adenosine deaminase (ADA) help a child with ADA-deficient SCID, and why the injections have to be repeated.
A: The injected enzyme replaces the missing ADA, so the toxic by-product no longer builds up and kills the lymphocytes, and the immune system can work. The injected protein is broken down in the body and the child's cells still can't make ADA, so injections are needed regularly, for life.
Q: Define the term genetic screening.
A: Testing a person, embryo or fetus for the presence of a particular allele that causes, or increases the risk of, a disease.
Q: In a screening programme, 36 of 90 000 newborn babies had a recessive disorder. Express this as 1 in \(n\).
A: \(90\,000 \div 36 = 2500\), so 1 baby in 2500.
Q: Explain what is meant by a liposome vector and why it can be used in gene therapy.
A: A liposome is a small sphere of phospholipid bilayer that contains the DNA. It can fuse with the phospholipid bilayer of the cell surface membrane and release the gene inside the cell, so no virus is needed.
Q: Distinguish between somatic and germ-line gene therapy.
A: Somatic therapy changes body cells of the patient and is not passed to children. Germ-line therapy changes gametes or an embryo, so every cell, including the gametes, has the change and it is passed to future generations.
Q: A couple who both carry the cystic fibrosis allele have embryos made by IVF. Explain how PGD helps them.
A: One or two cells are removed from each embryo and the DNA is tested for the faulty allele. Only embryos that do not have two copies of the allele are placed in the uterus, so the couple can have a child who does not have CF.
Q: In GM salmon the growth hormone gene is joined to a promoter from another fish. Suggest why the promoter is important.
A: The promoter makes the gene active in all conditions throughout the year (RNA polymerase can bind to it), so growth hormone is made all the time and the salmon grow faster than normal.
Q: Explain why growing Bt cotton reduces the amount of insecticide that farmers need to spray.
A: The cotton plants make the Bt toxin themselves, which kills the caterpillars that eat them, so fewer pests survive and the crop needs fewer sprays.
Q: Explain how a herbicide-resistance gene could pass from a GM crop to a wild relative growing nearby, and why this worries some people.
A: Pollen from the GM crop can fertilise the flowers of the wild relative, so the hybrid seeds carry the gene. Wild plants with the gene would survive spraying with the herbicide, so they could become weeds that are hard to control.
Q: Give one ethical concern about insurance companies using the results of genetic tests.
A: A person with a harmful allele might be refused insurance or charged more (discrimination), even if they never develop the disease, and this may put people off taking the test.

H: Think about the child, the unused embryos, and the cost.
A: Benefit: the child will not have cystic fibrosis. [B1]
Embryos found to have the disease are not used, and some people believe that an embryo has a right to life. [B1]
The technique may lead to selection of embryos for other characteristics, such as sex or appearance, rather than for disease. [B1]
IVF is expensive and stressful, so it is not available to everyone. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Phenylketonuria (PKU) is caused by a recessive allele. Of 200 000 newborn babies tested in one screening programme, 20 had PKU. Assume that the population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. (a) Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele, \(q\). (b) Calculate the frequency of carriers, \(2pq\). (c) Calculate the number of carriers expected among the 200 000 babies.
H: \(q^2\) is the frequency of babies with the condition.
H: \(p = 1 - q\).
A: (a) \(q^2 = 20 \div 200\,000 = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\) [M1] so \(q = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 0.010\) [A1]
(b) \(p = 1 - 0.010 = 0.990\), and \(2pq = 2 \times 0.990 \times 0.010\) [M1] \(= 0.0198\), so about 0.020 [A1]
(c) \(0.0198 \times 200\,000 = 3960\) carriers [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Farmers compared GM salmon and ordinary salmon (invented data). The ordinary salmon took 30 months to reach a market mass of 3.0 kg and the GM salmon took 16 months. (a) Calculate the percentage reduction in the time to reach market mass. (b) Explain how the GM salmon grow faster, and give one precaution that reduces the risk to wild salmon.
H: Percentage reduction = decrease ÷ original × 100.
A: (a) \((30 - 16) \div 30 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 46.7\%\) [A1]
(b) A growth hormone gene with a promoter that makes it active all year means that more growth hormone is made, so the fish grow all year. [B1]
The fish are sterile females in contained tanks, so they cannot escape and breed with wild salmon. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Discuss the social and ethical issues of germ-line gene therapy to correct a faulty allele in a human embryo.
H: Think about who gives consent and who is affected.
A: Benefit: the disease could be prevented in the child and in all of that person's descendants. [B1]
It could save the suffering and cost of treating a lifelong disease. [B1]
Benefit: it avoids the need to destroy embryos with the allele (as with PGD). [B1]
Concern: the people affected, future generations, cannot give consent. [B1]
Concern: the long-term effects are unknown and the change is passed on and cannot be reversed. [B1]
Concern: the technique could be used to choose characteristics such as height, not to treat disease, and it is likely to be expensive and unfair. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ In a trial of herbicide-resistant soybean (invented data), non-GM soybean and GM soybean were grown on adjoining plots. The weed cover was 35% on the non-GM plots and 4% on the GM plots when sprayed with the herbicide. The yield was 2.4 tonnes per hectare for non-GM and 3.0 for GM. After 8 years of growing GM soybean, 30% of the weeds on the GM plots survived the spraying. (a) Calculate the percentage increase in yield. (b) Explain why the yield was higher on the GM plots. (c) Explain, using natural selection, why 30% of the weeds survived after 8 years.
H: Link weed cover to competition for resources.
H: Use variation, selection pressure and the allele frequency.
A: (a) \((3.0 - 2.4) \div 2.4 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 25\%\) [A1]
(b) The herbicide killed most weeds, so there was less competition for light, water and minerals, and the soybean grew better. [B1]
(c) Some weeds had an allele that gives resistance to the herbicide (from mutation). [B1]
The herbicide was a selection pressure: resistant weeds survived and reproduced and passed on the allele, so its frequency increased. [B1]
Over eight years of spraying, a larger proportion of each generation was resistant. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A virus is used as the vector in gene therapy for an inherited eye disease. (a) Explain why the gene put into the virus is cDNA made from mRNA and not the gene from the patient's DNA. (b) Suggest why the vector is injected into the eye and not into the blood. (c) Explain why a promoter that is active in the retinal cells must be part of the construct.
H: Think about introns, the target tissue, and when the gene is transcribed.
A: (a) The cDNA has no introns, so it is short enough to fit in the virus, and the retinal cells can make the correct protein. [B1]
(b) Injection into the eye keeps the vector in the target tissue, so a smaller dose is needed and less spreads to other cells. [B1]
It also avoids the immune system attacking the virus in the blood. [B1]
(c) RNA polymerase must bind to the promoter to start transcription. [B1]
A promoter active in the retinal cells makes sure that the gene is transcribed in the cells that need the protein. [B1]
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ In a trial of gene therapy for ADA-deficient SCID (invented data), 20 children were treated with their own stem cells carrying a working ADA gene. After 3 years, 14 children had a normal immune system, 4 had improved but needed some injections of ADA, and 2 had developed leukaemia caused by the gene being inserted next to a gene that controls cell division. Without treatment, such children die in infancy. Alternative: regular ADA injections for life. (a) Calculate the percentage of children with a normal immune system and the percentage who developed leukaemia. (b) Evaluate the use of gene therapy compared with injections of ADA.
H: Percentages out of 20.
H: Compare what each treatment achieves, the risks, and the cost over a lifetime.
A: (a) \(14 \div 20 \times 100 = 70\%\) [A1]
\(2 \div 20 \times 100 = 10\%\) [A1]
(b) For gene therapy: 70% had a normal immune system, which could be a long-lasting treatment that removes the need for repeated injections. [B1]
Against: 10% developed leukaemia, a serious risk of the gene inserting at a random place, which is not caused by injections. [B1]
Injections of ADA have no risk of insertion, but they must be repeated for life and do not give a normal immune system, so a patient's life is affected. [B1]
Overall: the benefits of gene therapy are large, but the risk is serious, so it may be best for children who cannot have injections or who have no matched donor, and safer vectors would improve the balance. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Bt toxin resistance in cotton pests is a recessive trait. Farmers are required to plant 20% of their land with non-Bt cotton (a refuge) next to the Bt cotton. (a) Explain why resistance to Bt would spread quickly if all the cotton were Bt cotton. (b) Explain how the refuge slows the spread of resistance. (c) Explain why the refuge works better if resistance is recessive.
H: In (b), where do resistant insects mate?
H: In (c), what is the genotype of the offspring of a resistant insect and a susceptible insect, and does the Bt kill it?
A: (a) Mutation gives a few insects an allele for resistance. [B1]
If every plant is toxic, only the resistant insects survive and breed, so the allele's frequency increases rapidly (natural selection). [B1]
(b) Susceptible insects survive on the non-Bt cotton in the refuge, and they stay in large numbers. [B1]
Resistant insects that emerge from the Bt cotton mate with these susceptible insects, so resistant alleles are mixed with susceptible alleles in the population, and the frequency of resistance rises more slowly. [B1]
(c) If resistance is recessive, the offspring of a resistant (rr) and a susceptible (RR) insect are heterozygous (Rr), [B1]
and heterozygotes are killed by the toxin as they don't show the resistant phenotype, so the resistant allele is lost from the Bt cotton population in each generation. [B1]
:::

:::quiz
? A child with ADA-deficient SCID is treated with injections of recombinant ADA. Which statement is correct?
+ The injections must be repeated, because the child's cells still cannot make ADA.
- The injections change the child's faulty alleles into working alleles.
- The injections are needed only once, because enzymes are not broken down.
- The injected enzyme is passed on to the child's own children.
> Enzyme replacement treats the symptoms. The injected protein is broken down and the genes are not changed, so treatment continues for life.
? Which statement about genetic screening for BRCA1 is correct?
+ A positive result shows a much higher risk of breast cancer, not a certainty.
- A positive result shows that the person has breast cancer.
- A positive result cures the disease.
- A negative result shows that the person can never develop breast cancer.
> Carrying a faulty allele increases the risk. Many carriers do not develop cancer, and non-carriers can still develop breast cancer from other causes.
? A man has Huntington's disease and is heterozygous. His partner does not carry the allele. What is the probability that their child inherits the allele?
+ 1/2
- 1/4
- 3/4
- 1
> The dominant allele is in half of the man's gametes and none of his partner's. A quarter would be the figure for a recessive condition with two carrier parents.
? What is the main advantage of using stem cells from bone marrow, not mature T-lymphocytes, in gene therapy for SCID?
+ Stem cells divide, so they go on producing modified lymphocytes for years.
- Stem cells do not need to take up the gene.
- Mature T-lymphocytes can't be returned to the patient.
- Stem cells are not recognised by the immune system.
> Stem cells give rise to new blood cells all the time. Mature cells have a limited life, so the effect of treating them would not last.
? Which statement describes somatic gene therapy?
+ A working gene is added to body cells and is not passed on to the patient's children.
- A working gene is added to the zygote so that every cell has it and it is passed on.
- The faulty allele is removed from every cell in the body.
- The gene is added to the patient's gametes.
> Somatic therapy treats body cells of the patient. Changes to gametes or zygotes are germ-line changes, which are inherited.
? Why are GM salmon farmed as sterile females in contained tanks?
+ To reduce the risk that escaped fish breed with wild salmon.
- To make the fish grow more quickly.
- To stop the fish from making growth hormone.
- Because the growth hormone gene only works in females.
> Sterility and containment are precautions against gene flow into wild populations. They do not affect the growth rate.
? What do herbicide-resistant soybean plants contain?
+ A gene that lets the plant survive the herbicide.
- A gene that makes the plant produce the herbicide.
- A gene that kills the weeds around them.
- A gene that makes the soil acidic.
> The inserted gene gives resistance to the herbicide. The farmer sprays the herbicide, which kills weeds but not the crop.
? Which of the following is a concern about Bt cotton?
+ Insects may evolve resistance to the Bt toxin.
- The toxin kills humans who wear the cotton.
- Bt cotton needs more insecticide than ordinary cotton.
- The toxin kills all insects, including bees, in the field.
> The toxin acts on particular insects, and when it is used widely, insects with resistance alleles survive and spread. It does not kill all insects or harm humans.
? Which is an ethical concern about germ-line gene therapy but not about somatic gene therapy?
+ The changes are inherited by future generations who have not consented.
- The vector may cause an immune response.
- The treatment is expensive.
- The gene may be inserted in the wrong place.
> The other three concerns apply to both types. Only germ-line changes are passed on to descendants.

:::recap
- Recombinant human proteins (insulin, factor VIII, ADA) are identical to human proteins, so there is less immune response, they carry no risk of infection from donors, and the supply is large and avoids ethical objections. They treat symptoms and are usually injected for life.
- Genetic screening tests for alleles: BRCA1 and BRCA2 (higher risk of breast and ovarian cancer), Huntington's (dominant, late onset, no cure), cystic fibrosis (recessive: carrier tests, PGD, newborn screening).
- Gene therapy gives cells a working gene using a virus or liposome vector. SCID: ADA gene into stem cells or T-lymphocytes (ex vivo). Eye disease: vector injected into the retina. Problems: immune response, short-lived, wrong insertion (cancer), cost. Medical gene therapy is somatic; germ-line changes are inherited.
- Ethics: consent, privacy, discrimination, anxiety, choice about embryos, cost and access, germ-line changes in future generations.
- GM salmon (growth hormone gene with a promoter active all year; contained and sterile), herbicide-resistant soybean (spray kills weeds not crop), Bt cotton (toxin gene from *Bacillus thuringiensis*; fewer insecticide sprays). Concerns: gene flow, resistance, non-target effects, cost and control by companies, safety testing and labelling.
- Discuss answers give both sides, with reasons.
:::
