## Ways across a membrane

Substances can cross a cell surface membrane in three broad ways, using six processes. The first question to ask is always: **is energy from ATP used?**

- **Passive transport** needs no energy from the cell. Molecules move because of their own random movement. This includes **simple diffusion**, **facilitated diffusion** and **osmosis**.
- **Active transport** uses energy from ATP to move molecules or ions **against** a concentration gradient.
- **Bulk transport** uses energy from ATP to move large particles or large amounts of material in vesicles: **endocytosis** (into the cell) and **exocytosis** (out of the cell).

**Six processes at a glance**

| Process | Direction | Protein needed? | ATP used? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Simple diffusion | down a concentration gradient | no | no | oxygen into a cell |
| Facilitated diffusion | down a concentration gradient | channel or carrier | no | glucose into a red blood cell |
| Osmosis | to lower water potential | no (aquaporins speed it) | no | water into a root hair cell |
| Active transport | against a concentration gradient | carrier | yes | mineral ions into a root hair cell |
| Endocytosis | into the cell | no (vesicle) | yes | a white blood cell engulfing a bacterium |
| Exocytosis | out of the cell | no (vesicle) | yes | secretion of digestive enzymes |

## Simple diffusion

Molecules in a liquid or gas are always moving at random. In a region where there are many molecules, the molecules bump into each other and spread out into places where there are fewer. Without any energy being supplied, the molecules end up spread evenly.

:::def Diffusion
The net movement of molecules or ions from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random movement.
:::

:::def Concentration gradient
The difference in concentration of a substance between two regions. The steeper the gradient, the faster the net movement.
:::

Two words in the definition matter. **Net**: molecules move in both directions all the time, but more move down the gradient than up it. **Random**: each molecule moves with no goal. When the concentrations are equal the molecules keep moving, but the net movement is zero (this is called dynamic equilibrium).

In **simple diffusion** the molecules pass straight through the phospholipid bilayer, with no protein. Only some molecules can do this:
- **small, non-polar (lipid-soluble)** molecules, which dissolve in the hydrophobic centre: oxygen, carbon dioxide, fatty acids, steroid hormones, and some small uncharged molecules;
- the process is passive, so no ATP is used.

### What affects the rate of diffusion

:::key Factors that change the rate of diffusion
1. **Concentration gradient**: a steeper gradient gives a faster rate.
2. **Distance**: a shorter diffusion distance (a thinner membrane or layer) gives a faster rate.
3. **Surface area**: a larger surface area (for example folded membranes) gives a faster rate.
4. **Temperature**: higher temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy, so they move faster and the rate is higher.
5. **Size of the molecule**: smaller molecules diffuse faster than larger ones.
6. **Lipid solubility and charge**: lipid-soluble, uncharged molecules cross a bilayer faster than charged or polar ones.
:::

You will see these again in exchange surfaces such as the alveoli in [The gas exchange system](#bio.gasexchange) and the villi of the small intestine: each is adapted for a large surface area, a short distance and a steep gradient.

## Facilitated diffusion

Ions such as \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\), and polar molecules such as glucose and amino acids, cannot dissolve in the hydrophobic centre of the bilayer. They cross by **facilitated diffusion**, through transport proteins.

:::def Facilitated diffusion
The passive net movement of molecules or ions from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration through a channel protein or a carrier protein in the membrane. No energy from ATP is used.
:::

- **Channel proteins** have a water-filled pore lined with hydrophilic R groups. A channel is specific: only ions or molecules of the right size and charge fit. Many channels are **gated**: they open only when a signal arrives.
- **Carrier proteins** bind a specific molecule on one side of the membrane, change shape, and release the molecule on the other side. The molecule moves down its concentration gradient, so no ATP is needed.

Because the proteins are specific, the membrane is selective, and because there is only a fixed number of proteins, facilitated diffusion has a **maximum rate**. When all the carrier proteins are busy, a steeper gradient makes no difference. Simple diffusion has no such limit.

## Surface area to volume ratio

Diffusion is fast over short distances and slow over long ones. This sets a limit on the size of a cell and shapes every exchange surface in the body. To see why, compare cubes of different sizes.

:::def Surface area to volume ratio
The surface area of an object divided by its volume (SA:V). It tells you how much surface there is for exchange for every unit of volume that needs supplying.
:::

For a cube of side \(L\):

\[\begin{aligned} \text{surface area} &= 6L^2 \\ \text{volume} &= L^3 \\ \text{SA:V} &= \frac{6L^2}{L^3} = \frac{6}{L} \end{aligned}\]

For a cylinder of radius \(r\) and length \(h\):

\[\begin{aligned} \text{surface area} &= 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h \\ \text{volume} &= \pi r^2 h \end{aligned}\]

The surface area of a cylinder is two circular ends (\(2\pi r^2\)) plus the curved surface (\(2\pi r h\)). For a cuboid with sides \(a\), \(b\) and \(c\), the surface area is \(2(ab + ac + bc)\) and the volume is \(abc\).

:::eg Calculate the surface area, volume and SA:V of cubes with sides 1 cm, 2 cm and 3 cm.
Use \(SA = 6L^2\) and \(V = L^3\), then divide.

| side / cm | SA / cm² | V / cm³ | SA:V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 1 | 6 : 1 |
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 : 1 |
| 3 | 54 | 27 | 2 : 1 |

Doubling the side from 1 cm to 2 cm multiplies the surface area by 4 (\(2^2\)) but the volume by 8 (\(2^3\)). The ratio falls from 6 : 1 to 3 : 1. Tripling the side multiplies the surface by 9 and the volume by 27.

As an object gets bigger, its volume grows faster than its surface area, so **SA:V decreases**.
:::

:::eg Calculate the SA:V of a cylinder of potato of radius 0.50 cm and length 4.0 cm.
Work out the surface area and volume separately, keeping unrounded values until the end.

\[\begin{aligned} SA &= 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h \\ &= 2\pi (0.50)^2 + 2\pi (0.50)(4.0) \\ &= 1.571 + 12.566 \\ &= 14.1\,\mathrm{cm^2} \end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned} V &= \pi r^2 h \\ &= \pi (0.50)^2 (4.0) \\ &= 3.14\,\mathrm{cm^3} \end{aligned}\]

\[\text{SA:V} = \frac{14.137}{3.1416} = 4.5 \text{, so } 4.5 : 1 \text{ (or } 4.5\,\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\text{)}\]

The units of SA:V are \(\mathrm{cm^{-1}}\) (square centimetres per cubic centimetre), but most mark schemes accept a ratio such as "4.5 : 1".
:::

:::warn SA:V is not "the bigger surface"
A large object has a larger surface area than a small one. It is the **ratio** of surface area to volume that is smaller. Write "the SA:V decreases", never "the surface area decreases".
:::

### Why this matters for living things

- A single-celled organism such as an amoeba is small, with a large SA:V. Oxygen and glucose diffuse in over a short distance to reach every part of the cell, so it needs no transport system.
- A large organism has a small SA:V. Its body surface is too small to supply all its cells, and the distance from the surface to the centre is too large for diffusion to be fast enough. It needs **exchange surfaces** with a large surface area (the alveoli of the lungs, the villi of the small intestine) and a **transport system** (the blood) to carry substances to every cell. You will meet these in [The gas exchange system](#bio.gasexchange) and [Blood vessels, blood and tissue fluid](#bio.mammaltransport).
- Cells stay small for the same reason, and cells that must absorb a lot have microvilli (folds of the membrane) that increase the surface area.

## Water potential and osmosis

Water is a polar molecule and dissolves ions and polar molecules; it also crosses membranes. Water moves into and out of cells all the time, so we need a way to predict which way it moves.

:::def Water potential
The tendency of water molecules to move from one region to another, measured in kilopascals (kPa) and given the symbol \(\psi\) (the Greek letter psi). Pure water has the highest water potential, 0 kPa. Adding solute lowers the water potential, so every solution has a **negative** water potential.
:::

Why does a solute lower the water potential? Solute molecules and ions attract water molecules (water forms hydrogen bonds with them), so fewer water molecules are free to move. The more solute, the more negative the water potential. Compare:
- pure water: \(\psi = 0\) kPa (the highest possible);
- a dilute solution: for example \(-200\) kPa;
- a concentrated solution: for example \(-1000\) kPa (lower).

:::def Osmosis
The net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential, through a partially permeable membrane, down a water potential gradient.
:::

:::key Predicting the direction of osmosis
1. Water moves from the **less negative** (higher) water potential to the **more negative** (lower) water potential.
2. It crosses a **partially permeable membrane**. The solutes stay where they are: only water moves.
3. Movement continues until the water potentials are equal, when there is no net movement.
:::

Water molecules are small, and a few cross the bilayer directly. Many membranes also have **aquaporins**, channel proteins that let water pass quickly.

:::note Other words you may meet
Textbooks and other courses sometimes call a solution with a higher water potential than a cell "hypotonic" and one with a lower water potential "hypertonic". The syllabus asks for water potential, so use that in your answers. This course does not use solute potential or pressure potential: it asks only that you compare water potentials.
:::

:::warn Say what is high or low
"Water moves from a low concentration to a high concentration" is a classic mistake, because it is not clear of what. Write "from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential". Do not say "osmosis is the diffusion of solutes", and do not say water moves "down its concentration gradient" unless you mean its water potential.
:::

### Plant cells in solutions

A plant cell has a strong **cell wall** outside its cell surface membrane. The wall is fully permeable (water and solutes pass through it) and cannot stretch much. What matters is the water potential of the solution outside compared with the cell.

:::html
@@PLANT@@
:::

- **Turgid.** In pure water, or a solution with a higher water potential than the cell, water enters by osmosis. The vacuole and cytoplasm swell and push the cell surface membrane against the cell wall. The wall resists, so the cell does not burst. The cell is firm: this firmness supports soft plant tissue such as leaves.
- **Flaccid.** If the water potentials inside and outside are equal, there is no net movement of water. The cell is soft (limp) and the membrane just touches the wall.
- **Plasmolysed.** In a solution with a lower water potential than the cell, water leaves the vacuole and cytoplasm by osmosis. The cell contents shrink and the cell surface membrane pulls away from the cell wall. The gap fills with the external solution, because the wall is fully permeable.

:::def Turgid
Describes a plant cell that has taken in water by osmosis until the cell surface membrane is pressed hard against the cell wall, which resists any further expansion.
:::

:::def Incipient plasmolysis
The point at which the cell surface membrane has only just begun to pull away from the cell wall, because the cell has lost enough water to be flaccid. At this point the water potential of the cell is equal to that of the solution outside.
:::

:::def Plasmolysis
The loss of water from a plant cell by osmosis so that the cell surface membrane and cytoplasm shrink and pull away from the cell wall.
:::

### Animal cells in solutions

An animal cell has no wall to resist, so its fate depends only on the membrane.

:::html
@@RBC@@
:::

- In a solution with a **lower water potential** than the cell, water leaves by osmosis and the cell shrinks. A red blood cell becomes shrivelled and spiky: it is **crenated**.
- In a solution with a **higher water potential** than the cell, for example pure water, water enters by osmosis. With no wall to stop it, the membrane stretches until the cell **bursts** (lysis; for a red blood cell, haemolysis).

:::exam Never say a plant cell "bursts"
A plant cell in pure water becomes turgid but does not burst, because the wall resists. An animal cell has no wall, so it can burst. Use these words (turgid, flaccid, plasmolysed, crenated, lysis) and explain every one with water potentials and a partially permeable membrane.
:::

:::eg Explain why a red blood cell placed in pure water bursts but a plant cell placed in pure water does not.
Both cells have a negative water potential inside. Pure water has a water potential of 0 kPa.

Water moves into both cells by osmosis, from the higher water potential (pure water) to the lower water potential in the cell. [B1]

It crosses the partially permeable cell surface membrane. [B1]

The red blood cell has no cell wall, so its membrane is stretched until it bursts (lysis). [B1]

The plant cell has a strong cell wall that resists expansion, so as the cell becomes turgid the wall pushes back and the cell does not burst. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the plant cell has a wall so water cannot get in". The cell wall is fully permeable; it stops the cell expanding, not the water entering. That answer scores one mark.
:::

:::eg Explain what happens to a plant cell that is placed in a sucrose solution with a lower water potential than the cell.
The cell has a water potential of about −500 kPa and the solution about −900 kPa.

The solution has a lower (more negative) water potential than the cell. [B1]

Water moves out of the cell by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, through the partially permeable cell surface membrane. [B1]

The vacuole and cytoplasm shrink. [B1]

The cell surface membrane pulls away from the cell wall, so the cell is plasmolysed. [B1]

(Also credit: the gap between the wall and the membrane is filled with the external solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable.)
:::

:::note Real context: why oral rehydration solution contains glucose and salt
In diarrhoea, a person loses large amounts of water and ions. Oral rehydration solution is made of water, glucose and salt. Cells of the gut lining absorb sodium ions and glucose together, which lowers their water potential, so water follows by osmosis. This is why glucose and salt together work better than water alone. (Cholera, which causes this kind of diarrhoea, is in [Infectious diseases](#bio.disease).)

### Investigating osmosis with plant tissue

You can estimate the water potential of a plant tissue by putting pieces of it in solutions of different concentration. Where the solution has the same water potential as the tissue, there is no net movement of water and the mass does not change.

:::lab Estimating the water potential of potato tissue
**Aim**: find the concentration of sucrose solution that has the same water potential as potato tissue, and so estimate the water potential of the tissue.

:::steps
1. Make sucrose solutions of 0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 mol dm⁻³ from a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ stock and distilled water, by serial or simple dilution. For example, mix 2 cm³ of stock with 8 cm³ of water to make 10 cm³ of 0.2 mol dm⁻³ solution. Put 10 cm³ of each in a labelled test tube.
2. Use a cork borer to cut cylinders from one potato, and cut them to the same length (for example 3 cm). Cut off the skin. Blot each piece dry and measure its mass to 0.01 g. Use three pieces per tube.
3. Put the pieces in the solutions, cover the tubes (to stop evaporation) and leave for the same time at the same temperature, for example 30 minutes.
4. Take the pieces out, blot them dry in the same way and measure the mass again.
5. Calculate the **percentage change in mass** for each solution, using the initial mass:
   \(\text{percentage change} = \dfrac{\text{final mass} - \text{initial mass}}{\text{initial mass}} \times 100\).
6. Plot percentage change (y-axis) against sucrose concentration (x-axis) and draw a line of best fit. Read the concentration where the line crosses the x-axis (0% change).
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is the sucrose concentration. The dependent variable is the percentage change in mass. Standardise the volume of solution, the time, the temperature, the potato (same potato, same length and surface area) and the blotting.

**Control**: the pieces in distilled water (0.0 mol dm⁻³), which show the gain with a higher water potential outside.

**Typical results**: pieces gain mass in weak solutions (water enters by osmosis) and lose mass in strong ones (water leaves); the line crosses the x-axis between them.

**Sources of error**: pieces differ in starch content and in cell size; uneven blotting adds or removes water; the surface area changes the rate. Improvements: use pieces from the same potato, standardise blotting, and use a smaller interval of concentration around the crossing point.

**Safety**: take care with the cork borer and the blade: cut on a tile, away from your hand. Sucrose solutions are harmless. Risk: low.
:::

Why use a percentage change rather than the change in mass? The pieces do not all start with the same mass, and a percentage change allows a fair comparison.

### Investigating diffusion and osmosis with Visking tubing

**Visking tubing** (dialysis tubing) is a thin plastic tube with tiny pores. It is partially permeable: small molecules such as water and glucose can pass through the pores, but large molecules such as starch cannot. It is therefore a simple model of a cell membrane.

:::html
@@VISKING@@
:::

:::lab Diffusion through Visking tubing
**Aim**: show that small molecules can diffuse through a partially permeable membrane but large ones cannot.

:::steps
1. Soak a piece of Visking tubing in water to open it. Tie one end tightly with thread.
2. Fill the tube with starch suspension and glucose solution, then tie the other end and rinse the outside.
3. Test samples of the contents and of the water in the beaker with iodine solution (starch) and Benedict's solution (reducing sugar, glucose) at the start.
4. Put the tube in a beaker of distilled water for 20–30 minutes.
5. Test samples of the water in the beaker and the contents of the tube again.
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is the substance (starch or glucose). The dependent variable is the result of the test outside the tube. Standardise the volumes, the time and the temperature.

**Control**: a tube filled with water only, in a beaker of water, tested in the same way.

**Typical results**: the water in the beaker gives a positive Benedict's test (glucose has crossed, so brick-red) and stays orange-brown with iodine (starch has not crossed); the contents of the tube still give a blue-black colour with iodine.

**Sources of error**: leaks at the ties; insufficient time; contamination from the outside of the bag. Improvements: rinse the bag, check for leaks first and use a colorimeter to compare Benedict's results.

**Safety**: Benedict's solution is heated: use a water bath and eye protection, and hold tubes with tongs. Iodine solution stains. Risk: low.
:::

:::lab Osmosis using Visking tubing
**Aim**: show that water moves by osmosis and find which way, by measuring the change in mass of Visking tubing bags.

:::steps
1. Make bags of Visking tubing, each tied at both ends, filled with 10 cm³ of sucrose solution of a different concentration (for example 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6 mol dm⁻³). Rinse and blot each bag and measure its mass.
2. Put each bag in a beaker of distilled water for 30 minutes.
3. Take out the bags, blot them dry and measure the mass again.
4. Calculate the percentage change in mass of each bag and plot a graph of percentage change against sucrose concentration.
:::

**Typical results**: the bags with a more concentrated solution inside gain more mass, because the inside has a lower water potential than the water outside, so water enters by osmosis. A bag containing only water stays the same. A variation: attach a capillary tube to the top of the bag and measure how far the liquid rises in it.

**Variables**: independent variable: sucrose concentration inside the bag. Dependent variable: percentage change in mass. Standardise the volume inside, the time, the temperature and the blotting.
:::

## Active transport

Facilitated diffusion can only move a substance down its gradient. Cells often need to move a substance the other way: root hair cells collect mineral ions from soil water that contains very few of them, and the cells lining the small intestine absorb glucose when the gut already holds little. To do this the cell must use energy.

:::def Active transport
The movement of molecules or ions through a carrier protein in a cell surface membrane, from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration (against a concentration gradient), using energy from the hydrolysis of ATP.
:::

:::html
@@ACTIVE@@
:::

How it works, step by step:

:::steps
1. A molecule or ion binds to a **specific binding site** on a carrier protein, on the side with the lower concentration.
2. **ATP** binds to the carrier and is **hydrolysed to ADP and phosphate**, which releases energy.
3. The energy makes the carrier protein **change shape**, so the binding site opens to the other side of the membrane.
4. The molecule or ion is **released** on the side with the higher concentration.
5. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to bind another molecule.
:::

:::eg The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein found in the cell surface membrane of animal cells. Describe how it works.
Use the steps of active transport and give the numbers.

The pump binds three sodium ions from the inside of the cell, and ATP binds to the pump. [B1]

ATP is hydrolysed to ADP and phosphate, which releases energy and makes the carrier change shape. [B1]

The three sodium ions are released outside the cell, against their concentration gradient. [B1]

Two potassium ions are picked up from outside, and then released inside the cell as the carrier returns to its original shape. [B1]

(Also credit: the pump keeps the concentration of sodium ions low inside the cell and potassium ions high; you meet it again in [Neurones and action potentials](#bio.nerves).)
:::

### How to recognise active transport in data

Active transport needs ATP, which comes from respiration. So:
- it needs **oxygen** and **glucose**: if the oxygen supply is cut off, or a **respiratory inhibitor** such as cyanide is added, the rate falls;
- the rate **rises with temperature** (to a limit, as the carrier proteins are denatured above their optimum) because respiration, and so ATP production, is faster;
- it can continue until the concentration inside is **higher than outside**, which diffusion cannot do;
- it has a **maximum rate** because there are only so many carrier proteins;
- cells with a lot of active transport have many **mitochondria**, to make the ATP.

:::warn Active transport is not "energy from diffusion"
Active transport uses energy from the hydrolysis of **ATP**, not from the diffusion gradient. Write "against a concentration gradient using ATP". Do not describe a carrier as a "hole" either: a carrier binds the ion and changes shape, whereas a channel has a pore.
:::

## Bulk transport: endocytosis and exocytosis

Large molecules (proteins), particles (bacteria) and large volumes of liquid cannot cross through a channel or carrier. They are moved in **vesicles**, which are small sacs of membrane. Making and moving vesicles uses ATP.

:::def Endocytosis
The bulk transport of material into a cell. The cell surface membrane folds inwards, engulfs the material and pinches off to form a vesicle inside the cell. There are two types: phagocytosis (solid particles) and pinocytosis (liquid droplets).
:::

:::def Exocytosis
The bulk transport of material out of a cell. A vesicle containing the material moves to the cell surface membrane and fuses with it, releasing the contents outside the cell.
:::

- **Phagocytosis** ("cell eating") takes in a **solid particle**, for example a macrophage or a neutrophil engulfing a bacterium. The cell surface membrane flows round the bacterium and pinches off, forming a phagocytic vesicle. The vesicle fuses with a lysosome, whose enzymes digest the bacterium (see [The immune response](#bio.immunity)).
- **Pinocytosis** ("cell drinking") takes in a **droplet of liquid** with its dissolved contents, in a tiny vesicle.
- **Exocytosis** releases material made in the cell: digestive enzymes from pancreatic cells, hormones, and mucus. The vesicles come from the Golgi body ([Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells)). The membrane of the vesicle becomes part of the cell surface membrane.

Why is ATP needed? It supplies energy to move the vesicles along the cytoskeleton, and to change the shape of the membrane as it folds. Why does the membrane make vesicles so easily? Because it is **fluid**: the phospholipids can move and the bilayer can bend, break and fuse without leaking (see [Membrane structure and cell signalling](#bio.membranes)).

:::exam Which process? Use the clue words
"Large particle", "vesicle", "engulf" or "secrete" point to bulk transport. "Against a gradient" and "ATP" point to active transport. "Down a gradient" with a protein is facilitated diffusion, "down a gradient" with no protein is simple diffusion, and water movement through a membrane is osmosis. Naming the process is often the first mark.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: Define diffusion.
A: The net movement of molecules or ions from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random movement.
Q: State two factors that increase the rate of diffusion across an exchange surface.
A: Any two of: a larger surface area, a shorter diffusion distance (a thinner exchange surface), a steeper concentration gradient, a higher temperature.
Q: Calculate the SA:V of a cube of side 4 cm.
A: Surface area \(= 6 \times 16 = 96\,\mathrm{cm^2}\); volume \(= 64\,\mathrm{cm^3}\); SA:V \(= 96 \div 64 = 1.5\), so \(1.5 : 1\).
Q: A cell has a water potential of −700 kPa and is placed in a solution of −300 kPa. State the direction of net water movement and the name of the process.
A: Into the cell, because the solution has a higher (less negative) water potential. The process is osmosis.
Q: State what is meant by a turgid plant cell.
A: A plant cell that has taken in water by osmosis so that the cell surface membrane presses against the cell wall, which stops it expanding further.
Q: State three differences between active transport and facilitated diffusion.
A: Any three of: active transport moves substances against the concentration gradient, facilitated diffusion down it; active transport uses ATP, facilitated diffusion does not; active transport uses only carrier proteins, facilitated diffusion uses channel or carrier proteins; active transport can build up a concentration difference, facilitated diffusion cannot.
Q: Explain why glucose cannot cross a cell surface membrane by simple diffusion.
A: Glucose is a large polar molecule, so it cannot dissolve in the hydrophobic fatty acid tails in the centre of the bilayer. It crosses through carrier proteins.
Q: Give two differences between endocytosis and exocytosis.
A: Endocytosis brings material into the cell, exocytosis takes it out. In endocytosis the membrane folds inwards and pinches off a vesicle; in exocytosis a vesicle fuses with the cell surface membrane.
Q: Explain why a large animal needs a transport system but an amoeba does not.
A: The amoeba has a large SA:V and a short diffusion distance, so oxygen and nutrients diffuse to all parts fast enough. The large animal has a small SA:V and a long diffusion distance from the surface to its inner cells, so diffusion alone is too slow and a transport system is needed.
:::

:::recap
- Passive transport (simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis) uses no ATP. Active transport and bulk transport (endocytosis, exocytosis) use ATP.
- Diffusion is net movement down a concentration gradient; its rate is increased by a steeper gradient, a shorter distance, a larger surface area, a higher temperature and smaller or lipid-soluble molecules.
- Facilitated diffusion: channel and carrier proteins for ions and polar molecules, with a maximum rate when all the proteins are in use.
- SA:V decreases as size increases. Cube: \(SA = 6L^2\), \(V = L^3\), SA:V \(= 6/L\). Cylinder: \(SA = 2\pi r^2 + 2\pi r h\), \(V = \pi r^2 h\).
- Water potential (kPa): pure water 0, solutions negative. Osmosis: net movement of water from a higher to a lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane.
- Plant cells: turgid, flaccid, incipient plasmolysis, plasmolysed; the wall stops them bursting. Animal cells: crenated or lysed.
- Percentage change in mass \(= \dfrac{\text{final} - \text{initial}}{\text{initial}} \times 100\); the x-intercept of percentage change against concentration is the concentration with the same water potential as the tissue.
- Active transport: carrier protein, against the gradient, ATP hydrolysed so the carrier changes shape; the sodium–potassium pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in.
- Endocytosis (phagocytosis, pinocytosis) and exocytosis move materials in vesicles using ATP.
:::
