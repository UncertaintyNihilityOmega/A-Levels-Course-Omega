## Natural selection

Individuals in a population differ from one another (see [Variation and the t-test](#bio.variation)). Some of the differences are inherited. Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace explained how, when the environment favours some of these differences, the population changes over generations.

:::def Gene pool
All the alleles of all the genes in all the individuals of a population at one time.
:::

:::def Allele frequency
The proportion of all the copies of a gene in a population that are a particular allele, given as a fraction between 0 and 1 (or as a percentage).
:::

:::def Natural selection
The process in which individuals with characteristics that make them best adapted to their environment are more likely to survive and reproduce, so they pass on their alleles to the next generation in greater numbers, and the frequency of those alleles in the population increases.
:::

Natural selection is a chain of events. Every link has to be named in an answer.

:::steps
1. **Variation.** Individuals in the population differ, because new alleles arise by mutation and existing alleles are shuffled by meiosis and fertilisation.
2. **Overproduction.** Populations have the capacity to produce many more offspring than can survive. A cod releases millions of eggs, and an oak tree produces thousands of acorns.
3. **Struggle for existence.** The offspring compete for limited resources (food, space, light, mates) and face predators, disease and harsh conditions. Many die. The population does not usually grow without limit.
4. **Selection.** An environmental factor, the **selection pressure**, favours some phenotypes. Individuals with the alleles that make them **best adapted** are more likely to survive.
5. **Reproduction.** The survivors **reproduce** and pass on their **alleles** to their offspring.
6. **Change in allele frequency.** The advantageous alleles become more frequent in the next generation. After many generations the population is better adapted to its environment.
:::

:::key Selection acts on phenotypes and changes allele frequencies
The environment selects **phenotypes**, but what changes from one generation to the next is the **frequency of alleles** in the gene pool. An individual does not change its alleles. A successful individual is one that survives **and reproduces**.
:::

:::warn Four wrong ideas about natural selection
**"Organisms adapt because they need to."** No: the variation was already there (arising by chance, before the change in the environment). The environment selects among it.
**"Survival of the fittest."** Survival alone is not enough: an animal that survives for 20 years but leaves no offspring contributes nothing. Selection is about survival **and reproduction**, and passing on alleles.
**"The mutation was caused by the antibiotic (or the cold, or the poison)."** No: mutations are random. The environmental factor only selects.
**"An individual evolves."** No: populations evolve over generations. An individual cannot change its alleles to suit its environment.
:::

## Types of selection

The effect of selection on a **characteristic that shows continuous variation** (a bell-shaped distribution) depends on which phenotypes the environment favours. There are three patterns.

:::def Stabilising selection
Selection that favours the **intermediate** phenotypes and acts against both extremes. The mean stays the same and the spread of the distribution decreases.
:::

:::def Directional selection
Selection that favours **one extreme** phenotype. It acts against the other extreme and the mean moves in the direction of the favoured extreme.
:::

:::def Disruptive selection
Selection that favours **both extremes** and acts against the intermediate phenotypes. The distribution develops two peaks, and the population may split into two groups.
:::

- **Stabilising selection** is common in a stable environment. Very small babies lose heat quickly and are more likely to die of infection, and very large babies are more likely to cause difficulties at birth, so babies of intermediate mass have the highest survival.
- **Directional selection** occurs when the environment changes. When a new antibiotic is used, the bacteria that have a mutation for resistance survive and the mean resistance of the population increases. The beetle example above is also directional selection.

## Genetic drift, the founder effect and the bottleneck effect

Natural selection is not the only way that allele frequencies change. Allele frequencies also change **by chance**.

:::def Genetic drift
Random changes in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, caused by chance in which individuals reproduce and which alleles are passed on, not by differences in adaptation. Its effects are strongest in small populations.
:::

Each generation, the alleles in the offspring are a **sample** of the alleles of the parents. A small sample is likely to differ from the whole simply by chance, in the same way that tossing a coin ten times may give 7 heads, but a thousand tosses will give close to half. So in a small population an allele frequency can rise or fall at random from one generation to the next. If an allele is lost completely (frequency 0), it cannot return, unless there is new mutation. If it reaches a frequency of 1, it is **fixed**. Over time, drift in a small population reduces genetic variation.

:::key Drift compared with natural selection
- **Natural selection** is **non-random**: it favours alleles that increase survival and reproduction, so a population becomes better adapted.
- **Genetic drift** is **random**: the alleles that increase or decrease are not better or worse. It has the largest effect in **small** populations.
:::

### The founder effect

:::def Founder effect
The reduction in genetic variation, and the change in allele frequencies, that occurs when a small number of individuals leave a population and found a new one. The founders carry only a sample of the alleles of the original population, so the allele frequencies in the new population can be very different from those of the original.
:::

For example, a few birds are blown to a remote island and found a new population. The founders carry only a small sample of the original population's alleles. A rare allele that happens to be carried by a founder can be common in the new population, and an allele that is common on the mainland may be absent from the island.

:::eg On the mainland, the frequency of a recessive allele a is 0.02. Eight individuals found a new population on an island. Of the eight, three are heterozygous (Aa) and five are homozygous AA. Calculate the frequency of the allele a in the founders and compare the proportion of aa individuals expected on the island with that on the mainland. Explain the difference.
The eight founders have 16 alleles in total. The three heterozygotes carry 3 copies of a.

\[\begin{aligned} q_{\text{island}} &= \frac{3}{16} \\ &= 0.19 \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

On the mainland, \(q^2 = 0.02^2 = 0.0004\), or 0.04%, of individuals are aa. On the island, \(q^2 = (3/16)^2 = 0.035\), or 3.5%. This is about 88 times as many. [B1]

The founders carried only a small sample of the mainland gene pool by chance, and this sample happened to include more copies of the allele than are normal on the mainland. So the allele is much more frequent in the new population, which is the founder effect. Natural selection has not caused the difference: the allele need not be advantageous. [B1]
:::

### The bottleneck effect

:::def Bottleneck effect
A large, sudden reduction in the size of a population (for example by disease, a natural disaster or hunting) that leaves only a few survivors. The survivors carry only a sample of the original gene pool, so the genetic variation is reduced and the allele frequencies may be very different. Even if the population recovers, the reduced variation remains.
:::

Compare the two:

| | founder effect | bottleneck effect |
|---|---|---|
| cause | a few individuals found a **new** population | the **same** population is reduced to a few survivors |
| genetic result | the new population has a sample of the alleles of the original | the surviving population has a sample of the alleles it had before |
| effect on variation | reduced variation in the new population | reduced variation, even when numbers recover |
| example | island colonisation | northern elephant seals after hunting; cheetahs |

The effects of a bottleneck are serious for a rare species. With little variation, the species has fewer alleles that could be useful if the environment changes or a new disease appears, and individuals are more likely to be homozygous for harmful recessive alleles because related individuals mate. This is why conservation programmes try to keep populations large and varied (see [Conservation](#bio.conservation)).

## Antibiotic resistance as natural selection

Resistance to antibiotics is one of the clearest examples of natural selection happening now. It follows the chain exactly (details in [Antibiotics and resistance](#bio.antibiotics)).

:::steps
1. **Variation.** In a population of bacteria, a gene mutation (random, with or without the antibiotic) gives one bacterium an allele for resistance, for example an enzyme that breaks down penicillin.
2. **Overproduction.** Bacteria reproduce very quickly, so there are large numbers.
3. **Selection pressure.** The antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria. This is the environmental factor that selects.
4. **Survival and reproduction.** The resistant bacteria survive, with less competition, and reproduce by binary fission. They pass the resistance allele to their offspring.
5. **Change in allele frequency.** After a few generations, most of the bacteria in the population carry the resistance allele. This is directional selection. Bacteria can also pass the allele to other bacteria on plasmids.
:::

The same chain explains why overuse of antibiotics, and failure to complete a course, increases resistance: each use is a selection pressure that favours the resistant bacteria.

## The Hardy–Weinberg principle

How can you tell whether allele frequencies are changing? You need a standard for comparison: what would the frequencies be if nothing were changing? The Hardy–Weinberg principle gives this standard.

:::def Hardy–Weinberg principle
In a population that is large, with random mating and no mutation, no migration and no selection, the allele frequencies and genotype frequencies stay constant from one generation to the next. For a gene with two alleles, the genotype frequencies can be calculated from the allele frequencies.
:::

Take a gene with two alleles, a dominant allele **A** and a recessive allele **a**. Let \(p\) be the frequency of A and \(q\) the frequency of a, each as a fraction between 0 and 1.

:::formula The Hardy–Weinberg equations (given in the exam when needed)
\[p + q = 1\]
\[p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\]
\(p\) = frequency of the dominant allele; \(q\) = frequency of the recessive allele; \(p^2\) = frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype (AA); \(2pq\) = frequency of the heterozygous genotype (Aa, the carriers); \(q^2\) = frequency of the homozygous recessive genotype (aa).
:::

Why? Every individual gets one allele from each parent. If the gametes combine at random, the probability that both are A is \(p \times p = p^2\); both a is \(q^2\); and A with a, in either order, is \(2pq\). So the three genotype frequencies add up to \((p + q)^2 = 1\).

The Hardy–Weinberg equations are only true if these **conditions** hold. In real populations, some of them never hold exactly.

- The population is **large** (so drift is negligible).
- **Random mating**: individuals choose mates without regard to genotype.
- **No mutation**: no new alleles arise and the alleles do not change.
- **No migration**: no alleles enter or leave the population.
- **No selection**: all genotypes are equally likely to survive and reproduce.

:::key Calculating with Hardy–Weinberg
1. Identify what you are given. Usually this is the frequency of the **recessive phenotype** (the only phenotype that shows the genotype aa), which is \(q^2\).
2. Take the square root to find \(q\).
3. Find \(p = 1 - q\).
4. Calculate \(p^2\), \(2pq\) and \(q^2\), and check that they add up to 1.
5. For a number of individuals, multiply the frequency by the size of the population.
:::

:::warn Where students lose marks
**\(q^2\) is the recessive phenotype, not \(q\).** If 1 in 2500 people have the condition, \(q^2 = 1/2500\), and you must take the square root.
**The carriers are \(2pq\), not \(q^2\) and not \(q\).**
**The dominant phenotype is \(p^2 + 2pq\)**, not \(p^2\). You cannot take \(p\) from it directly. Use \(q^2 = 1 - (\text{dominant phenotype})\).
**Keep unrounded numbers** until the final answer, then give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data, or one more.
:::

:::eg Cystic fibrosis is a recessive disorder. In a European population, about 1 in 2500 babies is born with the condition. Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele and the frequency of carriers, and the number of carriers in a population of 1 000 000.
The people with the condition are homozygous recessive (aa).

\[\begin{aligned} q^2 &= \frac{1}{2500} = 0.0004 \\ q &= \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02 \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

\[\begin{aligned} p &= 1 - q = 0.98 \\ 2pq &= 2 \times 0.98 \times 0.02 \\ &= 0.0392 \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

The frequency of carriers is 0.0392 (3.9%), about 1 in 26 people.

Number of carriers in 1 000 000 people \(= 0.0392 \times 1\,000\,000 = 39\,200\). [A1]

Check: \(p^2 = 0.9604\), and \(0.9604 + 0.0392 + 0.0004 = 1\).
:::

Use the calculator to check your own working, or to see how the three genotype frequencies change when the recessive allele is rare or common. Enter \(q^2\), \(p^2\) or \(2pq\) as a frequency, a percentage or "1 in N".

:::eg In a population of plants, 91% have purple flowers (dominant) and 9% have white flowers (recessive). Assume the population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. Calculate the frequency of the heterozygous plants.
White-flowered plants are aa, so \(q^2 = 0.09\).

\[\begin{aligned} q &= \sqrt{0.09} = 0.30 \\ p &= 1 - 0.30 = 0.70 \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

\[\begin{aligned} 2pq &= 2 \times 0.70 \times 0.30 \\ &= 0.42 \end{aligned}\]

[A1]

So 42% of the plants are heterozygous. The three genotypes are: \(p^2 = 0.49\) (AA), \(2pq = 0.42\) (Aa), \(q^2 = 0.09\) (aa), and \(0.49 + 0.42 + 0.09 = 1\). Note that the purple-flowered plants (91% of the population) are 49% AA and 42% Aa, so about 46% of them (42 out of 91) carry the allele for white.
:::

### Using Hardy–Weinberg to test for change

If the genotype frequencies of a real population match the Hardy–Weinberg values, the population is in equilibrium for that gene: nothing is changing. If they do not match, or if \(q\) changes from one generation to the next, at least one condition is not met. Selection, migration, mutation, non-random mating or drift is acting.

:::eg A sample of 500 mice was genotyped for a gene with alleles A and a. There were 280 AA, 140 Aa and 80 aa. Calculate the allele frequencies and the numbers of each genotype expected in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, and suggest what the difference shows.
Each mouse has two alleles, so there are 1000 alleles. Copies of A: \(2 \times 280 + 140 = 700\), so \(p = 700/1000 = 0.70\) and \(q = 0.30\). [M1] [A1]

Expected: AA \(= 0.49 \times 500 = 245\), Aa \(= 0.42 \times 500 = 210\), aa \(= 0.09 \times 500 = 45\). [A1]

There are fewer heterozygotes than expected (140 compared with 210), and more of each homozygote. [B1]

The population is not in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, so one of the conditions is not met. This pattern suggests non-random mating (mice mating with relatives or with similar genotypes), or selection against heterozygotes, but the data alone do not show which. [B1]
:::

:::eg The frequency of homozygous recessive white rabbits in a population was 9% in 1990 and 20.25% in 2020. Calculate \(q\) in each year and suggest what the change shows.
\[\begin{aligned} 1990:\ q &= \sqrt{0.09} = 0.30 \\ 2020:\ q &= \sqrt{0.2025} = 0.45 \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

The frequency of the recessive allele has risen from 0.30 to 0.45, so the allele frequency has changed. [B1]

So the population is not in equilibrium. One or more of the conditions is not met: a likely cause is natural selection in favour of the white allele (for example, because white rabbits are better camouflaged, perhaps after a change in habitat). [B1]
:::

## Selective breeding

:::def Selective breeding (artificial selection)
The process in which humans choose which individuals with desired characteristics will breed, over many generations, so that the frequency of the alleles for those characteristics increases in the population.
:::

In natural selection, the environment selects. In selective breeding, **humans are the selective agent**: they decide which plants and animals reproduce. The principles are the same as natural selection, so the chain of ideas is similar.

:::steps
1. **Decide on the characteristics** wanted (for example high yield, disease resistance, or high milk production). There must be genetic variation for the characteristic.
2. **Select** the individuals with the best expression of the characteristic as parents.
3. **Breed** the selected individuals together.
4. **Select** the best offspring (those with the characteristic most strongly expressed) and use them as parents.
5. **Repeat** for many generations. The alleles for the characteristic become more frequent.
:::

| | natural selection | selective breeding |
|---|---|---|
| selecting agent | the environment (predators, disease, climate, competition) | humans |
| what is selected | survival and reproduction in the environment | the characteristics humans want |
| characteristics favoured | those that increase survival and reproduction | those useful to humans, even if harmful to the organism |
| speed | slow (many generations) | can be faster, as breeders control which individuals mate |
| genetic variation | maintained (usually) | usually reduced |

The disadvantages of selective breeding include a loss of genetic variation, so a new disease may affect all the individuals, and the build-up of harmful recessive alleles when related individuals are bred together.

### Disease resistance in wheat and rice

Wheat and rice crops are attacked by fungi, bacteria and viruses. A high-yielding variety may have no resistance, while a wild relative or an old variety may carry an allele for resistance. A breeder:

:::steps
1. **Crosses** the high-yielding variety with the resistant variety.
2. **Tests** the offspring by infecting the young plants with the pathogen, and selects the offspring that are resistant.
3. **Backcrosses** the resistant offspring with the high-yielding parent, and again tests and selects, over several generations. This brings back the high yield of one parent while keeping the resistance allele of the other.
4. **Selects** a variety that has both high yield and resistance, and breeds enough seeds to sell to farmers.
:::

The pathogen is also under selection: a new strain may overcome the resistance allele. So breeders must keep looking for new sources of resistance.

### Inbreeding and hybridisation in maize

Maize (corn) shows two methods: **inbreeding** and **hybridisation**.

- **Inbreeding**: maize plants are self-pollinated, or crossed with close relatives, for several generations. The plants become **homozygous** at more and more genes and all the plants in an inbred line become genetically similar, so the line is **uniform**. But harmful recessive alleles also become homozygous, so inbred lines are weak and have a low yield. This is **inbreeding depression**.
- **Hybridisation**: two different inbred lines are crossed. The offspring (the F1 hybrids) are heterozygous at many genes, so the harmful recessive alleles are not expressed. The F1 hybrids are **vigorous** and high-yielding (**hybrid vigour**), and they are all genetically identical, so they are **uniform** in height and ripening time, which suits mechanical harvesting.

Seed saved from the F1 plants gives an F2 generation in which the alleles have segregated, so it is not uniform or as vigorous. Farmers therefore buy new F1 seed each year.

:::eg Outline how inbreeding and hybridisation are used to produce vigorous, uniform maize for sale to farmers.
Maize plants are self-pollinated (inbred) over several generations to produce inbred lines that are homozygous for most genes. [B1]

The inbred lines are uniform but have low vigour and yield (inbreeding depression), because harmful recessive alleles are homozygous. [B1]

Two different inbred lines are crossed with each other. [B1]

The F1 offspring are heterozygous at many genes, so harmful recessive alleles are not expressed and the plants show hybrid vigour (high yield). [B1]

All the F1 plants have the same genotype, so they are uniform. [B1]

(Also credit: farmers have to buy new seed each year because the F2 generation would not be uniform; the inbred lines are kept as pure parent lines.)

A weaker answer says "the best maize plants are bred together". It does not name inbreeding (homozygous lines), crossing two lines, or why the hybrids are vigorous and uniform, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

### Milk yield in dairy cattle

The characteristic wanted is a high yield of milk. It can only be measured in **females**, because only cows make milk, but a bull carries alleles for milk yield too, and passes them to his daughters. A breeder:

- chooses **bulls** by **progeny testing**: a bull is mated with many cows and the milk yield of his **daughters** is recorded. Bulls whose daughters have the highest mean yield are the ones that carry the best alleles.
- uses **artificial insemination (AI)**: semen from the best bulls is collected, stored and used to fertilise cows on many farms, so the alleles of a few bulls spread through a large number of cattle.
- chooses **cows** with a high milk yield as mothers.
- keeps the best calves and repeats the process each generation.

:::eg Explain how the milk yield of a herd of dairy cattle can be increased by selective breeding.
Bulls cannot be selected by their own milk yield, so bulls are chosen by progeny testing: the milk yield of their daughters is measured and the bulls with the highest mean yield are selected. [B1]

Semen from the best bulls is used for artificial insemination of many cows, so the alleles for high yield are spread widely. [B1]

Cows with the highest milk yields are chosen as mothers. [B1]

The best offspring are chosen as parents in each generation, so the frequency of alleles for high milk yield increases and the mean yield of the herd rises. [B1]
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State what is meant by the term gene pool.
A: All the alleles of all the genes of all the individuals in a population at one time.
Q: Explain why "survival of the fittest" is a poor description of natural selection.
A: Survival alone does not matter: an individual must also reproduce and pass on its alleles. What changes is the frequency of alleles in the population over generations, not just which individuals survive.
Q: State the type of selection in each case: (a) the mean mass of babies stays the same, and the number of very small and very large babies decreases; (b) in a population of bacteria, the mean resistance increases after antibiotics are used.
A: (a) Stabilising selection. (b) Directional selection.
Q: Explain why genetic drift has a greater effect in a small population than in a large one.
A: The alleles passed on are a small sample of the population's alleles, so chance can change the frequency a lot. In a large population the sample is large and so more likely to represent the whole.
Q: A recessive disorder occurs in 1 in 10 000 births. Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele and of carriers.
A: \(q^2 = 0.0001\), so \(q = 0.01\). \(p = 0.99\). \(2pq = 2 \times 0.99 \times 0.01 = 0.0198\) (about 2%).
Q: In a population, the frequency of allele A is 0.8. Calculate the expected frequencies of the three genotypes.
A: \(q = 0.2\). AA: \(0.8^2 = 0.64\). Aa: \(2 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.32\). aa: \(0.2^2 = 0.04\). Total \(= 1\).
Q: State three conditions that must be met for the Hardy–Weinberg principle to apply.
A: Any three of: a large population, random mating, no mutation, no migration (no gene flow), no selection.
Q: Describe the difference between the founder effect and the bottleneck effect.
A: In the founder effect, a few individuals leave a population and found a new one, which carries a sample of the alleles. In the bottleneck effect, an existing population is reduced to a few survivors by an event, and the survivors carry a sample of the alleles it had.
Q: Suggest one disadvantage of selective breeding for the species concerned.
A: Any one of: it reduces genetic variation, so a new disease or a change in conditions could affect all individuals; related animals or plants are bred together, so harmful recessive alleles become homozygous; the characteristics chosen by humans may reduce the organism's health or survival.
Q: State three differences between natural selection and selective breeding.
A: Any three of: the selecting agent is the environment in natural selection and humans in selective breeding; natural selection favours survival and reproduction, while breeders favour characteristics useful to humans; selective breeding can be faster; selective breeding usually reduces genetic variation more.

Q: [3 marks] ★ A pair of blue tits can raise more than 10 chicks in a year, but the number of blue tits in a wood stays about the same from year to year. Explain why.
A: The birds produce many more offspring than can survive (overproduction). [B1]
The birds compete for limited resources such as food and nest sites, and are killed by predators and disease (struggle for existence). [B1]
So many die before they can reproduce and the population size stays about the same. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ The table shows the percentage of rats in a population that were resistant to a rat poison, measured over 12 years of heavy use of the poison.

|---|---|
| 0 | 2 |
| 3 | 6 |
| 6 | 19 |
| 9 | 48 |
| 12 | 74 |

(a) Describe the change. (b) Explain the change. (c) Name the type of selection.
H: Quote the percentage at the start and the end, and say when the increase was fastest.
H: Use the chain: variation, selection pressure, survival and reproduction, allele frequency.
A: (a) The percentage of resistant rats increases from 2% to 74% over 12 years. [B1]
The increase is fastest between years 6 and 9 (from 19% to 48%). [B1]
(b) The poison killed the non-resistant rats (the selection pressure), so the rats with the resistance allele (which arose by mutation) survived and reproduced. [B1]
They passed the resistance allele to their offspring, so its frequency increased in each generation. [B1]

Q: [4 marks] ★★ In a population of snails, 16% have yellow shells, a recessive characteristic. The population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. (a) Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele. (b) Calculate the percentage of snails that are heterozygous. (c) How many snails in a sample of 500 are heterozygous?
H: Yellow shells are the genotype aa, so \(q^2 = 0.16\).
A: (a) \(q = \sqrt{0.16}\) [M1] \(= 0.4\) [A1]
(b) \(p = 0.6\), so \(2pq = 2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 = 0.48\), or 48%. [A1]
(c) \(0.48 \times 500 = 240\) snails. [A1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ A population of 5000 field voles was reduced to 12 by a flood. The survivors bred and the population recovered to 5000 within 10 generations. (a) Name this effect and explain how it affects genetic variation. (b) Explain why the recovered population is more at risk from a new disease than the original population.
H: Did the flood select voles with better alleles?
A: (a) The bottleneck effect. [B1]
The 12 survivors carry only a small sample of the alleles of the original population, so some alleles are lost and the frequencies of others change by chance. [B1]
The genetic variation of the recovered population is lower than that of the original population. [B1]
(b) With less variation there is less chance that some individuals have an allele that gives resistance to the new disease. [B1]
So a larger proportion of the population is likely to be killed. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A sample of 400 ladybirds was genotyped for a gene with alleles B and b. The numbers were 144 BB, 192 Bb and 64 bb. (a) Calculate the frequencies of the alleles B and b. (b) Calculate the numbers of each genotype expected if the population was in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, and state what the comparison with the observed numbers shows. (c) Ten years later the frequency of b is 0.3. Suggest two possible explanations.
H: Each ladybird has two alleles: count the copies of B and divide by \(2 \times 400\).
A: (a) Copies of B \(= 2 \times 144 + 192 = 480\), out of 800, so \(p = 0.6\) and \(q = 0.4\). [M1] [A1]
(b) Expected BB \(= 0.36 \times 400 = 144\), Bb \(= 0.48 \times 400 = 192\), bb \(= 0.16 \times 400 = 64\), the same as the observed numbers. [A1]
So the population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium for this gene: the genotype frequencies are as expected from the allele frequencies. [B1]
(c) Natural selection against ladybirds with the b allele (they are less likely to survive or reproduce). [B1]
Migration of ladybirds carrying B into the population (or genetic drift if the population is small, or mutation). [B1]

People who are \(\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}\) are less likely to die of malaria than people who are \(\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^A}\), but people who are \(\mathrm{Hb^S Hb^S}\) usually die young. (a) Calculate the frequency of the \(\mathrm{Hb^S}\) allele and the percentage of the population who have sickle cell trait. (b) Explain why the \(\mathrm{Hb^S}\) allele is common here although it causes a serious disease.
H: \(q^2 = 0.01\). Carriers are \(2pq\).
H: Which genotype has the highest chance of surviving and reproducing in this region?
A: (a) \(q = \sqrt{0.01} = 0.1\) [M1] [A1]
\(p = 0.9\), so \(2pq = 2 \times 0.9 \times 0.1 = 0.18\), or 18% [A1]
(b) The heterozygotes are more likely to survive malaria than \(\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^A}\) people, so they are more likely to survive and reproduce. [B1]
They pass on both alleles, \(\mathrm{Hb^A}\) and \(\mathrm{Hb^S}\), so the \(\mathrm{Hb^S}\) allele stays in the gene pool. [B1]
Selection against \(\mathrm{Hb^S Hb^S}\) (anaemia) and \(\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^A}\) (malaria) keeps both alleles in the population. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The table shows the mean milk yield of the first 50 daughters of each of four bulls kept at an artificial insemination centre, in dm³ per cow per year.

|---|---|
| 1 | 5900 |
| 2 | 6450 |
| 3 | 6150 |
| 4 | 5600 |

(a) State which bull the centre should use to breed and explain how the data are used. (b) Calculate the percentage by which the mean yield of the daughters of the best bull is greater than that of the worst bull. (c) State one advantage and one disadvantage of using AI semen from a few bulls on many farms.
H: Remember that a bull cannot produce milk.
A: (a) Bull 2, because the mean yield of his daughters (6450) is the highest. [B1]
The daughters' yields show the alleles for milk yield that the bull carries and passes on. [B1]
(b) \(\dfrac{6450 - 5600}{5600} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 15.2\%\) [A1]
(c) Advantage: the alleles for high yield spread quickly to many herds, so the mean yield rises faster. [B1]

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ Outline how a breeder could produce a variety of wheat that has both high yield and resistance to a fungal disease, given a high-yielding variety that is susceptible to the fungus and a wild relative that is resistant but has a low yield.
H: Think about crosses, testing and backcrossing.
A: Cross the high-yielding variety with the resistant wild relative. [B1]

Backcross the resistant offspring with the high-yielding variety. [B1]
Test and select again, repeating over several generations, so that the high yield is recovered and the resistance allele kept. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ In a plant population the allele a is recessive and lethal when homozygous: aa seedlings die before they reproduce. Before selection, \(q = 0.20\). Assume random mating and Hardy–Weinberg proportions at fertilisation. (a) Calculate the frequencies of the genotypes AA, Aa and aa at fertilisation. (b) Calculate the proportion of the surviving plants that are Aa. (c) Calculate the value of \(q\) among the gametes made by the survivors. (d) Explain why a lethal recessive allele declines only slowly.

:::recap
- Natural selection: variation, overproduction, struggle for existence, best adapted survive and reproduce, pass on alleles, allele frequency changes. Populations evolve, not individuals.
- Stabilising selection favours the intermediate (mean unchanged, spread decreases); directional selection favours one extreme (mean moves); disruptive selection favours both extremes (two peaks).
- Genetic drift: random change in allele frequency, strongest in small populations. Founder effect: a few individuals found a new population. Bottleneck effect: a population is reduced to a few survivors. Both reduce genetic variation.
- Antibiotic resistance: random mutation, antibiotic as selection pressure, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, allele frequency rises (directional selection).
- Hardy–Weinberg: \(p + q = 1\), \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\). \(q^2\) is the recessive phenotype; take the square root for \(q\). Carriers are \(2pq\). Conditions: large population, random mating, no mutation, migration or selection.
- A change in \(q\) from one generation to the next shows that a condition is not met, for example selection.
- Selective breeding: humans choose parents with the desired characteristics, breed them, select the best offspring and repeat. Wheat and rice: cross, test, backcross. Maize: inbred lines then crossed to give uniform, vigorous F1 hybrids (new seed each year). Dairy cattle: progeny testing of bulls, AI.
:::
