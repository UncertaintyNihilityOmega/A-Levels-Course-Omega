Q: What is the difference between a dominant and a recessive allele? (from [Monohybrid inheritance](#bio.inheritance))

## From gene to protein to phenotype

An allele is one form of a gene. Different alleles have different base sequences, and so they can code for polypeptides with different amino acid sequences. Almost every feature you can see or measure in an organism, its **phenotype**, is the result of what its proteins do. Enzymes make pigments, haemoglobin carries oxygen, clotting proteins stop bleeding, and hormone receptors respond to signals. So the link from DNA to feature always runs the same way.

:::key The chain from gene to phenotype
1. A gene (an allele) has a particular **base sequence**.
2. Transcription copies it into **mRNA**, whose codons are read in translation.
3. The codons decide the **amino acid sequence** (primary structure) of the polypeptide.
4. The primary structure decides the **tertiary structure** (the shape) of the protein.
5. The shape decides what the protein **does** (or whether it does anything).
6. What the protein does (or fails to do) produces the **phenotype**.
:::

A mutation can break the chain at any link. A base substitution can change one amino acid and change the shape. A deletion can shift the reading frame. A mutation that removes the start of the gene can mean no protein at all. Whether the mutant allele is dominant or recessive depends on what its protein does, as the four examples below show.

:::exam Write the whole chain
A common question asks you to "explain how a change in a gene causes a disease". Marks go to each link in the chain: the changed base or codon, the changed amino acid, the changed shape of the protein, the lost or new function, and the effect on the person. Writing "the gene is faulty so he gets the disease" scores nothing.
:::

## Albinism: the TYR gene and tyrosinase

Melanin is the dark pigment in skin, hair and the iris of the eye. It is made in special cells by a pathway of enzyme-catalysed reactions that starts from the amino acid tyrosine. The **TYR gene** codes for the enzyme **tyrosinase**, which catalyses the first steps: tyrosine is converted to a molecule called DOPA, and then, through further steps, to melanin.

:::steps
1. The TYR gene is transcribed and translated into the enzyme tyrosinase.
2. Tyrosinase catalyses the conversion of **tyrosine** to **DOPA**, the first steps on the pathway to melanin.
3. The pathway continues and **melanin** is made.
4. Melanin gives colour to skin, hair and eyes, and absorbs some ultraviolet light.
:::

**Albinism** is a condition in which little or no melanin is made. People with albinism have very pale skin, white or pale hair and pale eyes. They burn easily in sunlight and often have poor eyesight. The usual form is caused by recessive alleles of TYR on an autosome. The mutant alleles code for a tyrosinase that is not made or that is **non-functional** (for example, because an amino acid change alters the active site).

- A person with two non-functional alleles has **no working tyrosinase**. Tyrosine cannot be converted to DOPA, so no melanin is made.
- A **heterozygous** person has one working allele. It makes enough tyrosinase for normal pigment, so the recessive allele has no effect on the phenotype. This person is a **carrier**.
- Two carriers have a 1 in 4 chance of an affected child in each pregnancy (the monohybrid cross in [Monohybrid inheritance](#bio.inheritance)).

:::warn Albinism is not a lack of tyrosine
Tyrosine is an amino acid from food and from the person's own proteins, and people with albinism have plenty. What is missing is the **enzyme** that uses it, so the substrate cannot be converted. Write "no functional tyrosinase", not "no tyrosine".
:::

## Sickle cell anaemia: the HBB gene and haemoglobin

Adult haemoglobin has four polypeptide chains: two α-globin and two β-globin (see [Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide](#bio.oxygen)). The **HBB gene** codes for the β-globin chain. In sickle cell anaemia a single base in HBB is substituted. In the mRNA, the codon for the **sixth** amino acid changes from GAG to GUG, so **glutamic acid** is replaced by **valine**.

| | normal allele | sickle cell allele |
|---|---|---|
| name | \(\mathrm{Hb^A}\) | \(\mathrm{Hb^S}\) |
| mRNA codon 6 | GAG | GUG |
| amino acid 6 | glutamic acid | valine |
| R group | hydrophilic, negatively charged | hydrophobic |
| haemoglobin | HbA | HbS |

Follow the chain from the key box:

:::steps
1. A **base substitution** changes the codon for amino acid 6 of β-globin from GAG to GUG.
2. Valine replaces glutamic acid. Glutamic acid has a charged, hydrophilic R group; valine has a non-polar, **hydrophobic** R group.
3. HbS now has a hydrophobic patch on its **surface**. When the oxygen concentration is low, HbS molecules stick together by hydrophobic interactions and form long **fibres** inside the red blood cell.
4. The fibres distort the red cell into a rigid **sickle** shape.
5. Sickled cells cannot squeeze through narrow capillaries, so they **block** them and cut off the blood supply to tissues. They also carry less oxygen and break down sooner than normal cells, so the person becomes **anaemic**.
:::

Both alleles are expressed in a heterozygote, so the alleles are **codominant**. A person who is \(\mathrm{Hb^A Hb^S}\) makes both HbA and HbS: this is **sickle cell trait**. Most of their haemoglobin works normally, so they usually have no symptoms. Only \(\mathrm{Hb^S Hb^S}\) people have sickle cell anaemia. The genotype can be shown with the codominance notation (the allele symbols are the same capital letter with different superscripts), as you learnt in [Monohybrid inheritance](#bio.inheritance).

:::warn One base, not a deletion
Sickle cell anaemia is caused by a **substitution** of one base, not by a deletion or by "missing" haemoglobin. A deletion would shift the whole reading frame and change many amino acids; here exactly one amino acid is changed and the rest of the chain is normal.
:::

:::eg Explain how a change of a single base in the HBB gene causes the symptoms of sickle cell anaemia. In the mRNA, the codon for amino acid 6 of β-globin changes from GAG, which codes for glutamic acid, to GUG, which codes for valine.
A full-mark answer follows the chain from the gene to the person, one marking point per link.

The substitution of one base changes the mRNA codon from GAG to GUG. [B1]

So the amino acid valine replaces glutamic acid in the primary structure of β-globin. [B1]

Glutamic acid has a hydrophilic, charged R group but valine has a hydrophobic R group, so the surface of the haemoglobin molecule is different. [B1]

At a low oxygen concentration, HbS molecules stick together and form long fibres inside the red blood cell. [B1]

The red cell is distorted into a sickle shape, and these cells block capillaries. [B1]

The sickled cells carry less oxygen and are destroyed more quickly, so the person is anaemic. [B1]

(Also credit: the tertiary structure of the protein is changed; the other amino acids are unchanged; tissues receive too little oxygen, causing pain and damage.)

A weaker answer says "the gene is faulty so the haemoglobin is faulty and the red blood cells are the wrong shape". It names no codon, no amino acid, no change in R group and no reason why the shape changes, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## Haemophilia: the F8 gene and factor VIII

When a blood vessel is damaged, a series of reactions called the clotting cascade turns soluble fibrinogen into insoluble **fibrin**, which forms a mesh that traps blood cells and seals the wound. Each step in the cascade needs a protein, called a clotting factor. **Factor VIII** is one of these proteins, found in the blood plasma. The **F8 gene** codes for factor VIII.

The F8 gene is on the **X chromosome**, and the Y chromosome has no copy. Mutant alleles that code for no factor VIII, or for a non-functional one, are **recessive**. In **haemophilia A**, blood clots very slowly after an injury, so bleeding is prolonged and there can be bleeding into joints and under the skin. The condition is **sex-linked** and shows the pattern you met in [Monohybrid inheritance](#bio.inheritance):

- A **male** has one X chromosome. If it carries the mutant allele (\(\mathrm{X^h Y}\)), he has no second copy to make up for it, so he has haemophilia.
- A **female** has two X chromosomes. A female with one mutant allele (\(\mathrm{X^H X^h}\)) is a **carrier**: her working allele makes enough factor VIII for normal clotting. She has haemophilia only if both alleles are mutant, which is rare.
- A father passes his Y chromosome to his sons, so a father **cannot pass an X-linked allele to a son**. He passes his X chromosome, with its allele, to all his daughters.

| | \(\mathrm{X^H}\) (father) | \(\mathrm{Y}\) (father) |
|---|---|---|
| \(\mathrm{X^H}\) (carrier mother) | \(\mathrm{X^H X^H}\) normal girl | \(\mathrm{X^H Y}\) normal boy |
| \(\mathrm{X^h}\) (carrier mother) | \(\mathrm{X^H X^h}\) carrier girl | \(\mathrm{X^h Y}\) boy with haemophilia |

A carrier mother and an unaffected father therefore have a 1 in 2 chance that any son has haemophilia, and a 1 in 2 chance that any daughter is a carrier. No daughter has haemophilia.

Many different mutations in F8 can cause haemophilia: substitutions, deletions and others. They all end in the same way. No working factor VIII means the cascade is slowed, little fibrin forms and clotting is slow. People with haemophilia A are treated by injecting factor VIII, which is a protein and has to be replaced regularly because it does not last in the blood.

## Huntington's disease: the HTT gene and huntingtin

The **HTT gene** codes for a protein called **huntingtin**, which is found in nerve cells (neurones); its exact normal role is not fully understood. Near the start of the gene the base triplet CAG is repeated many times in a row. CAG codes for the amino acid glutamine, so the polypeptide contains a stretch of glutamines. Most people have between about 10 and 35 CAG repeats.

In **Huntington's disease**, the allele has an **expanded repeat**: 40 or more CAG repeats, so a much longer stretch of glutamine. The longer the repeat, the earlier the disease begins.

:::steps
1. The mutant HTT allele has **more CAG repeats** than normal (the repeat has expanded).
2. The huntingtin polypeptide has a longer stretch of glutamines, and so it folds into an **abnormal protein**.
3. The abnormal huntingtin forms clumps and damages the nerve cells that contain it.
4. **Neurones die** in parts of the brain, including regions that control movement.
5. The person develops uncontrolled movements, changes of mood and personality, and loss of mental ability. Symptoms usually begin in **adulthood**, often between 30 and 50 years of age, and get worse.
:::

Huntington's disease is caused by a **dominant** allele on an autosome. One copy of the mutant allele is enough, because the abnormal protein is harmful even in a cell that also makes normal huntingtin: it gains a harmful property, rather than losing a function. A person who is heterozygous has a 1 in 2 chance of passing the allele to each child. Because symptoms usually start after the person has had children, the allele is passed on before the disease shows.

:::warn Dominant does not mean common
"Dominant" describes how an allele is expressed in the phenotype, not how frequent it is. The Huntington's allele is dominant but rare. Never write that the HTT allele is "recessive", and never write that the disease is caused by "a missing gene": the problem is an **extra-long repeat** in a gene that is present.
:::

:::eg Explain how the expanded CAG repeat in the HTT gene causes Huntington's disease, and why a person with one mutant allele is affected.
Follow the chain, then deal with the dominance.

The mutant HTT allele has more CAG repeats than the normal allele (40 or more). [B1]

CAG codes for glutamine, so the huntingtin polypeptide has an abnormally long stretch of glutamines. [B1]

The protein folds abnormally (its shape is changed) and damages nerve cells, which die in the brain. [B1]

The abnormal protein is harmful even when normal huntingtin is also present, so one copy of the allele is enough to cause the disease: the allele is dominant. [B1]

(Also credit: symptoms start in adulthood; each child of a heterozygous parent has a 50% chance of inheriting the allele.)

A weaker answer says "the gene is faulty so it kills nerve cells". It does not mention the repeat, the glutamines, the abnormal shape of the protein or why a single copy is enough.
:::

The four genes side by side:

| gene | protein | what goes wrong | inheritance |
|---|---|---|---|
| TYR | tyrosinase (enzyme) | no functional enzyme, so no melanin | autosomal, recessive |
| HBB | β-globin in haemoglobin | valine replaces glutamic acid, HbS sticks together | autosomal, codominant |
| F8 | factor VIII (clotting protein) | no functional factor VIII, slow clotting | X-linked, recessive |
| HTT | huntingtin | expanded CAG repeat, abnormal protein harms neurones | autosomal, dominant |

## Gibberellin and the gene Le

Plants grow tall because their stem cells elongate, and gibberellin is a plant hormone that stimulates this (see [Control and coordination in plants](#bio.hormones)). A plant makes active gibberellin from an inactive precursor in a series of steps, and each step is catalysed by an enzyme coded by a gene. The gene **Le** in pea plants is the gene for one of these enzymes.

:::def Le and le
**Le** is the dominant allele that codes for a **functional enzyme** in the gibberellin synthesis pathway; the enzyme converts an inactive precursor into active gibberellin (\(\mathrm{GA_1}\)). **le** is the recessive allele that codes for a **non-functional enzyme**.
:::

- A plant that is **Le Le** makes functional enzyme from both alleles, makes plenty of active gibberellin, and is **tall**.
- A plant that is **Le le** makes functional enzyme from the one working allele. That is enough enzyme to make enough gibberellin, so it is also **tall**.
- A plant that is **le le** makes only non-functional enzyme. Little active gibberellin is made, the stem cells hardly elongate and the plant is **dwarf**.

This is the gene that controls the tall and dwarf pea plants in Mendel's famous crosses, and the molecular explanation shows why tall is dominant: one working allele is enough to give the whole pathway.

A dwarf le le plant can be made to grow tall by spraying it with gibberellin, because the only thing it lacks is the hormone. But the spray changes the **phenotype**, not the **genotype**: its seeds still carry le le, and its offspring will be dwarf unless they are sprayed too.

:::eg Two heterozygous tall pea plants (Le le) are crossed. Explain why the offspring include dwarf plants, and why the heterozygous parents are tall.
Write the genetic diagram first.

| | Le | le |
|---|---|---|
| **Le** | Le Le tall | Le le tall |
| **le** | Le le tall | le le dwarf |

The offspring are 3 tall to 1 dwarf, because the gametes are Le or le and combine at random. [B1]

The le le plants have no functional enzyme in the gibberellin pathway, because le codes for a non-functional enzyme. [B1]

So they make little active gibberellin, and the stem cells do not elongate: the plant is dwarf. [B1]

The parents are tall because each has one Le allele, which codes for a functional enzyme, and one functional allele makes enough active gibberellin. [B1]

(Also credit: the le allele is recessive because its non-functional enzyme has no effect when a functional enzyme is also present.)
:::

## Structural genes and regulatory genes

Every cell contains all of an organism's genes, but it does not use all of them all the time. Producing a protein costs energy and raw materials, so a cell makes a protein only when it needs it. Genes fall into two groups according to their job.

:::def Structural gene
A gene that codes for a protein, for example an enzyme or a protein that forms part of a cell structure, which the cell uses in its metabolism or structure. Structural genes make the proteins that do the work.
:::

:::def Regulatory gene
A gene that codes for a protein (or an RNA) that controls the expression of other genes, for example a repressor protein or a transcription factor. Regulatory genes switch structural genes on and off.
:::

The proteins made by regulatory genes do not do the work themselves. They switch other genes on or off, in response to the needs of the cell. This leads to two patterns of enzyme production in bacteria.

:::def Inducible enzyme
An enzyme that is made only when its substrate is present. The substrate acts as the signal that switches on the gene. An example is β-galactosidase, which hydrolyses lactose.
:::

:::def Repressible enzyme
An enzyme that is made all the time unless the end product of its pathway is present. When the end product reaches a high concentration it switches off the genes for the enzymes that make it. An example is the set of enzymes that make the amino acid tryptophan.
:::

| | inducible enzyme | repressible enzyme |
|---|---|---|
| made when | substrate is present | end product is **absent** |
| switched off when | substrate is absent | end product is present |
| the signal molecule is | the substrate | the end product |
| why useful | no energy wasted on a useless enzyme | no energy wasted making a product that is already there |
| example | β-galactosidase (lactose) | enzymes for making tryptophan |

## The lac operon

A bacterium such as *Escherichia coli* usually feeds on glucose. If there is no glucose but there is lactose, it can use lactose instead, but only if it makes three proteins: a **permease** that carries lactose into the cell, the enzyme **β-galactosidase** that hydrolyses lactose to glucose and galactose, and a third enzyme, a transacetylase. Making all three when there is no lactose would waste energy. The genes for these three proteins are switched on only when lactose is present. This control is the **lac operon**.

:::def Operon
A group of structural genes in a prokaryote that are next to each other, share one promoter and one operator, and are transcribed together as one mRNA. Switching the operator on or off switches all of the genes on or off together.
:::

The parts of the lac operon are:

- **lacI**, the **regulatory gene**. It codes for the **repressor protein**. The repressor is made all the time, at a low rate.
- The **promoter**, the DNA sequence where RNA polymerase binds before it starts to transcribe.
- The **operator**, a short DNA sequence next to the promoter. The repressor protein can bind to it.
- Three **structural genes**: **lacZ** (β-galactosidase), **lacY** (lactose permease) and **lacA** (transacetylase).

The repressor protein has two binding sites: one fits the operator DNA, and the other fits lactose. This is why the system can sense lactose.

:::steps
1. **No lactose: the operon is off.** The repressor protein is made from lacI and binds to the **operator**. It stands in the way of RNA polymerase, so RNA polymerase cannot transcribe the structural genes. No β-galactosidase or permease is made.
2. **Lactose present: the operon is on.** Lactose binds to the repressor protein. This changes the repressor's shape, so it can no longer bind to the operator and leaves it.
3. RNA polymerase can now bind at the promoter and move along the DNA, **transcribing lacZ, lacY and lacA** as one mRNA.
4. The mRNA is translated, so β-galactosidase, permease and the transacetylase are made. The cell takes in lactose and hydrolyses it.
5. When the lactose has been used up, the repressor is no longer bound to lactose, binds to the operator again and the operon is switched off.
:::

So lactose is the **inducer**: it switches on the production of an enzyme that uses it, which makes β-galactosidase an inducible enzyme. The repressor acts as a switch that responds to the substrate.

:::note The true inducer
Inside the cell a little of the lactose is converted by β-galactosidase to a similar molecule, allolactose, and it is allolactose that binds to the repressor. At this level the syllabus says that lactose binds to the repressor, so write "lactose" in your answers.
:::

How can the cell make any β-galactosidase to begin with, when the operon is off? The repressor does not stay bound to the operator for ever: now and then it leaves, and RNA polymerase transcribes a few molecules of mRNA before the repressor returns. A few molecules of permease and β-galactosidase are therefore always present, so when lactose arrives some can enter the cell and switch the operon on. You do not need the role of cAMP or the effect of glucose.

:::warn The three commonest wrong statements about the lac operon
**"Lactose binds to the operator."** No: lactose binds to the **repressor**. The repressor binds to the operator.
**"The repressor is made only when there is no lactose."** No: lacI is expressed all the time. What lactose changes is whether the repressor can bind to the operator.
**"RNA polymerase binds to the operator."** No: RNA polymerase binds to the **promoter**. The repressor sits at the operator, next to the promoter, and stops RNA polymerase transcribing the genes.
:::

:::eg Explain how the lac operon is switched off when there is no lactose in the environment of an E. coli cell, and switched on when lactose is present.
Take the off condition first, then the on condition.

The regulatory gene lacI codes for a repressor protein, which is made all the time. [B1]

With no lactose, the repressor binds to the operator, so RNA polymerase cannot transcribe the structural genes (lacZ, lacY and lacA) and no enzymes are made. [B1]

When lactose is present, it binds to the repressor protein and changes its shape. [B1]

The repressor can no longer bind to the operator. [B1]

RNA polymerase can bind to the promoter and transcribe the structural genes. [B1]

β-galactosidase and lactose permease are made, so lactose can enter the cell and be hydrolysed. [B1]

(Also credit: the genes are transcribed as one mRNA; the transacetylase is also made; when lactose is used up, the repressor binds again.)

A weaker answer says "lactose switches the gene on" with no repressor, operator or promoter, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## Transcription factors in eukaryotes

Eukaryotes have no operons, but they too must switch genes on and off. In a human, for example, the gene for haemoglobin is switched on in developing red blood cells but not in neurones. Eukaryotic cells use proteins called transcription factors.

:::def Transcription factor
A protein that binds to a specific sequence of DNA near a gene and so controls the rate of transcription of that gene. Some transcription factors **increase** the rate of transcription; others **decrease** it.
:::

- A transcription factor is a **regulatory protein** coded by a regulatory gene. It has a shape (like an enzyme's active site) that fits a particular DNA sequence, usually near the **promoter** of the gene it controls.
- An **activating** transcription factor helps RNA polymerase bind to the promoter, so the gene is transcribed faster. A **repressing** transcription factor blocks the binding, so the gene is transcribed slower.
- A neurone makes a different set of proteins from a skin cell because different transcription factors are active in it. They are the reason why cells with identical DNA differ in structure and function.
- Transcription factors can be switched on or off by signals such as hormones. The next section shows an example.

The lac repressor and a eukaryotic transcription factor are both proteins that bind to DNA, and both change the rate of transcription. The differences are the setting (prokaryote with an operon, eukaryote without) and that eukaryotic genes usually need several transcription factors acting together.

## Gibberellin, DELLA proteins and gene expression

In the section on [Control and coordination in plants](#bio.hormones) you saw that gibberellin makes stems elongate and makes barley seeds produce amylase. Both happen because gibberellin switches on genes. The key is a group of proteins called DELLA proteins.

:::def DELLA protein
A protein in plant cells that acts as a repressor of growth. DELLA proteins bind to **transcription factors** that promote transcription and stop them from working, so the genes for growth (stem elongation) or for enzymes such as amylase are not transcribed.
:::

:::steps
1. **No gibberellin.** DELLA proteins are present. They bind to transcription factors (for example PIF proteins) and **inhibit** them, so the genes are not transcribed. The stem cells do not elongate.
2. **Gibberellin present.** Gibberellin binds to a receptor in the cell.
3. The gibberellin–receptor complex causes the **breakdown of the DELLA proteins**.
4. With the DELLA proteins gone, the transcription factors are **free** to bind to the promoters of their genes.
5. RNA polymerase transcribes the genes, and the proteins needed for **cell elongation** (or amylase in the aleurone layer of a barley grain) are made.
6. The stem cells elongate and the stem grows longer.
:::

Gibberellin therefore works by **removing a brake**. It does not switch on genes itself: it removes the protein that was holding the transcription factors back.

This connects the two halves of the chapter. A **le le** plant lacks the enzyme that makes active gibberellin, so DELLA proteins are never broken down, the transcription factors stay inhibited and the plant is dwarf. In a plant with a mutant DELLA that gibberellin cannot break down, the plant is dwarf however much gibberellin is present. In wheat, dwarf varieties with this kind of mutation were important in the Green Revolution, because short stems resist lodging (being flattened by wind and rain) when they carry heavy heads of grain.

Q: State the difference between a structural gene and a regulatory gene.
A: A structural gene codes for a protein (such as an enzyme) used in metabolism or structure. A regulatory gene codes for a protein that controls whether other genes are expressed (switched on or off).
Q: Explain the difference between an inducible enzyme and a repressible enzyme.
A: An inducible enzyme is made only when its substrate is present (and not made when it is absent). A repressible enzyme is made unless its end product is present: a high concentration of the product switches the gene off.
Q: State two reasons why an albino person has no melanin even though the diet supplies tyrosine.
A: The person has two non-functional alleles of the TYR gene, so no functional tyrosinase is made. Without the enzyme, tyrosine cannot be converted to DOPA and then to melanin.
Q: Two parents have normal pigment. They have a child with albinism. Give the genotypes of the parents, using T and t, and state the probability that their next child has albinism.
A: Both parents are heterozygous (Tt) carriers. Each child has a 1 in 4 (25%) chance of albinism (tt).
Q: In another person the sixth codon of the β-globin mRNA, GAG, has changed to GAA. Glutamic acid is coded by both GAA and GAG. Predict the effect on the haemoglobin and on the person.
A: No effect. GAA still codes for glutamic acid (the code is degenerate), so the amino acid sequence, the shape of β-globin and the haemoglobin are unchanged. This is a silent mutation and the person is healthy.
Q: A man with haemophilia and a woman who is not a carrier have children. State the probability that a son has haemophilia, and that a daughter is a carrier. Explain your answers.
A: Son: 0. He receives his father's Y chromosome, and his X from the mother, whose X chromosomes carry only the normal allele. Daughter: 1 (all). She receives her father's X chromosome, which carries the mutant allele, and a normal X from her mother, so she is a carrier.
Q: A person has 44 CAG repeats in one HTT allele and 18 in the other. Will this person develop Huntington's disease? Explain.
A: Yes, probably. The allele with 44 repeats is the expanded, dominant allele, so one copy is enough. The 18-repeat allele is in the normal range and does not prevent the disease.
Q: In the lac operon, state the function of (a) the promoter and (b) the operator.
A: (a) The promoter is the DNA sequence where RNA polymerase binds to start transcription. (b) The operator is the DNA sequence where the repressor protein binds, blocking RNA polymerase from transcribing the structural genes.
Q: A transcription factor binds to DNA near a gene and the gene is transcribed more slowly. Is it an activator or a repressor? Explain.
A: A repressor (a transcription factor that decreases the rate of transcription). Binding blocks or hinders RNA polymerase, so less mRNA is made.
Q: A pea plant of genotype le le is dwarf. Identify what is faulty in its gibberellin pathway and explain why spraying it with gibberellin makes it taller but does not change its seeds.
A: The enzyme coded by the Le gene is non-functional, so little active gibberellin is made. Spraying supplies the missing hormone, so the stem grows; the genotype (le le) in its gametes is unchanged, so the seeds still carry le.

- Le codes for a functional enzyme in the gibberellin pathway; le is non-functional; le le plants are dwarf; spraying changes the phenotype, not the genotype.
- Structural genes code for proteins used by the cell; regulatory genes code for proteins that control other genes. Inducible enzymes are made only with their substrate; repressible enzymes are made unless the end product is present.
- lac operon: lacI makes the repressor all the time. No lactose: the repressor binds to the operator and RNA polymerase cannot transcribe lacZ, lacY, lacA. Lactose: it binds to the repressor, the repressor leaves the operator and the genes are transcribed.
- Transcription factors are proteins that bind to DNA and increase or decrease the rate of transcription in eukaryotes.
- Gibberellin causes DELLA proteins to be broken down, freeing transcription factors to switch on genes for growth (or amylase).
