:::try Warm-up
Q: State the formula for magnification and rearrange it to find the actual size. (from [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy))
A: \(M = I/A\), so \(A = I/M\), where \(I\) is the image size and \(A\) the actual size, in the same units.
Q: A stage micrometer scale has 100 divisions in 1 mm. What is the length of one division in µm? (from [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy))
A: \(1000\,\mathrm{\mu m} \div 100 = 10\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).
Q: What does a plan diagram of a tissue show, and what does it not show? (from [Gas exchange in humans](#bio.gasexchange))
A: It shows the outlines of the tissue layers, in the right proportions. It does not show individual cells.
:::

## The microscope question in Paper 3

On Paper 3 the microscope question is set on prepared slides or on slides you make, and often on a photomicrograph as well. The microscope has a ×10 eyepiece and ×10 and ×40 objective lenses only. You are told what to find and draw, you are given the specimen and the rules for marks are always the same, so this chapter teaches the rules. Animals are not dissected in Paper 3.

You are asked to do some or all of these:
- set up the microscope, find a tissue or a cell and draw it;
- make a temporary slide of stained cells or tissue;
- draw a plan diagram of a section, with no cells in it;
- draw cells in detail, with the cell walls and the contents you can see;
- label the drawings, and compare two specimens;
- measure, using the scale bar or a graticule, and calculate magnification or actual size;
- count cells or structures in a given area, and scale up the count.

### Finding and focusing a specimen

:::key Using the microscope
1. Put the slide on the stage, section under the objective, and clip it down. Turn the objective to the **lowest power** (the ×4 or the ×10 of this paper) before you look.
2. Look through the eyepiece and use the **coarse focus** to bring the specimen into view. Move the slide so the part you want is at the **centre** of the field.
3. Use the **fine focus** to make it sharp. Adjust the light so the image is bright enough without glare.
4. To change to ×40, turn the objective so that the lens clicks into place and focus with the **fine focus only**; the coarse focus can crush the slide.
5. Look for the structure you were asked about; check that you have chosen the correct part of the specimen before you start to draw.
:::

A temporary slide is made as in [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy): a thin piece of tissue on a drop of liquid, a stain if needed, and a coverslip lowered at an angle so that air bubbles are not trapped.

:::lab Making a temporary slide of onion epidermis stained with iodine
**Aim**: make a slide of a single layer of plant cells, and find the nucleus, the cell wall and the cytoplasm.

:::steps
1. Put a drop of water or iodine solution on a clean slide.
2. Cut a small piece of onion bulb scale, and use forceps to peel off a thin, transparent layer of the inner surface (the epidermis).
3. Lay the epidermis flat on the drop, with no folds. Add a drop of iodine solution if you did not use it first.
4. Lower a coverslip with a mounted needle or forceps, at an angle, so that air bubbles are not trapped. Blot the edges with paper.
5. View at low power, find a clear area of cells, then view at high power.
:::

**Variables and control**: this is an observation, not an experiment. To check that the iodine stains starch, compare with a slide in water only.

**Typical results**: a layer of rectangular cells in a brick-like pattern. The nucleus stains darker, the cell wall is visible between neighbours, and a large vacuole fills most of the cell. There are no chloroplasts in an onion bulb.

**Sources of error**: a thick piece or a fold makes the cells overlap; an air bubble looks like a dark circle; too much stain hides detail.

**Safety**: iodine solution stains skin and clothes and irritates the eyes: wear eye protection. Cut away from your hand, with a sharp blade on a tile. Risk: low.
:::

## Rules for drawings

Drawings are marked on a short list of rules. Learn them, because they apply to every drawing in the paper.

:::key The rules for a good drawing
1. **Sharp pencil.** Lines are fine, clear and **unbroken**: one confident line, not several short or sketchy ones. A rubber is used to remove mistakes completely.
2. **Large.** Use most of the space available. A drawing should fill at least half of the area given.
3. **No shading and no colouring.** Show a dense or dark area with a label, not with shading.
4. **Draw what you see.** Draw the observable features only, not what you expect to be there or a diagram from a book.
5. **Correct proportions.** Check them by measuring (see the next section) before you draw.
6. **Labels** with ruled lines that touch the structure, written horizontally, with no arrowheads and no crossing lines, placed beside the drawing.
7. **Title** and, if asked, the magnification or a scale bar.
:::

### Labels and annotations

A **label** names a structure. An **annotation** adds a note that describes or explains it. Paper 3 usually asks for labels, so each label line must end exactly on the part named: a line that ends in the gap between two structures, or that crosses a third structure, does not earn the mark. Draw the label lines with a ruler, from the structure out to the margin, and never let two cross. Write the labels horizontally, and not on the drawing.

## Plan diagrams

:::def Plan diagram
A low-power drawing that shows the distribution and the proportions of the tissues in an organ. It shows the outlines of the tissue regions only, with single lines, and no individual cells.
:::

A plan diagram is a map of where the tissues are. Use the low-power lens, so that you can see the whole section, and draw only the boundaries between tissues. If the question asks for the xylem and phloem, you draw the boundary of each region and label it.

:::eg Calculate the widths for a plan diagram of a stem TS. Using an eyepiece graticule, a student measures the radius of the section as 40 units, the width of the cortex as 12 units and the radius of the pith as 18 units. The student plans a drawing with a radius of 60 mm. Find the widths on the drawing.
First find the scale: the drawing is 60 mm for 40 units.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{scale} &= \frac{60}{40} \\ &= 1.5\,\mathrm{mm\ per\ unit} \end{aligned}\]

Multiply each measurement by the scale.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{cortex} &= 12 \times 1.5 \\ &= 18\,\mathrm{mm} \end{aligned}\]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{pith radius} &= 18 \times 1.5 \\ &= 27\,\mathrm{mm} \end{aligned}\]

The ring of tissue between the cortex and the pith (which contains the vascular bundles) is \(40 - 12 - 18 = 10\) units wide, so \(10 \times 1.5 = 15\) mm on the drawing. A check: \(18 + 27 + 15 = 60\) mm, the radius of the drawing.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: a student looked at drawing B in the figure above and wrote the following comments about their own plan diagram of a stem. Find what is wrong with each comment. [4]
Line 1: "I drew all the cells in the cortex so that it shows plenty of detail."
Line 2: "I shaded the pith so that you can see where it is."
Line 3: "I drew the vascular bundles large so that they are easy to see."
Line 4: "I put arrowheads on the label lines so that it is clear what each one points to."

**Line 1 is wrong: a plan diagram shows tissues, not cells, so no cells are drawn [B0].** The cortex is labelled, not filled with cells.

**Line 2 is wrong: no shading is allowed in a drawing; the label identifies the pith [B0].**

**Line 3 is wrong: the proportions must match the specimen, so the bundles should be drawn at the size they are, measured with the graticule or a ruler [B0].**

**Line 4 is wrong: label lines are ruled lines that touch the structure, with no arrowheads, and they must not cross [B0].**

Drawing B is also small and off centre and the outline is made of two sketchy lines. All four comments corrected, with a large drawing in single clear lines, would earn the 4 marks.
:::

## Drawing cells

A **cell drawing** is a high-power drawing of a few cells in detail. Unlike a plan diagram it does show cells, with their shapes and relative sizes, the cell walls where they have walls, and the contents you can see: nucleus, cytoplasm, vacuole, chloroplasts, starch grains. You draw only what is visible, so you do not draw mitochondria or ribosomes that a light microscope cannot show.

:::key Drawing cell walls
- Cell walls have a thickness. Draw a wall with **two lines**: one for each edge of the wall.
- Where **two cells touch**, draw the wall they share with **three lines**: one for the edge of each cell's wall and one in the middle where the two meet.
- Draw cells in their true shapes and in the same relative sizes as on the slide. Include several cells, as the question asks.
:::

| feature | plan diagram | cell drawing |
|---|---|---|
| lens | low power | high power |

## Comparing specimens

You may be asked to record the similarities and differences between two specimens, either two slides or a slide and a photomicrograph. Use a table, and observe the rules:

:::key A comparison table
1. A heading for the **feature**, then one column for each **specimen**.
2. **One feature per row**, with an observation for **both** specimens in the same row. A row with an observation of only one specimen does not compare.
3. Use **observable features only** (what you can see: the number of layers, the thickness, the shape, the presence of a structure), not explanations (what it is for).
4. Include **similarities** as well as differences, and use measurements where you can (a thickness in µm, a count).
5. Be specific: "two layers of palisade cells" and not "more palisade".
:::

:::eg Two leaves, P and Q, were examined as TS under low power. Complete a table to compare them. [4]
| feature | leaf P | leaf Q |
|---|---|---|
| thickness of leaf / µm | 180 | 320 |
| layers of palisade cells | 1 | 2 |
| air spaces in the spongy mesophyll | large and many | small and few |
| upper and lower epidermis | one layer of cells in each | one layer of cells in each |

Each row has an observation for both leaves. The first three rows are differences, and the last is a similarity. Mark scheme: a difference in the thickness, with figures, scores [B1]. A difference in the palisade layers [B1], a difference in the air spaces [B1], and a similarity, such as one layer of cells in each epidermis [B1]. A row about the function ("leaf Q is better at photosynthesis") would not score, because it is not an observable feature.

## Measuring and calculating sizes

There are three ways to find a size from a specimen, and all use the same ideas as in [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy). Check each unit, because the conversion between mm and µm is a source of lost marks.

**With a ruler on a photomicrograph or a drawing.** Measure the structure in mm, then divide by the magnification: actual size \(= I \div M\). If the answer is wanted in µm, multiply by 1000. Draw a line across the widest part, and state where you measured.

**With a scale bar.** The scale bar shows a length on the photomicrograph that stands for a real length printed beside it. Measure the bar and the structure in the same unit, then compare them.

**With an eyepiece graticule.** The graticule is a scale in the eyepiece whose units have no fixed size, so you **calibrate** it against a stage micrometer for each objective, then use the calibrated value to measure.

:::eg A photomicrograph of a stem TS has a scale bar that is 15 mm long and labelled "100 µm". A vascular bundle is 36 mm wide on the photomicrograph. Calculate the actual width of the bundle.
The scale bar shows that 15 mm on the photomicrograph stands for 100 µm. Find the magnification from the scale bar, after converting the 15 mm to µm.

\[\begin{aligned} M &= \frac{15\,000\,\mathrm{\mu m}}{100\,\mathrm{\mu m}} \\ &= \times 150 \end{aligned}\]

Convert the width of the bundle to µm and divide by the magnification.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{actual width} &= \frac{36\,000\,\mathrm{\mu m}}{150} \\ &= 240\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

Check by proportion instead: 36 mm is \(36 \div 15 = 2.4\) bar lengths, and \(2.4 \times 100 = 240\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).
:::

:::eg At high power, 24 divisions of an eyepiece graticule lined up with 7 divisions of a stage micrometer, each of which is 10 µm. A cell measured 17 graticule units long. Calculate the length of the cell.
Calibrate first: 7 divisions are \(7 \times 10 = 70\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), and this equals 24 graticule units.

\[\begin{aligned} 1\ \text{unit} &= \frac{70}{24} \\ &= 2.9167\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

Then use the unrounded value for the cell.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{length} &= 17 \times \frac{70}{24} \\ &= 49.6\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

The cell is about 50 µm long (2 significant figures, as the data have). The calibration is only for the objective lens used: it must be repeated for the other.
:::

## Counting and sampling

You cannot count every cell in a tissue, so you count in a sample and scale up. Two methods use fields of view or a grid.

**Fields of view.** Count in several fields of view chosen at random, calculate the mean per field, and divide by the area of the field to give a density.

:::formula Density from fields of view
\[\text{density} = \frac{\text{mean number per field}}{\text{area of one field}}\]
The field of view is a circle of area \(\pi r^2\), with the diameter measured against a stage micrometer or graticule (the radius is half the diameter). The density is per unit area, such as per mm².
:::

:::eg Stomata were counted on a leaf surface in five fields of view, and a field of view has a diameter of 0.50 mm. The counts were 17, 21, 19, 22 and 16. Calculate the stomatal density.
Mean number per field:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mean} &= \frac{17 + 21 + 19 + 22 + 16}{5} \\ &= 19.0 \end{aligned}\]

Area of the field, with radius 0.25 mm:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{area} &= \pi \times 0.25^2 \\ &= 0.19635\,\mathrm{mm^2} \end{aligned}\]

Density:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{density} &= \frac{19.0}{0.19635} \\ &= 97\,\mathrm{mm^{-2}} \end{aligned}\]

There are about 97 stomata per mm² (2 significant figures, as the diameter has two). Five fields are better than one because stomata are not evenly spread, and the mean of several fields is a better estimate of the density than a single count.
:::

**Grids.** A grid of squares is laid over the specimen or the image. Count the cells in a **few** squares, find the mean per square, then multiply by the total number of squares.

## In the exam room

:::exam What the microscope question marks
Marks come from: a focused specimen and the correct tissue found; drawings that follow the rules (large, sharp, no shading, correct proportions, ruled labels); records of observations that are accurate and complete; correct measurements and calculations, with units and significant figures; and comparisons that are specific. **Describe** or **compare** means use observable features. Take a pencil with a sharp point, a spare, a rubber and a ruler. Draw what is on your slide, not what is in a book; if your slide looks different from the usual diagram, draw your slide.
:::

:::warn Drawing what you expect
A common loss of marks is drawing a textbook picture instead of the specimen: all the cells the same size when they are not, structures the slide does not show, or equal layers when one is much thicker. Also, if you are asked to draw "three cells", do not draw one or ten.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State three rules for a good drawing of a specimen.
A: Any three of: a sharp pencil and clear unbroken lines; a large drawing using most of the space; no shading or colouring; correct proportions; draw only what is seen; ruled label lines that touch the structure and do not cross; no arrowheads.
Q: How many lines are used to draw the wall between a plant cell and the outside, and the wall shared by two touching plant cells?
A: Two lines for the outer wall; three lines for the shared wall.
Q: Explain why cells are not drawn in a plan diagram.
A: A plan diagram shows the distribution and proportions of tissues, so only the boundaries between tissues are drawn; cells would hide this and are drawn at high power in a cell drawing.
Q: A photomicrograph has a magnification of ×120, and a cell measures 8.4 mm on it. Calculate its actual length in µm.
A: \(8.4 \div 120 = 0.070\,\mathrm{mm}\), and \(0.070 \times 1000 = 70\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).
Q: At low power, 30 graticule units lined up with 9 stage micrometer divisions of 10 µm. Calculate the length of one unit and the length of a cell that measures 22 units.
A: \(9 \times 10 = 90\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) for 30 units, so 1 unit \(= 3.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}\). The cell is \(22 \times 3.0 = 66\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).
Q: A field of view has a radius of 0.40 mm. Counts of cells in five fields were 8, 10, 6, 9 and 7. Calculate the density in cells per mm².
A: Mean \(= 40 \div 5 = 8.0\); area \(= \pi \times 0.40^2 = 0.50265\,\mathrm{mm^2}\); density \(= 8.0 \div 0.50265 = 16\,\mathrm{mm^{-2}}\).
Q: Why should the fields of view for a count be chosen at random?
A: To avoid bias, such as choosing the fields that look to have the most cells, so that the mean represents the whole specimen.
Q: A drawing of a cell is 54 mm long and the cell is 0.90 mm long. What is the magnification of the drawing?
A: \(M = I/A = 54 \div 0.90 = \times 60\).
Q: A student draws a label line from a structure to the margin freehand, with an arrowhead, and across another structure. Give three corrections.
A: Use a ruler; remove the arrowhead; do not cross another structure or line (draw it so that it only touches the structure named).
Q: Explain why it is wrong to draw mitochondria in a drawing of a leaf cell made with a light microscope.
A: They are too small to be seen with the resolution of a light microscope (about 200 nm), and a drawing shows only observable features.
:::

Q: [3 marks] ★ A scale bar on a photomicrograph is 25 mm long and stands for 50 µm. A cell on the photomicrograph is 40 mm long. Calculate the actual length of the cell.
A: Magnification \(= 25\,000 \div 50\) [M1] \(= \times 500\) [A1]
Actual length \(= 40\,000 \div 500 = 80\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ State four features of a good drawing of a plant cell seen with a light microscope.
A: Large (uses most of the space). [B1]
Sharp, continuous lines with no shading. [B1]
Cell wall drawn with two lines, and three lines where cells touch. [B1]
Correct shapes and proportions. [B1]
(Also credit: only observable contents drawn; ruled label lines that touch the structures, without arrowheads.)
Q: [5 marks] ★★ At ×40, 12 graticule units lined up with 5 divisions of a stage micrometer, each division 10 µm. (a) Calculate the length of one graticule unit. (b) A cell is 9.0 units long. Calculate its length in µm. (c) State why the calibration cannot be used with the ×10 objective.
H: Five divisions of 10 µm cover 50 µm.
A: (a) \(5 \times 10 \div 12\) [M1] \(= 4.17\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) [A1]
(b) \(9.0 \times 50 \div 12\) [M1] \(= 38\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) [A1]
(c) A different objective changes how much the specimen is magnified but not the graticule, so the unit stands for a different length. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Stomata were counted in five fields of view, each of diameter 0.30 mm. The counts were 31, 27, 35, 29 and 33. (a) Calculate the mean count. (b) Calculate the area of a field of view. (c) Calculate the stomatal density. (d) Explain why five fields were used.
A: (a) \((31 + 27 + 35 + 29 + 33) \div 5 = 31.0\) [A1]
(b) \(\pi \times 0.15^2\) [M1] \(= 0.0707\,\mathrm{mm^2}\)
(c) \(31.0 \div 0.070686\) [M1] \(= 4.4 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{mm^{-2}}\) [A1]
(d) The stomata are not evenly distributed, so the mean of several fields is more representative than one count. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Cells of onion epidermis (X) and cheek cells (Y) were viewed at high power. X: rectangular cells in a regular layer, each with a wall, a nucleus and a large vacuole. Y: small, irregular, single cells with a nucleus and no wall. Complete two similarities and two differences in a table.
A: Similarity: both have a nucleus [B1]. Another similarity: both have cytoplasm (and a cell surface membrane) [B1]. Difference: X has a cell wall and Y does not [B1]. Difference: X has a large vacuole and Y does not, or X is a regular rectangular shape and Y is irregular [B1]. (Each must be stated for both specimens.)
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A TS of a leaf measured 56 graticule units in thickness: palisade layer 22 units, spongy mesophyll 24 units, upper epidermis 6 units and lower epidermis 4 units. A plan diagram is to be drawn 112 mm thick. (a) Calculate the thickness of each layer in the drawing. (b) State three further features of the plan diagram.
H: Find the scale in mm per unit first.
A: (a) Scale \(= 112 \div 56\) [M1] \(= 2.0\,\mathrm{mm}\) per unit [A1]
Palisade \(= 22 \times 2.0 = 44\) mm; spongy mesophyll \(= 48\) mm (and epidermis 12 mm and 8 mm) [A1]
(b) Any three from: lines are single and unbroken; no cells drawn; no shading; labels with ruled lines that touch the structures; the drawing uses most of the space; the vascular bundle drawn as an outline. [B1] [B1] [B1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ A photomicrograph of a root TS has a magnification of ×25. (a) A line across the vascular tissue measures 22 mm. Calculate the actual diameter in µm. (b) State two observable differences you might find between your slide of a root and the photomicrograph. (c) Explain why a mean of several measurements is better than one. (d) State where to place the measuring line.
H: Divide by the magnification, then convert mm to µm.
A: (a) \(22 \div 25\) [M1] \(= 0.88\,\mathrm{mm}\) [A1] \(= 880\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) [A1]
(b) Any two from: the stain colour; the angle at which the root is cut (shape of the vascular tissue); the size of the section on the slide; the number of cells or root hairs visible. [B1] [B1]
(c) One measurement may not be representative: the sizes of the cells vary, so a mean reduces the effect of random differences. [B1]
(d) Across the widest part of the structure, through the centre, so that the measurement is the diameter. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A grid was divided into 25 squares, each of side 0.20 mm. The cells in five squares were counted: 9, 6, 8, 7 and 10. (a) Calculate the mean number of cells per square. (b) Calculate the density in cells per mm². (c) The tissue has an area of 4.5 cm². Estimate the total number of cells. (d) Suggest a reason why the estimate may be unreliable.

:::quiz
? How is the wall shared by two touching plant cells drawn?
+ with three lines
- with one line
- with two lines
- with shading
> Each cell wall has two edges, and the middle line is where the two cells meet, which gives three lines. A single line is for a cell with no wall, and shading is never used.
? Which statement about a plan diagram is correct?
+ It shows tissue regions in the correct proportions and does not show individual cells.
- It shows every cell in each tissue at high power.
- It is drawn freehand with shaded regions for the tissues.
- It shows only the tissue that you can name.
> A plan diagram maps the tissues at low power. Cells are for high-power drawings, shading is not allowed, and the tissues you cannot name are still outlined.
? Which label line is correct?
+ ruled with a pencil, ending on the structure, with no arrowhead
- freehand, ending near the structure, with an arrowhead
- ruled, crossing another label line to reach the structure
- written on the drawing so that no line is needed
> Labels are ruled lines that touch the structure. Arrowheads, crossing lines and writing on the drawing lose the mark.
? A student calibrates an eyepiece graticule with the ×10 objective and then changes to ×40. What should the student do?
+ calibrate again with the stage micrometer
- use the same value, since the graticule has not changed
- multiply the old value by 4
- measure with a ruler on the stage
> The graticule does not change but the specimen is magnified more with the ×40 objective, so one unit now covers a different distance on the slide. The calibration must be repeated.
? A cell drawing is 45 mm long. The cell is really 0.30 mm long. What is the magnification?
+ ×150
- ×15
- ×0.0067
- ×13.5
> \(M = I/A = 45 \div 0.30 = 150\). The others are mistakes: dividing the other way gives ×0.0067, and mixing up the decimal point gives ×15.
? Which row would earn a mark in a table comparing two leaf sections?
+ number of layers of palisade cells: one in leaf X, two in leaf Y
- palisade cells: many in leaf Y
- leaf Y has a thicker cuticle to stop water loss
- leaf X is better at photosynthesis
> A comparison needs an observable feature, with an observation for both specimens. The others describe one specimen only, or explain a function.
? A student counts cells in the fields of view that look to contain the most cells. What is wrong?
+ the fields are chosen with bias, so the mean is too high
- the number of fields is too large
- the fields are too small to count
- the count must be done at low power
> Fields should be chosen at random so that the sample represents the whole specimen. Choosing the fullest fields biases the mean upwards.
? How is the density of cells calculated from counts in fields of view?
+ the mean number per field divided by the area of one field
- the total number of fields divided by the mean count
- the mean count multiplied by the diameter of the field
- the largest count divided by the number of fields
> Density is a number per unit area, so divide the mean count by the area of one field, \(\pi r^2\).
:::

:::recap
- Microscope: lowest power first, centre the specimen, fine focus at ×40. Paper 3 uses a ×10 eyepiece and ×10 and ×40 objectives.
- Drawings: sharp pencil, continuous lines, large, no shading, correct proportions, draw only what you observe, ruled label lines that touch the structure with no arrowheads and no crossing.
- A plan diagram is low power, shows tissue outlines in proportion, and has no cells. A cell drawing is high power: walls with two lines, or three where two cells touch.
- Comparison table: one feature per row, both specimens in each row, observable features, similarities as well as differences.
- Actual size \(= I \div M\). With a scale bar: \(M\) = bar length ÷ stated length (same unit). Graticule: calibrate for each objective against the stage micrometer (1 division = 10 µm). 1 mm = 1000 µm.
- Counting: random fields or squares; density = mean count ÷ area (\(\pi r^2\) for a field). Use a rule for cells on the edge.
:::
