## Extinction and its causes

:::def Extinction
The permanent loss of a species: the last living individual of the species has died. A species that is likely to become extinct if the causes of its decline continue is **endangered**.
:::

A species does not die out in one step. Something changes, the number of individuals falls, the remaining groups become small, and a chance event finishes them. Exam answers that earn full marks give this chain: **a cause, then the effect on the population, then the reason that leads to extinction**.

### Climate change

Climate change means long-term shifts in temperature and rainfall. Each species is adapted to a range of conditions, so as the climate changes the area where it can live moves.

- Species may have to **migrate** towards cooler places (towards the poles or up a mountain). Many cannot move fast enough, particularly plants, slow-breeding animals and animals blocked by farmland, cities or the sea.
- A mountain-top species has nowhere higher to go: the cool habitat shrinks and finally disappears.
- Species that cannot migrate must **adapt**, but natural selection needs variation and many generations, and the climate may change faster than the species can evolve.
- Changes in temperature and rainfall also upset the timing of life cycles. If a bird chooses when to breed using temperature but the caterpillars it feeds its chicks on emerge earlier, the chicks starve.
- Reef-building corals are an example from the sea. Warmer water makes corals expel the photosynthetic algae that live inside them and give them food. The coral turns white (bleaching) and may die, and the animals that depend on the reef lose their habitat.

### Competition

Two species compete when they use the same limited resource, such as food, water, light or nesting sites. If a new competitor arrives (usually an **introduced** species, see "Invasive alien species" below) that is better at using the resource, the native species gets less of it. Fewer individuals survive and reproduce, so its population falls. If the two species occupy the same niche, one can eliminate the other completely. A population already made small by another cause is the most likely to be driven out.

### Hunting by humans

Hunting for meat, fur, horn, tusks or sport removes adults faster than the population can replace them by reproduction. Large animals are especially vulnerable because they breed slowly: few young are born, and they take years to reach breeding age.

1. Hunting kills more individuals than are born, so the **population falls**.
2. When few individuals are left, mates are hard to find and the **birth rate falls** further.
3. The small population has a **small gene pool**. It loses alleles by drift and the remaining individuals become inbred, which reduces fertility and resistance to disease.
4. A chance event, such as a disease or a drought, can then kill the last individuals.

The passenger pigeon, once among the most numerous birds in North America, was hunted in such large numbers that none were left in the wild at the end of the nineteenth century, and the last captive bird died in 1914.

### Degradation and loss of habitats

:::def Habitat loss and degradation
**Loss** of a habitat means that the area is destroyed or converted to another use (for example forest cleared for farms, wetland drained, coastline built on). **Degradation** means the habitat remains but is damaged, so it supports fewer species or fewer individuals (for example by pollution, overgrazing, or soil washing off cleared land into streams).
:::

Habitat is the place where a species finds food, shelter, mates and nest sites. When it shrinks, the carrying capacity falls and fewer individuals can live there. Loss of habitat is the most widespread cause of species becoming endangered.

Even when some habitat survives, it is often broken into pieces, and this is called **fragmentation**. Roads, farms and towns divide a large forest into small patches that are separated by land the species cannot cross.

Fragmentation harms species in four ways.

1. Each patch can hold only a **small population**, and a species that needs a large territory (a big predator, for example) may not be able to live in a small patch at all.
2. Individuals cannot move between patches, so there is **no gene flow** and little chance of finding a mate. Each isolated population is inbred, with reduced genetic diversity.
3. Patches have a larger **edge** compared with their area. At an edge the light, wind, temperature and humidity differ from the interior, and predators and invasive species enter from outside. Only the **core** area is suitable for forest-interior species.
4. A small isolated population can be wiped out by one fire, disease or storm, and there is no neighbouring population to recolonise the patch.

:::eg A square forest reserve measures 4 km by 4 km. New roads and fields divide it into sixteen separate square patches of 1 km by 1 km each. Conditions within 0.25 km of an edge are different from the interior, so only land further than 0.25 km from every edge is core habitat. Calculate the core area, as a percentage of the total area, before and after the division, and the percentage of the core area that is lost.
**Before**: the core is a square whose side is shorter by 0.25 km at each of the two opposite edges, so it is \(4 - 2 \times 0.25 = 3.5\,\mathrm{km}\) wide.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{core area} &= 3.5^2 \\ &= 12.25\,\mathrm{km^2} \\ \text{core as a percentage of 16 km}^2 &= \frac{12.25}{16} \times 100 \\ &= 76.6\% \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

**After**: each patch has a core of side \(1 - 2 \times 0.25 = 0.5\,\mathrm{km}\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{core of one patch} &= 0.5^2 = 0.25\,\mathrm{km^2} \\ \text{core of sixteen patches} &= 16 \times 0.25 = 4.0\,\mathrm{km^2} \\ \text{percentage of 16 km}^2 &= \frac{4.0}{16} \times 100 = 25\% \end{aligned}\]

[A1]

**Core area lost**:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{12.25 - 4.0}{12.25} \times 100 &= 67.3\% \end{aligned}\]

[A1]

The total area of forest has not changed at all, yet two thirds of the core habitat has gone. The total length of edge has risen from 16 km to \(16 \times 4 = 64\,\mathrm{km}\), which is why fragmentation harms species that need the interior of a forest. A weaker answer says "the forest area is the same, so there is no effect".
:::

A full-mark answer builds the chain: patches, small populations, loss of genetic diversity, extinction.

Roads and farmland divide the forest into small, isolated patches. [B1]

Each patch supports only a few individuals, and a patch may be too small to provide the food or territory the mammal needs. [B1]

Individuals cannot move between patches, so there is no gene flow and individuals in a patch may be unable to find a mate. [B1]

Each small population loses alleles by genetic drift and becomes inbred, so the genetic diversity falls, and fertility and resistance to disease are reduced. [B1]

The population falls below the size that can survive, and a chance event (disease, fire) can kill all the remaining individuals, so the species becomes extinct in the patch and may be lost completely. [B1]

(Also credit: edge effects reduce the area of suitable core habitat; predators or invasive species enter from the edges.)

A weaker answer says "the animals have less space so they die", without the isolation, the lack of gene flow or the loss of genetic diversity.
:::

Pollution, drainage, overgrazing and invasive species degrade habitats that still look intact. In an exam answer, say whether the habitat has been **lost** (destroyed or converted) or **degraded** (damaged), and say what each change does to the **population** that lives there.

### Small populations and the extinction vortex

Every cause above ends in the same place: a small population. Small populations are at special risk because:

- genetic drift removes alleles quickly (see [Natural and artificial selection](#bio.selection)), so the genetic diversity falls;
- inbreeding makes harmful recessive alleles more likely to meet and be expressed (see [Monohybrid inheritance](#bio.inheritance)), so fewer offspring are born or survive;
- with little variation, the species cannot adapt to a new disease or a change of climate;
- a chance event can kill every individual.

A small population is therefore less able to reproduce and survive, which makes it smaller still. The loop of ever-fewer individuals and ever-less variation is sometimes called the extinction vortex.

:::key Causes of extinction: cause, effect, result
1. **Habitat loss and fragmentation:** less living space and smaller, isolated patches, so smaller populations, no gene flow and more edge; the species cannot find mates or enough food.
2. **Climate change:** the area with suitable conditions moves faster than the species can migrate or evolve, and life-cycle timings (breeding and food supply) fall out of step.
3. **Overexploitation (hunting):** adults are removed faster than they are replaced, which matters most for large, slow-breeding species; the population falls below a viable size.
4. **Invasive (introduced) species:** compete for food and space, prey on natives or bring disease, so native numbers fall.
5. **Pollution:** poisons individuals or degrades the habitat, so fewer survive and reproduce.
6. In every case the final stage is a small population: lost alleles, inbreeding, and one chance event that kills the rest.
:::

## Why maintain biodiversity?

Conservation takes money and land, so scientists must give reasons. The reasons fall into five groups and an exam answer names the group and gives an example.

| Reason | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ethical (moral) | every species has a right to exist and we should leave a living world to later generations | people feel a duty not to cause extinctions |
| ecological | species are linked in food webs and nutrient cycles, so losing one can harm many others | a **keystone species** such as a top predator controls the numbers of its prey, so its loss changes the whole community |
| economic | we obtain food, timber, fuel, fibres and medicines from living things; ecosystems give free services | bees and other insects pollinate crops; wetlands purify water and reduce flooding; reefs and forests attract tourists |
| genetic resources | wild species hold alleles that may be useful in future | wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance that breeders can cross into crops |
| aesthetic and cultural | many people value the beauty and variety of nature | enjoyment of wildlife, art, and cultural ties to particular species |

:::def Keystone species
A species that has a much larger effect on the structure of its community than its numbers would suggest, so that removing it changes the numbers of many other species.
:::

Species may also hold medical value we have not found yet. Once a species is extinct, its genes and the chemicals it makes are gone, and no later discovery can recover them. Keeping many species is therefore a form of insurance, as well as a duty.

:::exam Outline means a short answer with a clear point each time
"Outline the reasons for maintaining biodiversity" wants three or four **different** reasons, each with a named example (an economic reason with pollination, a genetic reason with crop wild relatives). Giving three examples of one reason scores once.
:::

## Conserving species: in situ and ex situ

:::def In situ conservation
Protecting a species in its **natural habitat**, for example in a national park or marine park.
:::

:::def Ex situ conservation
Protecting a species **outside** its natural habitat, for example in a zoo, a botanic garden, a seed bank or a frozen zoo.
:::

### Conserved areas: national parks and marine parks

A national park or marine park is a protected area. Laws limit what people may do there, so the habitat and all the species in it are protected together.

- Habitats are protected from clearance, building, farming, logging and pollution.
- Fishing and hunting are banned or limited (a marine park may have no-fishing zones where fish stocks recover and then spread to nearby waters).
- Rangers patrol to prevent poaching, and managers control invasive species, fire and the number of visitors.
- Visitors pay fees, and tourism gives local people jobs, so they have a reason to protect the park.
- Species stay in their own food web with natural behaviour, so this is the best way to conserve a species when it can be done.

Limitations: protection is hard to enforce over a large area; poachers still enter; a park with fixed boundaries cannot follow a species whose range moves as the climate changes; a small park is itself an isolated fragment, so corridors (strips of habitat joining parks) may be needed.

### Zoos

Zoos keep endangered animals in captivity. Their roles are:

- **Captive breeding**: animals are bred to increase the numbers. A studbook records the parentage of every animal, and animals are moved between zoos (or sperm are exchanged) so that breeding partners are not close relatives, to keep the **genetic diversity** high.
- **Reintroduction**: captive-bred animals can be returned to the wild once the threat that nearly killed them is controlled.
- **Research and veterinary care**: knowledge of the diet, breeding and diseases of a species helps wild populations.
- **Education and funds**: visitors learn about endangered species, and entrance fees help to pay for conservation in the wild.

Problems with zoos: only a small number of founding individuals, so a small gene pool and risk of inbreeding; animals may lose their natural behaviours (finding food, avoiding predators), so released animals may not survive; breeding in captivity is difficult for some species; space and cost limit the number of species that can be kept; and if the original cause (hunting, habitat loss) has not been removed, there is nowhere safe to return the animals. Zoos cannot save all endangered species.

:::story Returning the Arabian oryx
The Arabian oryx, an antelope of desert regions, was hunted until no wild animals were left in the early 1970s. A herd had been kept in captivity, and the animals were bred in zoos and reserves. From 1982 captive-bred animals were released in Oman, with protection from hunting, and the wild population became established again. The success depended on three things: a captive herd with enough genetic variation, an end to the hunting, and protected habitat to return to.
:::

### Botanic gardens

Botanic gardens grow living collections of plants, including rare and endangered ones. They grow plants from seed or by taking cuttings or growing tissue culture, and some also run seed banks. They study the plants (growing conditions, genetics, uses), educate visitors, and supply plants for restoring habitats in the wild. Plants can be kept in a smaller space than animals and are easier to propagate, so very many species can be kept; but a garden holds only a few individuals of each species, so genetic variation is limited, and the plants are not in their natural ecosystem with their pollinators and soil organisms.

### Seed banks

A seed bank stores the seeds of wild plants and crop varieties for the future.

:::steps
1. Seeds are collected from **many** individuals of a species in different places, so that the stored seeds hold as much genetic variation as possible.
2. Seeds are cleaned and **dried** to a low water content. With little water, the seed's metabolism is very slow and it cannot germinate or rot.
3. The dry seeds are sealed in containers and stored at a **low temperature** (often around −20 °C), which slows ageing.
4. Samples are **germination-tested** regularly to check that the seeds are still viable. When viability falls, the seeds are grown into plants, and new seeds are collected for storing again.
5. Stored seeds can be used to **re-establish** a species in the wild or to restore a crop variety after crop failure.
:::

Seed banks hold huge numbers of species in small spaces, cheaply, and the seeds can survive for decades. Limitations: not every seed survives drying and freezing (the seeds of many tropical trees do not); stored seeds stop evolving, so they do not adapt to a changing environment; and the bank saves only the plant, not the habitat, the pollinators or the other species of the community. A seed bank stores **seeds**, which are the way many plants spread and survive hard times; it is not a place for storing whole plants.

:::eg Outline the role of seed banks in the conservation of endangered plant species.
A full-mark answer covers collection, storage conditions, testing and use.

Seeds are collected from many plants of the endangered species, so that the stored seeds hold the genetic diversity of the species. [B1]

The seeds are dried to a low water content so that they do not germinate and metabolism is very slow. [B1]

They are kept at a low temperature, so that they stay viable for a long time. [B1]

Samples are germination-tested at intervals, and when viability falls the seeds are grown to produce new seeds; stored seeds can be grown to re-establish the species in the wild. [B1]

(Also credit: takes up little space; many species can be kept cheaply; crop wild relatives are stored as a genetic resource.)

A weaker answer says "seeds are stored so that the plants can be saved" with no storage conditions and no mention of testing or genetic diversity.
:::

### Frozen zoos

:::def Frozen zoo
A store of living material from endangered animals (sperm, eggs, embryos and body cells) kept frozen in liquid nitrogen, at about −196 °C, so that it can be used later.
:::

At −196 °C all chemical reactions stop, so the cells can be stored for a very long time and then thawed. Cells are first treated with a protective solution, because ice crystals would otherwise damage the cell membranes.

- A frozen zoo can hold material from **many individuals**, so more genetic diversity is kept than a zoo could keep alive, in a very small space.
- Gametes and embryos can be used for IVF and embryo transfer (see the next section) after the donor has died, and stored sperm from unrelated males can be used to add genetic diversity to a captive population.

Limitations: the stored material is only useful if a female of the species (or a related species) can carry the embryo, which is hard to do for many species; freezing and thawing damages some cell types; storage needs liquid nitrogen to be topped up and a reliable supply of electricity; and, like any ex situ method, it does not protect habitats.

| | In situ | Ex situ |
|---|---|---|
| where | in the natural habitat | zoo, botanic garden, seed bank, frozen zoo |
| methods | national parks, marine parks, wildlife corridors | captive breeding, living collections, stored seeds, frozen gametes and embryos |
| advantages | whole community protected; natural behaviour and evolution continue; large numbers | protects species whose habitat is lost or very dangerous; breeding can be managed; supports research and education |
| disadvantages | hard to police; vulnerable to climate change, fire, disease and poaching | small gene pools; loss of natural behaviour; costly per species; the cause of the decline is not solved |

:::key In situ v ex situ in one breath
- **In situ** protects the species **where it lives**, so the whole community and natural selection carry on; it is best for large numbers but is hard to protect from poaching, fire and climate change.
- **Ex situ** protects it **somewhere else** (zoo, botanic garden, seed bank, frozen zoo); it is a safety net for species whose habitat is lost, but the gene pool is small and the cause of the decline is not removed.
- The best plan uses both: ex situ stock for reintroduction once the habitat is safe.
:::

:::eg Discuss the role of zoos in the conservation of endangered mammals.
A discussion needs points for **and** points against. Any six creditworthy points can score the six marks.

Zoos breed endangered mammals in captivity, which increases the number of individuals. [B1]

Studbooks and the exchange of animals between zoos keep breeding partners unrelated and so maintain genetic diversity. [B1]

Captive-bred animals can be reintroduced to the wild once the cause of the decline (for example hunting or habitat loss) has been removed. [B1]

Zoos carry out research into diet, breeding and disease, and educate visitors, which raises money and public support for conservation. [B1]

However, a captive population comes from a few founders, so its gene pool is small and there is a risk of inbreeding. [B1]

Animals may lose natural behaviours, such as hunting or avoiding predators, so reintroduced animals may not survive, and zoos can keep only a small number of species, so they cannot solve the problem of extinction unless the habitat is protected too. [B1]

(Also credit: breeding is difficult for some species; zoos are costly.)
:::

## Assisted reproduction in endangered mammals

Many endangered mammals breed slowly, and the few survivors may be unable to find mates. **Assisted reproduction** uses techniques from human medicine and farming to produce young.

:::def In vitro fertilisation (IVF)
Fertilisation outside the body: eggs taken from a female are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory (*in vitro* means "in glass"), and the zygote that results grows into an early embryo.
:::

:::def Embryo transfer
Placing an early embryo into the uterus of a female, where it implants and develops until birth.
:::

:::def Surrogacy
A pregnancy in which the embryo is carried and the young are born by a female who is not the genetic mother of the young.
:::

:::steps
1. **Collect** eggs from a female of the endangered species (she may be given hormones that stimulate several follicles to develop eggs), and collect sperm from a male. Both can come from animals that are far apart, or from the frozen zoo.
2. **IVF**: the eggs and sperm are mixed in a dish. Fertilised eggs divide to form early embryos, which are checked and can be frozen.
3. **Embryo transfer**: an embryo is placed in the uterus of a **surrogate** female whose hormones have been prepared for pregnancy.
4. The surrogate **carries** the embryo, gives birth to the young, and may rear them. The young are genetically those of the two parents, not of the surrogate.
:::

Why use a surrogate? Several eggs can be taken from one female at a time, and a female who normally has a single young every few years can produce many embryos. These embryos can be given to surrogates, so the endangered species can **breed faster**. Embryos can be frozen and moved between zoos instead of moving whole animals, which avoids stress and cost and spreads genetic variation. A female that is too old, injured or too rare to risk through pregnancy can still pass on her genes. The surrogate is often a female of a **related species or subspecies that is common**, because there may be too few females of the endangered species left; the surrogate must have a uterus of the right size and be able to accept the embryo.

Limits: success rates are low; the technique must be developed for each species; the surrogate may reject the embryo or have trouble in birth; and the young may need to be taught natural behaviour. It adds young but does not remove the cause of the decline.

:::eg Real context, the northern white rhinoceros: the last male died in 2018 and only two females, neither of them able to carry a calf, were left. Stored sperm and eggs taken from the surviving females are used to make embryos. Describe how IVF, embryo transfer and surrogacy could produce northern white rhinoceros calves.
Eggs are collected from the living females. [B1]

They are fertilised in a dish (in vitro) with sperm that was collected from males and stored frozen in the frozen zoo. [B1]

The fertilised eggs divide to form early embryos, which can be frozen until they are needed. [B1]

An embryo is transferred into the uterus of a surrogate female of a closely related, more common subspecies, the southern white rhinoceros, who carries it to birth. [B1]

The calf has the genes of the northern white parents, not of the surrogate. The method adds individuals but the population is still tiny and has very little genetic diversity, so protecting the habitat and preventing poaching are still needed.
:::

:::warn The surrogate is not always the same species
In conservation, the surrogate can be a female of a **related, more common** species or subspecies, because there may be no female of the endangered species able to carry the young. Do not write that the surrogate is "the mother": the **genetic** parents are the egg donor and the sperm donor.
:::

## Invasive alien species

:::def Invasive alien species
A species that has been introduced (deliberately or by accident) to a place where it does not occur naturally, and which spreads there and harms the native species or the environment.
:::

Invasive species can be animals, plants, fungi, microorganisms: not only animals. Examples include the cane toad in Australia, the grey squirrel in Britain, water hyacinth in tropical lakes and the rats and cats that reached islands on ships.

A successful invader has: no natural predators or diseases in the new place; fast reproduction; wide tolerance of conditions; a generalist diet.

Harm they cause:

- they **outcompete** native species for food, light, water or nesting sites;
- they **prey on** native species that have no defences against them, such as birds that nest on the ground on islands where there were no mammal predators;
- they **carry diseases** to which native species have no resistance;
- they **hybridise** with related native species, so that the native alleles are lost;
- they change the habitat itself, for example a plant that takes over a lake and cuts out the light;
- and they cost money by damaging crops, buildings and fisheries.

:::eg Real context: the cane toad was introduced to Australia in the 1930s to eat beetles that damaged sugar cane. Explain why it became a serious invasive alien species and how it harms native species.
The cane toad had no natural predators or diseases in Australia, so its population was not kept in check. [B1]

It reproduced quickly and spread over a wide area. [B1]

It competes with native frogs and other animals for food. [B1]

Its skin glands contain a poison, and native predators that eat the toad have not evolved a defence, so they are killed and their populations fall. [B1]

(The beetles were not controlled either, and the toad became a pest itself.)
:::

### Controlling invasive species

- **Prevention**: control of imports, checking ships and cargo (ballast water, soil, wood), and quarantine.
- **Physical removal**: hunting, trapping or poisoning animals; pulling up or cutting plants. It works best early, while the numbers are small, and on islands, where animals cannot return.
- **Chemical control**: herbicides or poisons, with the risk of harming native species.
- **Biological control**: introducing a natural predator, parasite or disease of the invader from its home range. It needs years of testing first, because the control agent can itself become invasive, as the cane toad did.
- **Habitat management** to favour native species; and public education.

Reasons for controlling invasive species: to protect native species and biodiversity; to protect crops and fisheries (economic loss); to protect the structure and function of ecosystems.

## IUCN and CITES

### The IUCN

:::def IUCN
The International Union for Conservation of Nature: an international organisation of governments, scientists and conservation groups that assesses the conservation status of species and advises on conservation.
:::

The IUCN does not make laws. It is best known for the **Red List of Threatened Species**, which assigns species to categories according to how likely they are to become extinct. The categories, from safest to most at risk, include Least Concern, Near Threatened, Vulnerable, Endangered and Critically Endangered, then Extinct in the Wild (living only in captivity) and Extinct; Data Deficient is used where information is too poor to judge. The IUCN also publishes information, gives advice to governments, and helps to plan conservation action. The Red List helps governments and charities decide which species and which places need protection first, and repeated assessments show whether a species is recovering or declining.

### CITES

:::def CITES
The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora: an international agreement between governments that controls trade in listed species and in their products (ivory, skins, timber, living plants and animals) so that trade does not threaten their survival.
:::

Species are listed in three appendices.

| Appendix | Meaning |
|---|---|
| I | species threatened with extinction: international trade in wild-caught specimens is banned (with rare exceptions, such as research) |
| II | species not yet threatened with extinction but which could become so unless trade is controlled: trade needs a permit from the exporting country |
| III | species that one country asks others to help it protect: trade needs a permit or certificate of origin |

Countries that sign the agreement make it law in their own country, with customs officers checking permits and punishing illegal trade. Roles: reduce the demand and the profit that drives hunting and collecting; allow sustainable trade in some species, under licence; and give a way for countries to cooperate.

CITES is about **trade**. It does not protect habitats and it cannot stop hunting or collecting for local use inside a country. It works only if countries enforce it.

:::warn Do not mix up the two
**IUCN** assesses the risk (Red List) and advises. **CITES** controls international trade through appendices. Neither one sets up parks: that is done by national governments.
:::

## Reading data about conservation

Data questions on conservation give the numbers in a protected population over time, the success of a captive breeding programme or the viability of stored seeds. Describe the trend with figures, then explain it.

:::eg A species of antelope was reintroduced into a fenced reserve. The table shows the numbers counted in the reserve (invented data for practice). Calculate the percentage increase in the population over 20 years and the mean increase per year, and describe the trend.
| year of reserve | number of antelope |
|---|---|
| 0 | 24 |
| 5 | 38 |
| 10 | 61 |
| 15 | 97 |
| 20 | 150 |

\[\begin{aligned} \text{increase} &= 150 - 24 = 126 \\ \text{percentage increase} &= \frac{126}{24} \times 100 \\ &= 525\% \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

\[\text{mean increase per year} = \frac{126}{20} = 6.3\ \text{animals per year}\]

[A1]

**Trend**: the population increased from 24 to 150 animals in 20 years, and the increase got larger each 5 years (14 animals in the first 5 years, 53 in the last 5). The percentage increase in each 5 years was about the same (58% from 0 to 5 years; 55% from 15 to 20 years), so the growth was exponential: each year more animals were born because more adults were breeding. [B1]

The population cannot go on increasing like this, because the reserve is fenced: food, water or space will limit it, and the rate will fall. The reserve's carrying capacity must be monitored. [B1]
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State what is meant by an endangered species.
A: A species that is likely to become extinct if the factors causing its decline continue (it has a small or falling population).
Q: Explain why climate change might cause a species that lives only at the top of a mountain to become extinct.
A: As temperatures rise, the cool conditions it is adapted to move uphill. The area with suitable conditions becomes smaller and finally disappears, and it cannot move higher or adapt fast enough, so it dies out.
Q: A reserve measuring 2 km by 2 km is divided into four square patches of 1 km by 1 km. Calculate the total length of edge before and after.
A: Before: \(4 \times 2 = 8\,\mathrm{km}\). After: \(4 \times 4 = 16\,\mathrm{km}\) (each patch has a perimeter of 4 km). The edge length doubles for the same area.
Q: Give two reasons for maintaining biodiversity, each of a different kind.
A: Economic: insects pollinate crops / medicines come from plants. Genetic: wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance. (Also: ethical, ecological, aesthetic.)
Q: In a germination test, 170 of 200 seeds from a seed bank germinated. Calculate the percentage viability.
A: \(170 \div 200 \times 100 = 85\%\).
Q: Distinguish between in situ and ex situ conservation, with an example of each.
A: In situ is protection of a species in its natural habitat, for example in a national park. Ex situ is protection outside its natural habitat, for example in a zoo or a seed bank.
Q: Seeds for a seed bank are collected from many plants at different sites rather than from one. Explain why.
A: Different plants have different alleles, so seeds from many plants hold more of the genetic diversity of the species. This makes the stored population more able to adapt, and less inbred when plants are grown from it.
Q: Frozen embryos are stored in liquid nitrogen. Explain why this allows them to be kept for many years.
A: At about −196 °C all chemical reactions, including those that damage cells, stop, so the cells do not age or break down; a protective solution prevents ice crystals from damaging the membranes.
Q: A surrogate for an embryo of an endangered mammal is often a female of a related, common species. Suggest why.
A: There may be too few females of the endangered species, or the available females may be too old or unable to carry a pregnancy. A related species has a similar uterus and pregnancy, so it can carry the embryo to term.
Q: State what it means when a species is listed in Appendix I of CITES.
A: It is threatened with extinction, and international trade in wild-caught specimens is banned (except in very special cases such as research).
Q: Explain why an invasive alien plant spreads quickly in a new country.
A: It has no natural herbivores, parasites or diseases there, so more of its seedlings survive; it grows and reproduces quickly, and it outcompetes native plants for light and nutrients.

Q: [3 marks] ★ Outline three ways in which a national park conserves species.
A: Laws protect the habitat from clearance, building and farming. [B1]
Rangers patrol to prevent poaching and hunting, and control invasive species. [B1]
The whole community is protected in its natural habitat, so species keep their normal behaviour and food webs. [B1]
(Also credit: tourist income pays for protection and gives local people jobs; the number of visitors is limited.)
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Reef-building corals contain photosynthetic algae. Explain how a rise in sea temperature can lead to the loss of a coral reef and the species that live on it.
H: Name what the coral does when the water is too warm and what it loses.
H: Then think about the animals that use the reef as a habitat and a food source.
A: In warmer water, the corals expel the algae that live in their tissues. [B1]
The algae supply the coral with food from photosynthesis, so the coral is starved and dies (bleaching). [B1]
The reef structure breaks down, so animals that shelter, breed and feed on it lose their habitat and food. [B1]
Their populations fall and some species may become extinct. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Seeds of a wild plant were stored in a seed bank, and samples were tested for germination (invented data). After 0, 10, 20, 30 and 40 years of storage, the percentages of seeds that germinated were 97, 95, 92, 84 and 61. (a) Describe the change in viability of the stored seeds. (b) The seed bank regrows the seeds when the viability falls below 85%. State when this should have been done. (c) Explain how new seeds are obtained for the bank, and why the new seeds are stored.
H: (a) Quote figures from the start and the end, and say where the fall speeds up.
H: (b) Which test result is the first below 85%?
A: (a) The viability fell from 97% to 61% over 40 years. [B1]
The fall was small (97% to 92%) for the first 20 years, and then faster, by 31 percentage points between 20 and 40 years (92% to 61%). [B1]
(b) After 30 years the viability was 84%, which is the first result below 85%, so the seeds should be regrown at about 30 years. [B1]
(c) Seeds are germinated and grown into mature plants, which are allowed to produce new seeds. [B1]
The new seeds, which are fresh and highly viable, are dried and stored, to keep the plant's genetic diversity for the future. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Rabbits were introduced into Australia in the nineteenth century and the myxoma virus was later released to control them. (a) Explain why biological control of an invasive species must be tested carefully before it is released. (b) State two ways, other than biological control, of controlling an invasive animal on an island.
H: What might the control agent do besides attacking the pest?
A: (a) The control agent might also attack native species. [B1]
It might itself become invasive, if it has no predators or disease in the new place, so the problem would be made worse (or the pest could evolve resistance to it). [B1]
(Also credit: it might not control the pest at all; it may take years to find out.)
(b) Trapping, hunting or poisoning the animals. [B1]
Prevent more from arriving, by inspecting ships and cargo (quarantine). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The African elephant is hunted for its ivory. The trade in ivory is regulated by CITES, which lists the species in an appendix. (a) Explain how hunting could lead to the extinction of a large, slow-breeding mammal. (b) Discuss how far CITES can protect the species.
H: For (a), follow the chain from hunting to a small population to loss of genetic diversity.
H: For (b), give one thing CITES does and two reasons why it may not be enough.
A: (a) Hunting kills adults faster than the population can replace them, because elephants breed slowly. [B1]
The population falls, so it is hard to find a mate and the birth rate falls further. [B1]
The small gene pool loses diversity by drift and the animals are inbred, so a chance event can kill the last individuals. [B1]
(b) CITES bans or controls international trade in ivory, which reduces the demand and profit that drive hunting. [B1]
But CITES only controls trade between countries, and does not protect the habitat or stop hunting within a country. [B1]
It also depends on each country enforcing it: illegal trade continues when enforcement is weak. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A rare mammal fell to 12 individuals in 1980 because of hunting. After hunting was banned the population recovered, and by 2020 there were 400 individuals. A study found that the 400 animals had much lower genetic variation than a related species of similar numbers, and that many young died from a disease that spread rapidly. (a) Explain why the population of 400 has low genetic variation. (b) Explain why a disease spreads easily through such a population. (c) Suggest how a zoo could help to increase the genetic diversity of the wild population.
H: (a) Which effect is it when a large population is cut to a few survivors?
H: (c) Think of the unrelated animals and gametes that the zoo may hold.
A: (a) This is the bottleneck effect: the 12 survivors carried only a sample of the alleles of the original population. [B1]
Alleles that were not in the survivors were lost, and the 400 animals are all descended from the 12. [B1]
(b) With little variation, most or all individuals are susceptible to the same pathogen; none may have an allele for resistance. [B1]
So the pathogen can spread from animal to animal and kill many. [B1]
(c) The zoo may hold animals, or frozen sperm and eggs, descended from other individuals that were not in the wild bottleneck. [B1]
Releasing these animals or using their sperm, eggs or embryos (IVF, embryo transfer) adds new alleles to the wild population, increasing its genetic diversity. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ An international team wants to conserve a rare, slow-breeding deer. Only 9 females and 2 males survive, in two small groups far apart. Outline how assisted reproduction and the frozen zoo could be used, and explain two limitations of this approach.
H: Use the words IVF, embryo transfer and surrogacy, and think about moving the animals around.
A: Eggs from the females and sperm from the males are collected, and eggs are fertilised in a dish (IVF) to give embryos. [B1]
The embryos can be transferred into surrogate females of the species, or of a related deer, so many calves are born from few females. [B1]
Sperm, eggs and embryos are frozen in liquid nitrogen, so they can be moved between the two groups, and the genes of old or dead animals can be used later. [B1]
Limitation: all the offspring come from only 11 animals, so the genetic diversity stays low (inbreeding). [B1]
Limitation: success rates are low, and the cause of the decline must also be tackled (protecting the habitat or stopping hunting). [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Two plans are proposed for a forest reserve of 36 km². Plan A keeps it as one block, 6 km by 6 km. Plan B splits it into nine blocks of 2 km by 2 km. Edge effects extend 0.5 km from every edge, so only land further than 0.5 km from every edge is core habitat. A forest-interior bird needs at least 2 km² of core habitat for each breeding pair. (a) Calculate the core area and the number of breeding pairs that each plan could support. (b) Suggest two ways of improving Plan B without increasing its area.
H: The core of a square of side \(s\) has side \(s - 2 \times 0.5\).
H: For Plan B, find the core of one block and multiply by nine.
H: For (b), think about how patches can be joined and how the edge can be reduced.
A: (a) Plan A: core side \(= 6 - 1 = 5\,\mathrm{km}\), core area \(= 25\,\mathrm{km^2}\) [M1] so \(25 \div 2 = 12\) pairs (12.5 rounded down) [A1]
Plan B: core of one block \(= (2 - 1)^2 = 1\,\mathrm{km^2}\), so nine blocks give \(9\,\mathrm{km^2}\) in all [M1]
Each block's core (1 km²) is less than the 2 km² one pair needs, so Plan B supports 0 pairs [A1]
The total core of 9 km² looks big enough for four pairs (\(9 \div 2 = 4.5\)), but the core is split into pieces that are each too small, so the pairs cannot use it. [B1]
(b) Join the blocks with corridors of forest so that birds can move between them and the habitat becomes larger. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A conservation agency has money for one project. Project 1 is a captive breeding programme for a rare frog, with 40 frogs bred per year at a cost per frog of £500, of which half survive release. Project 2 pays rangers to protect 200 km² of the frog's habitat, which holds 600 frogs and is predicted to increase the wild population by 5% per year, at an annual cost of £30,000. The frog is threatened by a fungal disease. (a) Calculate the cost per surviving animal for Project 1 and the number of frogs added by Project 2 in one year. (b) Evaluate the two projects.
H: (a) Project 1 costs 40 × £500 and gives 40 ÷ 2 survivors.

A: (a) Project 1 total cost \(= 40 \times 500 = £20\,000\); survivors \(= 40 \div 2 = 20\), so \(20\,000 \div 20 = £1000\) per surviving frog. [M1] [A1]

(b) Project 2 protects the habitat so also protects other species and keeps the natural food web, but it is more expensive overall (£30,000 compared with £20,000), the gain is slow, and it cannot deal with the fungal disease. [B1]

:::recap
- Extinction is the permanent loss of a species. Causes: climate change (habitats shift faster than species migrate or adapt), competition from introduced species, hunting (population below viable size, loss of genetic diversity), and loss, degradation and fragmentation of habitats.
- Small populations lose alleles by drift, become inbred and can be wiped out by chance events (extinction vortex).
- Reasons to maintain biodiversity: ethical, ecological (food webs, keystone species), economic (food, medicines, pollination, tourism), genetic resources, aesthetic.
- In situ: national parks, marine parks, corridors. Ex situ: zoos (captive breeding, studbooks, reintroduction, research, education), botanic gardens, seed banks (dried, cold, germination-tested), frozen zoos (gametes, embryos, cells in liquid nitrogen at about −196 °C).
- Assisted reproduction: eggs collected, IVF in a dish, embryo transfer, surrogacy (often a related, more common species). The surrogate does not pass on her genes.
- Invasive alien species have no natural predators, outcompete, prey on, spread disease to, or hybridise with natives; control by prevention, removal, chemicals or biological control (tested carefully).
- IUCN: assesses status and publishes the Red List, and advises. CITES: regulates international trade in endangered species, using three appendices. CITES does not protect habitats.
:::
