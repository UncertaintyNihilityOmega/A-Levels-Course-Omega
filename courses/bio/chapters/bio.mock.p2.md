(b) State the function of the structure labelled E. [1]

(c) On the printed micrograph, the whole cell was 75 mm wide. The actual width of the cell was 30 µm. Calculate the magnification of the micrograph. Show your working. [2]

(d) On the same micrograph, the structure labelled B was 20 mm long. Calculate its actual length in µm. [2]

(e) Explain why the internal structure of the structure labelled E can be seen in an electron micrograph but not clearly in a light microscope image. [2]

(f) State three differences between this plant cell and a bacterial cell. [3]

Q: [12 marks] (#bio.enzymes) A student investigated the effect of temperature on the activity of lipase. The mixture contained milk, sodium carbonate solution and phenolphthalein indicator, which is pink in alkali and colourless in acid. The lipase solution was added at each temperature, and the student timed how long it took for the pink colour to disappear.

| temperature / °C | time for pink colour to disappear / s |
|---|---|
| 30 | 480 |
| 40 | 240 |
| 50 | 120 |
| 60 | 60 |
| 70 | 150 |
| 80 | no change in 600 |

(a) Describe the results. [3]

(b) The rate of reaction can be calculated as 1 ÷ time. Calculate the percentage increase in the rate of reaction between 40 °C and 60 °C. Show your working. [3]

(c) Explain the results between 30 °C and 60 °C, and between 60 °C and 80 °C. [4]

(d) State two variables that the student should keep constant in this investigation. [2]
H: For (a) quote the shortest time and its temperature, and the change above it. For (b) work out the two rates before the percentage.
A: (a) The time falls as the temperature rises, from 480 s at 30 °C to a minimum of 60 s at 60 °C [B1]. Above 60 °C the time increases, to 150 s at 70 °C [B1]. At 80 °C there was no colour change in 600 s [B1].

(b) Rate at 40 °C \(= \dfrac{1}{240} = 0.00417\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\); rate at 60 °C \(= \dfrac{1}{60} = 0.0167\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) [M1]. Percentage increase \(= \dfrac{0.0167 - 0.00417}{0.00417} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 300\%\) [A1]

(c) 30–60 °C: as the temperature rises, the molecules have more kinetic energy and move faster, so there are more frequent collisions between enzyme and substrate [B1], so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second [B1]. 60–80 °C: the high temperature breaks the hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure, so the shape of the active site changes (the enzyme is denatured) [B1], and the substrate no longer fits (no enzyme–substrate complexes form) [B1].

(d) Any two of: the volume (and concentration) of milk [B1]; the volume (and concentration) of lipase solution [B1]. (Also credit: the pH (volume of sodium carbonate); the volume of indicator; the time allowed for the mixture to reach the temperature before adding the enzyme; the same batch of lipase.)
:::

Q: [8 marks] (#bio.movement) A student cut strips of celery stalk, each 60.0 mm long, and placed one in each of six sucrose solutions. After 30 minutes she measured the length of each strip.

| sucrose / mol dm⁻³ | final length / mm |
|---|---|
| 0.0 | 63.0 |
| 0.2 | 61.8 |
| 0.4 | 60.6 |
| 0.6 | 59.4 |
| 0.8 | 58.2 |
| 1.0 | 57.0 |

(a) Calculate the percentage change in length of the strip in 0.8 mol dm⁻³ sucrose solution. Show your working. [2]

(b) Explain why the strip placed in 0.0 mol dm⁻³ sucrose solution (distilled water) became longer. [2]

(c) The concentration of sucrose solution that has the same water potential as the celery cells is the concentration at which there is no change in length. Use the data to estimate this concentration. [2]

(d) A celery stalk that is left out of water for several hours becomes limp. Explain why. [2]
H: For (c) look for the two concentrations on either side of 60.0 mm.
A: (a) Change \(= 58.2 - 60.0 = -1.8\,\mathrm{mm}\); percentage change \(= \dfrac{-1.8}{60.0} \times 100\) [M1] \(= -3.0\%\) (a decrease of 3.0%) [A1]

(b) The cells have a lower (more negative) water potential than pure water (0 kPa), so water enters the cells by osmosis through the partially permeable membrane, down the water potential gradient [B1]. The cells become turgid and the contents push against the cell walls, so the strip lengthens [B1].

(c) The length is 60.6 mm at 0.4 and 59.4 mm at 0.6 mol dm⁻³, so the length is 60.0 mm midway between them [B1], at 0.50 mol dm⁻³ [B1].

(d) Water is lost by transpiration (evaporation from the surface of the stalk) and is not replaced [B1]. The cells lose water and their turgor, so they can no longer push against the cell walls and support the stalk [B1].

Q: [13 marks] (#bio.synthesis) The template strand of a short gene reads 3′–TAC CTT AAA CGG ACT–5′. Some of the mRNA codons and the amino acids they code for are in the table.

| mRNA codon | amino acid or signal |
|---|---|
| AUG | methionine (start) |
| GAA | glutamic acid |
| AAA | lysine |
| UUU | phenylalanine |
| GCC | alanine |
| UGA | stop |

(a) Write the base sequence of the mRNA transcribed from this template strand. Label the ends of the mRNA. [2]

(b) Use the table to write the sequence of amino acids in the polypeptide. [2]

(c) A mutation changes the second triplet of the template strand from CTT to TTT. State the name of this type of mutation and describe its effect on the polypeptide. [2]

(d) A different mutation deletes the first base of the second triplet of the template strand. Explain why this mutation is likely to have a much larger effect on the polypeptide than the mutation in (c). [3]

(e) Describe the role of RNA polymerase in transcription. [2]

(f) Describe the role of tRNA in translation. [2]
H: For (a) pair each base and replace thymine. For (d) think about what happens to every triplet after the deletion.
A: (a) 5′–AUGGAAUUUGCCUGA–3′ [B1], with the 5′ end on the left and the 3′ end on the right (antiparallel to the template) [B1].

(b) Methionine – glutamic acid – phenylalanine – alanine [B1], then the stop codon UGA ends translation (no amino acid) [B1].

(c) A substitution [B1]. The second codon changes from GAA to AAA, so lysine replaces glutamic acid at position 2, and the rest of the polypeptide is unchanged [B1].

(d) The deletion shifts the reading frame (a frameshift) [B1]. Every triplet after the deletion is read differently, so the codons after it are different [B1]. Many amino acids change (or a stop codon appears early), so the tertiary structure and the function of the protein are lost [B1].

(e) RNA polymerase binds to the DNA at the start of the gene, breaks the hydrogen bonds between the strands and uses the template strand [B1]. It joins RNA nucleotides that are complementary to the template strand by phosphodiester bonds, to make the mRNA [B1].

(f) A tRNA molecule has an anticodon that pairs with a complementary codon on the mRNA at the ribosome [B1], and it carries the specific amino acid for that codon to the ribosome [B1].

Q: [8 marks] (#bio.mammaltransport) The table shows the mean velocity of blood and the total cross-sectional area of the vessels in three parts of the circulation of a resting adult (invented, rounded data).

| vessel | total cross-sectional area / cm² | mean velocity / cm s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| aorta | 4 | 20 |
| capillaries | 4000 | 0.02 |
| venae cavae | 8 | 10 |

(a) Describe the change in the velocity of the blood from the aorta to the capillaries and then to the venae cavae. [2]

(b) Calculate how many times greater the total cross-sectional area of the capillaries is than that of the aorta. [2]

(c) Explain why the blood moves slowest in the capillaries. [2]

(d) The capillaries around the alveoli of the lungs have a slow blood flow. Explain why this is an advantage. [2]
H: For (c) use your answer to (b). For (d) think about the time available.
A: (a) The velocity falls from 20 cm s⁻¹ in the aorta to 0.02 cm s⁻¹ in the capillaries [B1], then rises again to 10 cm s⁻¹ in the venae cavae [B1].

(b) \(\dfrac{4000}{4}\) [M1] \(= 1000\) times [A1]

(c) There are very many capillaries, so their total cross-sectional area is much greater than that of the aorta [B1]. The same volume of blood per second flows through a much larger area, so the velocity is lower [B1].

(d) The blood stays in the capillaries beside the alveoli for longer, giving more time for oxygen to diffuse into the red blood cells (and carbon dioxide to diffuse out) [B1]. This allows the haemoglobin to become nearly saturated with oxygen [B1].

Q: [6 marks] (#bio.antibiotics) Tuberculosis (TB) is treated with a combination of several antibiotics taken for six months. Children in many countries are given the BCG vaccine against TB.

(a) State the type of organism that causes TB and the name of the pathogen. [1]

(b) Explain why TB patients are treated with several antibiotics for many months, instead of one antibiotic for a few days. [2]

(c) Explain how a vaccine gives a person long-term immunity to a disease. [3]
H: For (b) think of bacteria that survive and what could happen to them if the treatment stopped early.
A: (a) A bacterium, *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (also credit: *M. bovis*) [B1].

(b) Several antibiotics together and a long course kill all of the bacteria, including slow-growing ones that survive a short course [B1]. This reduces the chance of a resistant mutant surviving and multiplying, so resistant bacteria do not spread [B1].

(c) The vaccine contains antigens from the pathogen (weakened or dead), and the antigens stimulate B-lymphocytes with complementary receptors to divide and make plasma cells and memory cells, without causing the disease [B1]. Memory cells stay in the blood for years [B1]. When the person is exposed to the real pathogen, the memory cells quickly divide into plasma cells that make large amounts of antibody (the secondary response), so the pathogen is destroyed before it causes disease [B1].
