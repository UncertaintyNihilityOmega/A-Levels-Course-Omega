## Why classify?

:::def Classification
The arrangement of organisms into groups, based on features they share, so that the groups reflect how closely related the organisms are.
:::

Classification does four jobs:
- It **organises** the enormous variety of living things so that each organism has a place.
- It lets biologists **identify** an unknown organism and **predict** its features: a new mammal found in a forest will have hair and feed its young on milk.
- It gives every species a **name** that is the same all over the world, which avoids confusion (the word "robin" is a different bird in Britain and in North America).
- A good classification **reflects evolutionary relationships**: organisms in the same group share a recent common ancestor. Modern classification uses DNA and RNA sequence data (see [Evolution and speciation](#bio.evolution)) to test and improve the groups.

## What is a species?

The species is the basic unit of classification, but there is no single perfect definition. The syllabus asks for three **species concepts**, each with strengths and weaknesses.

:::def Biological species concept
A species is a group of organisms with similar morphological, physiological, biochemical and behavioural features, which can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, and which are reproductively isolated from other species.
:::

:::def Morphological species concept
A species is a group of organisms that share the same structural (morphological) features, which are different from the features of other groups.
:::

:::def Ecological species concept
A species is a group of organisms that occupy the same ecological niche, which is different from the niches of other species.
:::

:::cols
:::col Biological
**Strength**: it uses the test that matters for gene pools, whether two groups can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
**Problems**:
- Cannot be used for organisms that reproduce **asexually** (most bacteria).
- Cannot be tested on **fossils** (breeding cannot be observed).
- Hard to test for populations that live far apart (they never meet) or that are only partly isolated: some different species can interbreed to produce fertile hybrids.
:::
:::col Morphological
**Strength**: simple, quick, and needs only the specimen. It works on fossils, dried museum specimens and organisms that reproduce asexually.
**Problems**:
- Individuals of one species can **look very different** (males and females, young and adult, the breeds of a dog).
- Different species can **look alike** (cryptic species).
- Where to draw the line is a **judgement**.
:::
:::col Ecological
**Strength**: works for asexual organisms, and links a species to its habitat and role.
**Problems**:
- Niches **overlap**, so two species can share a niche and one species can have more than one.
- A niche is **hard to measure** completely.
- The niche of one species can change with age or place (tadpole and frog).
:::
:::

Biologists use the concepts together, and now add **DNA sequence data**. The concepts are a toolkit, not rivals.

:::eg A horse and a donkey can mate and produce a mule. Mules are almost always sterile. Use this information, and the three species concepts, to discuss what is meant by the term species.
By the biological species concept, a horse and a donkey are different species, because they cannot interbreed to produce fertile offspring (the mule is sterile). [B1]

However, this concept cannot be used for organisms that reproduce asexually, such as bacteria, or for fossils, where breeding cannot be observed. [B1]

By the morphological species concept, they are different species because they have different structural features (such as ear length and body shape). [B1]

However, this concept can be unreliable, because individuals of one species can look different from each other (for example, the breeds of a dog) and different species can look alike. [B1]

By the ecological species concept, species occupy different niches, but horses and donkeys both graze, so their niches overlap and the concept alone does not separate them. [B1]

So no one concept works for every organism, and biologists use several together, with DNA sequence data. [B1]
:::

:::note Ring species
In a few cases a chain of neighbouring populations around a barrier, such as a mountain range or a valley, can all interbreed with the next one in the chain, but the two populations at the ends of the chain, where they meet again, cannot interbreed. The biological species concept then cannot say how many species there are. Such a **ring species** shows that a species can change gradually.
:::

### Naming species: the binomial system

Every species has a two-part scientific name in Latin, the **binomial system**, introduced by Carl Linnaeus.

:::def Binomial system
The internationally agreed way of naming species, using two names: the **genus** (with a capital letter) followed by the **species** (with a lower-case letter). The name is printed in italics (or underlined when handwritten).
:::

The scientific name of humans is *Homo sapiens*: genus *Homo*, species *sapiens*. The name is the same in every language, and a whole genus is related, so the name also tells you which other species an organism is closest to.

:::warn Writing a binomial
Genus with a capital, species without. *Homo sapiens*, not "homo Sapiens" and not "Homo Sapiens". Italics when typed; underline each word separately when handwritten. When you write the name again, you may shorten the genus to its first letter (*H. sapiens*).
:::

## Three domains

Early classifications used two kingdoms, plants and animals. Then bacteria, fungi and single-celled eukaryotes were given their own kingdoms. In 1977 Carl Woese compared the sequences of **ribosomal RNA** (rRNA) in many organisms. Every cell has ribosomes, so rRNA can be compared in all cells, and it changes slowly. The sequences showed that the prokaryotes were not one group. Some of them, now called Archaea, were as different from the Bacteria as they were from the eukaryotes. This led to a higher rank than kingdom.

:::def Domain
The highest rank in the classification of living things. There are three domains: Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya.
:::

- **Bacteria**: prokaryotes, such as *Escherichia coli* and the cyanobacteria.
- **Archaea**: prokaryotes, many of which live in extreme places such as hot springs, very salty lakes and very acidic water, although others live in soil and the sea.
- **Eukarya**: organisms whose cells have a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. It contains the four kingdoms Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia.

Archaea and Bacteria are both **prokaryotes**: they have no nucleus, their DNA is circular and not surrounded by a membrane, and they have no membrane-bound organelles with double membranes (see [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes)). The syllabus asks you to know three differences between them:

| feature | Bacteria | Archaea |
|---|---|---|
| membrane lipids | fatty acid chains that are unbranched, joined to glycerol by **ester bonds** | hydrocarbon chains that are **branched**, joined to glycerol by **ether bonds** |
| ribosomal RNA | base sequences that differ from those of Archaea | different base sequences from Bacteria (and rRNA sequences closer to those of Eukarya) |
| cell wall | **peptidoglycan** | no peptidoglycan: walls of other materials (such as protein or other polysaccharides), or no wall |

:::key Archaea and Bacteria: what to write
Both are prokaryotes. The three differences to quote are the **membrane lipids** (ether bonds and branched chains in Archaea; ester bonds and unbranched chains in Bacteria), the **base sequences of rRNA**, and the **cell wall** (peptidoglycan in Bacteria only). Always say which domain each feature belongs to.
:::

:::eg State three differences between Archaea and Bacteria.
The membrane lipids of Archaea have branched hydrocarbon chains joined to glycerol by ether bonds, but in Bacteria the chains are unbranched and joined by ester bonds. [B1]

Bacteria have a cell wall of peptidoglycan, but Archaea have no peptidoglycan in their cell wall. [B1]

The base sequences of ribosomal RNA are different in Archaea and Bacteria. [B1]

(Also credit: Archaea RNA sequences are more similar to those of eukaryotes; many Archaea live in extreme environments.)

A weaker answer says "Archaea live in hot places and bacteria do not" (many bacteria live in hot places too, and many Archaea do not), or "bacteria are prokaryotes and Archaea are not", which is wrong because both are prokaryotes. It scores no marks.
:::

:::analogy Two prokaryote families
Archaea and Bacteria look alike down a microscope, like two kinds of bicycle with the same frame. They differ in the parts: the chain, the brakes and the paint. Only a close look at lipids, walls and rRNA shows that they are different designs.
:::

## The taxonomic hierarchy

Within Eukarya, organisms are arranged in a **hierarchy** of groups. Each group is called a **taxon** (plural taxa), and each rank is contained in the one above it.

:::def Taxonomic hierarchy
The arrangement of taxa in ranks. For Eukarya, from the largest to the smallest group: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
:::

A mnemonic for the seven ranks: **K**ing **P**hilip **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup. The domain sits above kingdom.

Going **down** the ranks, the groups get **smaller**, with **fewer organisms**, and the organisms in each group share more features and a more recent common ancestor. Two organisms in the same genus are more closely related than two in the same family but different genera.

:::eg Write out the classification of a human, and explain what it shows.
The classification is, from the top:

| rank | name |
|---|---|
| domain | Eukarya |
| kingdom | Animalia |
| phylum | Chordata |
| class | Mammalia |
| order | Primates |
| family | Hominidae |
| genus | *Homo* |
| species | *Homo sapiens* |

Each rank is a smaller group inside the one above. *Homo sapiens* is in the kingdom Animalia with all animals, in the phylum Chordata with all animals with a backbone (and similar structures), in the class Mammalia with all mammals, and so on. Chimpanzees share the order Primates and the family Hominidae with humans but belong to a different genus, so they are less closely related to us than another species of *Homo* would be.
:::

:::warn Common mistakes with the ranks
- Writing the ranks in the wrong order. Use the mnemonic. The order is largest to smallest: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
- Treating "bacteria" as a kingdom (the old system). Use the domain Bacteria.
- Forgetting that a species name always needs the genus too.
:::

## The kingdoms of Eukarya

The Eukarya domain is divided into four kingdoms.

| | Protoctista | Fungi | Plantae | Animalia |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cells | eukaryotic | eukaryotic | eukaryotic | eukaryotic |
| number of cells | mostly **single-celled**; some multicellular, with no true tissues | most are multicellular, made of threads (**hyphae**); yeasts are single-celled | **multicellular** | **multicellular** |
| cell wall | present in some, absent in others | **chitin** | **cellulose** | **none** |
| chloroplasts | present in some (algae), absent in others | **none** | **present** | **none** |
| nutrition | photosynthesis (algae) or ingestion or absorption, depending on the organism | **heterotrophic**: secrete enzymes (extracellular digestion) and **absorb** the products | **autotrophic**: photosynthesis | **heterotrophic**: usually ingest food |
| other features | very diverse; the group of eukaryotes that do not fit the other three kingdoms | reproduce by spores; no chloroplasts | large permanent vacuole; store starch; do not move from place to place | nervous coordination; usually move; store glycogen |
| examples | *Amoeba*, *Paramecium*, *Chlorella*, *Plasmodium* | mushrooms, *Mucor*, yeast | mosses, ferns, conifers, flowering plants | insects, fish, birds, mammals |

:::key How to place a eukaryote in a kingdom
Ask: **Is it multicellular? Does it have a cell wall, and of what? Does it have chloroplasts? How does it feed?**
- Cellulose wall + chloroplasts + photosynthesis → **Plantae**.
- Chitin wall + hyphae + extracellular digestion and absorption + no chloroplasts → **Fungi**.
- Multicellular, no wall, no chloroplasts, ingests food, nervous system → **Animalia**.
- A eukaryote that is none of these, usually single-celled → **Protoctista**.
:::

:::warn Fungi are not plants
Fungi grow in soil and on wood and have cell walls, so they were once classed with plants. But fungal walls are chitin, not cellulose; fungi have no chloroplasts and cannot photosynthesise; they feed by extracellular digestion and absorption, and they store glycogen, not starch. Protoctista is not "simple animals" or "plants": it is a varied group, and some members photosynthesise while others feed on other organisms.
:::

:::eg A student collects a thread-like organism from decaying leaves. It is made of fine threads (hyphae) containing nuclei, has cell walls that give a positive test for chitin, has no chloroplasts, makes spores, and digests the leaf by releasing enzymes and absorbing the products. Place it in a domain and kingdom, giving the evidence.
It has nuclei, so it is eukaryotic, in the domain Eukarya. [B1]

The cell walls contain chitin, not cellulose, so it is not in the kingdom Plantae. [B1]

It has no chloroplasts and feeds by extracellular digestion and absorption, so it is heterotrophic and not a plant. [B1]

It is made of hyphae and reproduces by spores, so it is in the kingdom Fungi. [B1]
:::

## Viruses

Viruses are not made of cells, and they cannot carry out life processes outside a host cell (see [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes)). They have no ribosomes, so they cannot be compared by rRNA, and they fit into none of the domains or kingdoms. At this level they are classified by their **nucleic acid**.

:::def Virus classification
Viruses are classified by the type of nucleic acid in the virus particle, DNA or RNA, and by whether that nucleic acid is single stranded or double stranded.
:::

That gives four groups:

| group | nucleic acid | example |
|---|---|---|
| dsDNA | double-stranded DNA | bacteriophage T4 (a virus that infects bacteria) |
| ssDNA | single-stranded DNA | parvovirus |
| dsRNA | double-stranded RNA | rotavirus |
| ssRNA | single-stranded RNA | HIV, influenza virus, tobacco mosaic virus |

HIV is an ssRNA virus (a retrovirus): it carries an enzyme, reverse transcriptase, that copies its RNA into DNA inside the host cell.

You can tell the type of nucleic acid from the bases. **DNA contains thymine (T); RNA contains uracil (U) instead.** In a **double strand** the bases pair, A with T (or U) and G with C, so the percentages of A and T (or U) are equal and the percentages of G and C are equal. In a **single strand** they are not.

:::eg The percentages of each base in the nucleic acid of three virus particles are shown. Classify each virus.

| virus | A / % | T / % | U / % | G / % | C / % |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P | 22 | 22 | 0 | 28 | 28 |
| Q | 31 | 0 | 27 | 24 | 18 |
| R | 28 | 33 | 0 | 18 | 21 |

**Step 1: DNA or RNA?** Virus P and virus R contain thymine, so they have DNA. Virus Q contains uracil and no thymine, so it has RNA.

**Step 2: single or double stranded?** In P, A = T (22 = 22) and G = C (28 = 28), so the bases pair and the DNA is double stranded. In R, A ≠ T (28 and 33) and G ≠ C (18 and 21), so the DNA is single stranded. In Q, A ≠ U (31 and 27) and G ≠ C (24 and 18), so the RNA is single stranded.

**Conclusion**: P is a dsDNA virus, Q is an ssRNA virus and R is an ssDNA virus.
:::

:::exam Classify from a description
When you are asked to classify an organism, **list the features in the description, and for each one say what it shows**, then name the group last. Use the order: cellular or not → nucleus or not → cell wall → chloroplasts → feeding → body plan. Mark schemes give one mark per feature that is linked to a group. A bare name ("Fungi") often scores nothing without the reasons.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: state three differences between Archaea and Bacteria. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Archaea have membrane lipids with branched chains and ether bonds, but bacteria have unbranched chains and ester bonds.
Line 2: Archaea have a peptidoglycan cell wall, but bacteria do not.
Line 3: The base sequences of ribosomal RNA differ between the two domains.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: it is the other way round. Bacteria have a peptidoglycan cell wall, and Archaea do not. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct Line 2: "Bacteria have a cell wall of peptidoglycan, but Archaea do not have peptidoglycan." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State the ranks of the taxonomic hierarchy of Eukarya, from the largest to the smallest.
A: Kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species (domain above kingdom).
Q: The domestic dog has the genus *Canis* and the species *familiaris*. Write its scientific name correctly.
A: *Canis familiaris*: genus with a capital letter, species with a lower-case letter, in italics (or underlined if handwritten).
Q: Explain why biologists use scientific names rather than common names.
A: A scientific name is the same in every language, so it is unambiguous. A common name can refer to different species in different places (for example, the robin is a different bird in Britain and in North America), and one species can have many common names.
Q: A species of bacterium reproduces only by binary fission. Explain why the biological species concept cannot be used for it, and name a concept that can.
A: The biological species concept needs organisms to interbreed to produce fertile offspring, and there is no breeding in asexual reproduction. The morphological species concept (similar structure) or the ecological species concept (same niche) can be used.
Q: A palaeontologist has found fossil bones of an extinct animal. State which species concept she must use and explain why.
A: The morphological species concept. She cannot test whether the animals could interbreed, but she can compare the structural features of the bones.
Q: Name the kingdom of an organism that is multicellular, has no cell walls, has no chloroplasts, ingests its food and has nerve cells.
A: Animalia.
Q: *Paramecium* is a single-celled organism with a nucleus and no cell wall that feeds by taking in bacteria. Name its domain and kingdom.
A: Domain Eukarya (it has a nucleus); kingdom Protoctista (a single-celled eukaryote that is not a fungus, plant or animal).
Q: State the one structural feature of the cell wall that shows that the domain Archaea differs from Bacteria.
A: Archaea have no peptidoglycan in their cell wall; the cell wall of Bacteria contains peptidoglycan.
Q: Explain why viruses are not placed in any of the three domains.
A: Viruses are not made of cells and have no ribosomes, so they cannot be compared with cells by their rRNA, and they do not fit the definition of a domain of cellular organisms.
Q: The nucleic acid of a virus contains uracil, and the percentages of adenine and uracil are different. Classify the virus.
A: It contains uracil, so it is RNA; A and U are not equal, so the bases are not paired and it is single stranded. It is an ssRNA virus.
:::

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A species of salamander lives in a ring of populations around a large valley. Each population interbreeds freely with the next one in the ring and the offspring are fertile. The two populations that meet at the end of the ring, on the far side of the valley, live side by side but do not interbreed. The populations vary gradually in colour round the ring. (a) Explain why the biological species concept makes it difficult to say how many species of salamander there are. (b) Suggest how the ring may have formed. (c) Evaluate the use of the morphological species concept for these salamanders.
H: For (b), the ancestor spread in one direction around the valley. What happened to each population on the way?
A: (a) The neighbouring populations can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so each pair seems to be in one species. [B1]
The populations at the ends of the ring cannot interbreed, so they seem to be separate species, so there is no clear answer. [B1]
(b) An ancestral population spread around both sides of the valley, and each population diverged a little from the one before it (different selection pressures, mutations). [B1]
When the two ends met again, they had diverged so much that they could no longer interbreed. [B1]
(c) The colour changes gradually round the ring, so there is no clear point at which to draw a line between one species and another. [B1]
The populations at the ends look different but the neighbours look alike, so the concept gives a judgement that depends on who is making it (it is subjective). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The table shows the number of differences in a sequence of 100 bases of the gene for ribosomal RNA in four organisms. B1 and B2 are bacteria, A1 is an archaeon and E1 is a eukaryote.

| organism | B1 | B2 | A1 | E1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| B1 | – | 12 | 48 | 48 |
| B2 | 12 | – | 48 | 48 |
| A1 | 48 | 48 | – | 30 |
| E1 | 48 | 48 | 30 | – |

(a) Name the two organisms that are most closely related and state what this shows about the archaeon. (b) Explain why the archaeon A1 and the bacteria are placed in different domains. (c) Suggest why ribosomal RNA is a suitable molecule for comparing organisms in all three domains.
H: For (a), the smallest number between A1 and another organism, not B1 and B2.
H: For (c), which structures do all cells contain, and does rRNA change quickly or slowly?
A: (a) B1 and B2 are most closely related (12 differences). [B1]
The archaeon A1 is more closely related to the eukaryote E1 (30 differences) than to either bacterium (48). [B1]
(b) A1 differs from both bacteria by 48 bases, which is a lot more than the 12 between B1 and B2, so A1 is not closely related to the Bacteria. [B1]
The difference between the archaeon and the bacteria is as large as the difference between the bacteria and the eukaryote, so Archaea are a separate group (domain). [B1]
(c) All cells have ribosomes, so rRNA is present in organisms of all three domains. [B1]
rRNA changes slowly, so the differences are still readable between distantly related organisms. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A single-celled organism lives in pond water. It has a nucleus, a flagellum and chloroplasts, and no cell wall. It carries out photosynthesis in the light, but in the dark it takes in dissolved organic matter and engulfs small particles. (a) Name the domain and the kingdom, and justify the kingdom. (b) Explain why this organism does not fit a simple classification into plant or animal. (c) State which species concept would be most useful for classifying it, if it reproduces mainly by dividing in two, and explain.
H: Think about which two kingdoms each match part of the description.
A: (a) Domain Eukarya (it has a nucleus). [B1]
Kingdom Protoctista, because it is single-celled and does not fit the features of Plantae, Fungi or Animalia (it has no cell wall and no tissues). [B1]
(b) It has chloroplasts and photosynthesises like a plant, [B1] but it has no cell wall, moves by a flagellum and takes in food like an animal. [B1]
(c) The biological species concept cannot be used as it divides asexually, so there is no interbreeding. [B1]
The morphological concept (or the ecological concept) can be used, comparing structure or niche. [B1]

(a) State which organism has rRNA most similar to the rRNA of the mitochondrion, and what this suggests about the origin of mitochondria. :::quiz
? Which statement describes the biological species concept?
+ Organisms that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, and are reproductively isolated from other species.
- Organisms that look alike and share structural features.
- Organisms that occupy the same niche.
- Organisms that have exactly the same DNA sequence.
> The biological concept is based on breeding. Looking alike is the morphological concept, and the same niche is the ecological concept. Members of one species do not have identical DNA.
? Why is the biological species concept difficult to use for bacteria?
+ Bacteria reproduce asexually, so there is no interbreeding to test.
- Bacteria have no DNA.
- Bacteria are too large to be seen.
- Bacteria always interbreed with Archaea.
> The test of fertile offspring from interbreeding does not apply to organisms that reproduce asexually.
? Which domain contains a prokaryote with peptidoglycan in its cell wall and ester-linked, unbranched membrane lipids?
+ Bacteria
- Archaea
- Eukarya
- Protoctista
> Peptidoglycan and ester bonds are features of Bacteria. Archaea have ether bonds and no peptidoglycan, and Eukarya and Protoctista are not prokaryotes.
? Which is the correct order of ranks, largest to smallest?
+ class, order, family, genus
- order, class, family, genus
- class, family, order, genus
- genus, family, order, class
> The order of the ranks is kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
? How should the scientific name of the African elephant be written?
+ *Loxodonta africana*
- *loxodonta Africana*
- *Loxodonta Africana*
- Loxodonta africana, with no italics or underlining
> The genus has a capital letter, the species a small letter, and the name is italic (or underlined).
? An organism has a cell wall of chitin, no chloroplasts and absorbs nutrients from dead matter. Which kingdom?
+ Fungi
- Plantae
- Animalia
- Protoctista
> Chitin walls, extracellular digestion and absorption, and no chloroplasts show a fungus. A plant has cellulose and chloroplasts, and an animal has no cell wall.
? Which kingdom contains most single-celled eukaryotes and has the most varied features?
+ Protoctista
- Fungi
- Plantae
- Animalia
> Protoctista is the kingdom for eukaryotes that do not fit the other three.
? A virus has a nucleic acid with thymine, in which the percentage of A equals that of T and the percentage of G equals that of C. How is it classified?
+ dsDNA
- ssDNA
- dsRNA
- ssRNA
> Thymine means DNA, and equal A and T and equal G and C show pairing, so it is double stranded.
? Why can viruses not be placed in a domain?
+ They are not cells and have no ribosomes.
- They are too small to be seen.
- They have no nucleic acid.
- They are all in Archaea.
> Domains group cellular organisms. A virus is not a cell, and has a nucleic acid core inside a protein coat.

:::recap
- Classification groups organisms by shared features and, now, by DNA and RNA sequences, so the groups reflect relationships. The binomial name (genus capital, species small, italics) is the same worldwide.
- Species concepts: biological (interbreed to give fertile offspring; fails for asexual organisms and fossils), morphological (shared structure; variation within species, similar-looking species) and ecological (shared niche; niches overlap).
- Three domains: Archaea, Bacteria (both prokaryotes) and Eukarya. Archaea differ from Bacteria in membrane lipids (branched chains, ether bonds v unbranched, ester bonds), rRNA sequences and cell walls (no peptidoglycan v peptidoglycan).
- Hierarchy: kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species. Down the ranks, fewer organisms and closer relationship.
- Kingdoms: Protoctista (mostly single-celled, varied), Fungi (chitin, hyphae, extracellular digestion, absorb), Plantae (cellulose, chloroplasts, multicellular), Animalia (no wall, ingest, nervous coordination).
- Viruses: not cells and in no domain; classified by nucleic acid: dsDNA, ssDNA, dsRNA, ssRNA.
:::
