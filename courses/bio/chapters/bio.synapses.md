:::why
An action potential cannot jump across the gap between one neurone and the next, or between a neurone and a muscle. A chemical messenger carries the signal, and this one idea explains how poisons, nerve gases, insecticides and medicines act on the body. At the end of the chain, a muscle fibre turns the signal into movement by sliding two kinds of protein filament past each other. Exam questions ask you to follow the chain from impulse to contraction one step at a time, so every step is a mark.
:::

## Synapses

A neurone ends in a swelling, the **presynaptic knob** (axon terminal). It does not touch the next cell: there is a gap of about 20 nm between them. The whole junction is a **synapse**. The signal must cross this gap, and it does so as a chemical.

:::def Synapse
A junction between two neurones, or between a neurone and an effector cell, where the signal passes across a narrow gap, the synaptic cleft.
:::

:::def Neurotransmitter
A chemical, made by a neurone and released at a synapse, that diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptors on the next cell to pass the signal on.
:::

:::def Cholinergic synapse
A synapse at which the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine (ACh).
:::

Compare the voltage-gated channels in [Neurones and action potentials](#bio.nerves), which open when the voltage changes.

### How an impulse crosses the synapse

:::steps
1. An **action potential** arrives at the presynaptic knob and depolarises the presynaptic membrane.
2. **Voltage-gated calcium ion channels open**. Calcium ions (Ca²⁺), which are at a higher concentration in the cleft than in the knob, **diffuse in**.
3. Calcium ions cause **vesicles** containing acetylcholine to move to the presynaptic membrane and **fuse** with it. ACh is released into the cleft by **exocytosis**.
4. ACh **diffuses across** the synaptic cleft.
5. ACh **binds to receptor proteins** on the postsynaptic membrane. This opens the **sodium ion channels**.
6. **Sodium ions diffuse in** through them, **depolarising** the postsynaptic membrane. If the **threshold** is reached, an **action potential** starts in the postsynaptic neurone.
7. **Acetylcholinesterase** hydrolyses ACh into choline and ethanoate (acetate), so the sodium channels close and the response ends. The choline is taken back into the presynaptic knob and used, with ATP from the mitochondria, to remake ACh, which is stored in new vesicles.
:::

Step 7 matters. If ACh stayed in the cleft, it would keep binding to the receptors and the postsynaptic neurone would be stimulated again and again by a single impulse. Removing it means that each impulse gives one brief signal.

:::key The marking points for synaptic transmission
- action potential arrives; **calcium channels open**; **Ca²⁺ diffuse in**
- **vesicles fuse** with the presynaptic membrane; ACh released by **exocytosis**
- ACh **diffuses** across the cleft and **binds to receptors** (not "enters the next neurone")
- **sodium channels open**; **Na⁺ diffuse in**; **depolarisation** and, if threshold is reached, an **action potential**
- **acetylcholinesterase** breaks down ACh, so the response stops
:::

:::eg Describe how an action potential in a presynaptic neurone leads to an action potential in the postsynaptic neurone at a cholinergic synapse.
Follow the chain in order; each step is a marking point.

The action potential arrives at the presynaptic knob, and voltage-gated calcium ion channels open. [B1]

Calcium ions diffuse into the knob, down their concentration gradient. [B1]

Vesicles containing acetylcholine fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release the acetylcholine into the cleft by exocytosis. [B1]

Acetylcholine diffuses across the synaptic cleft and binds to receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane. [B1]

Sodium ion channels open and sodium ions diffuse in, which depolarises the postsynaptic membrane. [B1]

If the threshold is reached, an action potential is generated in the postsynaptic neurone. [B1]

(Also credit: acetylcholinesterase hydrolyses ACh so the signal ends; the choline is reabsorbed and ACh is resynthesised using ATP from mitochondria.)

A weaker answer says "the nerve impulse jumps across the synapse and carries on in the next neurone". The impulse does not cross: it is a chemical signal that crosses, and the answer names no calcium, no acetylcholine and no receptors.
:::

:::warn The impulse does not "jump" the gap
An action potential cannot cross the synaptic cleft, because there is no membrane continuity and no local circuit across the gap. **ACh** crosses by diffusion. Also note: ACh binds to receptors on the **outside** of the postsynaptic membrane. It does not enter the postsynaptic neurone, and it is not the sodium ions that cross the cleft.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: explain how acetylcholine causes depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane. [3]

Line 1: Acetylcholine diffuses across the synaptic cleft.
Line 2: It enters the postsynaptic neurone through a carrier protein and causes depolarisation.

Correct Line 2: "It binds to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, which opens sodium ion channels, and sodium ions diffuse in." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.
:::

### What synapses are for

:::note Synapses do more than pass on a signal
- **One-way transmission.** Vesicles of neurotransmitter are only in the presynaptic knob, and receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane, so the signal can pass in one direction only.
- **Integration.** One neurone can receive signals from many others. If each gives a small depolarisation, the effects can be added together: this is **summation**. Together they may reach the threshold when no single one does.
- **Filtering.** A weak, occasional signal may be too small to reach the threshold, so it is not passed on.
- **Fatigue.** If a presynaptic neurone fires very often, the supply of vesicles can run out and the synapse stops passing on the signal for a time.
:::

:::eg Calcium ions are needed at the synapse. In an experiment on a synapse, the concentration of calcium ions in the solution around the presynaptic knob was changed and the depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane was measured after one impulse. Describe and explain the results.
The data are invented to show the pattern.

| calcium ions outside / mmol dm⁻³ | depolarisation / mV |
|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0 |
| 0.5 | 3 |
| 1.0 | 7 |
| 2.0 | 12 |
| 4.0 | 14 |

With no calcium ions the depolarisation is 0 mV, and it increases as the concentration rises, from 3 mV at 0.5 to 12 mV at 2.0 mmol dm⁻³. [B1]

Above 2.0 mmol dm⁻³ the increase is small (12 to 14 mV), so the effect levels off. [B1]

Calcium ions diffuse into the knob and make vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane, so with none, no acetylcholine is released and the postsynaptic membrane is not depolarised. [B1]

More calcium means more vesicles fuse and more acetylcholine is released, so more sodium channels open. It levels off when the receptors, or the supply of vesicles, are limiting. [B1]
:::

:::eg Real context: organophosphate insecticides stop the enzyme acetylcholinesterase working. An insect (or a farm worker) poisoned with one shows continuous muscle twitching. Explain why.
The enzyme cannot hydrolyse acetylcholine, so acetylcholine stays in the cleft of the neuromuscular junction. [B1]

It keeps binding to the receptors, so sodium ion channels stay open (or reopen) and the postsynaptic membrane is depolarised repeatedly. [B1]

Action potentials are generated again and again in the muscle fibre. [B1]

So the muscle contracts continuously and cannot relax, giving twitching and then paralysis. [B1]
:::

## The neuromuscular junction

A **neuromuscular junction** is a cholinergic synapse between a motor neurone and a muscle fibre. It works in the same steps as the synapse above. The postsynaptic membrane is the cell surface membrane of the muscle fibre, the **sarcolemma**, and the part directly under the knob is the **motor end plate**.

:::def Neuromuscular junction
A synapse between the end of a motor neurone and a muscle fibre. Acetylcholine released by the neurone binds to receptors on the muscle fibre's surface membrane (the motor end plate) and starts an action potential in the muscle fibre.
:::

| | Synapse between neurones | Neuromuscular junction |
|---|---|---|
| Postsynaptic cell | another neurone | a muscle fibre |
| Postsynaptic membrane | cell surface membrane of the dendrite or cell body | sarcolemma, folded into many **folds** (motor end plate) to give a larger surface area for receptors |
| Result of one impulse | may or may not reach the threshold (summation is possible) | **always** reaches the threshold: the muscle fibre gets an action potential |
| After depolarisation | may start an action potential in a neurone | action potential spreads over the sarcolemma and **down the T-tubules** |

A single action potential in a motor neurone releases enough acetylcholine to bring the sarcolemma to the threshold every time, so one impulse is always enough to stimulate a fibre.

A muscle is made of bundles of **muscle fibres**. A fibre is a single, very long cell that formed when many cells fused, so it is **multinucleate** (has many nuclei), with the nuclei near the surface. Its parts:

- The **sarcolemma** is the cell surface membrane. At the neuromuscular junction it receives acetylcholine.
- The **sarcoplasm** is the cytoplasm. It contains many **mitochondria** (to make ATP by aerobic respiration, close to the filaments) and the **myofibrils**.
- **Myofibrils** are long cylinders of protein filaments that run the length of the fibre. They are made of a repeating pattern of **sarcomeres** and give the striped appearance.
- **T-tubules** (transverse tubules) are narrow tubes formed by folds of the sarcolemma that pass deep into the fibre between the myofibrils.
- The **sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR)** is a network of flattened sacs, a specialised endoplasmic reticulum, lying against the T-tubules and round the myofibrils. It **stores calcium ions** and releases them when stimulated.

:::def Sarcoplasmic reticulum
The specialised endoplasmic reticulum of a muscle fibre. It stores calcium ions and releases them into the sarcoplasm when an action potential arrives, and takes them back by active transport afterwards.
:::

### From nerve impulse to calcium release

:::steps
1. An action potential in the motor neurone makes the neuromuscular junction release acetylcholine, which depolarises the **sarcolemma** to the threshold.
2. An action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and **down the T-tubules** into the fibre.
3. The T-tubules carry the depolarisation close to the **sarcoplasmic reticulum**. Calcium channels in the SR open and **calcium ions diffuse out** of the SR into the sarcoplasm, among the myofibrils.
:::

The T-tubules matter because a muscle fibre is wide. If the depolarisation travelled only over the surface, the filaments at the centre would be stimulated last. T-tubules carry the signal inside, so that **all the myofibrils contract together**.

## The ultrastructure of a sarcomere

The sarcomere is the unit of contraction.

:::def Sarcomere
The repeating unit of a myofibril, between two Z lines. It contains the thin (actin) and thick (myosin) filaments, and shortens when the muscle contracts.
:::

Two types of protein filament, both very small, are parallel to each other in a sarcomere.

- **Thin filaments** are made of **actin**. Each is two chains of actin molecules twisted together, with a long thin strand of **tropomyosin** lying in the groove, and small globular **troponin** molecules attached to it at intervals. Thin filaments are attached to the **Z lines** at the ends of the sarcomere.
- **Thick filaments** are made of **myosin**. Each myosin molecule has a long tail and a **head**. The tails are bundled together, and the heads project sideways. The head can bind to actin, and it also has a site that binds **ATP** and hydrolyses it. Thick filaments are held together in the middle, at the **M line**.

In an electron micrograph of a longitudinal section, the dense A bands are dark and the I bands light. Look for the thin dark Z line in the middle of each I band, and the paler H zone with the M line in the centre of each A band. Between the myofibrils you often see mitochondria. (The figures in this chapter are drawings: there are no photographs in this guide.)

### What changes when the muscle contracts

When a muscle contracts, each sarcomere gets shorter:

- the **I band** gets shorter,
- the **H zone** gets shorter (and may disappear),
- the **A band stays the same length**,
- the **Z lines move closer together**: the sarcomere is shorter.

This is evidence that the filaments themselves do not change length. The thin filaments slide inwards between the thick ones, so there is less of the sarcomere where only thin filaments (I band) or only thick filaments (H zone) are present.

:::eg The figures are measured on an electron micrograph with a magnification of ×20 000. In a relaxed myofibril one sarcomere is 50 mm long, its A band is 32 mm long and its H zone is 10 mm long. (b) Describe the changes between the relaxed and the contracted state. (c) Explain these changes.
**(a)** Actual size \(= \dfrac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}}\), so convert mm to µm first.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{length} &= \frac{50\,\mathrm{mm}}{20\,000} \\ &= 0.0025\,\mathrm{mm} \\ &= 2.5\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

The method is image size divided by magnification. [M1]

The answer is 2.5 µm. [A1]

**(b)** The sarcomere is shorter in the contracted myofibril (40 mm against 50 mm, a decrease of 20%). [B1]

The A band stays at 32 mm, but the H zone shortens from 10 mm to nothing, and the I band decreases from 18 mm to 8 mm. [B1]

**(c)** The thin (actin) filaments slide towards the centre of the sarcomere between the thick (myosin) filaments. [B1]

The filaments do not shorten: the A band, which is the length of the thick filaments, is unchanged. The overlap increases, so the H zone and I band are shorter. [B1]

(The I band is the sarcomere minus the A band: \(50 - 32 = 18\) mm and \(40 - 32 = 8\) mm. The percentage decrease is \(10 \div 50 \times 100 = 20\%\).)

## The sliding filament model

How do the thin filaments slide? Myosin heads pull on them, using energy from ATP. The sliding filament model describes this.

:::def Sliding filament model
The model of muscle contraction in which myosin heads bind to actin and tilt, pulling the thin filaments towards the centre of the sarcomere between the thick filaments, so that the filaments slide past each other and the sarcomere shortens. Neither type of filament changes length.
:::

### The thin filament at rest

In a relaxed muscle the sarcoplasm has a very low concentration of calcium ions (the SR has taken them up). On the thin filament, **tropomyosin** lies over the **myosin-binding sites** on the actin and **blocks** them, so the myosin heads cannot bind. The heads are attached to ADP and Pi after an earlier ATP hydrolysis: the head is "cocked", with its stored energy ready.

:::steps
1. **Calcium ions are released from the SR** and bind to **troponin**. Troponin changes shape and pulls the **tropomyosin** away from the binding sites on actin. The binding sites are uncovered.
2. A **myosin head** (with ADP and Pi attached) **binds** to a binding site on actin, forming a **cross-bridge**.
3. The head releases ADP and Pi and **tilts**, pulling the thin filament towards the M line. This is the **power stroke**: the actin slides past the myosin.
4. A molecule of **ATP binds** to the myosin head. This makes the head **detach** from actin.
5. The myosin head **hydrolyses the ATP** to ADP and Pi (myosin is an ATPase). The energy released **returns the head to its cocked position**.
6. If calcium ions are still bound to troponin, the head attaches to a binding site further along the actin and the cycle repeats. Many heads on many thick filaments do this many times each second, so the filaments slide a long way.
:::

When the nerve stops sending impulses, the SR takes up calcium ions again, by **active transport**, using ATP and calcium pumps. Troponin returns to its original shape, tropomyosin covers the binding sites again, no cross-bridges can form, and the muscle relaxes. The filaments are pulled back to their original positions as the muscle is stretched, usually by the contraction of an opposing muscle.

:::key The roles in the sliding filament model
| Component | Role |
|---|---|
| **Calcium ions** | bind to **troponin**; released from the SR when the fibre is stimulated |
| **Troponin** | binds Ca²⁺ and changes shape, which moves tropomyosin |
| **Tropomyosin** | covers the myosin-binding sites on actin at rest; moved away when Ca²⁺ bind troponin |
| **Myosin head** | binds to actin (cross-bridge), tilts in the power stroke, and hydrolyses ATP |
| **ATP** | binding to the head makes it **detach** from actin; its **hydrolysis gives the energy** to recock the head; also used to pump Ca²⁺ back into the SR |
:::

:::warn Where ATP works
ATP is **not** the energy for the power stroke itself. That energy comes from the cocked head, which was loaded by hydrolysing an earlier ATP. ATP binding is what **detaches** the head, and ATP hydrolysis **re-cocks** it. Also, **calcium ions bind to troponin, not to tropomyosin**, and neither the actin nor the myosin filaments get shorter.
:::

:::eg Real context: rigor mortis is the stiffening of muscles after death. Explain why muscles become stiff after death.

Without ATP the myosin heads cannot detach from the actin, because ATP binding is needed for detachment, so the cross-bridges stay in place. [B1]

The filaments are locked together, so the muscles cannot relax or be stretched and they are stiff. [B1]

Q: Name the enzyme in the synaptic cleft of a cholinergic synapse and state its function.
A: Acetylcholinesterase. It hydrolyses acetylcholine (into choline and ethanoate), so the response ends.
Q: Explain why the presynaptic knob contains many mitochondria.
A: Mitochondria make ATP by aerobic respiration. ATP is needed to make acetylcholine from choline, and for the movement of vesicles and their fusion with the membrane (and for removing calcium ions).
Q: Explain why transmission at a synapse is in one direction only.
A: Vesicles with neurotransmitter are only in the presynaptic knob, and the receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane. So the neurotransmitter can only pass from the presynaptic to the postsynaptic neurone.
Q: State the function of the T-tubules in a muscle fibre.
A: They carry the action potential from the sarcolemma into the fibre, close to the sarcoplasmic reticulum, so that the calcium ions are released throughout the fibre and all the myofibrils contract at the same time.
Q: State what happens to the A band, the I band and the H zone when a sarcomere contracts.
A: The A band stays the same length; the I band and the H zone get shorter.
Q: A sarcomere shortens from 2.6 µm to 2.1 µm. Calculate the percentage decrease in length.
A: \((2.6 - 2.1) \div 2.6 \times 100 = 19.2\%\), or 19% to 2 s.f.
Q: State two roles of ATP in muscle contraction.
A: Any two of: binding to the myosin head so that it detaches from actin; hydrolysis to give energy to return the head to its cocked position; active transport of calcium ions into the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
Q: Explain why a muscle fibre has many nuclei.
A: A fibre is a very long cell that formed by the fusion of many cells, so it keeps many nuclei. They provide the instructions to make the large amounts of protein (such as actin and myosin) that it needs.

:::recap
- A cholinergic synapse: the action potential opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels; Ca²⁺ diffuse in; vesicles fuse and release ACh; ACh diffuses across the cleft (about 20 nm), binds to receptors, and opens Na⁺ channels; the postsynaptic membrane depolarises and, at threshold, an action potential starts. Acetylcholinesterase removes ACh. Mitochondria make the ATP.
- Synapses give one-way transmission, allow summation and integration.
- The neuromuscular junction is a cholinergic synapse between a motor neurone and the sarcolemma (motor end plate); one impulse always stimulates the fibre.
- Muscle fibre: multinucleate, sarcolemma, T-tubules, sarcoplasmic reticulum (stores Ca²⁺), many mitochondria, myofibrils.
- Sarcomere (Z line to Z line): thin actin filaments (with tropomyosin and troponin) and thick myosin filaments. I band: actin only; A band: thick filaments; H zone: myosin only; M line: middle.
- In contraction the I band and H zone shorten, the A band stays the same, and the sarcomere shortens: the filaments slide, they do not shorten.
- Sliding filament: Ca²⁺ released from the SR bind troponin; tropomyosin moves; myosin heads bind actin (cross-bridge); power stroke; ATP binds and the head detaches; ATP hydrolysis recocks it. Ca²⁺ are pumped back into the SR (active transport, ATP) and the muscle relaxes. No ATP means rigor.
