:::why
Paper 2 and Paper 4 together are 160 marks, more than every other paper added together. Students rarely lose these marks because they have never met the biology. They lose them because the answer does not match what the examiner is looking for: it explains when it should describe, it repeats the question, it leaves out the units, or it is vague where a precise term was needed. The biology chapters teach the content. This guide teaches the skill of turning that content into marks.
:::

## The two papers

| | Paper 2 | Paper 4 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | AS Level Structured Questions | A Level Structured Questions |
| Content | AS content, topics 1 to 11 | A Level content, topics 1 to 19; AS knowledge is required |
| Time | 1 h 15 min | 2 h |
| Marks | 60 | 100 |
| Weight | 46% of AS, 23% of A Level | 38.5% of A Level |
| Marks go to | AO1 50%, AO2 50% | AO1 50%, AO2 50% |

On both papers you write on the question paper, in black or dark blue pen, and use an HB pencil for diagrams and graphs. The front page says: "You should show all your working and use appropriate units." The marks for each part are printed in brackets [ ]. You may use a calculator, and **there is no data booklet or formula sheet**. When a formula is needed (A Level statistics, Hardy–Weinberg, Simpson's index), it is printed inside the question. Degrees of freedom are never given.

To see what the papers are like, look at the question counts in the real June 2024 papers: Paper 2 had 6 questions worth 14, 12, 6, 17, 5 and 6 marks (60 in all), and Paper 4 had 10 questions worth 8, 9, 11, 11, 10, 11, 9, 15, 8 and 8 marks (100 in all). Each question starts with a context and then breaks into parts, (a), (b), (c), with the marks beside them. Some parts are one mark, some are six.

:::warn Both papers can ask anything
Paper 2 questions mix topics freely, and Paper 4 is not only A Level content: AS knowledge is required, so a Paper 4 question on gene therapy may need the structure of DNA, and a question on respiration may need membranes and enzymes. See "Synoptic questions" below.
:::

## How long is a mark?

\(75 \div 60 = 1.25\) minutes per mark in Paper 2, and \(120 \div 100 = 1.2\) minutes per mark in Paper 4. That is 75 seconds and 72 seconds. The number of marks beside a part tells you how long to spend on it and how much to write.

| Marks | Paper 2 (1.25 min per mark) | Paper 4 (1.2 min per mark) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.25 min | 1.2 min |
| 2 | 2.5 min | 2.4 min |
| 3 | 3.75 min | 3.6 min |
| 4 | 5 min | 4.8 min |
| 6 | 7.5 min | 7.2 min |
| 8 | 10 min | 9.6 min |

Plan to finish five minutes early to check your answers and fill in any blanks. That leaves 70 minutes for 60 marks in Paper 2 (\(70 \times 60 \div 60 = 70\) seconds a mark) and 115 minutes for 100 marks in Paper 4 (\(115 \times 60 \div 100 = 69\) seconds a mark). Both are about 70 seconds. If a part has taken twice its time, leave it, put a mark beside it and come back.

:::exam Read the whole question first
Read all the parts of a question before you write the first. Later parts often give data that helps an earlier one, and a part such as "(c) suggest why …" tells you what the context is really about. Do not write the answer to part (b) inside part (a): you will not be given the mark twice, and you will have nothing left to write.
:::

## Command words

The first word of a question tells you what kind of answer is wanted. Cambridge uses seventeen command words in Biology, with these meanings (syllabus page 50).

| Command word | Meaning | The answer must |
|---|---|---|
| **define** | give the precise meaning | match a textbook definition; every part counts |
| **state** | express in clear terms | be short and clear, with no explanation |
| **give** | produce an answer from a given source or recall | be a name, a value or an example, taken from the information or from memory |
| **identify** | name, select or recognise | name the thing; choose from the data or the diagram |
| **describe** | state the points of a topic; give characteristics and main features | say what happens or what it looks like, with data if there is a graph or table |
| **outline** | set out the main points | give the main points only, briefly |
| **compare** | identify or comment on similarities and/or differences | give both sides, using a linking word such as "whereas" |
| **contrast** | identify or comment on differences | give only differences, both sides each time |
| **explain** | set out purposes or reasons; make the relationships between things evident; provide why and/or how and support with relevant evidence | give a reason with a biological link: "because", "so that", "which causes" |
| **suggest** | apply knowledge and understanding to situations where there are a range of valid responses in order to make proposals or put forward considerations | use your biology on an unfamiliar situation; several answers can score |
| **predict** | suggest what may happen based on available information | say what will happen, and usually why |
| **determine** | establish an answer using the information available | work it out from the data, with working |
| **discuss** | write about issue(s) or topic(s) in depth in a structured way | give points on more than one side and weigh them up |
| **assess** | make an informed judgement | use evidence for and against, and give a judgement |
| **comment** | give an informed opinion | give a reasoned view, often on data or on someone's conclusion |
| **calculate** | work out from given facts, figures or information | show working and give units |
| **sketch** | make a simple freehand drawing showing the key features, taking care over proportions | have labelled axes, the right shape and the key values, without needing exact plotting |

Below is a short original question for each word, with a model answer. Read the answer and then notice the two things it is doing: how many points it has (one per mark) and what kind of point they are. In the answers, each marking point ends with the token for a mark.

### Words that ask for recall

:::try Define, state, give and identify
Q: [2 marks] **Define** the term *osmosis*.
A: Osmosis is the net movement of water (molecules) from a region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential [B1] through a partially permeable membrane [B1]. A definition needs every part: "water moving from a weak solution to a strong solution" is too vague, and "through a membrane" without "partially permeable" loses the second mark.

Q: [1 mark] **State** the name of the part of the heart that acts as the pacemaker.
A: The sinoatrial node (SAN) [B1]. No explanation is wanted; adding "because it starts the heartbeat" does no harm but earns nothing more.

Q: [1 mark] **Give** one example of a polysaccharide that is used as a storage compound in animals.
A: Glycogen [B1].

Q: [1 mark] A section through a root shows a ring of cells with a waterproof band of suberin in their walls (the Casparian strip) surrounding the vascular tissue. **Identify** these cells.
A: Endodermis (cells) [B1].
:::

### Words that ask you to say more

:::try Describe, outline, compare, contrast and explain
Q: [3 marks] **Describe** the structure of a collagen molecule.
A: It is made of three polypeptide chains [B1] wound around each other in a triple helix [B1]. Every third amino acid is glycine, which is small, so the chains can pack closely together [B1]. (Also credit: hydrogen bonds between the chains; covalent cross-links between the molecules.) The word "describe" asks what the structure is, and it does not ask why collagen is strong.

Q: [3 marks] **Outline** how a vaccine protects a person against a disease.
A: The vaccine contains antigens (or a weakened or killed pathogen) from the pathogen [B1]. B-lymphocytes with receptors complementary to the antigen divide and form plasma cells and memory cells [B1]. If the real pathogen enters later, the memory cells respond faster, so the antibody level rises quickly and the pathogen is destroyed before disease develops [B1]. Three marks need three short points, and no long detail on each.

Q: [2 marks] **Compare** the structure of DNA with that of messenger RNA (mRNA).
A: DNA has two polynucleotide strands whereas mRNA has one [B1]. DNA contains deoxyribose and the base thymine whereas mRNA contains ribose and the base uracil [B1]. (Also credit: both have a sugar–phosphate backbone with phosphodiester bonds.) Each point has two sides joined by "whereas": a comparison must have both halves.

Q: [2 marks] **Contrast** active transport with facilitated diffusion.
A: Active transport needs energy from ATP whereas facilitated diffusion does not [B1]. Active transport moves substances against the concentration gradient (from low to high) whereas facilitated diffusion moves them down the gradient [B1]. A contrast gives only differences. "Both use carrier proteins" is a similarity and earns nothing here.

Q: [3 marks] **Explain** why an enzyme with an optimum pH of 7 has almost no activity at pH 2.
A: The high concentration of hydrogen ions at pH 2 changes the charges on the R groups of the amino acids, so ionic and hydrogen bonds break [B1]. This changes the tertiary structure, so the shape of the active site changes [B1]. The substrate is no longer complementary to the active site, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form [B1]. Each mark is a link in a chain: cause → bonds → shape → fewer complexes.
:::

### Words that ask you to apply, judge or give a view

:::try Suggest, predict, determine, discuss, assess and comment
Q: [2 marks] The cells that line the small intestine have many mitochondria. **Suggest** why.
A: The cells absorb glucose and amino acids by active transport, which needs ATP [B1]. The ATP is made by aerobic respiration in the mitochondria [B1]. A "suggest" question has a context you have not seen before, so the mark scheme is more open: any sensible use of the biology scores.

Q: [2 marks] A cylinder of potato is weighed, left in distilled water for one hour, and weighed again. **Predict** what will happen to its mass, and say why.
A: The mass increases [B1] because water enters the cells by osmosis, since the water potential of the cells is lower than that of the distilled water [B1].

Q: [2 marks] The template strand of part of a gene has the base sequence 3′-TAC GGA-5′. **Determine** the base sequence of the mRNA transcribed from it.
A: Complementary base pairs are used, with uracil in place of thymine [B1]. The mRNA is 5′-AUG CCU-3′ [B1].

Q: [4 marks] **Discuss** the use of antibiotics to treat bacterial infections.
A: Antibiotics kill bacteria (or stop their growth) without harming human cells, so infections that were once fatal can be cured [B1]. Antibiotics are used correctly only against bacteria, since they have no effect on viruses, so they must not be used for colds or influenza [B1]. Overuse and not finishing a course allow resistant bacteria to survive and reproduce, so the antibiotic becomes less useful [B1]. Therefore doctors prescribe them only when needed, and patients should complete the course [B1]. A "discuss" answer has benefits, problems and a balanced ending.

Q: [3 marks] In a trial, 1000 people were vaccinated and 2 of them caught the disease. Of 1000 people who were not vaccinated, 80 caught it. **Assess** how effective the vaccine is.
A: In the vaccinated group 0.2% caught the disease compared with 8% of the unvaccinated group [B1]. The vaccine reduced the number of cases by \(\dfrac{80 - 2}{80} \times 100 = 97.5\%\) [B1]. It is very effective but not perfect, because 2 vaccinated people still caught the disease [B1]. An "assess" answer uses the evidence and ends with a judgement.

Q: [2 marks] A student grew three plants with fertiliser and three without. The mean height was 12.4 cm with fertiliser and 11.9 cm without. The student concludes: "Fertiliser makes plants taller." **Comment** on this conclusion.
A: The difference is only 0.5 cm (about 4% of 11.9 cm), which is small compared with the variation expected between plants, and no range or standard deviation is given [B1]. Only three plants were used in each group, which is too few to be confident, so the conclusion is not reliable [B1].
:::

### Words that ask you to work or draw

:::try Calculate and sketch
Q: [2 marks] A red blood cell has a diameter of 7.5 µm. In a drawing its diameter is 30 mm. **Calculate** the magnification of the drawing.
A: Convert to the same unit: 30 mm = 30 000 µm [M1]. Magnification \(= 30\,000 \div 7.5 = 4000\) (×4000) [A1].

Q: [2 marks] **Sketch** a graph, in the space provided, to show the effect of temperature on the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction.
A: Axes labelled: temperature / °C on the horizontal axis and rate of reaction on the vertical axis [B1]. A curve that rises from near the origin to a peak at the optimum and then falls steeply to zero [B1]. A sketch needs the right shape and labels, and not plotted points.
:::

## How marking points work

A mark scheme is a list of **marking points**. Each point is one idea that earns one mark, and the scheme often lists more points than the marks available ("any 3 from 5"). This is what that means for the way you write.

- **One idea, one mark.** A long answer that says the same thing three ways gets one mark. Move on to the next point.
- **Precise terms.** The scheme uses the biological word, so use it: "active site", not "the bit where the substrate goes"; "complementary", not "the same shape"; "partially permeable membrane", the syllabus term; "water potential gradient", not "water concentration".
- **No credit for repeating the stem.** If the question says "explain why the rate falls", the sentence "the rate falls" earns nothing. A statement that is already in the question is not a new point.
- **Contradictions cancel.** If you write a correct point and, in the same answer, a statement that contradicts it ("the enzyme is denatured, because it is killed"), the examiner cannot give the credit.
- **Alternatives and "ignore" or "reject".** A scheme may list several accepted answers with a slash, and some wrong but common wordings are marked "reject". A wrong statement that is merely extra does not lose marks unless it contradicts a creditworthy one.
- **Error carried forward (ecf).** If a calculation goes wrong at one step but the later method is right, you still earn the method and the later marks that follow from your own value. That only works if you **show the working**.

:::eg Explain how the structure of a phospholipid molecule leads to the formation of a bilayer in water.
A phospholipid has a **hydrophilic (polar) phosphate head** and two **hydrophobic (non-polar) fatty acid tails**. [B1]

In water, the heads face the water and form hydrogen bonds with it (interact with it), while the tails are pushed away from the water. [B1]

The molecules therefore arrange themselves in two layers with the tails facing each other in the middle, away from the water, and the heads on the two outer surfaces facing the water on each side: a bilayer. [B1]

(Also credit: the bilayer is held together by hydrophobic interactions between the tails; a bilayer is stable and acts as a barrier to polar molecules and ions.)

Each paragraph holds a different idea. A weaker answer says "the heads like water and the tails do not like water" and then repeats it as "so they go in a bilayer". It uses non-technical words and describes only the first idea, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

:::exam Spend your words on new points
If a question is worth 3 marks, plan 3 distinct ideas. Do not spend a whole sentence rewriting the question. After you write each sentence, ask: "Is this a different mark from my last one?" If not, stop and move on.
:::

## Describing graphs and tables

To **describe** is to say what the data show; to **explain** is to say why. The two are marked separately, and most students blur them. For a graph or table, describe in this order:

:::steps
1. **The overall trend**: increases, decreases, rises then falls, no change.
2. **Figures with units**, read from both axes: "from 7 µmol min⁻¹ at 20 °C to 14 µmol min⁻¹ at 40 °C". At least two paired values, or the start and end.
3. **Turning points and plateaus**: where the maximum or minimum is, and where the rate becomes constant.
4. **Changes of rate**: steeper or shallower, with figures if you can ("the rate falls more steeply than it rose").
5. **A calculation** if there is one to make: a difference, a percentage change or a ratio.
:::

:::plot Rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction at different temperatures (invented data): the rate rises to 14 µmol min⁻¹ at 40 °C, then falls to zero at 60 °C.
--f "0.0004375*x**2*(60-x)+0*sqrt(60-x)" --x 0 100 --xstep 20 --y 0 20 --xlabel "temperature / °C" --ylabel "rate / µmol min⁻¹" --label "rate of reaction" --pt "40,14,maximum 14 at 40 °C@ne" --pt "20,7,7 at 20 °C@nw" --legend tr --alt "The rate rises from zero at 0 °C to 7 µmol per minute at 20 °C and a maximum of 14 at 40 °C, then falls steeply to zero at 60 °C."
:::

For this graph, a **description** of the whole curve would be: "As temperature rises from 0 to 40 °C, the rate increases, from 0 to 14 µmol min⁻¹ (7 µmol min⁻¹ at 20 °C), reaching a maximum at 40 °C. Above 40 °C the rate falls, more steeply than it rose, to 0 µmol min⁻¹ at 60 °C." Nothing in it says why. The **explanation** is separate: more kinetic energy and more collisions up to the optimum, and then the breaking of the hydrogen and ionic bonds of the tertiary structure, so that the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured.

:::eg Spot the weak answer: describe the change in rate between 40 °C and 60 °C in the graph above, and explain it. [4]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: The rate falls quickly after 40 °C.
Line 2: This is because the heat kills the enzyme.
Line 3: The active site changes shape so the substrate no longer fits.

Line 1 is correct as far as it goes [B1]. **But a describe mark also needs figures with units: the rate falls from 14 µmol min⁻¹ at 40 °C to 0 at 60 °C. Without them the description earns one mark and not two.** **Line 2 is wrong: an enzyme is a protein and is not alive, so it cannot be killed; the heat breaks hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds and denatures it. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

The answer scores 2 of the 4 marks. With the data added to Line 1 and "the heat breaks the hydrogen and ionic bonds, which denatures the enzyme" for Line 2, it scores 4.
:::

## Extended answers

Some parts are worth 5 or 6 marks and have a lot of lines. They are marked in the same way as short ones: one marking point per mark. The risk is not that you know too little but that your points are buried in a long paragraph.

:::steps
1. **Underline the command word and the number of marks.** "Explain" and 6 marks means six separate reasons.
2. **Jot a plan** in the margin: six or seven key terms or steps, in order.
3. **Write one point per sentence**, using the key term in each. Order the points as a chain of cause and effect.
4. **Use the context.** If the question is about a named organism, tissue or experiment, refer to it ("in the xylem vessels …"), because a general answer scores less.
5. **Count.** Check you have as many different points as marks, and add one more if you have space.
:::

:::tip Make every point easy to find
Marks go to ideas, not to neat paragraphs. A clear answer is still easier to mark, because the examiner can find every point. Short sentences, one idea in each, are best, and a point hidden at the end of a long sentence can be missed.
:::

## Calculations

Calculation parts are worth 2 to 4 marks, and nearly always have **M** marks for the method and **A** marks for the answer. Follow these rules.

- **Write every step.** If the final number is wrong, the method still scores.
- **Use the same unit throughout** (convert µm to mm, or the other way, before you divide).
- **Give units** in the answer. The front page says to use appropriate units, and an answer without them may lose the last mark.
- **Significant figures**: use the same number as the data, or one more. Do not round in the middle of the calculation. Round only at the end.
- **Check the size.** A magnification of ×0.004 for a microscope drawing is not sensible.

:::eg A student collected 24 cm³ of oxygen from a catalase reaction in 80 s. Calculate the mean rate of reaction in cm³ s⁻¹, and then the time needed to collect 60 cm³ at this rate.
The rate is the volume divided by the time.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{rate} &= \frac{24}{80} \\ &= 0.30\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

Now divide the new volume by the rate.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{time} &= \frac{60}{0.30} \\ &= 200\,\mathrm{s} \end{aligned}\]

[A1]

Suppose a student wrote \(24 \div 80 = 0.03\). The first answer is wrong (so the A mark is lost), but the method is right (M1), and \(60 \div 0.03 = 2000\) s follows correctly from that value, so the last A mark is given by error carried forward. This is the reason to show the working: it is the only way to earn marks for what is right.
:::

## Drawing and labelling in the answer space

Some parts ask you to draw a diagram, label a diagram or draw a graph. Use an HB pencil, which the front page allows for diagrams and graphs.

- **Use most of the space**, with a sharp pencil and clean lines. Do not shade.
- **Label with ruled lines** that touch the structure, never crossing each other. Use words, not just letters.
- **Annotate** if the question says "annotate": a short note on a function or feature, beside the label.
- **Graphs**: independent variable on the x-axis, labelled axes with units, a scale that fills more than half the grid, and a smooth curve or ruled lines.

The detailed rules for drawings and graphs are in [Practical skills: microscopy and drawing (Paper 3)](#bio.practical2) and [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical).

## Synoptic questions in Paper 4

Paper 4 is about A Level content, but it assumes the whole AS course. A synoptic question puts AS knowledge into an A Level context, so the marks come from combining them. Here are the common pairings.

| A Level topic | AS knowledge it uses |
|---|---|
| respiration (ATP synthase, the electron transport chain) | membranes, proton gradients, enzymes and coenzymes, mitochondrion structure |
| photosynthesis | enzymes (rubisco, temperature and pH), chloroplast structure, diffusion |
| the kidney, osmoregulation | water potential, osmosis, blood vessels and plasma, cell membranes |
| nerves and synapses | membrane structure, channel proteins, facilitated diffusion, active transport |
| inheritance, gene control | DNA structure, protein synthesis, mutation, meiosis |
| selection and evolution | antibiotic resistance, mutation, variation, DNA |
| genetic technology | restriction enzymes, DNA polymerase, enzyme and temperature effects (PCR), protein synthesis |
| classification, conservation | cell structure, DNA, biodiversity and sampling |

When you meet an unfamiliar A Level context, ask "which AS idea is this?". A question about a new cell-signalling hormone is, underneath, a question on receptors and the fluid mosaic model. Revise AS content in the last weeks as well: it is assumed.

## A full Paper 2 style question (8 marks)

Try this without the answer first, in 10 minutes. Then mark yourself with the mark scheme.

:::try Paper 2 style: uptake of nitrate by root cells
Q: [8 marks] Cells in the roots of a plant take up nitrate ions from the soil solution. In an investigation (invented data) pieces of root were placed in a solution containing nitrate ions. The concentration of oxygen in the solution was varied, and the uptake of nitrate ions was measured.

| oxygen in solution / % | nitrate uptake / µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| 0 | 1.0 |
| 5 | 5.0 |
| 10 | 9.0 |
| 20 | 13.0 |
| 30 | 13.0 |
| 40 | 13.0 |

(a) Describe the effect of the oxygen concentration on the uptake of nitrate ions. [2]

(b) Explain why the uptake of nitrate ions increases as the oxygen concentration increases. [3]

(c) Suggest why the uptake does not increase above an oxygen concentration of 20%. [1]

(d) Calculate the percentage increase in uptake when the oxygen concentration rises from 5% to 10%. [2]
H: For (a) quote figures with units from the first and the last rows of the part of the table that changes.
H: For (b) go from oxygen to a process to ATP to a type of transport.
H: For (d) use \((\text{new} - \text{old}) \div \text{old} \times 100\).
A: (a) As the oxygen concentration increases from 0 to 20%, the uptake increases, from 1.0 to 13.0 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹ [B1]. Above 20% oxygen the uptake stays constant at 13.0 µmol g⁻¹ h⁻¹ [B1].

(b) Oxygen is needed for aerobic respiration, so more oxygen means more ATP is made (in the mitochondria) [B1]. Nitrate ions are taken up by active transport, against a concentration gradient, which needs ATP [B1]. Carrier proteins use the energy from ATP to change shape and move the ions, so more ATP means a higher rate of uptake [B1].

(c) All the carrier proteins (transport proteins) are working at their maximum rate, or are saturated, so the number of carrier proteins is now the limiting factor [B1]. (Also credit: ATP is no longer limiting.)

(d) Change in uptake \(= 9.0 - 5.0 = 4.0\); percentage increase \(= \dfrac{4.0}{5.0} \times 100 = 80\%\) [M1] [A1]
:::

## A full Paper 4 style question (10 marks)

This question is synoptic: it uses the Hardy–Weinberg principle (A Level) with mutation and enzymes (AS). Time yourself: 12 minutes.

:::try Paper 4 style: an inherited enzyme deficiency
Q: [10 marks] An inherited disorder (invented) is caused by a recessive allele of a gene that codes for an enzyme. In a population of 40 000 people, 4 have the disorder. The Hardy–Weinberg equations are \(p + q = 1\) and \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\), where \(p\) is the frequency of the dominant allele and \(q\) is the frequency of the recessive allele.

(a) Calculate the frequency of the recessive allele, and the number of people in the population expected to be carriers of the allele. [4]

(b) State two assumptions that must be true before the Hardy–Weinberg equation can be used. [2]

(c) Explain how a mutation in the gene could result in an enzyme that does not work. [3]

(d) People with the disorder rarely have children, but the allele is not lost from the population. Explain why. [1]
H: In (a), the people with the disorder have the genotype \(qq\), so their frequency is \(q^2\).
H: In (c), follow the chain from DNA to the active site.
A: (a) \(q^2 = \dfrac{4}{40\,000} = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\) [M1] so \(q = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{-4}} = 0.010\) [A1]. Then \(p = 1 - 0.010 = 0.99\), and the frequency of carriers is \(2pq = 2 \times 0.99 \times 0.010 = 0.0198\), so the number of carriers is \(0.0198 \times 40\,000 = 792\) [M1] [A1].

(b) Any two from: random mating; no (or very small) mutation rate; no selection against either allele (or no selection); no migration (immigration or emigration); a large population. [B1] [B1]

(c) A change (substitution, insertion or deletion) in the base sequence of the gene changes the sequence of codons in the mRNA, and so the sequence of amino acids (the primary structure) [B1]. This changes the bonds (hydrogen, ionic, disulfide) that fold the polypeptide, so the tertiary structure and the shape of the active site change [B1]. The substrate is no longer complementary to the active site, so no enzyme–substrate complexes form and the reaction is not catalysed [B1].

(d) Heterozygous people (carriers) have one copy of the recessive allele but are not affected, so they survive, reproduce and pass the allele to the next generation [B1].
:::

## Putting it together: your routine

:::steps
1. **Before the paper**: practise with real questions by topic first, then with whole timed papers. Learn the command word table until you can say the meaning of each word without looking.
2. **In the paper**: read the whole question, underline the command word and the mark count, and look at the context. Write one point per mark.
3. **After each practice**: mark your answers with the mark scheme, not with your own sense of "I said something like that". If the scheme has a word you did not use, write the precise term in a notebook.
4. **Look for patterns**: do you lose marks by describing when you must explain, by missing units, by vague terms or by repeating the question? Fix the pattern that costs most.
:::
