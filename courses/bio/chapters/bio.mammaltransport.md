The blood vessels, the blood and the tissue fluid together carry each substance to within a fraction of a millimetre of every cell. :::try Warm-up
Q: Explain why a large animal cannot rely on diffusion across its body surface to supply all its cells. (from [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement))
A: A large animal has a small surface area to volume ratio, and the distance from the surface to the middle is long, so diffusion is far too slow. It needs exchange surfaces and a transport system (the blood).
Q: Name the property of water that lets glucose, ions and urea travel dissolved in blood. (from [Lipids and water](#bio.lipids))
A: Water is a solvent. Its polar molecules are attracted to ions and to polar molecules such as glucose, so these dissolve.
Q: A cell has a water potential of −400 kPa and is surrounded by a solution of −200 kPa. In which direction is there a net movement of water? (from [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement))
A: Into the cell. Water moves by osmosis from the higher (less negative) water potential, −200 kPa, to the lower (more negative) one, −400 kPa.
:::

## The closed double circulation

A mammal's blood is pumped around the body by the heart and travels inside tubes called blood vessels. Biologists describe this arrangement with two key words, **closed** and **double**.

:::def Closed circulatory system
A circulatory system in which the blood stays inside blood vessels all the time. Substances pass between the blood and the cells through the thin walls of the capillaries, not because the blood leaves the vessels.
:::

In an open circulatory system, such as an insect's, the blood is pumped out of vessels into the body cavity and bathes the organs directly. A closed system can keep the blood at higher pressure, can send more blood to the organs that need it and can move it faster, which suits a large, active animal.

:::def Double circulation
A circulation in which the blood passes through the heart twice in every complete circuit of the body: once on the way to the lungs (the pulmonary circulation) and once on the way to the rest of the body (the systemic circulation).
:::

A fish has a **single** circulation: the heart pumps blood to the gills, and from the gills it flows on to the body without going back to the heart. The blood has lost most of its pressure by the time it leaves the gills. A mammal's heart avoids that problem.

Follow the two loops in the figure.

:::steps
1. **Pulmonary circulation.** The right ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood along the **pulmonary artery** to the lungs. In the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses carbon dioxide. The oxygenated blood returns along the **pulmonary vein** to the left atrium.
2. **Systemic circulation.** The left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood into the **aorta**. Arteries carry it to every organ except the lungs. In the capillaries of the organs, oxygen and nutrients leave the blood and carbon dioxide and wastes enter it. The deoxygenated blood returns along the **vena cava** to the right atrium.
:::

| Vessel | Carries blood from → to | Oxygen content |
|---|---|---|
| pulmonary artery | right ventricle → lungs | deoxygenated |
| pulmonary vein | lungs → left atrium | oxygenated |
| aorta | left ventricle → body (all organs except the lungs) | oxygenated |
| vena cava | body → right atrium | deoxygenated |

:::key Why a double circulation?
- The blood loses pressure as it squeezes through the narrow capillaries of the lungs, so it returns to the heart at **low pressure**.
- The heart pumps it again, so the blood enters the **systemic** circulation at **high pressure**. High pressure carries blood quickly to the whole body and drives tissue fluid out of the capillaries (see below).
- The lungs, which have delicate thin-walled capillaries, receive blood at a **lower pressure** than the body, which avoids damaging them.
- A double circulation also keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart, so tissues receive fully oxygenated blood.
:::

:::warn Arteries are not "the oxygenated vessels"
Arteries carry blood **away from the heart** and veins carry blood **towards the heart**. Oxygen content does not decide which is which. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood and the pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood. In an answer, name the vessel and say where it goes: "the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs".
:::

## The types of blood vessel

On its way round, the blood passes through five kinds of vessel in order:

**artery → arteriole → capillary → venule → vein**

Arteries branch into smaller **arterioles**. Arterioles branch into a network of **capillaries** in every tissue. Capillaries join to form **venules**, and venules join to form **veins**. The walls of arteries, arterioles, venules and veins are built from three layers, and capillaries have only the first.

| Layer | Made of | Job |
|---|---|---|
| tunica intima (inner) | a single layer of flat **endothelium** cells on a thin elastic layer | lines the lumen; smooth surface so blood flows with little friction |
| tunica media (middle) | **smooth muscle** and **elastic fibres** | withstands pressure; stretches and recoils; narrows or widens the vessel |
| tunica externa (outer) | **collagen** fibres (and some elastic fibres) | strong, stops the vessel from bursting and holds it in place |

The **lumen** is the space in the middle through which the blood flows. The walls differ in the amount of each layer, and that difference is the key to every question on this topic.

### Elastic arteries

:::def Elastic artery
A large artery close to the heart, such as the aorta and the pulmonary artery, with a tunica media containing a great deal of elastic tissue. Its wall stretches as the ventricle pumps blood in and recoils afterwards.
:::

The heart pumps in pulses. Each contraction of the left ventricle sends a surge of blood into the aorta at high pressure, and between contractions the pressure falls.
- The **thick wall**, with large amounts of **collagen** in the tunica externa, withstands the high pressure without bursting.
- The large amount of **elastic tissue** lets the wall **stretch** when the pulse of blood arrives and then **recoil** inwards when the heart relaxes. The recoil squeezes the blood onwards.
- Because of this stretch and recoil, the pressure and flow **even out** into a smoother, nearly continuous flow along the artery, instead of stopping and starting with the heart. You can feel the stretch as a pulse at your wrist.

### Muscular arteries and arterioles

:::def Muscular artery
An artery further from the heart, with a tunica media that contains more smooth muscle and less elastic tissue than an elastic artery. It carries blood to a particular organ.
:::

The arteries that branch off the aorta towards the gut, kidneys and legs are muscular arteries. Their smooth muscle can **contract**, which narrows the lumen (vasoconstriction), or **relax**, which widens it (vasodilation). That changes how much blood each organ receives. Arterioles are even narrower, and their muscle controls the flow of blood into each capillary network: during exercise the arterioles in your leg muscles widen while the arterioles of your gut narrow, so more of the blood goes where oxygen is needed most.

:::note Muscular and elastic arteries share the plan
Both have a thick wall and a narrow lumen compared with a vein of the same size. The difference between them is the **proportion**: more elastic tissue in the aorta, more smooth muscle in the artery to a gut or a leg.
:::

### Capillaries

:::def Capillary
The smallest blood vessel, with a wall of a single layer of endothelium cells. A capillary has no muscle or elastic tissue. Substances are exchanged between the blood and the tissues through its wall.
:::

Every part of the body is close to a capillary, and the capillaries of a tissue make a dense network, the **capillary bed**. Each feature of the capillary suits exchange:
- The wall is **one cell thick**, so the **diffusion distance** between blood and cells is tiny and diffusion is fast.
- The **lumen is narrow**, about 7 µm, just wide enough for a red blood cell to squeeze through in single file. The red cell is pressed against the wall and moves slowly, so there is more time for oxygen to diffuse out.
- There are **gaps between the endothelial cells** in many capillaries, so water and small dissolved molecules can pass through. Cells and large proteins cannot.
- There are **very many** capillaries. Together they have a very large surface area, and the blood in all of them together moves much more slowly than in the aorta, which gives time for exchange.

### Venules and veins

:::def Vein
A vessel that carries blood towards the heart. A vein has a thin wall with little smooth muscle and little elastic tissue, a wide lumen and valves.
:::

By the time the blood has passed through the capillaries, its pressure is low. A vein therefore does not need a thick wall.
- The **wall is thin**, with few elastic fibres, because the blood pressure is low and there is no pulse to absorb.
- The **lumen is wide**, so blood meets little resistance as it flows.
- **Valves** (pocket valves, folds of the inner lining) prevent the blood flowing backwards. Blood can pass towards the heart but the valve fills and closes if it begins to move the wrong way.
- Many veins run between **skeletal muscles**. When the muscles contract they squeeze the veins, which pushes the blood on towards the heart (the valves stop it moving back). That is why blood pools in the legs if you stand still for a long time.

:::key Structure and function of the vessels
| Vessel | Wall | Lumen | Special feature | Why it suits the job |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| elastic artery | very thick; much elastic tissue and collagen | narrow | stretches and recoils | withstands high pressure; evens out the pulse |
| muscular artery | thick; more smooth muscle | narrow | muscle contracts or relaxes | controls the blood flow to an organ |
| capillary | one layer of endothelium | about 7 µm | gaps between cells | short diffusion distance for exchange |
| vein | thin; little muscle and elastic tissue | wide | valves | low pressure, no stretch needed; valves stop backflow |
:::

:::eg Compare the structure of an artery and a vein.
This is a typical "compare" question. A comparison needs both vessels in each point, so a table-style answer works. Each line below is a point in its own right.

The wall of an artery is thicker than the wall of a vein, because it has more smooth muscle, elastic fibres and collagen. [B1]

The lumen of an artery is narrower than that of a vein (for a vessel of the same size, the artery has a narrow lumen and the vein a wide one). [B1]

An artery has a lot of elastic tissue in its wall so that it stretches and recoils, which evens out the pulse; a vein has very little. [B1]

A vein has valves to stop the blood flowing backwards, but arteries do not have valves along their length. [B1]

An artery carries blood away from the heart at high pressure, but a vein carries blood towards the heart at low pressure. [B1]

(Also credit: both are lined with a single layer of endothelium; arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery.)

A weaker answer says "arteries are thick and veins are thin" and stops. That is one point at most: it does not say which layers make the wall thick, and it gives no reason. A comparison must name **both** vessels in each point, for example "an artery has a thick wall, whereas a vein has a thin wall".
:::

### Seeing a vessel under a microscope

You will be asked to recognise vessels on a slide, a photomicrograph or an electron micrograph. A photomicrograph is a picture taken through a light microscope; an electron micrograph is a picture from an electron microscope. This guide contains drawings, not photographs, so learn what the real picture looks like:
- An **artery** in transverse section is a round ring. The wall is thick and often wavy because the elastic layer has recoiled. The lumen is a clear, round, relatively small hole, and it may be empty.
- A **vein** in transverse section is a larger, floppy and often flattened or irregular ring with a thin wall and a wide lumen. It is often found next to an artery of the same size, which makes it easy to compare.
- A **capillary** is tiny. At high power you see a very thin line of one endothelium cell wrapped round a lumen that holds a single red blood cell, or a red blood cell squeezed into a line.
- In a **longitudinal section** (LS), a cut along the length of the vessel, you see two parallel walls with the lumen between them. A vein may show a **valve** as a pair of pockets that point in the direction of flow.

:::def Plan diagram
A low-power drawing that shows the **outlines and proportions of the tissues** in a specimen, with no individual cells drawn.
:::

:::lab Making a plan diagram of an artery and a vein
**Aim**: record the arrangement of the tissues in the wall of a vessel from a prepared slide of a transverse section (TS) or longitudinal section (LS).

:::steps
1. Put the slide on the stage and focus on **low power** (the ×4 or ×10 objective). A plan diagram is a low-power drawing.
2. Choose one vessel and make it fill about half of the page. Use a sharp pencil and draw with single, clean, continuous lines. Never shade, and never sketch.
3. Draw the outline of the whole section, then the **boundaries between the tissue layers**: lumen, endothelium (a line), tunica media, tunica externa. Keep the **proportions** the same as on the slide: if the wall is a third of the radius, draw it so.
4. Draw **no individual cells**. If you can see cells, you are on too high a power.
5. Label with ruled lines that do not cross, and write the **tissue** names (lumen, tunica media, smooth muscle, elastic tissue). Add a title and the magnification or a scale bar.
:::

**Typical results**: the artery has a round, thick wall and a narrow lumen; the vein has a thinner wall and a wider, less regular lumen. **Sources of error**: a section cut at an angle makes the wall look thicker; a section that is not perpendicular gives an oval lumen; tissue can shrink or fold when the slide is prepared. **Safety**: handle slides and cover slips gently, because glass breaks and can cut. Practical drawing skills, including how to calculate magnification, are developed in [Practical skills: microscopy and drawing (Paper 3)](#bio.practical2).
:::

:::exam Plan diagrams and calculations
In a plan diagram, mark scores come from: the size of the drawing, clear single lines, correct proportions of the layers, no cells, and labelled lines that do not cross. If you are asked to calculate a magnification, use \(M = I/A\) with both lengths in the same units (see [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy)).
:::

:::eg A student drew plan diagrams of the transverse sections of a muscular artery and a vein, and measured them. The artery had an outer diameter of 46 mm and a lumen diameter of 24 mm. The vein had an outer diameter of 40 mm and a lumen diameter of 34 mm. Compare the ratios of wall thickness to lumen diameter.
The wall surrounds the lumen on both sides, so the wall thickness is half of the difference between the outer diameter and the lumen diameter.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{artery wall} &= \frac{46 - 24}{2} \\ &= 11\,\mathrm{mm} \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{vein wall} &= \frac{40 - 34}{2} \\ &= 3\,\mathrm{mm} \end{aligned}\]

Now divide each wall thickness by the lumen diameter.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{artery: } \frac{11}{24} &= 0.46 \\ \text{vein: } \frac{3}{34} &= 0.088 \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

The artery's ratio is about five times the vein's (0.46 ÷ 0.088 = 5.2). The artery has a thick wall and a narrow lumen; the vein has a thin wall and a wide lumen.
:::

## Blood

Blood is a tissue: cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid called **plasma**. A little over half of the volume of blood is plasma, and plasma is about 90% water.

### Water in the blood

Water is the main component of blood and of tissue fluid. The two properties that matter here are in [Lipids and water](#bio.lipids).

:::def Solvent action of water (in transport)
Water molecules are polar, so they are attracted to ions and to polar molecules and surround them, so that they dissolve. Blood plasma and tissue fluid are watery solutions that carry dissolved substances around the body.
:::

- **Solvent action**: glucose, amino acids, mineral ions (such as sodium and chloride), urea and many hormones dissolve in the water of the plasma, and so can be carried to wherever they are needed or removed. The hydrogen carbonate ions that carry most of the carbon dioxide are also dissolved in plasma.
- **High specific heat capacity**: a large amount of energy is needed to change the temperature of water by 1 °C. The blood is mostly water, so it can absorb a lot of heat from active organs such as the liver and muscles with only a small rise in temperature. It then carries heat around the body and releases it at the skin, so it **distributes heat** and **resists sudden changes** in the temperature of the body.

:::warn "Water transports oxygen" is not enough
Most oxygen is carried by haemoglobin in the red blood cells, not dissolved in water. Write that water is the **solvent** for substances that dissolve (glucose, ions, urea, hormones), and use the term **specific heat capacity** for temperature control. Do not write that water "has a high heat capacity" and stop: say what it means for the blood.
:::

:::key Telling the white blood cells apart
1. Look at the **nucleus** first: several **lobes** means a **neutrophil**; **kidney shape** means a **monocyte**; a **round nucleus filling almost the whole cell** means a **lymphocyte**.
2. Then **size**: a lymphocyte is about the size of a red cell (a little larger); a monocyte is the biggest.
3. A **red blood cell** has **no nucleus** and a pale middle where it is thin (the biconcave shape), and it is far more numerous than any white cell.
:::

How these cells defend the body is in [The immune response](#bio.immunity). The red blood cell is built for carrying oxygen: it is small, so it can squeeze through capillaries; its **biconcave** shape gives a large surface area to volume ratio for rapid diffusion of oxygen; it has **no nucleus or other organelles**, so there is more room for haemoglobin; and it is flexible.

:::eg Count and magnify: a student looked at a stained blood film and counted 200 white blood cells: 124 neutrophils, 56 lymphocytes, 14 monocytes and 6 of other types. **Percentages**: divide each count by the total of 200 and multiply by 100.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{neutrophils} &= \frac{124}{200} \times 100 = 62\% \\ \text{lymphocytes} &= \frac{56}{200} \times 100 = 28\% \\ \text{monocytes} &= \frac{14}{200} \times 100 = 7\% \\ \text{other} &= \frac{6}{200} \times 100 = 3\% \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

The percentages add up to 100%, which checks the arithmetic.

**Magnification**: convert both lengths to the same unit. The drawing is 28 mm; the cell is 7 µm = 0.007 mm.

\[\begin{aligned} M &= \frac{I}{A} \\ &= \frac{28\,\mathrm{mm}}{0.007\,\mathrm{mm}} \\ &= 4000 \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

The drawing is \(\times 4000\). Quote the magnification as ×4000, with no units, because it is a ratio of two lengths.
:::

## Tissue fluid

The blood never leaves the vessels, but the cells are not touching the blood. Between the capillaries and the cells there is a watery liquid that bathes the cells: the tissue fluid.

:::def Tissue fluid
The fluid that surrounds the cells of a tissue. It is formed from the blood plasma that leaks out of capillaries, and it is the medium through which substances are exchanged between the blood and the cells.
:::

Tissue fluid is like plasma, but without the **red blood cells** and without most of the **plasma proteins**: these are too large to pass through the gaps in the capillary wall. It contains water, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, oxygen, mineral ions, urea and hormones, and some white blood cells that squeeze out through the wall.

:::key The functions of tissue fluid
- It **supplies** the cells with oxygen, glucose, amino acids and other nutrients, which diffuse (or are carried by facilitated diffusion or active transport) from the tissue fluid into the cell.
- It **removes** carbon dioxide and wastes such as urea, which diffuse out of the cells into the tissue fluid and then into the capillaries.
- It **bathes** the cells, so that they stay in a watery environment of stable composition (homeostasis).
:::

### How tissue fluid forms and returns

Two things decide which way water moves at a capillary: the **hydrostatic pressure** of the blood, which pushes fluid out, and the **water potential** of the plasma, which pulls water in.

:::def Hydrostatic pressure
The pressure exerted by a liquid on the walls of its container. In a capillary, it is the pressure of the blood that pushes fluid out through the gaps in the wall.
:::

:::steps
1. **At the arterial end** of a capillary, the blood is still at a high **hydrostatic pressure**, because it has just come from an arteriole, which is narrow and close to the heart. The pressure forces water and small dissolved molecules (glucose, amino acids, ions, oxygen) out through the gaps between the endothelium cells. This is **pressure filtration**.
2. **Red blood cells and the large plasma proteins stay inside** the capillary because they are too large to pass through the gaps. The fluid that has left is the **tissue fluid**.
3. **As the blood flows along the capillary**, so much fluid has left that the hydrostatic pressure falls. The proteins that stayed behind are now more concentrated, which gives the plasma a **lower (more negative) water potential** than the tissue fluid.
4. **At the venous end**, the hydrostatic pressure is low and the water potential of the plasma is lower than that of the tissue fluid. Water moves back into the capillary **by osmosis**, down the water potential gradient, through the partially permeable capillary wall. Carbon dioxide and wastes from the cells enter with it.
5. **Not all of the fluid returns.** Roughly one-tenth of the fluid that leaves the capillaries stays in the tissue and drains into **lymph vessels**, which take it back to the blood near the heart. The fluid in the lymph vessels is called **lymph**.
:::

:::warn Pressure pushes out; osmosis pulls in
Fluid leaves the capillary because of **hydrostatic pressure** (pressure filtration), not osmosis. Fluid returns because of **osmosis**, and what makes the water potential of the plasma lower is the **plasma protein**. If a question says "explain the formation of tissue fluid", start at the arterial end and use the words "high hydrostatic pressure", "gaps", "too large to pass" and "water potential".
:::

:::eg Explain how tissue fluid is formed at the arterial end of a capillary and how most of it returns to the blood.
A six-mark answer follows the capillary from the arterial end to the venous end, one point per sentence.

At the arterial end, the blood in the capillary is at a high hydrostatic pressure. [B1]

This pressure forces water and small dissolved molecules (glucose, amino acids, oxygen, ions) out through the gaps between the endothelium cells. [B1]

Red blood cells and plasma proteins are too large to pass through the gaps, so they stay in the capillary. [B1]

The liquid that has left forms the tissue fluid, which bathes the cells. [B1]

At the venous end, the hydrostatic pressure is lower and the plasma proteins give the plasma a lower (more negative) water potential than the tissue fluid. [B1]

So water moves back into the capillary by osmosis, down a water potential gradient; any excess tissue fluid drains into the lymph vessels. [B1]

A weaker answer says "water moves out by osmosis and back in by diffusion". Osmosis is the movement of water only from a higher to a lower water potential, so it explains the return but not the leaving, and diffusion is not used for the return of water.
:::

:::eg Real context, swollen ankles: a patient who has been starving for many weeks has a swollen abdomen and ankles (oedema). The blood protein concentration is much lower than normal. Explain why fluid collects in the tissues.
Plasma proteins are made from amino acids, so a diet without protein gives a low concentration of plasma protein.

With fewer proteins in the plasma, the plasma water potential is not as low (not as negative) as normal. [B1]

At the venous end of the capillary the water potential gradient between the tissue fluid and the plasma is smaller, so less water returns to the blood by osmosis. [B1]

More fluid stays in the tissues than the lymph vessels can drain away. [B1]

The tissue fluid accumulates, which makes the tissues swell (oedema). [B1]

The treatment is to restore the protein in the diet. Some liver and kidney diseases also lower the protein in the blood and cause the same swelling.
:::

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: the fluid is forced out by the hydrostatic pressure (pressure filtration). Osmosis is the movement of water down a water potential gradient, and it is the process that brings water back at the venous end. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State what is meant by a closed circulatory system.
A: The blood stays inside blood vessels all the time; substances are exchanged through the capillary walls.
Q: Name the blood vessel that carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart, and the one that carries deoxygenated blood from the heart to the lungs.
A: The pulmonary vein carries oxygenated blood from the lungs to the left atrium; the pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood from the right ventricle to the lungs.
Q: Explain why the wall of the aorta contains a large amount of elastic tissue.
A: The ventricle pumps blood into the aorta in pulses at high pressure. The elastic tissue lets the wall stretch as the blood surges in and recoil afterwards, which squeezes the blood on and evens out the flow and the pressure.
Q: Explain how the structure of a capillary is related to its function of exchanging substances.
A: The wall is one cell thick (a very short diffusion distance), the lumen is narrow so red cells pass slowly and close to the wall, there are gaps between the cells for water and small molecules, and there are very many capillaries (a large surface area).
Q: Explain why veins have valves but arteries do not.
A: Blood in veins is at low pressure, so it could flow backwards (for example under gravity). The valves close if the blood begins to flow backwards. Blood in arteries is at high pressure and flows in one direction, so no valves are needed.
Q: A drawing of an artery in transverse section has a wall of thickness 9 mm, and the lumen is 30 mm across. Calculate the ratio of wall thickness to lumen diameter.
A: \(9 \div 30 = 0.30\).
Q: A lymphocyte is drawn 36 mm across at a magnification of ×3000. Calculate its actual diameter in µm.
A: \(A = I/M = 36\,\mathrm{mm} \div 3000 = 0.012\,\mathrm{mm} = 12\) µm.
Q: In a blood film of 150 white blood cells, 9 were monocytes. Calculate the percentage of monocytes.
A: \(9 \div 150 \times 100 = 6\%\).
Q: State two differences between plasma and tissue fluid.
A: Tissue fluid contains no red blood cells (plasma does), and it contains little or no plasma protein (plasma does). (Also credit: tissue fluid is in contact with the cells.)
Q: Explain why not all of the fluid that leaves a capillary returns to it.
A: The water that returns does so by osmosis at the venous end, but the water potential gradient there is not enough to bring back all of it. About one-tenth stays in the tissue and drains into lymph vessels.

Q: [3 marks] ★ Describe the path of a red blood cell from the left ventricle to the right atrium of a mammal, naming the main vessels.
A: The red blood cell leaves the left ventricle and enters the aorta, which carries oxygenated blood to the body. [B1]
It passes through arteries, arterioles and then through capillaries in an organ. [B1]
It returns through venules and veins to the vena cava, which carries it to the right atrium. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ The table gives measurements made on three blood vessels from a mammal. The velocity is calculated from the blood flow in each vessel and the total cross-sectional area of all vessels of that type.

| vessel | total cross-sectional area / cm² | blood velocity / mm s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| aorta | 4.0 | 208 |
| arterioles | 400 | 2.08 |
| capillaries | 3000 | 0.278 |
| venules | 3500 | 0.238 |

(a) Describe the relationship between total cross-sectional area and the velocity of the blood. (b) Explain how the velocity in the capillaries helps their function.
H: For (a), quote figures with units for the aorta and the capillaries.
H: For (b), what happens to the time the blood spends next to the cells?
A: (a) As the total cross-sectional area increases, the velocity decreases. [B1] The area increases from 4.0 cm² in the aorta to 3000 cm² in the capillaries, and the velocity falls from 208 mm s⁻¹ to 0.278 mm s⁻¹. [B1]
(b) The blood moves slowly through the capillaries, so there is more time for exchange of substances between the blood and the tissue fluid. [B1] (Also credit: slow blood flow gives time for diffusion of oxygen and glucose out and carbon dioxide in.) The very large total surface area of the capillaries also increases the rate of exchange. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ The velocity of blood in the aorta in the table above is calculated from the blood flow (cardiac output) of 5.00 dm³ min⁻¹ and the cross-sectional area of 4.0 cm². Calculate the velocity in mm s⁻¹. 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³.
H: Convert the flow to cm³ s⁻¹ first.
H: Velocity \(=\) flow \(\div\) area gives cm s⁻¹; multiply by 10 for mm s⁻¹.
A: Flow \(= 5.00 \times 1000 \div 60 = 83.3\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}\) [M1]
Velocity \(= 83.3 \div 4.0 = 20.8\,\mathrm{cm\,s^{-1}}\) [M1]
\(= 208\,\mathrm{mm\,s^{-1}}\) [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain how the structure of a vein is related to its function.
A: Blood in a vein is at low pressure, so the wall can be thin with little muscle and elastic tissue. [B1]
The lumen is wide, so there is little resistance to blood flow. [B1]
Valves prevent backflow of the blood. [B1]
Contraction of skeletal muscles around the vein squeezes it and moves the blood towards the heart. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ The table shows the number of each type of white blood cell counted in a blood film from a patient with a bacterial infection and from a healthy person, out of 100 white cells counted in each film.

| type of cell | healthy person | patient |
|---|---|---|
| neutrophils | 65 | 80 |
| lymphocytes | 25 | 14 |
| monocytes | 6 | 4 |
| other types | 4 | 2 |

(a) Calculate the percentage increase in neutrophils in the patient compared with the healthy person. (b) Explain why the number of neutrophils may be higher in a patient with a bacterial infection. (c) Describe how you would recognise a monocyte from a lymphocyte in the film.
H: Percentage change \(=\) change \(\div\) original \(\times\) 100.
H: For (c), compare the shape of the nucleus and the size of the cells.
A: (a) \((80 - 65) \div 65 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 23\%\) [A1]
(b) Neutrophils engulf and destroy bacteria by phagocytosis, so more are needed. [B1]
(c) A monocyte has a kidney-shaped (bean-shaped) nucleus and is larger. [B1]
A lymphocyte has a round nucleus that fills most of the cell, and is about the size of a red blood cell. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.movement) ★★★ A runner has an arteriole supplying a leg muscle and an arteriole supplying the gut. During a race, the arteriole in the muscle becomes wider and the arteriole in the gut becomes narrower. (a) Explain how the structure of an arteriole allows this to happen. (b) Suggest why this change is an advantage to the runner. (c) A capillary in the muscle has a lumen 7 µm across. Explain why the red blood cells move through it slowly.
H: Which tissue in the wall changes the size of the lumen?
H: For (c), think about the size of a red blood cell compared with the capillary.
A: (a) The wall of an arteriole contains smooth muscle (in the tunica media). [B1]
Contraction of the muscle narrows the lumen, and relaxation widens it, so the blood flow can be changed. [B1]
(b) More blood flows to the muscle, which brings more oxygen and glucose and removes more carbon dioxide and heat. [B1]
Less blood flows to the gut, which is not needed during the race, so the blood is directed to where it is needed. [B1]
(c) The red cell is about as wide as the capillary (about 7 µm), so it is squeezed against the wall and is slowed by friction. [B1]
This gives more time for oxygen to diffuse from the red cell into the tissue. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.movement) ★★★ A patient's heart pumps weakly, so blood builds up in her veins and the hydrostatic pressure of the blood in the capillaries of her legs is higher than normal. Her plasma protein concentration is normal. Fluid collects in her ankles. (a) Explain why this happens. (b) Explain why the fluid that collects contains no red blood cells. (c) Name the system that removes some of the excess tissue fluid from tissues in a healthy person.
H: Compare the push of the hydrostatic pressure with the pull of the water potential gradient, at both ends of the capillary.
A: (a) The higher hydrostatic pressure forces more fluid out of the capillaries along their length. [B1]
At the venous end the hydrostatic pressure is still high, so it is not lower than the pull of the water potential gradient set up by the plasma proteins, and less water returns to the capillary by osmosis. [B1]
More fluid is left in the tissues than the lymph vessels can drain. [B1]
(b) The fluid is forced out of the capillaries by hydrostatic pressure through the gaps between the endothelium cells. [B1]
The gaps are too small for red blood cells to pass through, so they stay in the capillary. [B1]
(c) The lymphatic system (lymph vessels). [B1]

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ In a model of a capillary, the pressure that pushes fluid out is 4.0 kPa at the arterial end and 1.5 kPa at the venous end. The pull of the plasma proteins draws water in with a constant effect equal to 3.0 kPa along the capillary. (This is simplified data for the question; a real capillary is more complex.) (a) Calculate the net outward pressure at each end. (b) Suggest what happens to the direction of fluid movement along the capillary and explain why this happens. (c) A different capillary had a pull of the proteins of only 1.2 kPa. Suggest the effect on the fluid in the tissues.
H: Net outward pressure \(=\) outward pressure \(-\) inward pull.
H: A negative answer means that the fluid moves into the capillary.
A: (a) Arterial end: \(4.0 - 3.0 = +1.0\) kPa (net outward). Venous end: \(1.5 - 3.0 = -1.5\) kPa (net inward). [B1] [B1]
(b) Fluid moves out at the arterial end and back in at the venous end. [B1]
The hydrostatic pressure falls along the capillary while the pull of the proteins stays the same, so the balance changes from outward to inward. [B1]
(c) With a pull of only 1.2 kPa, the net force at the venous end is \(1.5 - 1.2 = +0.3\) kPa, still outward, so less (or no) fluid returns and the tissue fluid builds up (oedema). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Sharks have a single circulation, in which the heart pumps blood to the gills and the blood then flows on to the body. Mammals have a double circulation. A student suggests that a double circulation allows a mammal to be more active. Use your knowledge of pressure in the circulation to evaluate this suggestion.
H: What happens to blood pressure when blood passes through a capillary network?
H: Compare the pressure of blood arriving at the body in the two animals.
A: In a single circulation, the blood passes through the gill capillaries and loses much of its pressure. [B1]
It then travels to the body at low pressure, so the flow to the organs is slow. [B1]
In a double circulation, the blood returns to the heart after the lungs and is pumped again. [B1]
So the blood enters the systemic circulation at high pressure, and flows rapidly to the organs, bringing oxygen and glucose to the cells quickly. [B1]
High pressure supports a high rate of exchange (tissue fluid formation) in the active tissues, which supports a high metabolic rate. [B1]
The suggestion is supported, but other features (a higher concentration of haemoglobin, a more efficient lung) also matter, so the double circulation is one of several factors. [B1]

:::recap
- Mammals have a **closed** circulation (blood stays in vessels) and a **double** circulation (the blood passes through the heart twice per circuit: lungs, then body). The blood returns from the lungs at low pressure and is pumped again at high pressure.
- Pulmonary artery: right ventricle to lungs (deoxygenated). Pulmonary vein: lungs to left atrium (oxygenated). Aorta: left ventricle to the body (oxygenated). Vena cava: body to right atrium (deoxygenated).
- Artery → arteriole → capillary → venule → vein. Walls: tunica intima (endothelium), tunica media (smooth muscle and elastic fibres), tunica externa (collagen).
- Elastic artery: thick wall, much elastic tissue, stretches and recoils, evens out the pulse. Muscular artery and arteriole: smooth muscle controls the blood flow to an organ. Capillary: one cell thick, narrow lumen, gaps. Vein: thin wall, wide lumen, valves, helped by skeletal muscle.
- Plan diagram: low power, clear lines, proportions, no cells, labels.
- Red blood cell: biconcave, no nucleus, about 7 µm. Neutrophil: lobed nucleus. Monocyte: large kidney-shaped nucleus. Lymphocyte: large round nucleus filling most of the cell.
- Water is the main component of blood: solvent action (carries glucose, ions, urea, hormones) and high specific heat capacity (distributes heat, resists change in temperature).
- Tissue fluid forms when high hydrostatic pressure at the arterial end forces water and small molecules out through gaps; cells and proteins stay in. It returns by osmosis at the venous end because the proteins lower the water potential of the plasma; excess drains as lymph. It supplies cells and removes wastes.
:::
