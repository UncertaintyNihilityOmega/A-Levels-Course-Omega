## What an antibiotic is

Your body fights many infections itself (see [The immune response](#bio.immunity)), but when bacteria multiply faster than the immune system can cope, doctors give a medicine to kill them. The difficulty is that a bacterium and a human cell are both living cells and share a lot of chemistry. A medicine that poisoned both would be a poison, not a cure. An antibiotic works because it attacks something that bacteria have and human cells do not.

:::def Antibiotic
A chemical, originally made by microorganisms such as fungi and bacteria, that kills bacteria or stops them growing, with little or no harmful effect on the cells of the person taking it.
:::

Some antibiotics **kill** bacteria (they are bactericidal). Others **stop them dividing**, so the immune system can clear the infection (bacteriostatic). Penicillin is bactericidal for growing bacteria. Many antibiotics used today are made by chemists, or are natural antibiotics that chemists have modified, but they are still called antibiotics.

:::story A mould and a clear patch
In 1928 Alexander Fleming noticed that bacteria on one of his culture plates had not grown near a mould, *Penicillium*, that had drifted in by accident. The mould was making a substance that stopped the bacteria growing: penicillin. In the early 1940s Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and their colleagues showed that it could cure infections in patients, and it was soon made in large amounts. The clear patch around the mould is the same effect that you will measure in the disc diffusion experiment later in this chapter.
:::

:::def Spectrum of an antibiotic
The range of different bacteria that an antibiotic affects. A **broad-spectrum** antibiotic acts on many types of bacteria. A **narrow-spectrum** antibiotic acts on only a few types.
:::

An antibiotic can be selective because the structures it attacks are found only in bacteria. The next table shows what a bacterium, a virus and a human cell have. It explains this whole chapter.

| feature | bacterium | virus | human cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| peptidoglycan cell wall | yes | no | no |
| ribosomes | 70S | none | 80S (and 70S in mitochondria) |
| metabolism of its own | yes | none | yes |
| reproduces | by dividing (binary fission) | only inside a host cell | by mitosis |

An antibiotic that attacks the peptidoglycan wall, or the 70S ribosome, or a bacterial enzyme, has a target in a bacterium. It has no target in a virus, and no target (or a different one) in a human cell.

:::note Not only penicillin
Penicillin is the antibiotic you need to know in detail. Other antibiotics work in other ways, for example by blocking bacterial 70S ribosomes so that the bacterium cannot make proteins, or by stopping the copying of bacterial DNA. They all share the idea of **selective toxicity**: they harm the bacterium and not the patient because they act on something only the bacterium has, or something that differs enough between bacterium and human.
:::

:::key Antibiotics in one box
- An antibiotic **kills bacteria or stops them growing**, with little or no harm to human cells.
- It is selective because it attacks a **target that bacteria have and human cells do not** (such as the peptidoglycan wall).
- Antibiotics act on **bacteria only**: not on viruses, and not on the human cells that the viruses live in.
- A **broad-spectrum** antibiotic affects many types of bacteria; a **narrow-spectrum** one affects only a few.
:::

## How penicillin kills bacteria

Remember what the bacterial wall is. It is made of **peptidoglycan**: long chains of sugar-like units, with the chains joined to each other by short links of amino acids called **cross-links**. The cross-links make the wall like a strong net round the cell. Enzymes build the cross-links.

:::def Peptidoglycan
The polymer that makes the cell wall of a bacterium. Long chains are joined side to side by short cross-links, which make the wall strong and rigid.
:::

A bacterium has to keep its wall strong, because of osmosis. The cytoplasm of a bacterium is a concentrated solution of salts, sugars and proteins, so it usually has a **lower water potential** than the water around it. Water therefore tends to enter the cell by osmosis, through the partially permeable cell surface membrane. A cell with no wall, like an animal cell, would swell and burst. The strong wall stops the bacterium swelling too far, so the cell does not burst.

A bacterium that is growing and dividing has to build **new wall** all the time, and each new piece needs new cross-links. That is the weak point that penicillin attacks.

:::steps
1. A growing bacterium makes new peptidoglycan. **Enzymes** join the chains together with **cross-links**, so the new wall is as strong as the old.
2. **Penicillin binds to these enzymes and inhibits them.** The enzymes can no longer form the cross-links.
3. The cell goes on growing, but the new wall it makes is **weak**, because the chains are not joined together.
4. **Water enters the cell by osmosis**, because the cytoplasm has a lower water potential than the surroundings.
5. The weak wall **cannot withstand** the water entering. The cell **bursts** (osmotic lysis) and dies.
:::

:::analogy A fence that cannot be repaired
Think of the wall as a fence of wooden panels that the bacterium must add to as it grows. Penicillin stops the workers nailing the new panels to the old ones. The old fence is still standing, but every new section falls apart when the pressure of the cell inside pushes on it.
:::

Three details are worth learning, because they are marks:
- **Penicillin does not dissolve or break down the existing wall.** It stops new cross-links forming. So it acts only on bacteria that are **growing and dividing**. A bacterium that is not growing is not building wall and is not harmed.
- The cell bursts because of **osmosis**, not because penicillin is a poison to the cytoplasm.
- Cells of animals, plants and fungi do not make peptidoglycan, so the enzymes that penicillin inhibits are not there. **Human cells have no peptidoglycan wall** to be weakened, so penicillin does not harm them.

## Why antibiotics do not affect viruses

A virus is not a cell. From [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes): a virus is a core of nucleic acid in a protein capsid (and, for some, an envelope). It has no cell wall, no ribosomes, no cytoplasm and no metabolism of its own. It can reproduce only **inside a host cell**, using the host's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to make new virus particles.

Compare that with the target of an antibiotic:
- **Penicillin** acts on peptidoglycan cross-links. A virus has no peptidoglycan wall, so there is nothing for penicillin to act on.
- An antibiotic that blocks **70S ribosomes** has no target in a virus, which has no ribosomes at all.
- A virus has no metabolism of its own for a drug to block. The processes that it depends on are the host cell's own, so a drug strong enough to stop them would also harm the patient's cells.

So an antibiotic does not affect a virus because the virus has none of the structures or processes that the antibiotic targets, and the virus is inside a human cell where the antibiotic has no effect on it.

:::eg A patient with a cold asks the doctor for antibiotics. Explain why the doctor refuses.
A cold is caused by a virus, which is not a cell: it has no peptidoglycan cell wall, no ribosomes and no metabolism of its own. [B1]

The virus reproduces inside the patient's cells, using the host cell's ribosomes and enzymes, so there is nothing for an antibiotic to act on. [B1]

Taking an antibiotic for no benefit exposes the patient's own bacteria (for example on the skin and in the gut) to the drug, so resistant bacteria are more likely to survive and multiply. [B1]

The antibiotic may also kill harmless or useful bacteria and cause side effects. [B1]

A weaker answer says "antibiotics only work on bacteria", which is true but gives no reason, so it scores at most one mark.
:::

:::note Other drugs for other microorganisms
Fungal infections such as athlete's foot are treated with **antifungal** drugs, because fungi are eukaryotes and an antibiotic does not act on them. **Antiviral** drugs do exist, for example the antiretroviral drugs that control HIV (see [Infectious diseases](#bio.disease)). They block steps that are specific to how a particular virus reproduces. They are different medicines from antibiotics, and they are harder to design because the virus uses the host cell's own machinery.
:::

:::warn Wrong targets
Do not write that antibiotics "do not work on viruses because viruses are too small" or "because viruses are resistant". Give the reason in terms of structure: no cell wall, no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own, so no target.
:::

## How resistance arises

:::def Antibiotic resistance
The ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic that would normally kill them or stop them growing, because of a change in the bacterium's DNA that is passed on to the cells it produces.
:::

The usual wrong explanation is "bacteria get used to the antibiotic". Bacteria do not decide anything. Resistance comes from a **random mutation** followed by **natural selection**. Take the steps in order.

:::steps
1. **A random mutation.** When a bacterium copies its DNA, a rare **gene mutation** (see [Protein synthesis and gene mutation](#bio.synthesis)) may produce a new form of a gene that gives resistance. For example, it may code for an enzyme that **breaks down penicillin**, so that the antibiotic is destroyed before it can act. The mutation happens by chance, and it happens whether or not the antibiotic is present.
2. **The antibiotic is a selection pressure.** When the antibiotic is used, the non-resistant bacteria are killed or stop growing. The resistant one has an advantage.
3. **The resistant bacteria survive and reproduce.** They are left with less competition for food and space, and they divide.
4. **They pass on the gene** for resistance to all their offspring when they divide, so the next generation is resistant.
5. After more use of the antibiotic, **more and more of the population is resistant**, until the antibiotic no longer works.
:::

Natural selection is covered fully in [Natural selection](#bio.selection). Here you need only the outline above: **random mutation, selection pressure, survival and reproduction, passing on the gene**. Notice that the antibiotic does not cause the mutation. It only selects, from the bacteria that are already there, the ones that can survive it.

Why does this happen so fast in bacteria? Under ideal conditions some bacteria can divide as often as every 20 minutes. One cell then becomes 2 cells, 4, 8 and so on: after 10 hours there have been 30 divisions, and \(2^{30} \approx 1.1 \times 10^9\) cells. With so many cells copying their DNA so often, even a very rare mutation soon appears, and a resistant cell has a huge number of offspring in a day.

### How resistance spreads between bacteria

Resistance can pass from one bacterium to others in two ways.

:::def Vertical transmission (of a gene)
The passing of genes from parent to offspring when a cell divides. When a resistant bacterium divides, each daughter cell receives a copy of the gene for resistance.
:::

:::def Horizontal transmission (of a gene)
The passing of genes from one living cell to another that is not its offspring. In bacteria, a copy of a plasmid carrying a resistance gene can pass from one bacterium to another, even of a different species.
:::

Many resistance genes are carried on **plasmids**. A bacterium can pass a copy of a plasmid to another bacterium that touches it, so a harmless species can give resistance to a dangerous one without any division. A single plasmid may carry genes for resistance to several antibiotics at once.

:::eg Explain how a population of bacteria can become resistant to an antibiotic. (This is a 5-mark question.)
A random mutation in a bacterium produces a gene that gives resistance, for example a gene for an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic. [B1]

When the antibiotic is used it acts as a selection pressure: bacteria that are not resistant are killed or stop growing. [B1]

The resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, with less competition from the others. [B1]

They pass on the gene for resistance to their offspring, in vertical transmission. [B1]

So the proportion of resistant bacteria in the population increases (and the gene can also spread to other bacteria on plasmids). [B1]

(Also credit: the mutation was present before the antibiotic was used; horizontal transmission by plasmids, including to other species.)

A weaker answer says "the bacteria become used to the antibiotic and change to survive it". It has no mutation, no selection and no inheritance, and it suggests that the bacteria change on purpose, so it scores no marks.
:::

:::warn Who becomes resistant?
**Bacteria** become resistant, not people. A patient does not "become resistant to penicillin". The patient's cells have not changed: it is the bacteria in or on the patient that may be resistant, and they can then infect other people. Also, a **single bacterium** does not "build up" resistance: it either has the gene or it does not. The population changes, because resistant bacteria survive and the others do not.
:::

## The consequences of antibiotic resistance

When bacteria that cause disease become resistant, a treatment that used to work fails. The consequences spread outwards from one patient to the whole health service.

| consequence | why |
|---|---|
| infections are harder to treat | the first antibiotic chosen does not work, so a second or third drug must be tried |
| illness lasts longer and is more serious | the bacteria keep multiplying while the wrong drug is given |
| more patients die | some infections cannot be treated with any antibiotic |
| more spread to other people | patients stay infectious for longer, so resistant bacteria pass to others |
| treatment costs more | the last-resort antibiotics are expensive, and patients spend longer in hospital |
| surgery, chemotherapy and organ transplants are riskier | these treatments weaken the immune system or open the body to bacteria, and an infection after them may not be curable |

### Two named examples

:::def MRSA
Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*: a strain of the bacterium *S. aureus* that is resistant to methicillin (a penicillin-type antibiotic) and to several other antibiotics.
:::

*Staphylococcus aureus* lives harmlessly on the skin or in the nose of many people. If it enters a wound, a surgical cut or a drip, it can cause a serious infection. MRSA is very hard to treat, and it spreads easily in hospitals, where many patients have wounds and weakened immune systems and antibiotics are used heavily.

:::def MDR-TB
Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis: TB caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* that is resistant to at least the two most powerful of the antibiotics normally used to treat TB.
:::

A TB patient normally takes several antibiotics for months (see [Infectious diseases](#bio.disease)). If the patient stops too early, or the drugs are not taken properly, the bacteria that survive are the ones that are more resistant. MDR-TB then needs longer treatment with more toxic and more expensive drugs, and it can be passed on to other people by droplets, just like ordinary TB.

:::eg Real context, a hospital: explain why resistant bacteria such as MRSA spread easily in hospitals, and describe two measures that reduce the spread. (This is a 5-mark question.)
Antibiotics are used heavily in hospitals, so the bacteria there are under a strong selection pressure and resistant bacteria survive and become common. [B1]

Many patients have open wounds, drips or weakened immune systems, so they are easily infected. [B1]

The bacteria pass between patients on hands, instruments, bedding and surfaces, in a place where many patients are close together. [B1]

Measure 1: hand washing (or alcohol gel) by staff and visitors before and after contact with each patient. [B1]

Measure 2: isolating patients who have MRSA, or screening patients when they enter the hospital and sterilising instruments. [B1]

(Also credit: cleaning surfaces; staff wearing gloves and gowns; using antibiotics only when needed.)

A weaker answer says "hospitals have a lot of bacteria because they are dirty". It gives no link between antibiotic use and selection and no measure with a reason, so it scores at most one mark.
:::

## Reducing the impact of resistance

We cannot stop resistance appearing, because mutations are random and bacteria divide so often. What we can do is slow it down, by giving resistant bacteria fewer chances to be selected and fewer chances to spread, and by keeping treatments working.

| step | why it helps |
|---|---|
| prescribe antibiotics **only when needed**, never for viral infections such as colds | every use of an antibiotic is a selection pressure, so fewer uses means less selection of resistant bacteria |
| patients **finish the whole course** | the course kills all the bacteria, including those that are partly resistant; stopping when you feel better leaves survivors that multiply and spread |
| use a **narrow-spectrum** antibiotic, chosen by testing the bacteria (for example disc diffusion) | fewer other bacteria are exposed to the drug, and the right drug kills the infecting bacteria fully |
| **rotate** antibiotics, or give two or more together, and keep newer ones in reserve | bacteria resistant to one drug are killed by another; a bacterium with resistance to two drugs at once is very rare |
| **hygiene** in hospitals: hand washing, isolating infected patients, cleaning and sterilising | stops resistant bacteria passing from patient to patient |
| **reduce antibiotics in farming**: not for healthy animals or to make them grow faster | removes a large source of selection pressure, and of resistant bacteria that reach people |
| **develop new antibiotics** | provides new targets that existing resistance does not protect against |
| **monitor (survey) resistance**, and educate patients and doctors | shows which antibiotics still work and where resistance is spreading |

Each step also has costs and limits. Developing a new antibiotic takes many years and is very expensive, and bacteria may become resistant to it as well. A farmer who stops using antibiotics may lose animals to disease, or have higher costs. Patients may not be able to afford the whole course, or may feel better and stop. A discussion answer should say what each step does, **why** it works and what limits it.

:::eg Discuss the steps that can be taken to reduce the impact of antibiotic resistance. (This is a 6-mark question.)
Doctors should prescribe antibiotics only when they are needed, and never for infections caused by viruses, because each use is a selection pressure for resistance. [B1]

Doctors should test the bacteria and use a narrow-spectrum antibiotic that is known to work, so that fewer bacteria are exposed to the selection pressure. [B1]

Different antibiotics can be used in turn or together, and new ones kept in reserve, so that bacteria resistant to one are killed by another. [B1]

Hospitals can limit the spread of resistant bacteria by hand washing, isolating infected patients and sterilising equipment. [B1]

New antibiotics can be developed, but this is slow and expensive and bacteria may become resistant to them too, so developing drugs cannot replace the other steps. [B1]

(Also credit: reduce the use of antibiotics in farming; monitor resistance and educate people; the cost of the whole course may be too high for some patients.)

A weaker answer lists "finish your course, wash your hands, new drugs". It gives no reason for any step and no limit, so it is not a discussion and scores about two marks.
:::

:::warn "Finish the course" needs a reason
"Finish the course so that the bacteria do not come back" is not enough. Say that the first days kill the sensitive bacteria and the patient feels better, but the **surviving bacteria include any that are partly or fully resistant**. If treatment stops, they reproduce and pass on their resistance, and the patient may be infectious.
:::

## Testing antibiotics: disc diffusion

To find out which antibiotic will work on a particular bacterium, a laboratory grows the bacteria on **agar** (a jelly with nutrients) and tests how each antibiotic affects them. In **disc diffusion**, small paper discs soaked in different antibiotics are placed on a plate that has been covered with a layer of bacteria (a **lawn**). The antibiotic spreads out of each disc into the agar. If the bacteria are sensitive to it, they cannot grow near the disc, and a clear circle, the **zone of inhibition**, appears.

:::def Zone of inhibition
The clear area around an antibiotic disc on an agar plate where bacteria have not grown, because the antibiotic that has diffused into the agar has killed them or stopped them growing.
:::

:::lab Testing the effect of antibiotics on a bacterium with paper discs
**Aim**: compare the effect of different antibiotics on the growth of a bacterium, by measuring the zones of inhibition around paper discs.

:::steps
1. Wash your hands and wipe the bench with disinfectant. Work near a Bunsen flame (or in a clean, still place) and use **aseptic technique**: sterile equipment, and plate and culture bottle opened for as short a time as possible.
2. Spread a measured volume of a broth culture of a harmless bacterium (such as a laboratory strain of *Escherichia coli*) evenly over the surface of a sterile nutrient agar plate with a sterile spreader, to make a lawn.
3. Using sterile forceps, place paper discs soaked in different antibiotics on the agar, well apart. Add a **control disc** soaked in sterile water.
4. Lift the lid only far enough to put the discs on. Fix the lid with **two or four short pieces of tape**, leaving gaps so that air can enter. Do **not** seal it all round.
5. Label the base, turn the plate **upside down** (so that drops of water from condensation do not fall on the agar) and incubate it at about **25 °C** for 48 hours.
6. Without opening the plate, measure the diameter of each clear zone (with a ruler, across the middle of the zone, including the disc) in two directions at right angles, and take the mean. Calculate the area of each zone.
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is the antibiotic; the dependent variable is the diameter (or area) of the zone of inhibition. Standardise the bacterium and the volume of broth spread, the thickness and type of agar, the size of the discs and the volume of antibiotic on each, the incubation temperature and the time.

**Control**: a disc soaked in sterile water (no antibiotic). It should have no clear zone, which shows that the zone around the other discs is caused by the antibiotic and not by the paper or the water.

**Typical results**: a larger zone means that the bacterium is more sensitive to that antibiotic. No zone, with bacteria growing right up to the disc, means that the bacterium is resistant to it.

**Sources of error**: the lawn may be uneven; zone edges can be fuzzy and hard to measure; discs may hold different amounts of antibiotic; antibiotics diffuse through agar at different speeds, so zone sizes only fairly compare **one antibiotic against another when the conditions are the same**. Improve the result by using several plates, measuring each zone in two directions and using the mean.

**Safety**: bacteria are always a risk. Use a harmless laboratory strain, never culture bacteria from the body or the environment, and incubate at about 25 °C or below, so that human pathogens are unlikely to grow. Do not open the plates after incubation. Wash hands. Sterilise plates and equipment (an autoclave at about 121 °C) before disposal. Disinfect the bench.
:::

The size of a zone is a measure of how much the antibiotic affects the bacteria, and **area** shows the difference more clearly than diameter. The zone is a circle, so

\[A = \pi r^2, \qquad r = \frac{d}{2}\]

where \(d\) is the diameter you measured. Doubling the diameter makes the area four times as large.

:::eg The figure shows an agar plate with a lawn of bacteria and four paper discs, A to D, each soaked in a different antibiotic. The discs are 6 mm in diameter. The diameters of the clear zones, measured across the zone including the disc, are in the table. (a) Calculate the area of the zone around disc A. (b) How many times larger is the area around A than around B? (c) State which antibiotic is most effective and what the result for C shows.
| disc | diameter of clear zone / mm |
|---|---|
| A | 22 |
| B | 12 |
| C | 6 |
| D | 18 |

**(a) Area for A.** The radius is half the diameter, \(r = 22 \div 2 = 11\,\mathrm{mm}\). [M1]

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \pi r^2 \\ &= \pi \times 11^2 \\ &= 380\,\mathrm{mm^2} \end{aligned}\]

The area is 380 mm². [A1]

**(b) Compare A with B.** For B, \(r = 6\,\mathrm{mm}\) and \(A = \pi \times 6^2 = 113\,\mathrm{mm^2}\). The ratio is

\[\frac{380}{113} = 3.4\]

so the area around A is 3.4 times the area around B. The diameters differ by a factor of only 1.8, but the **area** depends on the square of the diameter, so the difference looks bigger. [B1]

**(c) Conclusions.** Disc A has the largest zone, so antibiotic A is the most effective against this bacterium: it stops the bacteria growing in the largest area. [B1]

Disc C has a zone of 6 mm, the same as the disc itself, so there is no clear zone: the bacteria grow right up to the disc, and are resistant to antibiotic C. [B1]

(If a question asks for the area of the clear ring only, subtract the area of the disc: for A, \(380 - \pi \times 3^2 = 352\,\mathrm{mm^2}\). Read the question for whether the disc is included.)
:::

:::exam Zones of inhibition
Always convert **diameter to radius** before using \(\pi r^2\) (the commonest slip), show the working, and give units of mm². Say what the result means: "larger zone, more sensitive", "no zone, resistant". If you are asked to compare, quote both figures and a ratio or a percentage. A zone size alone cannot compare **different bacteria** or different agar plates: only the same bacterium, the same conditions and different antibiotics.
:::

:::note Sensitive does not mean best for the patient
Choosing an antibiotic needs more than a big zone: a drug may be toxic, may not reach the infection, or may be too broad-spectrum. The test shows which antibiotics the bacteria are sensitive to, and a doctor then decides.
:::

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State what is meant by the term antibiotic.
A: A chemical, originally made by microorganisms, that kills bacteria or stops them growing, with little or no harm to the cells of the person taking it.
Q: A patient has influenza. State why an antibiotic will not help, in terms of the structure of the pathogen.
A: Influenza is caused by a virus, which has no peptidoglycan cell wall, no ribosomes and no metabolism of its own, so the antibiotic has no target. (The virus is also inside the patient's cells.)
Q: State what the enzymes that penicillin inhibits normally do.
A: They join the chains of peptidoglycan together with cross-links in the cell wall of a growing bacterium.
Q: Penicillin kills bacteria in a fresh, well-fed culture, but has almost no effect on a culture of the same bacteria that have stopped growing because they have no nutrients. Explain why.
A: Penicillin stops cross-links forming only in new wall, and only growing bacteria build new wall. The non-growing bacteria still have their strong, complete walls, so they do not burst.
Q: A bacterium lives in dilute pond water. Explain, using water potential, why water tends to enter it.
A: The cytoplasm contains dissolved solutes, so it has a lower (more negative) water potential than the pond water. Water moves down the water potential gradient, by osmosis, through the partially permeable cell surface membrane into the cell. The wall normally stops the cell bursting.
Q: Two discs on an agar plate with a lawn of bacteria have clear zones of 28 mm (disc S) and 14 mm (disc T) in diameter. Calculate the area of each zone and say how many times larger the area around S is than around T.
A: S: \(r = 14\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(A = \pi \times 14^2 = 616\,\mathrm{mm^2}\). T: \(r = 7\,\mathrm{mm}\), \(A = \pi \times 7^2 = 154\,\mathrm{mm^2}\). The ratio is \(616 \div 154 = 4.0\): double the diameter, four times the area.
Q: A bacterium divides every 30 minutes in a warm nutrient broth. Starting from one cell, how many cells are there after 6 hours, if none die?
A: 6 hours is 12 divisions, so \(2^{12} = 4096\) cells.
Q: In one patient, an infection contains \(3 \times 10^9\) bacteria. On average, 1 in \(10^8\) bacteria has a mutation giving resistance to an antibiotic. Estimate how many resistant bacteria there are before the antibiotic is given.
A: \(3 \times 10^9 \times 10^{-8} = 30\) resistant bacteria, present before treatment begins. When the antibiotic is used, these 30 survive and multiply.
Q: A resistant bacterium divides. Explain why its offspring are also resistant.
A: The gene for resistance is part of the bacterium's DNA (or plasmid). The DNA is copied before the cell divides, so each daughter cell receives a copy of the gene.
Q: A patient takes an antibiotic for three days of a seven-day course and then stops because the symptoms have gone. Explain why this is a bad idea.
A: The antibiotic has killed the sensitive bacteria, which is why the patient feels better, but some bacteria are still alive and may be partly or fully resistant. They reproduce and pass on their resistance, so the infection can return and the resistant bacteria can be spread to others.

- The cell surface membrane dissolves in penicillin.

- Viruses have thicker cell walls than bacteria.
> A virus is not a cell: it has no peptidoglycan wall, no ribosomes and no metabolism of its own. It lives inside a host cell, using the host's machinery.

+ A random mutation gives a few bacteria resistance, and the antibiotic selects them, so they survive and reproduce.
- The antibiotic makes the bacteria mutate so that they become resistant.
- Each bacterium gradually gets used to the antibiotic during treatment.
- The patient's body passes resistance to the bacteria.
> Mutations happen by chance, whether or not the antibiotic is present. The antibiotic only kills the other bacteria, so resistant bacteria survive and pass the gene to their offspring.

- Adding antibiotics to the feed of all farm animals.
> Fewer, targeted uses mean fewer chances for resistant bacteria to be selected. The other options increase the exposure of bacteria to antibiotics or leave survivors.
? What is MRSA?
+ a strain of Staphylococcus aureus that is resistant to methicillin and several other antibiotics
- a virus that causes a serious skin disease
- a strain of Mycobacterium tuberculosis resistant to several drugs
- an antibiotic that is used when penicillin does not work
> MRSA is a resistant bacterium (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus). The strain of TB that resists several drugs is MDR-TB.
? A patient says, "I have become resistant to penicillin." What is the best correction?
+ The bacteria infecting the patient may be resistant; the patient's own cells have not changed.
- The patient is correct: the body gets used to penicillin.
- The patient's immune system has mutated.
- Resistance is passed from the patient's DNA to the bacteria.
> Resistance is a property of bacteria, caused by changes in their DNA. The patient's cells do not become resistant.

:::recap
- An antibiotic kills or stops the growth of bacteria with little or no harm to human cells, because it acts on a target that bacteria have (selective toxicity). Broad-spectrum antibiotics affect many bacteria; narrow-spectrum ones affect few.
- Penicillin inhibits the enzymes that form cross-links in the peptidoglycan wall of growing bacteria. The new wall is weak, water enters by osmosis, and the cell bursts (lysis). It does not break down the old wall.
- Human cells have no peptidoglycan wall, so penicillin does not harm them. Viruses have no cell wall, ribosomes or metabolism of their own, so antibiotics do not affect them.
- Resistance: a random mutation gives a gene for resistance (for example an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic); the antibiotic is a selection pressure; resistant bacteria survive, reproduce and pass on the gene. The antibiotic does not cause the mutation.
- Resistance spreads by vertical transmission (parent to offspring) and horizontal transmission (plasmids passing between bacteria, even of different species).
- Consequences: infections harder to treat, longer illness, more deaths, more spread, higher cost, riskier surgery and chemotherapy. MRSA and MDR-TB are examples.
- Steps: prescribe only when needed (never for viruses), finish the course, narrow-spectrum drugs after testing, rotate or combine antibiotics, hospital hygiene, less use in farming, new antibiotics, monitoring and education. Each has costs and limits.
- Disc diffusion: sensitive bacteria do not grow around the disc (zone of inhibition); no zone means resistant; control disc with no antibiotic; area \(= \pi r^2\) with \(r = d \div 2\); aseptic technique, incubate at about 25 °C, do not open the plate.
:::
