## What Paper 3 is

Paper 3 (Advanced Practical Skills) lasts 2 hours and is marked out of 40. It has two or three questions, set in a laboratory, on AS content or on unfamiliar contexts. Any information you need about an unfamiliar organism or reagent is printed in the question. You use a calculator, and nothing is printed for you apart from the question itself (no formula sheet).

The marks are for skills, not for facts:

| skill | marks |
|---|---|
| manipulation, measurement and observation | 15 to 17 |
| presentation of data and observations | 11 to 13 |
| analysis, conclusions and evaluation | 11 to 13 |

Half of the candidates in a session start on the microscope question and half on the investigation. With 40 marks in 120 minutes, you have about 3 minutes for each mark. In June 2024 (paper 9700/32) there were two questions, with 21 and 19 marks, and the second was the microscope and photomicrograph question.

You cannot set up the apparatus at home, so this chapter teaches the decisions behind each step. A running example appears throughout: a protease (a protein-digesting enzyme) is added to a cloudy suspension of milk protein, and the milk clears as the protein is hydrolysed. The question is how temperature changes the time for the milk to clear. The equipment in a typical Paper 3 investigation is ordinary laboratory equipment: beakers, test-tubes and a rack, measuring cylinders, syringes or graduated pipettes, a thermometer, a stopwatch, a water bath or beaker of water, a balance, a ruler, glass rods, labels, stains and reagents (the full list is in the syllabus). Read every question first, note the times you are given, and follow the numbered instructions exactly.

## Planning the decisions of an investigation

Even when a method is given, Paper 3 asks you to decide things, or to say why the method does something. The decisions come in a fixed list.

:::def Independent variable
The variable that you change on purpose in an investigation. It goes on the x-axis of a graph.
:::

:::def Dependent variable
The variable that you measure, to see how it responds to the change in the independent variable. It goes on the y-axis.
:::

:::def Standardised variable
A variable, other than the independent variable, that could affect the dependent variable and is kept the same in every treatment, so that it cannot explain the results.
:::

:::def Control
A treatment or tube in which the independent variable has no effect, or the key part of the system is left out, to show that the dependent variable changes because of the independent variable and not for another reason.
:::

:::def Replicate
A repeat of the same treatment, done to check whether results agree and to calculate a mean. Replicates are repeats of the whole treatment, not just repeat readings of one measurement.
:::

Here are the decisions, each with the answer for the running investigation.

| decision | rule | in the milk and protease investigation |
|---|---|---|
| independent variable | the one thing you change | temperature of the reaction mixture |
| dependent variable | what you measure | time for the milk to clear |
| range | wide enough to show the whole pattern, from low to high | 20 °C to 60 °C: from a slow reaction up to where the enzyme is denatured |
| number of values | at least 5 | 5 temperatures: 20, 30, 40, 50 and 60 °C |
| interval | equal steps, small enough to show the pattern | 10 °C |
| how to change it | a method that is accurate and constant | water baths set by a thermometer |
| how to measure the dependent variable | the quantity itself, to a suitable precision | stopwatch to the nearest second, to the point where a cross becomes visible |
| replicates | at least 3 at each value | 3 tubes at each temperature |
| controls | a treatment to show the change is caused by the independent variable | boiled protease, or water instead of protease, at each temperature |
| standardised variables | everything else that matters | volume and concentration of milk and protease; pH (a buffer); the tube and the cross; how it is judged |

Not every variable needs to be standardised. A variable with a minimal effect, such as the small differences between test-tubes of the same type, does not have to be. The ones that matter are those that would change the rate: temperature (if it is not the independent variable), the amounts and concentrations of enzyme and substrate, the pH, and the time allowed.

:::warn Fewer than five values
A common loss of marks is a range with too few values, such as three temperatures, because a pattern cannot be seen with three points. Use at least five different values of the independent variable, in equal steps. Also check that the values are within what the equipment can do: a water bath at 5 °C needs ice.
:::

:::exam Two different "controls"
Do not confuse a **control** (a tube that shows the effect is caused by the independent variable) with a **standardised variable** (something kept the same). "Boiled enzyme" is a control. "The same volume of milk in every tube" is standardisation. A question that says "describe a suitable control" wants a treatment, not a list of the variables you will keep constant.
:::

## Dilutions

Many investigations need solutions of several concentrations. You make them by diluting a stock solution with water, and there are two ways to do it.

**Proportional dilution**: each solution is made on its own from the stock, using calculated volumes. The volume of stock needed follows from the fact that the amount of solute is unchanged by adding water.

:::formula Dilution
\[c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2\]
\(c_1\) and \(V_1\) are the concentration and volume of the stock solution used; \(c_2\) and \(V_2\) are the concentration and final volume wanted. So the volume of stock to use is \(V_1 = \dfrac{c_2 V_2}{c_1}\), and you add water to make up the final volume \(V_2\).
:::

:::eg A 0.80 mol dm⁻³ stock solution is available. Describe how to make 20 cm³ of each of five solutions: 0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40 and 0.50 mol dm⁻³.
Use \(V_1 = c_2 V_2 / c_1\) for each, with \(V_2 = 20\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) and \(c_1 = 0.80\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}\). For 0.10 mol dm⁻³:

\[\begin{aligned} V_1 &= \frac{0.10 \times 20}{0.80} \\ &= 2.5\,\mathrm{cm^3} \end{aligned}\]

So put 2.5 cm³ of stock in a measuring cylinder or syringe and add water to a total volume of 20 cm³ (17.5 cm³ of water). The others follow in the same way.

| final concentration / mol dm⁻³ | stock / cm³ | water / cm³ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.10 | 2.5 | 17.5 |
| 0.20 | 5.0 | 15.0 |
| 0.30 | 7.5 | 12.5 |
| 0.40 | 10.0 | 10.0 |
| 0.50 | 12.5 | 7.5 |

Label each container and mix gently. Use a separate clean syringe or pipette for each solution (or work from the most dilute to the most concentrated) so that a carry-over does not change the concentrations.
:::

**Serial dilution**: each solution is made from the previous one, using the same dilution factor each time. It is quick and uses small volumes. A tenfold serial dilution takes 1 cm³ of the solution and 9 cm³ of water, which divides the concentration by 10 each time. The solutions made from a 2.0% protease solution are 0.20%, 0.020% and 0.0020%. A halving series, with 5 cm³ of solution and 5 cm³ of water each time, is in [Testing for biological molecules](#bio.foodtests).

Choose the method from the range you need: proportional dilution gives equal steps (0.1, 0.2, 0.3 ...), a serial dilution gives a series that falls by the same factor (10, 1, 0.1, 0.01 ...), which suits concentrations that cover several powers of ten. An error in an early step of a serial dilution is carried through every later one, which is its weakness.

## Following instructions and taking readings

The manipulation marks are for doing what you are told, accurately, and for recording what happens. Some skills come up again and again.

**Water baths and temperature**. Enzyme and substrate must each reach the temperature of the bath before they are mixed, otherwise the reaction starts at the wrong temperature. A thermostatically controlled water bath keeps its temperature steady and has its own display; a beaker of water on a hotplate or with added hot or cold water needs a thermometer in it, checked during the investigation. Heating a bath is the main hazard of the investigation, so the temperature should be checked at the start and the end.

**Timing**. Start the stopwatch at the moment of mixing, and say so in a method ("start the stopwatch as the protease is added"). Keep the same person timing and the same judgement of the end point. A stopwatch reads to 0.1 s, but your own reaction time and your judgement of the end point are worth about a second or more, so quote a time to the nearest second.

**Judging an end point**. When the end point is a colour or cloudiness change, the judgement is subjective. Make it more objective by comparing the tube with a **standard**: a tube that shows the end point (for example 6 cm³ of water for "completely clear"), or a mark seen through the tube. In the running example, a cross drawn on a card is put under a boiling tube, and the end point is the moment the cross can first be seen clearly from above. Always use the same tube, the same card and the same lighting.

**Reading instruments**. Read a liquid in a measuring cylinder at the bottom of the meniscus, with your eye level with the surface. Read a syringe at the same mark of the plunger each time. Record readings to the precision of the instrument: if a ruler is marked in mm, a length is recorded to the nearest mm (or 0.1 cm); a balance reading to 0.01 g gives masses with 2 decimal places. Use the same number of decimal places for every reading of the same quantity.

**Observations**. A qualitative observation is a description, so it needs to be clear: "the solution changed from blue to orange" or "a brick-red precipitate formed", not "it changed colour" or "it went clear" when a colour has actually faded. Record colours before and after, and the time or temperature if relevant.

**Unknown solutions**. If the investigation asks you to identify unknown solutions, use the tests in [Testing for biological molecules](#bio.foodtests) and record each result in a table: the test, the observation, and the conclusion. For a semi-quantitative estimate, compare the colour with standards of known concentration; if the unknown falls between two standards, its concentration lies between the two.

:::eg An unknown glucose solution gave a yellow-orange colour with Benedict's solution. Tubes with known concentrations gave yellow at 1.0% and orange at 2.0% under the same conditions. Estimate its concentration and say how the estimate could be improved.
The unknown is a darker colour than the 1.0% standard and lighter than the 2.0% standard, so its concentration is between 1.0% and 2.0%. [B1]

To improve the estimate, make standards at smaller intervals between 1.0% and 2.0% (for example 1.2, 1.4, 1.6 and 1.8%) and compare the unknown with these, in the same conditions. [B1]

The answer is a range, not a single value, because the colours are judged by eye.
:::

## Hazards and risk

You may be asked to assess the risks of a procedure, using the hazard information given in the question.

:::def Hazard
Something that can cause harm, such as a hot water bath, a sharp blade or a chemical that irritates the skin.
:::

:::def Risk
How likely a hazard is to cause harm, and how serious that harm would be. In Paper 3 it is assessed as low, medium or high.
:::

A full answer names the hazard, rates the risk, and gives a **precaution** that deals with that hazard. A precaution is a specific action: "wear eye protection", "carry hot tubes with a holder", "keep away from naked flames". "Be careful" is not a precaution.

| substance or equipment | hazard | risk | precaution |
|---|---|---|---|
| water at 60 °C in a bath | scalds and burns | medium | use a holder or tongs for hot tubes, keep the bath away from the edge of the bench, wipe up spills |
| protease solution | may irritate the skin and eyes, and some people are allergic to enzymes | low | wear eye protection, wash splashes off with water |
| iodine solution | stains the skin and clothes, irritates the eyes | low | wear eye protection, wipe up spills |
| ethanol | flammable | medium | keep it away from flames and use a water bath, not a Bunsen burner, to warm it |
| scalpel or blade | cuts | medium | cut on a tile, away from the hand that holds the specimen, and carry it with the blade covered |

The rating depends on the concentration and the quantity: a dilute solution of a mild irritant is low risk, but the same chemical at a high concentration may be medium. Always say why (for example, "low risk because the solution is dilute").

:::eg The investigation uses a water bath at 60 °C and a protease solution. Assess the risk and describe one precaution for each hazard.
Water at 60 °C: it can scald, so the risk is medium. [B1] Precaution: use a test-tube holder to move tubes in and out of the bath. [B1]

Protease solution: the enzyme can irritate the eyes or skin, so the risk is low (the solution is dilute); wear eye protection. [B1]
:::

## Recording results

The presentation marks are for the table. A correct table has these features.

:::key Rules for a results table
1. A **ruled table** with a heading for every column (and every row, if the rows are labelled).
2. Each heading says the **quantity and the unit**, separated by a solidus: "temperature / °C", "time / s". **No units in the body** of the table.
3. The **independent variable** is in the left-hand column (or the top row), and the dependent variable to its right.
4. Raw data are recorded to the **precision of the instrument**, with the **same number of decimal places** in a column.
5. Replicates have their own columns (or rows), followed by the **mean** and any **calculated** value, with headings and units for these too.
6. Qualitative results are recorded in words that are clear and specific.
:::

Here are the results of the running investigation for three replicates at each temperature. In the 50 °C row one time is far from the other two.

| temperature / °C | time 1 / s | time 2 / s | time 3 / s | mean time / s | rate / 10⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20 | 396 | 404 | 400 | 400 | 2.50 |
| 30 | 197 | 203 | 200 | 200 | 5.00 |
| 40 | 102 | 104 | 106 | 104 | 9.62 |
| 50 | 158 | 164 | 245 | 161 | 6.21 |
| 60 | >600 | >600 | >600 | – | 0 |

The 245 s at 50 °C is an **anomalous result**: it does not fit the pattern of the other two replicates, which agree within 6 s. It is left out of the mean (the mean is of 158 and 164 only), and the answer should say so. At 60 °C, no tube cleared in the 600 s allowed, so the rate is recorded as 0 and no mean time can be given.

:::def Anomalous result
A result that does not fit the pattern of the other results and is much further from the others than the usual scatter. It is identified, left out of the mean, and explained if possible.
:::

When a result is anomalous, suggest what may have caused it (the tube was not at the right temperature, the timing was started late, the judgement of the end point differed) and say how you would deal with it: repeat that treatment, and calculate the mean without the anomaly.

## Calculations

State the formula, show each step, and use the correct number of significant figures (the same as the data, or one more).

:::eg Use the data for 40 °C to calculate the mean time and the rate of reaction.
The mean of the three times:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mean} &= \frac{102 + 104 + 106}{3} \\ &= 104\,\mathrm{s} \end{aligned}\]

The rate is the reciprocal of the time. To give a number that is easy to read, multiply by 1000:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{rate} &= \frac{1000}{104} \\ &= 9.62 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

so the table column is headed "rate / 10⁻³ s⁻¹" and the entry is 9.62. The data have three significant figures (104), so the answer also has three.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: a student's table and graph for the running investigation. [4]
A student wrote this table and drew this graph.

Table heading: "temperature (°C)" with the body entries "20 °C", "30 °C", "40 °C" and "time / s" with entries "396.0", "197", "102.0".
Graph: temperature on the y-axis, rate on the x-axis, points joined by ruled lines from dot to dot, with the line drawn on to 70 °C.

**Table** (two errors): the units are written in the body ("20 °C"), which is wrong because they belong in the heading only [B0]. The decimal places are inconsistent (396.0, 197, 102.0), so the precision of the data is not consistent [B0].

**Graph** (two errors): the axes are swapped, since the independent variable (temperature) must be on the x-axis [B0]; and the line is extended beyond the last point to 70 °C, which is extrapolation, not allowed unless you are told it is justified [B0].

With these four corrected, the table and graph earn all four marks.
:::

## Drawing graphs

A graph shows the pattern, and the marks are for its construction. The checklist is long, but each item is easy to meet.

:::key A graph that scores full marks
1. **Axes**: the independent variable on the x-axis, the dependent variable on the y-axis, each labelled with the **same quantity and unit as the table heading**.
2. **Scale**: simple steps (1, 2, 5 or 10 per large square), using **more than half of the grid** in each direction, so that each value can be read to within half a small square. Start at zero if that is sensible.
3. **Points**: plotted accurately as small crosses (×) or dots in circles (⊙), never large blobs that hide the position.
4. **Line**: a smooth **curve of best fit** or a ruled **straight line of best fit** if there is a clear trend and the points scatter around it; **ruled straight lines joining the points** if you cannot be sure what happens between the points, or if the data are not expected to follow a smooth curve.
5. **No extrapolation** past the last point, unless the question says it can be justified.
6. Sharp pencil, ruler for straight lines, a title if the question asks for one.
:::

Joining the points dot-to-dot is wrong when the results are expected to follow a trend and have some random scatter: the line should pass through the middle of the points. Drawing a smooth curve is wrong when the points are separate categories. When the question asks for a line of best fit, a line through every point is not best fit unless the points lie exactly on it.

:::plot Rate of clearing of milk by a protease at five temperatures. The smooth curve of best fit rises, peaks and falls; the 245 s result at 50 °C was left out. Rates in units of 10⁻³ s⁻¹.
--f "2.5*2**((x-20)/10)/(1+exp((x-48)/2.5))+0*sqrt(x-20)+0*sqrt(60-x)" --label "curve of best fit" --x 0 80 --y 0 13 --xlabel "temperature / °C" --ylabel "rate / 10⁻³ s⁻¹" --pt "20,2.5,2.50@se" --pt "30,5,5.00@nw" --pt "40,9.62,9.62@nw" --pt "50,6.21,6.21@ne" --pt "60,0,0@nw" --legend tr --alt "Five points: rate 2.50 at 20 degrees Celsius, 5.00 at 30, 9.62 at 40, 6.21 at 50 and 0 at 60, in units of 10 to the power minus 3 per second. A smooth curve rises through the first three points to a peak between 40 and 50 degrees and then falls steeply to almost zero at 60 degrees."
:::

Before plotting on squared paper, decide the scale. The temperatures run from 20 to 60 °C, so the x-axis from 0 to 70 °C with 10 °C to a large square (7 large squares; this page's graph goes on to 80 °C only to make room for its label) uses the width; the rates run up to 9.62, so the y-axis from 0 to 12 with 2 units to a large square (6 large squares) uses most of the height. A scale of 3 units per large square is hard to read, and one that takes only a quarter of the grid wastes marks.

## Describing patterns and drawing conclusions

"Describe" means say what the graph shows, with data. A full description gives the overall trend, the quantities that change, the **highest or lowest value and where it occurs**, and figures with units.

:::eg Describe the effect of temperature on the rate of clearing of milk by the protease, and explain the results.
Between 20 °C and 40 °C the rate increases, from \(2.50 \times 10^{-3}\) to \(9.62 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). [B1]

The highest rate measured is at 40 °C. Above 40 °C the rate falls, to \(6.21 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) at 50 °C and to 0 at 60 °C, where the milk did not clear. [B1]

Explanation: up to 40 °C, the enzyme and the milk protein have more kinetic energy, so they collide more often and more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second. [B1]

Above the optimum, hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds in the protease break, so the active site changes shape and the enzyme is denatured; at 60 °C the protein is no longer hydrolysed. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the rate goes up and then down because of the enzyme", with no figures and no mention of collisions, bonds or the active site.
:::

A **conclusion** is a short statement that links the dependent variable to the independent variable and says whether the **hypothesis** is supported. A hypothesis here would be "the rate at which the protease clears milk increases with temperature up to an optimum, then falls". The results support it, because the rate rose to a maximum and then fell. They do not prove it: only five temperatures were tested, and the exact optimum is between 40 and 50 °C. Say how confident you are, with a reason: the replicates agreed within 6 s except for one anomaly, which gives confidence, but one run at each temperature and only five values limits it.

**Predictions** use the graph. The curve gives a rate of about \(7 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\) at 35 °C, so the milk should clear in about \(1000 \div 7 \approx 140\) s. That is an interpolation, which is acceptable because 35 °C is within the range tested; a prediction for 70 °C would be an extrapolation and cannot be made from the data.

## Errors, improvements and extensions

Every investigation has errors. Examiners want two things: the **type** of error and the **specific source**.

:::def Random error
An error that varies unpredictably from one reading to the next, so repeating the measurement and taking a mean reduces its effect. It scatters the results.
:::

:::def Systematic error
An error that is the same in every reading, in the same direction, so that all the results are too high or all too low. Repeating the measurement does not remove it. It may not change the trend, but it affects the accuracy.
:::

Judging the end point by eye gives random error: sometimes you call it early, sometimes late. A thermometer that reads 2 °C too high gives a systematic error in every temperature, and the pattern (rise, peak, fall) is unchanged, although the optimum shifts. A tube that is not left in the bath long enough gives a systematic error in the direction of a too-low temperature for every tube.

**"Human error" is not an acceptable source of error**, because it is not specific. Name the actual measurement: "the judgement of when the cross becomes visible is subjective", "the temperature of the bath fell while the tubes were out", "the protease was measured with a 10 cm³ syringe marked every 0.2 cm³, so its uncertainty (±0.1 cm³) is 10% of the 1.0 cm³ used".

An improvement must be specific too, and say why it works. "Use more accurate equipment" earns nothing; "use a 1 cm³ syringe marked every 0.01 cm³ for the protease, so the uncertainty falls to ±0.005 cm³, which is 0.5% of 1.0 cm³" is a mark.

| weak answer | full answer |
|---|---|
| "human error" | judging when the cross became visible varied between readings (a random error) |
| "use more accurate equipment" | use a 1 cm³ syringe marked every 0.01 cm³ to measure the 1.0 cm³ of protease |
| "do more repeats" | do more replicates at each temperature and calculate the mean, so that a random error has less effect and an anomaly can be spotted |
| "control the variables" | put the milk and the protease in the water bath for 5 min before mixing, so both are at the temperature of the bath |
| "use smaller steps" | test 42, 44, 46 and 48 °C as well, because the highest rate is between 40 and 50 °C |

:::eg A student investigated the effect of temperature on the time for milk to clear. Identify two significant sources of error and suggest an improvement for each.
The temperature of the mixture was not kept at the temperature of the bath: when the tubes were taken out to add the protease and mixed, the mixture cooled, so the temperature of the reaction was lower than recorded. [B1] Improvement: add the protease to the milk while the tube stays in the water bath, or put the tubes in the bath for a few minutes after mixing and check the temperature of the mixture with a thermometer. [B1]

The end point was judged by eye, when the cross could be seen, which differs between readings (a random error). [B1] Improvement: compare with a standard, a tube of water that shows what "clear" looks like, or use a colorimeter, and have the same person judge each tube. [B1]
:::

**Extending the investigation** means suggesting a new investigation that follows from the results. Choose another independent variable and say what you would measure: "investigate the effect of pH on the time for the milk to clear, using buffer solutions from pH 3 to 9, at the optimum temperature of 40 °C". **Modifying** a method means changing the one step that has to change and leaving the others the same: to investigate pH, the independent variable changes from temperature to pH, so temperature becomes a standardised variable (40 °C in every tube) and buffers of different pH are added to the tubes. Describe the changes in a few clear lines or with a labelled diagram.

## In the exam room

:::exam Command lines in Paper 3 and how to answer them
- **"Identify the independent variable"**: the one thing the experimenter changed ("the temperature of the water bath"). Say what was changed in this experiment, not the dependent variable.
- **"State two variables you standardised"**: name them and, if asked, say how: "volume of milk, 5 cm³, measured with a syringe".
- **"Suggest one significant source of error"**: name a specific measurement or step, and say why it matters. Never "human error".
- **"Describe how you would modify the procedure to investigate …"**: change the independent variable, say how you would vary it, and which variable you now keep constant.
- **"Suggest how the confidence in the conclusion could be increased"**: more replicates, more values of the independent variable, smaller intervals, a better method of measuring the dependent variable.
- **"Calculate"**: show the formula, each step, the unit and the right number of significant figures.
- **"Describe"** a pattern: trend, figures with units, the maximum or minimum, any anomaly. **"Explain"**: the biology, linked to the data.
Read the whole question before starting: note any times that are given and any instruction that tells you to record a reading while a reaction runs.
:::

Q: A student investigates the effect of light intensity on the number of bubbles per minute from a piece of pondweed. Identify the independent variable and the dependent variable.
A: Independent variable: light intensity (the distance of the lamp from the pondweed). Dependent variable: the number of bubbles released per minute.
Q: Explain why at least five values of the independent variable should be used.
A: Five or more values show the shape of the pattern, such as a rise to a maximum then a fall, and a trend is more reliable; with two or three values you cannot tell the shape.
Q: Calculate the volumes of a 1.0 mol dm⁻³ stock solution and of water needed to make 25 cm³ of a 0.40 mol dm⁻³ solution.
A: \(V_1 = c_2 V_2 / c_1 = 0.40 \times 25 \div 1.0 = 10\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) of stock; \(25 - 10 = 15\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) of water.
Q: Starting with 1.0% enzyme solution, describe a tenfold serial dilution that makes 0.10%, 0.010% and 0.0010% solutions.
A: Put 9 cm³ of water in each of three tubes. Add 1 cm³ of the 1.0% solution to the first and mix: 0.10%. Take 1 cm³ of that into the second tube: 0.010%. Take 1 cm³ of that into the third: 0.0010%. Discard 1 cm³ from the last tube so each tube holds 9 cm³.
Q: A results table has the heading "temperature (°C)" and an entry "25 °C". Give two corrections.
A: The heading should be "temperature / °C" and the entry should be 25 with no unit in the body of the table.
Q: Five readings of a time were 12.1, 11.8, 12.3, 15.9 and 12.2 s. Identify the anomalous result and calculate the mean without it.
A: The anomalous result is 15.9 s, which is far from the other four. The other four total 48.4 s, so the mean is \(48.4 \div 4 = 12.1\) s.
Q: A thermometer is marked every 1 °C. A bath is read as 40 °C. Calculate the percentage error.
A: Uncertainty \(= 0.5\,\mathrm{°C}\) (half a division); \(0.5 \div 40 \times 100 = 1.3\%\).
Q: Explain why a student writes "human error" as a source of error and loses the mark.
A: It is not a specific source of error. The answer must name the measurement or step, such as "the time when the colour changed was judged by eye", and say why it matters.
Q: State whether a smooth curve or ruled straight lines joining points should be used for a graph of the rate of an enzyme reaction against temperature, and give a reason.
A: A smooth curve of best fit, because the rate is expected to change smoothly with temperature and the points scatter a little; joining the points dot-to-dot would turn random scatter into a false pattern.
Q: Give one hazard of using a water bath at 60 °C, rate the risk and give a precaution.
A: Hot water can scald, medium risk; use a test-tube holder to lift tubes from the bath.

A: Independent variable: the temperature of the solution. [B1]
Dependent variable: the time for the colour of the dye to fade (or disappear). [B1]
Standardised: any one of the volume of dye, the volume or concentration of the other solution, the pH (credit a sensible variable). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A stock solution of 0.60 mol dm⁻³ sodium chloride is used to make 30 cm³ of each of 0.10, 0.20 and 0.30 mol dm⁻³. (a) Calculate the volume of stock needed for 0.20 mol dm⁻³. (b) State how the volume of water is found and give its value. (c) State one way to avoid carry-over when measuring the three volumes of stock.
A: (a) \(V_1 = 0.20 \times 30 \div 0.60\) [M1] \(= 10\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) [A1]
(b) Subtract the stock volume from the final volume: \(30 - 10 = 20\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) of water. [B1]
(c) Use a separate clean syringe for each solution, or work from the most dilute to the most concentrated. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Seeds were kept at different temperatures for 5 days and the root lengths were measured. The table shows the mean root length of ten seeds at each temperature.

| temperature / °C | mean root length / mm |
|---|---|
| 10 | 4.2 |
| 15 | 9.8 |
| 20 | 17.5 |
| 25 | 24.1 |
| 30 | 19.6 |
| 35 | 6.3 |

(a) Describe the results. (b) Name the type of graph you would draw and state which variable goes on the x-axis. (c) Suggest how to find the temperature for the longest roots more precisely.
H: For (a), give the trend, the maximum with its temperature, and figures with units.
A: (a) The mean root length increases from 4.2 mm at 10 °C to a maximum of 24.1 mm at 25 °C. [B1]
Above 25 °C it decreases, to 6.3 mm at 35 °C. [B1]
(b) A line graph (the temperature is a continuous variable), with a smooth curve of best fit. [B1] Temperature, the independent variable, goes on the x-axis. [B1]
(c) Test more temperatures at smaller intervals around 25 °C, such as 22, 24, 26 and 28 °C. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A student measured the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed by counting the bubbles of gas released in one minute at different distances from a lamp. Identify two significant sources of error and suggest an improvement for each.
H: Is a bubble always the same size? What else does a lamp give out besides light?
A: The bubbles vary in size, so counting them does not measure the volume of gas accurately. [B1] Improvement: collect the gas in a capillary tube or gas syringe and measure its volume (or the length of the bubble) for a fixed time. [B1]
The lamp also warms the water, so the temperature changes as the lamp is moved closer, which affects the rate. [B1] Improvement: put a beaker of water (a heat shield) between the lamp and the pondweed, or use an LED lamp, and check the temperature with a thermometer. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Protein concentration in four solutions was found with the biuret test and a colorimeter. Standards of 0, 1.0, 2.0, 3.0 and 4.0 mg cm⁻³ gave absorbances of 0.00, 0.12, 0.24, 0.36 and 0.48. (a) Describe how the calibration graph is drawn. (b) A sample of unknown concentration gave an absorbance of 0.30. Find its concentration. (c) A second sample gave an absorbance of 0.66. Explain why its concentration should not be read from the graph and describe what to do.
H: What is the highest concentration of the standards, and what would the graph have to do to reach 0.66?
A: (a) Absorbance on the y-axis and concentration on the x-axis, with a ruled straight line of best fit (through the origin) because the points lie on a line. [B1]
Each standard is plotted as a small cross or dot in a circle, on a scale that uses more than half of the grid. [B1]
(b) \(0.30 \div 0.12\) (or read across from 0.30 to the line) [M1] \(= 2.5\,\mathrm{mg\,cm^{-3}}\) [A1]
(c) 0.66 is above the highest standard (0.48), so the concentration would be beyond the range of the standards and the line cannot be extended. [B1]
Dilute the sample by a known factor (for example 1 cm³ of sample and 1 cm³ of water, a factor of 2), re-test, read the concentration from the graph and multiply by the dilution factor. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Lipase breaks down the fat in milk into fatty acids and glycerol. The fatty acids lower the pH, so a mixture of milk, sodium carbonate solution and phenolphthalein indicator changes from pink to colourless. A student measures the time for the colour to change at 35 °C. (a) Describe how the method can be modified to investigate the effect of lipase concentration on the time for the colour to change. (b) The sodium carbonate solution is alkaline and irritant. Assess the risk and give a precaution.
H: Which variable now changes, and which one must now be kept the same?
A: (a) The independent variable is the concentration of lipase, which is made by diluting a stock solution (proportional or serial dilution). [B1] Use at least five different concentrations. [B1]
Keep the temperature the same (35 °C in a water bath) and standardise the volume and concentration of the milk, sodium carbonate and indicator. [B1]
Measure the time for the pink colour to disappear in each tube, using a stopwatch, with replicates (at least three) at each concentration. [B1]
(b) Risk: low (the solution is dilute, and the amount used is small). [B1] Precaution: wear eye protection, and wash splashes off the skin with water. [B1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ Pollen grains from one flower were placed in sucrose solutions of five concentrations. After 2 hours, the number of grains with a pollen tube was counted in 50 grains, three times for each concentration.

| sucrose / % | count 1 | count 2 | count 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 5 | 7 | 6 |
| 5 | 21 | 24 | 23 |
| 10 | 35 | 33 | 34 |
| 15 | 28 | 41 | 27 |
| 20 | 10 | 12 | 11 |

(a) Identify the anomalous result. (b) Calculate the mean count for 15% sucrose without it. (c) Calculate the mean percentage of grains that germinated at 10% sucrose. (d) State a conclusion from the results. (e) Suggest how to find the best concentration for pollen germination more precisely.
H: An anomalous result is far from its replicates and does not fit the pattern of the other concentrations.

(b) \((28 + 27) \div 2\) [M1] \(= 27.5\) [A1]
(c) Mean count \(= (35 + 33 + 34) \div 3 = 34\); \(34 \div 50 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 68\%\) [A1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Two preparations of an enzyme, A and B, were compared by the time for a milk suspension to clear. Three replicates for A took 49, 52 and 55 s and for B took 52, 56 and 60 s. The student judged the end point by eye and estimates its uncertainty as ±5 s. (a) Calculate the percentage difference between the mean times, taking A as the standard. (b) Explain why the student cannot conclude that B is slower. (c) Suggest two ways to increase confidence in the conclusion.
H: Compare the difference between the means with the uncertainty, and look at how much the replicates overlap.
A: (a) Means: A \(= 52\) s and B \(= 56\) s. \((56 - 52) \div 52 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 7.7\%\) [A1]
(b) The difference between the means (4 s) is smaller than the uncertainty of the end point (±5 s). [B1]
The replicates overlap (A from 49 to 55 s and B from 52 to 60 s), so the difference could be caused by the scatter of the results. [B1]
(c) Any two from: more replicates; a more objective end point such as a colorimeter reading; a standard tube to compare with; the same person judging every tube. [B1] [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A respirometer contains germinating seeds of mass 2.50 g. Carbon dioxide made by the seeds is absorbed by soda lime, so the coloured liquid in a capillary tube moves towards the seeds as oxygen is used up. The liquid moved 18 mm in 10 min. The capillary tube has an internal diameter of 1.0 mm. (a) Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake in mm³ per gram of seeds per minute. (b) State the purpose of a second tube with glass beads of the same volume as the seeds. (c) The temperature of the room rose during the investigation. A: (a) Volume \(= \pi r^2 h = \pi \times 0.50^2 \times 18\) [M1] \(= 14.1\,\mathrm{mm^3}\) in 10 min, so \(14.137 \div 10 \div 2.50\) [M1] \(= 0.57\,\mathrm{mm^3\,g^{-1}\,min^{-1}}\) [A1]

(c) A rise in temperature expands the gas in the tube, which pushes the liquid away from the seeds and makes the apparent oxygen uptake too low (or alters the rate of respiration of the seeds). [B1]

:::quiz
? A student investigates how the pH of a buffer affects the time for a colour to fade. Which is the independent variable?
+ the pH of the buffer
- the time for the colour to fade
- the colour of the solution
- the temperature of the room
> The independent variable is the one that the student changes: pH. The time is the dependent variable that is measured, and the room temperature is a variable to keep constant.
? Which table heading and entries are written correctly?
+ mass / g, with entries 2.50, 3.10 and 2.75
- mass, with entries 2.50 g, 3.10 g and 2.75 g
- mass (g), with entries 2.5, 3.10 and 2.75
- mass in grams, with entries 2.50 g, 3.1 g and 2.75 g
> The unit goes in the heading after a solidus and never in the body, and the number of decimal places must be the same for every entry. The others put units in the body, or give different numbers of decimal places.
? How should 50 cm³ of a 0.30 mol dm⁻³ solution be made from a 2.0 mol dm⁻³ stock solution?
+ 7.5 cm³ of stock, with water added to a total volume of 50 cm³
- 7.5 cm³ of stock added to 50 cm³ of water
- 30 cm³ of stock, with water added to a total volume of 50 cm³
- 0.30 cm³ of stock, with water added to a total volume of 50 cm³
> \(V_1 = c_2 V_2 / c_1 = 0.30 \times 50 \div 2.0 = 7.5\) cm³, with water added to make 50 cm³. Adding the stock to 50 cm³ of water gives a total of 57.5 cm³ and a weaker solution, 0.26 mol dm⁻³.
? Which is a systematic error?
+ a thermometer that reads 2 °C too high in every measurement
- judging the colour change by eye, sometimes early and sometimes late
- starting the stopwatch a little late on some tubes
- one tube that was not mixed properly
> A systematic error shifts all the readings in the same direction by about the same amount. The others change from reading to reading, so they are random errors or mistakes.
? Which answer to "suggest an improvement to the method" is most likely to score?
+ measure the 1.0 cm³ of enzyme with a 1 cm³ syringe marked every 0.01 cm³, which has a smaller uncertainty
- use more accurate equipment
- be more careful when measuring
- repeat the experiment and avoid human error
> An improvement must be specific and explain why it helps. "More accurate equipment", "be careful" and "avoid human error" are too vague.
? A graph of results covers 10 °C to 50 °C. Which prediction can be made from it?
+ the rate at 35 °C, read from the curve
- the rate at 80 °C, by extending the curve
- the optimum temperature of a different enzyme
- the rate at 0 °C, by extending the line
> A value inside the range of the data is an interpolation and is valid. A value outside the range is an extrapolation, which is not allowed unless the pattern is known to continue.
? What is the purpose of replicates?
+ to calculate a mean and to identify anomalous results
- to remove a systematic error
- to increase the range of the independent variable
- to make sure that every result is exactly the same
> Replicates show how much the results vary and reduce the effect of random errors when the mean is taken. They do not remove a systematic error.
? A student rates the risk of a dilute irritant solution as low. Which is the best precaution to add?
+ wear eye protection, and wash splashes off with water
- be careful
- do not touch the solution
- use a large volume to dilute it more
> A precaution is a specific action that deals with the hazard. "Be careful" is not specific, and a large volume does not remove the hazard to the eyes.

:::recap
- Paper 3 is 2 hours and 40 marks, all for skills: manipulation, measurement and observation (15 to 17), presentation (11 to 13), and analysis, conclusions and evaluation (11 to 13).
- Independent variable: changed (x-axis). Dependent variable: measured (y-axis). Standardise the others. A control shows the effect is caused by the independent variable.
- At least five values of the independent variable, in equal steps; at least three replicates; the mean leaves out anomalous results.
- Dilution: \(c_1 V_1 = c_2 V_2\). Proportional dilutions use calculated volumes; serial dilutions divide by the same factor each time.
- Judge an end point against a standard. Put enzyme and substrate at the temperature of the water bath before mixing.
- Hazard, risk (low, medium or high) and a specific precaution.
- Table: quantity / unit in the heading, no units in the body, the independent variable on the left, consistent decimal places. Graph: independent variable on x, labelled axes, scale using over half the grid, small crosses, best-fit line or ruled lines, no extrapolation.
- Describe with figures and units; explain with the biology. A hypothesis is supported or not, never proved.
- Random errors scatter the results; systematic errors shift them. Name the specific source and a specific improvement. "Human error" scores nothing.
:::
