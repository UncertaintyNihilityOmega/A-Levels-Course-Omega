## How to use this chapter

All the topics are in the course already. Here you practise them in the mixed order the paper uses. Every question has a `Revise` link under its answer that tells you which chapter to reread if you lose marks.

:::steps
1. **Quick questions first** (Section 1). Do each one in about a minute a mark, with a calculator, and mark it straight away.
2. **Then the data-response questions** (Section 2). Cover the answer, write a full response, then compare. Look at which mark types you missed: describing without figures, or explaining without the mechanism.
3. **Extended questions** (Section 3) take 12 to 15 minutes each. Plan the points first, then write.
4. **Statistics set** (Section 4): work out each calculation on paper, then check every step. The paper prints the formula and the critical values; you decide the degrees of freedom and the conclusion.
:::

Stars show difficulty: ★ one idea, ★★ two or three linked ideas, ★★★ several topics together. Mark honestly: give a mark only for a point in the scheme, in words that mean the same.

:::exam Paper 4 gives you the formula, not the method
Every statistics formula is printed in the question that needs it, with a table of critical values. Degrees of freedom are never given. Marks go for: the null hypothesis, the degrees of freedom, the working, the comparison with the critical value and a conclusion in words about the biology (not just "significant").
:::

:::key Which test? Choosing a statistical test
| Question | Data | Test | Degrees of freedom |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do observed counts fit an expected ratio? | frequencies in categories | chi-squared | number of classes − 1 |
| Do two sample means differ? | continuous, roughly normal, similar spread | t-test | \(n_1 + n_2 - 2\) |
| Do two variables change together? | continuous and normal, linear scatter | Pearson | – (use \(n\) pairs) |
| Do two variables change together? | not normal, or ranks, or ordinal | Spearman | – (use \(n\) pairs) |

Chi-squared: calculated value below the critical value means no significant difference. t, Pearson and Spearman: a calculated value (ignoring any minus sign) above the critical value means a significant difference or correlation. Correlation does not show cause.
:::

## Section 1: Quick recall and calculation (15 questions)

These test one skill each. Try to finish each in a minute or two.

:::try Quick questions 1–5
Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.energy) Stearic acid, a fatty acid, is respired completely: \(\ce{C18H36O2 + 26O2 -> 18CO2 + 18H2O}\).

(a) Calculate the respiratory quotient (RQ) for this reaction. [2]

(b) State what an RQ of about 0.7 suggests about the substrate being respired. [1]
A: (a) \(\mathrm{RQ} = \dfrac{\text{CO}_2 \text{ produced}}{\text{O}_2 \text{ taken in}}\) [M1] \(= \dfrac{18}{26} = 0.69\) [A1]

(b) The substrate is lipid (fat) [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.photosynthesis) In a chromatogram of leaf pigments, one spot has its centre 4.2 cm from the origin and the solvent front is 9.0 cm from the origin.

(a) Calculate the \(R_f\) value of the pigment. [2]

(b) Carotene has a higher \(R_f\) value than chlorophyll b. Suggest why. [1]
A: (a) \(R_f = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by the pigment}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}\) [M1] \(= \dfrac{4.2}{9.0} = 0.47\) [A1] (no units: it is a ratio)

(b) Carotene is more soluble in the solvent (or less strongly attracted to the paper) so it is carried further [B1].

Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#bio.dihybrid) A test cross between a heterozygous plant and a homozygous recessive plant is expected to give dominant and recessive offspring in the ratio 1 : 1. A student counted 100 offspring: 58 dominant and 42 recessive. The chi-squared formula is \(\chi^2 = \sum \dfrac{(O - E)^2}{E}\). Critical values at \(p = 0.05\): 1 degree of freedom 3.84; 2 degrees of freedom 5.99; 3 degrees of freedom 7.81.

Calculate \(\chi^2\) and state the conclusion.
A: Expected numbers are 50 and 50 [B1].

\(\chi^2 = \dfrac{(58-50)^2}{50} + \dfrac{(42-50)^2}{50}\) [M1] \(= 1.28 + 1.28 = 2.56\) [A1]

Degrees of freedom = 2 classes − 1 = 1, so the critical value is 3.84 [B1].

\(2.56 < 3.84\), so there is no significant difference between the observed and expected numbers: the results fit a 1 : 1 ratio and any difference is due to chance [B1].

Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#bio.selection) A recessive allele makes frogs albino. In a population of 25 000 frogs, 40 are albino. Use the Hardy–Weinberg equations \(p + q = 1\) and \(p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1\) to calculate the number of frogs expected to be carriers (heterozygous).
A: \(q^2 = \dfrac{40}{25\,000} = 0.0016\) so \(q = 0.04\) [M1] [A1]

\(p = 1 - 0.04 = 0.96\) and \(2pq = 2 \times 0.96 \times 0.04 = 0.0768\) [M1]

Number of carriers \(= 0.0768 \times 25\,000 = 1920\) [A1]

Q: [3 marks] ★ (#bio.biodiversity) On day 1, 72 freshwater shrimps are caught, marked and released. On day 3, 54 are caught and 18 of them are marked. The Lincoln index is \(N = \dfrac{n_1 \times n_2}{m_2}\).

Estimate the population size and state one assumption you made.
A: \(N = \dfrac{72 \times 54}{18}\) [M1] \(= 216\) [A1]

Any one assumption: marked shrimps mix randomly with the rest and are not harmed or made easier to catch by the mark; no (or equal) births, deaths, immigration or emigration between the samples; the mark does not wear off [B1].
:::

:::try Quick questions 6–10
Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.biodiversity) A pond sample contains four species with 28, 12, 6 and 4 individuals. Simpson's index of diversity is \(D = 1 - \sum \left(\dfrac{n}{N}\right)^2\). Calculate \(D\). A second pond has \(D = 0.85\): state what this shows.
A: \(N = 50\), so

\[\begin{aligned} \sum (n/N)^2 &= 0.56^2 + 0.24^2 + 0.12^2 + 0.08^2 \\ &= 0.3136 + 0.0576 + 0.0144 + 0.0064 \\ &= 0.392 \end{aligned}\] [M1]

\(D = 1 - 0.392 = 0.61\) [A1]

The second pond is more diverse (more species and/or a more even spread of individuals) [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.genetech) A PCR starts with 5 copies of a target DNA sequence.

(a) Calculate the number of copies after 12 cycles, assuming every strand is copied each time. [2]

(b) State what happens to the DNA in the first step of each cycle, at about 95 °C. [1]
A: (a) Each cycle doubles the number: \(5 \times 2^{12}\) [M1] \(= 20\,480\) [A1]

(b) The hydrogen bonds between the two strands break, so the DNA denatures into single strands [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.nerves) An action potential travels along a myelinated motor neurone 1.8 m long in 15 ms.

(a) Calculate the speed of conduction in m s⁻¹. [2]

(b) Explain why a myelinated neurone conducts faster than an unmyelinated neurone of the same diameter. [1]
A: (a) \(15\ \mathrm{ms} = 0.015\ \mathrm{s}\); speed \(= \dfrac{1.8}{0.015}\) [M1] \(= 120\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) [A1]

(b) The action potential jumps from one node of Ranvier to the next (saltatory conduction), because depolarisation can only occur at the nodes, so it does not have to be regenerated along the whole length [B1].

Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#bio.synapses) A relaxed sarcomere is 2.4 µm long and its A band is 1.6 µm long. When the muscle contracts the sarcomere shortens to 2.0 µm.

(a) Calculate the total length of the I bands in the relaxed and in the contracted sarcomere. [2]

(b) State the length of the A band in the contracted sarcomere and give the reason. [2]
A: (a) Relaxed: \(2.4 - 1.6 = 0.8\) µm; contracted: \(2.0 - 1.6 = 0.4\) µm [M1] [A1]

(b) 1.6 µm [B1], because the A band is the length of the thick (myosin) filaments, which do not shorten; the thin (actin) filaments slide between them [B1].

Q: [2 marks] ★ (#bio.microscopy) A mitochondrion in an electron micrograph measures 36 mm long. The magnification is ×12 000. Calculate its actual length in µm.
A: Actual length \(= \dfrac{36\ \mathrm{mm}}{12\,000}\) [M1] \(= 0.0030\ \mathrm{mm} = 3.0\ \mu\mathrm{m}\) [A1]
:::

:::try Quick questions 11–15
Q: [3 marks] ★ (#bio.photosynthesis) In the Calvin cycle, name the enzyme that fixes carbon dioxide, the 5-carbon compound it combines with, and the 3-carbon compound formed.
A: Rubisco [B1]; ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP) [B1]; glycerate 3-phosphate (GP) [B1]. (Two molecules of GP form from each RuBP and CO₂.)

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.genecontrol) In \(E.\,coli\), the enzyme β-galactosidase is made only when lactose is present and glucose is absent. Explain how lactose switches on the gene for the enzyme.
A: Lactose binds to the repressor protein [B1], changing its shape so it cannot bind to the operator [B1]; RNA polymerase can then bind to the promoter and transcribe the structural genes [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★ (#bio.homeostasis) State where ADH is made, where it is released from, and how it changes the collecting duct.
A: ADH is made in the hypothalamus [B1] and released from the posterior pituitary gland [B1]; it makes the collecting duct cells more permeable to water by causing more aquaporins to be inserted into their membranes, so more water is reabsorbed [B1].

Q: [2 marks] ★ (#bio.evolution) Distinguish between allopatric and sympatric speciation.
A: Allopatric speciation happens when populations are separated by a geographical barrier [B1]; sympatric speciation happens in the same area, when populations become separated by an ecological or behavioural difference [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.genetech) During gel electrophoresis, DNA fragments move towards the positive electrode, and small fragments travel further than large ones. Explain both observations.
A: DNA is negatively charged because of its phosphate groups, so the fragments move towards the positive electrode [B1]. The gel is a mesh of pores [B1]; small fragments pass through it more easily than large ones, so they move further in the same time [B1].
:::

## Section 2: Data-response questions (8 questions)

Each gives data. Quote figures with units when you describe, and give a biological reason when you explain.

:::try Data 1: respiration in germinating seeds
Q: [8 marks] ★★ (#bio.energy) Oily seeds were germinated and their gas exchange measured at three ages. The results are per hour per 1 g of seeds (invented data).

| age of seedling / days | oxygen taken in / mm³ | carbon dioxide produced / mm³ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 50 | 35 |
| 3 | 64 | 48 |
| 5 | 80 | 76 |

(a) Calculate the RQ on day 1 and on day 5. [2]

(b) Suggest the main respiratory substrate on day 1 and on day 5. Give a reason each time. [2]

(c) Calculate the percentage increase in oxygen uptake from day 1 to day 5. [2]

(d) Explain why oxygen uptake increases as the seedling grows. [2]
H: RQ = carbon dioxide produced divided by oxygen taken in, using the same units.
A: (a) \(\mathrm{RQ} = \dfrac{\mathrm{CO_2}}{\mathrm{O_2}}\) [M1]; day 1: \(35/50 = 0.70\) and day 5: \(76/80 = 0.95\) [A1]

(b) Day 1: lipid, because an RQ of about 0.7 is typical of lipid [B1]. Day 5: carbohydrate (or mainly carbohydrate), because an RQ close to 1.0 is typical of carbohydrate [B1].

(c) \(\dfrac{80 - 50}{50} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 60\%\) [A1]

(d) The seedling grows, so it has more cells (more mitochondria) respiring [B1]; more ATP is needed for cell division, DNA replication, protein synthesis and active transport, so more aerobic respiration takes in more oxygen [B1].

:::try Data 2: an action spectrum
Q: [8 marks] ★★ (#bio.photosynthesis) The graph above shows the rate of photosynthesis at different wavelengths of light.

(a) Describe the shape of the action spectrum. [3]

(b) Explain the shape. [3]

(c) Distinguish between an action spectrum and an absorption spectrum. [1]

(d) Suggest why the action spectrum is not exactly the same shape as the absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a alone. [1]
A: (a) Two peaks, of 90 units at 440 nm (blue) and 100 units at 675 nm (red) [B1]; a low rate between them with a minimum of 15 units at about 550 nm (green) [B1]; the rate rises steeply from 400 nm to the first peak, and the red peak is higher than the blue peak [B1]. (Any three; credit comparing the peak values.)

(b) Chlorophyll a and b absorb red and blue light best, so these wavelengths drive the light-dependent stage fastest [B1]. Accessory pigments, carotene and xanthophyll, absorb in the blue range and pass the energy to chlorophyll a [B1]. Green light is mostly reflected or transmitted, not absorbed, so the rate is lowest there [B1].

(c) An action spectrum shows the rate of photosynthesis at different wavelengths, but an absorption spectrum shows how much light a pigment absorbs at different wavelengths [B1].

(d) Other pigments (chlorophyll b, carotenoids) also absorb light and contribute to the rate [B1].
:::

:::try Data 3: a glucose tolerance test
Q: [9 marks] ★★★ (#bio.glucose) Two volunteers drank a glucose solution at time 0. Their blood glucose concentration was measured over two hours (invented data).

| time / min | healthy person / mmol dm⁻³ | person with type 2 diabetes / mmol dm⁻³ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 4.8 | 5.6 |
| 30 | 7.9 | 11.2 |
| 60 | 6.5 | 13.0 |
| 90 | 5.2 | 12.4 |
| 120 | 4.9 | 11.8 |

(a) Describe the differences between the two sets of results. [3]

(b) Explain the fall in blood glucose concentration in the healthy person between 30 and 90 minutes. [3]

(c) Calculate the percentage increase in the healthy person's blood glucose concentration between 0 and 30 minutes. [2]

(d) Suggest why the blood glucose concentration of the person with type 2 diabetes stays high. [1]
A: (a) The healthy person's concentration peaks at 7.9 mmol dm⁻³ at 30 minutes; the person with diabetes peaks higher, at 13.0 mmol dm⁻³ at 60 minutes [B1]. The healthy person's concentration falls back to 4.9 mmol dm⁻³ (almost the starting value) by 120 minutes [B1]; the person with diabetes is still at 11.8 mmol dm⁻³ [B1].

(b) The high concentration is detected by β cells in the islets of Langerhans, which secrete insulin [B1]. Insulin binds to receptors on liver and muscle cells and increases the uptake of glucose (more glucose transporter proteins in the membrane) [B1]. The liver and muscle convert glucose to glycogen, so blood glucose falls (negative feedback) [B1].

(c) \(\dfrac{7.9 - 4.8}{4.8} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 65\%\) [A1]

(d) The target cells have fewer insulin receptors, or the receptors do not respond (insulin resistance), so glucose uptake and conversion to glycogen are reduced [B1].
:::

:::try Data 4: conduction speed
Q: [8 marks] ★★ (#bio.nerves) Four neurones were compared (invented data).

| neurone | diameter / µm | myelinated | speed / m s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1.0 | no | 1.2 |
| B | 8.0 | no | 3.4 |
| C | 4.0 | yes | 24 |
| D | 16 | yes | 96 |

(a) Describe the effect of myelination and of diameter on speed of conduction. [3]

(b) Explain why myelinated neurones conduct faster. [3]

(c) Calculate the time taken for an action potential to travel 1.2 m along neurone D. Give your answer in ms. [2]
A: (a) Myelinated neurones are much faster than unmyelinated neurones: C is 4 µm wide and conducts at 24 m s⁻¹, but B is wider (8.0 µm) and conducts at only 3.4 m s⁻¹ [B1]. A wider neurone is faster in both groups: A to B (1.0 to 8.0 µm) increases the speed from 1.2 to 3.4 m s⁻¹, and C to D (4.0 to 16 µm) from 24 to 96 m s⁻¹ [B1]. D (myelinated and widest) is fastest, at 96 m s⁻¹ [B1]. (Any three, quoting data.)

(b) The myelin sheath is an electrical insulator, so ions cannot cross the membrane where it is present [B1]. Depolarisation occurs only at the nodes of Ranvier [B1]; local circuits of current carry the depolarisation from one node to the next, so the action potential "jumps" (saltatory conduction) [B1].

(c) \(\text{time} = \dfrac{1.2}{96}\) [M1] \(= 0.0125\ \mathrm{s} = 12.5\ \mathrm{ms}\) [A1]
:::

:::try Data 5: gibberellin and stem height
Q: [9 marks] ★★★ (#bio.genecontrol) Pea plants have the gene \(Le\), which codes for an enzyme needed to make gibberellin. The allele \(le\) codes for a non-functional enzyme. Plants of two genotypes were grown with or without added gibberellin (invented data, mean of 20 plants).

| genotype | gibberellin added? | mean height / cm |
|---|---|---|
| \(LeLe\) | no | 84 |
| \(LeLe\) | yes | 91 |
| \(lele\) | no | 23 |
| \(lele\) | yes | 80 |

(a) Describe the results. [3]

(b) Explain why \(lele\) plants grow so little without added gibberellin. [2]

(c) Explain how gibberellin makes the stem elongate. [2]

(d) Calculate the percentage increase in height of the \(lele\) plants caused by gibberellin. [2]
A: (a) Without gibberellin, \(lele\) plants (23 cm) are much shorter than \(LeLe\) plants (84 cm) [B1]. Gibberellin raised the height of \(lele\) plants to 80 cm, almost the same as \(LeLe\) plants [B1]. It had only a small effect on \(LeLe\) plants (84 to 91 cm) [B1].

(b) The \(le\) allele codes for a non-functional enzyme in the pathway that makes gibberellin [B1], so little gibberellin is made and the stem does not elongate [B1].

(c) Gibberellin causes the breakdown of DELLA proteins [B1], which inhibit the transcription factors that switch on growth genes; with DELLA gone, the transcription factors increase transcription, so cells elongate [B1].

(d) \(\dfrac{80 - 23}{23} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 248\%\), which is 250% to 2 significant figures [A1]
:::

:::try Data 6: stabilising selection
Q: [8 marks] ★★ (#bio.selection) The percentage of chicks that survive to leave the nest was recorded for broods of different sizes in a bird population (invented data).

| clutch size | chicks surviving / % |
|---|---|
| 2 | 52 |
| 3 | 74 |
| 4 | 88 |
| 5 | 70 |
| 6 | 55 |
| 7 | 31 |

(a) Describe the relationship between clutch size and survival. [2]

(b) Name the type of selection shown and explain how it operates here. [4]

(c) Predict the effect on the mean and on the range of clutch size over many generations. [2]
A: (a) Survival rises from 52% at a clutch of 2 to a maximum of 88% at a clutch of 4 [B1], then falls to 31% at a clutch of 7 [B1].

(b) Stabilising selection [B1]. Birds with an intermediate clutch (3 to 5 eggs) have more chicks that survive [B1]; birds with very small or very large clutches have lower survival (for example, too little food for each chick in large broods) [B1]; so alleles for intermediate clutch size are passed on more often and the others less often [B1].

(c) The mean clutch size stays about the same (4) [B1]; the range (variation) becomes smaller [B1].

:::try Data 7: a bottleneck
Q: [9 marks] ★★★ (#bio.conservation) An island bird declined and was then bred in captivity (invented data). The number of alleles at one gene locus was counted each time.

| year | adult birds | alleles at the locus |
|---|---|---|
| 1970 | 400 | 9 |
| 1985 | 12 | 3 |
| 2005 | 95 | 3 |
| 2020 | 310 | 3 |

(a) Describe the change in the number of adult birds between 1970 and 2020. [2]

(b) Explain the decrease in the number of alleles between 1970 and 1985. [3]

(c) The population increased from 1985 to 2020 but the number of alleles did not. Explain why, and suggest one risk for the population. [2]

(d) Calculate the percentage decrease in adult birds from 1970 to 1985. [2]
A: (a) The number fell from 400 in 1970 to 12 in 1985 [B1], then increased to 310 by 2020, which is still below the 1970 figure [B1].

(b) The population was cut to very few birds (a bottleneck) [B1], so only a small sample of the alleles was passed on, and rare alleles were lost by chance (genetic drift) [B1]. Alleles that disappear cannot return unless there is new mutation or immigration [B1]. (Any three; credit the founder effect idea for a captive population started from few birds.)

(c) All the birds descend from the few survivors, so no alleles other than the 3 that survived exist to be passed on [B1]. The low genetic variation (and inbreeding) makes the population vulnerable: one disease or change could affect all the birds, and harmful recessive alleles are more likely to be homozygous [B1]. (Any sensible risk.)

(d) \(\dfrac{400 - 12}{400} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 97\%\) [A1]
:::

:::try Data 8: restriction fragments in a family
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.genetech) A DNA sample from each person was cut with a restriction endonuclease and the fragments separated by gel electrophoresis. The fragment sizes of one stretch of DNA are in base pairs (invented data). One allele has a recognition site in this stretch (fragments 800 and 300); the other allele does not (one fragment of 1100). The recessive disorder is caused by the allele without the site.

| person | fragments seen / bp |
|---|---|
| mother | 1100, 800, 300 |
| father | 1100, 800, 300 |
| child 1 | 1100 |
| child 2 | 800, 300 |
| child 3 | 1100, 800, 300 |

(a) Explain why the mother's DNA gives three bands. [2]

(b) State which child has the disorder and give a reason. [2]

(c) Suggest how a mutation could stop the enzyme from cutting this allele. [2]

(d) State why 800 + 300 equals 1100. [1]

(e) Calculate the probability that another child of these parents will have the disorder. [1]
A: (a) She is heterozygous [B1]: one allele is cut into 800 and 300 bp, and the other is not cut and gives 1100 bp [B1].

(b) Child 1 [B1], because it has only the uncut 1100 bp fragment, so it is homozygous for the allele without the recognition site [B1].

(c) A base substitution (or other mutation) changes the base sequence of the recognition site [B1], so the site no longer has a shape complementary to the active site of the restriction endonuclease [B1].

(d) The same length of DNA is cut into two pieces, so the lengths add up to the uncut length (the mutation does not change the length) [B1].

(e) \(\dfrac14\) or 0.25 [B1] (both parents are carriers).

## Section 3: Extended synoptic questions (6 questions)

These need long, structured answers that combine topics. Plan the points first, then write a sentence per mark.

:::try Extended 1: chemiosmosis in two organelles
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ (#bio.respiration) ATP is made by chemiosmosis in both mitochondria and chloroplasts.

(a) Describe how ATP is made in the inner membrane of a mitochondrion. [5]

(b) State two similarities and one difference between ATP synthesis in a mitochondrion and in the thylakoid membrane of a chloroplast. [3]

(c) Cyanide stops the last carrier in the electron transport chain from passing electrons to oxygen. Explain why ATP production stops. [2]
H: In (b), compare the source of the energy that moves the electrons.
A: (a) Reduced NAD and reduced FAD deliver hydrogen atoms to carriers in the inner membrane [B1]; the hydrogen atoms split into protons and energetic electrons [B1]; the electrons pass along the electron transport chain, releasing energy at each step [B1]; the energy is used to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space, making a proton gradient [B1]; the protons flow back through ATP synthase (facilitated diffusion), which makes ATP from ADP and phosphate (chemiosmosis) [B1]. (Also credit: oxygen is the final electron acceptor, forming water.)

(b) Similarities, any two: electrons pass along an electron transport chain releasing energy; the energy pumps protons across a membrane, making a proton gradient; protons return through ATP synthase, which makes ATP [B1] [B1]. Difference: in the chloroplast the energy to move electrons comes from light absorbed by chlorophyll (photoactivation), but in the mitochondrion it comes from the oxidation of reduced NAD and reduced FAD [B1].

(c) Electrons cannot be passed on, so the carriers stay reduced and the chain stops, releasing no energy [B1]; no protons are pumped, there is no proton gradient, and ATP synthase makes no ATP [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 2: sweating and ADH
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ (#bio.homeostasis) A runner loses 2 dm³ of sweat during a race and does not drink.

(a) Explain the effect on the water potential of the blood plasma. [2]

(b) Describe how antidiuretic hormone (ADH) helps to restore the water potential. [6]

(c) The runner's blood contains glucose but the urine contains none. Explain why. [2]
A: (a) The plasma loses water but not solutes (sweat is mostly water) [B1], so the plasma is more concentrated and its water potential becomes more negative (lower) [B1].

(b) Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect the fall in water potential [B1]; the hypothalamus stimulates the posterior pituitary gland to release more ADH [B1]; ADH travels in the blood and binds to receptors on collecting duct cells [B1]; more aquaporins are inserted into the cell surface membranes, so the collecting duct becomes more permeable to water [B1]; more water is reabsorbed by osmosis down the water potential gradient into the medulla and blood [B1]; a small volume of concentrated urine is produced, the plasma water potential rises towards normal and the osmoreceptors cause less ADH to be released (negative feedback) [B1].

(c) Glucose is a small molecule, so it is filtered from the blood into the Bowman's capsule [B1]; all of it is reabsorbed by the cells of the proximal convoluted tubule (by co-transport and facilitated diffusion), so none is left in the urine [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 3: insulin from gene to receptor
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ (#bio.genetech2) Human insulin is made by bacteria that contain the human gene.

(a) Describe how the human insulin gene can be obtained and put into bacteria. [5]

(b) Explain why a bacterium can make human insulin from the human gene. [1]

(c) Explain how insulin lowers the blood glucose concentration when it reaches a liver or muscle cell. [4]
A: (a) mRNA for insulin is extracted from β cells of the pancreas, which make a lot of it [B1]; reverse transcriptase makes a DNA copy (cDNA) of the mRNA, and DNA polymerase makes it double stranded [B1]; a plasmid is cut open with a restriction endonuclease, leaving sticky ends [B1]; the gene, with a promoter, is joined into the plasmid by DNA ligase, forming recombinant DNA [B1]; the recombinant plasmids are taken up by bacteria, and a marker gene (for a fluorescent product) is used to identify the bacteria that took them up [B1]. (Any five; credit cutting the gene out of human DNA with the same restriction enzyme.)

(b) The genetic code is universal, so the bacterium's ribosomes translate the human mRNA to the same amino acid sequence [B1].

(c) Insulin binds to specific receptors on the cell surface membrane, because its shape is complementary to the receptor [B1]. More glucose transporter proteins are placed in the membrane, so more glucose enters by facilitated diffusion [B1]. Enzymes are activated that convert glucose to glycogen [B1]. The blood glucose concentration falls, and this stops the release of insulin (negative feedback) [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 4: natural selection and antibiotic resistance
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ (#bio.antibiotics) A hospital ward treated many patients with the same antibiotic. Over two years, most of the bacteria of one species on the ward became resistant to it.

(a) Explain how the population of bacteria became mostly resistant. [6]

(b) Describe how resistance can spread between bacteria that are not related by reproduction. [2]

(c) Suggest two ways in which hospitals can slow the spread of antibiotic resistance. [2]
A: (a) A gene mutated by chance (random mutation), making some bacteria resistant, before the antibiotic was used [B1]. The antibiotic was a selection pressure (an environmental factor that selects) [B1]; sensitive bacteria were killed, but resistant bacteria survived [B1]; the survivors reproduced with less competition [B1]; they passed the resistance allele to their offspring [B1]; so the frequency of the resistance allele increased in each generation [B1].

(b) A resistance gene on a plasmid is copied [B1] and the plasmid is passed from one bacterium to another (even of a different species), so the recipient is resistant without reproducing [B1]. (Credit conjugation.)

(c) Any two: do not prescribe antibiotics unnecessarily (for example for viral infections); complete the full course; use a narrow-spectrum antibiotic when possible; isolate infected patients; high standards of hygiene [B1] [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 5: speciation and meiosis
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ (#bio.evolution) Two populations of one lizard species were separated when rising sea level split an island into two.

(a) Explain how the two populations may become two species. [5]

(b) Explain how meiosis produces genetic variation among the gametes of a lizard. [3]

(c) A horse has 64 chromosomes and a donkey has 62. A mule, the hybrid of the two, has 63 chromosomes and is infertile. Explain why. [2]
A: (a) The sea is a geographical barrier, so there is no gene flow between the populations [B1]. The two islands have different environments, so there are different selection pressures [B1], and different alleles are selected, with different mutations in each population [B1], so the allele frequencies in the two gene pools change in different ways [B1]. In time, individuals from the two populations can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so they are different species [B1].

(b) Crossing over between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes in prophase I swaps alleles, making new combinations [B1]. Independent assortment: homologous pairs line up in random orientation at metaphase I, so each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes [B1]. The random orientation of chromatids at metaphase II adds further variation [B1].

(c) The mule has an odd number of chromosomes (63), so some chromosomes have no homologous partner [B1]; they cannot pair in prophase I, so meiosis cannot be completed and no gametes form [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 6: gene therapy and the immune response
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ (#bio.genetech2) Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) can be caused by a lack of the enzyme adenosine deaminase (ADA). It has been treated by gene therapy.

(a) Explain why a person with SCID is very susceptible to infection. [2]

(b) Describe how gene therapy with a viral vector could treat SCID. [3]

(c) Explain why the immune response could make gene therapy with a viral vector less effective. [3]

(d) Describe one risk of gene therapy. [2]
A: (a) Without ADA, the T and B lymphocytes die or fail to develop, so there are few of them [B1]; with few lymphocytes, few antibodies are made and the person cannot mount an adequate immune response to pathogens [B1].

(b) A functional copy of the ADA gene is put into a harmless viral vector [B1]; the vector infects the patient's cells (for example bone marrow stem cells that have been removed, then returned) [B1]; the gene is transcribed and translated to make functional ADA in the patient's cells [B1].

(c) The vector has antigens that the immune system may recognise as non-self, so T-helper cells activate B cells, which divide into plasma cells that make antibodies against the vector [B1]. The vector is destroyed before it can deliver the gene [B1]. Memory cells make a quicker and larger response to a second dose [B1].

(d) The gene might insert into the wrong place in the genome and disrupt another gene (or switch on a cancer-causing gene) [B1], which could cause a harmful effect such as cancer [B1]. (Credit: the effect may be only temporary; very high cost; ethical concerns about changing germ-line cells.)
:::

## Section 4: Statistics set (6 questions)

The formula and critical values are printed, as in Paper 4. Always state degrees of freedom and compare with the critical value.

:::try Statistics 1–3
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#bio.dihybrid) Two plants heterozygous for two genes (on different chromosomes) were crossed. A 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio is expected. Of 320 offspring, the numbers in the four phenotype classes were 171, 68, 57 and 24. \(\chi^2 = \sum \dfrac{(O-E)^2}{E}\). Critical values at \(p = 0.05\): 1 degree of freedom 3.84; 2 degrees of freedom 5.99; 3 degrees of freedom 7.81; 4 degrees of freedom 9.49.

(a) State the null hypothesis. [1]

(b) Calculate the expected numbers. [1]

(c) Calculate \(\chi^2\). [2]

(d) State the degrees of freedom and the conclusion. [2]
A: (a) There is no significant difference between the observed and expected numbers (they fit 9 : 3 : 3 : 1), and any difference is due to chance [B1].

(b) 180, 60, 60 and 20 [B1]

(c)

\[\begin{aligned} \chi^2 &= \dfrac{(171-180)^2}{180} + \dfrac{(68-60)^2}{60} + \dfrac{(57-60)^2}{60} + \dfrac{(24-20)^2}{20} \\ &= 0.45 + 1.07 + 0.15 + 0.80 \end{aligned}\] [M1]

\(\chi^2 = 2.47\) [A1]

(d) Degrees of freedom \(= 4 - 1 = 3\), critical value 7.81 [B1]. \(2.47 < 7.81\), so the difference is not significant: accept the null hypothesis; the results fit a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 ratio [B1].

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (#bio.variation) The areas of ivy leaves from a sunny wall and a shady wall were compared. Sun: 12 leaves, mean 34.2 cm², standard deviation 4.1. Shade: 12 leaves, mean 29.6 cm², standard deviation 3.6. \(t = \dfrac{|\bar x_1 - \bar x_2|}{\sqrt{\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}}}\). Critical values of \(t\) at \(p = 0.05\): 20 degrees of freedom 2.09; 21, 2.08; 22, 2.07; 23, 2.07; 24, 2.06.

(a) State the degrees of freedom. [1]

(b) Calculate \(t\). [2]

(c) State the conclusion. [1]

(d) State two conditions that must be true for a \(t\)-test to be valid. [2]
A: (a) \(12 + 12 - 2 = 22\) [B1]

(b) \(t = \dfrac{|34.2 - 29.6|}{\sqrt{\dfrac{4.1^2}{12} + \dfrac{3.6^2}{12}}} = \dfrac{4.6}{\sqrt{1.401 + 1.080}}\) [M1] \(= 2.92\) [A1]

(c) \(2.92 > 2.07\), so the difference in mean leaf area is significant: reject the null hypothesis [B1].

(d) Any two: the data are continuous; the data are normally distributed; the standard deviations of the two samples are similar; the samples are random and independent [B1] [B1].

Q: [7 marks] ★★★ (#bio.biodiversity) The soil water content (%) and moss cover (%) were measured at eight sites.

| site | soil water / % | moss cover / % |
|---|---|---|
| A | 23 | 14 |
| B | 41 | 52 |
| C | 15 | 20 |
| D | 36 | 33 |
| E | 52 | 68 |
| F | 28 | 24 |
| G | 47 | 57 |
| H | 33 | 41 |

\(r_s = 1 - \dfrac{6\sum D^2}{n^3 - n}\). The critical value of \(r_s\) at \(p = 0.05\) for 8 pairs is 0.738.

(a) Rank the data (rank 1 for the highest value), calculate \(\sum D^2\) and then \(r_s\). [4]

(b) State the conclusion. [1]

(c) Explain why the result does not prove that soil water content causes moss cover. [1]

(d) Explain why Spearman's rank correlation is chosen rather than Pearson's. [1]
A: (a) Ranks of soil water: A 7, B 3, C 8, D 4, E 1, F 6, G 2, H 5; ranks of moss cover: A 8, B 3, C 7, D 5, E 1, F 6, G 2, H 4 [B1]. Differences \(D\): −1, 0, 1, −1, 0, 0, 0, 1, so \(\sum D^2 = 4\) [B1]. \(r_s = 1 - \dfrac{6 \times 4}{8^3 - 8} = 1 - \dfrac{24}{504}\) [M1] \(= 0.95\) [A1]

(b) \(0.95 > 0.738\), so there is a significant positive correlation between soil water and moss cover [B1].

(c) A correlation shows that the variables change together, not that one causes the other; another factor (for example shade or soil type) could affect both [B1].

(d) Spearman is used when the data are not known to be normally distributed (or are ranks); it makes no assumption about the distribution [B1]. (Accept: the relationship may not be linear.)
:::

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (#bio.stats) The temperature (°C) and the growth rate of a culture (mm per day) were measured at eight temperatures: 12, 15, 18, 21, 24, 27, 30, 33 °C. A student plotted the points, which lie close to a straight line. The student started the Pearson calculation: \(\bar x = 22.5\), \(\bar y = 5.1875\), \(s_x = 7.35\), \(s_y = 1.49\), \(\sum xy = 1009.2\) and \(n = 8\). \(r = \dfrac{\sum xy - n\bar x \bar y}{(n-1)\, s_x s_y}\). The critical value of \(r\) at \(p = 0.05\) for 8 pairs is 0.707.

(a) Complete the calculation of \(r\). [3]

(b) State the conclusion. [1]

(c) State two reasons why Pearson's correlation is suitable for these data. [2]
A: (a) Numerator: \(1009.2 - 8 \times 22.5 \times 5.1875 = 1009.2 - 933.75 = 75.45\) [M1]. Denominator: \(7 \times 7.35 \times 1.49 = 76.66\) [M1]. \(r = \dfrac{75.45}{76.66} = 0.98\) [A1]

(b) \(0.98 > 0.707\), so there is a significant positive correlation between temperature and growth rate [B1].

(c) The scatter graph shows a straight-line relationship [B1]; the data are continuous (measurements) and normally distributed (or at least 5 pairs of data) [B1].

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (#bio.stats) Two groups of 25 seedlings were grown with different fertilisers and their heights measured. Group A: mean 18.4 cm, standard deviation 2.6 cm. Group B: mean 16.9 cm, standard deviation 2.2 cm. Use \(SE = \dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}\) and 95% confidence interval = mean ± 2 × SE.

(a) Calculate the standard error and the 95% confidence interval for group A. [3]

(b) The 95% confidence interval for group B is 16.0 to 17.8 cm (to 1 decimal place of cm). Compare the two intervals. [1]

(c) State what the comparison suggests about the difference between the means and what further step is needed. [2]
A: (a) \(SE = \dfrac{2.6}{\sqrt{25}} = 0.52\) cm [M1] [A1]; 95% CI \(= 18.4 \pm 2 \times 0.52\), which is 17.4 to 19.4 cm [B1]

(b) The intervals overlap, from 17.4 to 17.8 cm [B1].

(c) Because the intervals overlap, the difference between the means may not be significant (it could be due to chance) [B1]; a statistical test such as a \(t\)-test is needed to decide [B1].

Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#bio.stats) Name the most suitable statistical test for each investigation, with one reason. [5]

(i) Counting purple-flowered and white-flowered offspring and comparing them with a 3 : 1 ratio.

(ii) Comparing the mean mass of mice fed on two different diets, with 14 mice in each group and normally distributed masses.

(iii) Looking for a relationship between the percentage cover of a lichen and the distance from a road when the lichen data are strongly skewed.

(iv) Looking for a relationship between leaf length and leaf width, both normally distributed, which lie on a straight line on a scatter graph.

(v) Comparing the number of beetles found in four habitat types with the numbers expected if the beetles were spread evenly.
A: (i) Chi-squared, because the data are frequencies in categories compared with an expected ratio [B1]

(ii) t-test, because it compares the means of two samples of continuous, normally distributed data [B1]

(iii) Spearman's rank correlation, because the data are not normally distributed [B1]

(iv) Pearson's correlation, because both variables are continuous and normal with a linear relationship [B1]

(v) Chi-squared, because the data are observed frequencies compared with expected frequencies [B1]
