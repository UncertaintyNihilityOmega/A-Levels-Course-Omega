@summary Bacteria are prokaryotic cells: small, with a peptidoglycan wall, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes and no nucleus or other organelles with double membranes. You will compare a bacterium with plant and animal cells, calculate bacterial sizes from micrographs, and learn what all viruses have in common: a nucleic acid core inside a protein capsid, with an envelope in some.

:::why
Bacteria cause diseases such as cholera and tuberculosis, but they also digest our food, make yoghurt and recycle the dead. Viruses cause influenza, measles and HIV/AIDS. To understand why antibiotics kill bacteria but not viruses or our own cells, you first need to know what is different about their structure. These differences are also the evidence for how all cells are related, and exam questions often ask you to compare the three kinds of structure in a table or in words.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know the organelles of eukaryotic cells and what 80S and 70S ribosomes are from [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells), and how to calculate sizes from micrographs from [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy).

## Prokaryotic cells

Cells fall into two groups. A eukaryotic cell has a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. A **prokaryotic cell** has neither: its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm.

:::def Prokaryotic cell
A cell with no nucleus (its DNA is not enclosed by a membrane) and no organelles surrounded by double membranes. Bacteria are prokaryotes.
:::

The typical prokaryote in this course is a **bacterium**. These are its structural features.

:::key Structure of a typical bacterium
- **Unicellular**: each bacterium is one cell that lives and reproduces on its own.
- **Small**: generally **1–5 µm** in diameter, roughly ten times smaller than most eukaryotic cells.
- **Cell wall made of peptidoglycan**, a polymer of sugar chains cross-linked by short chains of amino acids. It is rigid and strong, and it is **not** cellulose.
- **Cell surface membrane** inside the wall, which controls what enters and leaves, as in eukaryotic cells.
- **Circular DNA**: one loop of DNA in the cytoplasm, not surrounded by a nuclear envelope and not wound around histone proteins (the proteins that DNA is wound around in eukaryotes) as eukaryotic DNA is. The region where it lies is the **nucleoid**.
- **70S ribosomes** in the cytoplasm, which make proteins. They are smaller than the 80S ribosomes of eukaryotic cells.
- **No organelles surrounded by double membranes**: no nucleus, mitochondria or chloroplasts. Bacteria also have no endoplasmic reticulum or Golgi body.
:::

Some bacteria have further structures. These are not found in all bacteria, so do not write them as "features of all bacteria":
- **Plasmids**: small loops of circular DNA, separate from the main loop, that carry extra genes (a gene is a length of DNA that codes for one polypeptide), such as genes for resistance to antibiotics.
- **Capsule**: a layer of slime outside the cell wall that protects the cell and helps it to stick to surfaces.
- **Flagellum** (plural flagella): a long, whip-like tail made of protein that rotates and moves the bacterium.
- **Pili** (singular pilus): short, hair-like projections that attach the cell to surfaces and to other cells.

:::warn Bacteria do have DNA and ribosomes
A common wrong answer is "bacteria have no DNA" or "no ribosomes". They have both: the DNA is circular and lies free in the cytoplasm, and the ribosomes are the smaller 70S type. What bacteria lack is a **nucleus** and any organelle with a **double** membrane. Do not write "bacteria have a nucleus", or that "70S ribosomes are found only in bacteria": mitochondria and chloroplasts have them too.
:::

:::note Why is the bacterial cell wall important?
Peptidoglycan is found only in bacteria, not in plant, animal or fungal cells. Some antibiotics, such as penicillin, stop bacteria making new peptidoglycan cross-links, so the wall becomes weak and the cell bursts (water enters the cell by osmosis and pushes outwards). They do not harm human cells, which have no cell wall. This is explained in [Antibiotics and resistance](#bio.antibiotics).
:::

## Comparing prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

| Feature | Bacterium | Animal cell | Plant cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| size | generally 1–5 µm | about 10–30 µm | about 10–100 µm |
| nucleus | absent: DNA free in cytoplasm | present | present |
| DNA | circular, not wound around histones, no nuclear envelope | linear, in chromosomes inside the nucleus | linear, in chromosomes inside the nucleus |
| ribosomes in the cytoplasm | 70S | 80S | 80S |
| mitochondria | absent | present (with 70S ribosomes and circular DNA) | present |
| chloroplasts | absent | absent | present in photosynthetic cells |
| cell wall | present: peptidoglycan | absent | present: cellulose |
| membrane-bound organelles (ER, Golgi body, lysosomes) | absent | present | present (no lysosomes usually drawn) |
| large permanent vacuole | absent | absent | present |

A good comparison answer states a feature of **both** cell types in each sentence: "a bacterium has circular DNA free in the cytoplasm, whereas a plant cell has linear DNA inside a nucleus". It is also worth remembering the sizes. A bacterium is about the size of a mitochondrion, which is one of the reasons for the suggestion that mitochondria evolved from bacteria (see the note in [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells)).

:::eg Compare the structure of a bacterium with that of a plant cell.
This is a 5-mark question. Each point compares both cells.

A bacterium has no nucleus, and its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, whereas a plant cell has its DNA inside a nucleus. [B1]

The DNA of a bacterium is circular, but plant DNA is linear (in chromosomes). [B1]

A bacterium has 70S ribosomes in its cytoplasm, whereas a plant cell has larger 80S ribosomes. [B1]

The wall of a bacterium is made of peptidoglycan, but the wall of a plant cell is made of cellulose. [B1]

A bacterium has no membrane-bound organelles (no mitochondria or chloroplasts), whereas a plant cell has mitochondria, chloroplasts and a Golgi body. [B1]

(Also credit: a bacterium is much smaller, 1–5 µm, than a plant cell, about 10–100 µm; a plant cell has a large permanent vacuole; a bacterium may have a capsule or flagellum.)

A weaker answer says "bacteria are smaller and simpler than plant cells". That gives no structural feature, so it scores nothing.
:::

:::eg A transmission electron micrograph of a rod-shaped bacterium has a scale bar 20 mm long, labelled 0.5 µm. The bacterium is 72 mm long and 26 mm wide. Calculate the magnification and the actual length and width of the bacterium.
**Magnification**: convert the bar to µm (\(20\,\mathrm{mm} = 20\,000\,\mathrm{\mu m}\)).

\[\begin{aligned} M &= \frac{I}{A} \\ &= \frac{20\,000\,\mathrm{\mu m}}{0.5\,\mathrm{\mu m}} \\ &= 40\,000 \end{aligned}\]

**Length** (use the ratio method, as in [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy)):

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{72}{20} \times 0.5\,\mathrm{\mu m} \\ &= 1.8\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

**Width**: \(\dfrac{26}{20} \times 0.5 = 0.65\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).

The magnification is ×40 000, and the bacterium is 1.8 µm long and 0.65 µm wide. The length lies in the range of 1–5 µm quoted for bacteria. The width is a little below it, which is possible because 1–5 µm is a general range, not a limit.
:::

Bacteria are big enough to see with a light microscope, as tiny dots or rods. A light microscope cannot show their inside: structures smaller than about 200 nm (0.2 µm) cannot be resolved. An electron microscope shows the cell wall and the DNA region clearly.

:::key What every virus has, and what it lacks
- **A nucleic acid core**: DNA or RNA (never both in the same virus).
- **A capsid**: a coat of protein subunits around the nucleic acid.
- **Some viruses have an envelope**, a layer of phospholipid (from the host cell) with proteins in it. Viruses with no envelope are called non-enveloped.
- A virus is **not a cell**. It has no cytoplasm, no cell surface membrane (the envelope, where present, is not one), no ribosomes and no way of releasing energy for itself.
- A virus is very small: about **20–300 nm**, much smaller than a bacterium, and below the resolution of a light microscope (200 nm) for many viruses.
:::

A virus can reproduce only **inside a host cell**. It attaches to a host cell, gets its nucleic acid inside and uses the cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to make new virus particles. Outside a host cell it does nothing. HIV, which causes AIDS, is an enveloped RNA virus (see [Infectious diseases](#bio.disease)).

:::note Are viruses alive?
Biologists disagree. Viruses have genetic material (which can mutate and evolve) and they reproduce. But they are not cells, show no metabolism (the chemical reactions of a cell) or growth, and cannot reproduce without a host. Many biologists therefore describe them as non-living particles that take over living cells, or as on the border of life. The syllabus does not ask you to decide, only to describe their structure accurately: **non-cellular**.
:::

Because viruses have no cell wall, no ribosomes of their own and no metabolism (no chemical reactions of their own), **antibiotics do not work against them**. Antibiotics target bacterial structures and processes: the peptidoglycan wall, the 70S ribosome, bacterial enzymes.

:::eg A particle is about 100 nm across. It has a core of RNA surrounded by a protein coat, outside which is a membrane of phospholipid with protein spikes. Identify the particle and state three reasons why it is not a cell.
**Identification**: a core of nucleic acid (RNA) inside a protein coat (the capsid) with an outer phospholipid layer (the envelope) is an **enveloped virus**.

**Three reasons it is not a cell**, in marking-point form:

It has a core of RNA only, with no cytoplasm or nucleus; it is non-cellular. [B1]

It has no ribosomes, so it cannot make proteins on its own. [B1]

It cannot release energy (no respiration) or reproduce outside a host cell. [B1]

(Also credit: its size, 100 nm, is much smaller than a bacterium (1–5 µm).)

**Size check**: a bacterium of 2 µm is \(2000\,\mathrm{nm} \div 100\,\mathrm{nm} = 20\) times as long as this particle. At a magnification of ×200 000 the particle's image would be \(100\,\mathrm{nm} \times 200\,000 = 20\,000\,000\,\mathrm{nm} = 20\,\mathrm{mm}\) long: only an electron microscope can reach that magnification and still resolve it.
:::

:::eg Real context, penicillin: explain why penicillin kills growing bacteria but does not harm human cells or kill viruses.
This is a 4-mark question.

Penicillin stops the bacterium making the cross-links in its peptidoglycan cell wall. [B1]

The wall becomes weak and cannot resist the pressure of the cell contents (water enters the cell by osmosis and pushes outwards; see [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement)), so the cell bursts. [B1]

Human cells have no peptidoglycan cell wall, so they are not affected. [B1]

Viruses have no cell wall or metabolism of their own (they are non-cellular), so there is nothing for penicillin to act on. [B1]
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State the usual range of diameters of a bacterium, in µm and in nm.
A: 1–5 µm, which is 1000–5000 nm.
Q: Name the polymer that makes up the cell wall of a bacterium. How does it differ from the cell wall of a plant?
A: Peptidoglycan. A plant cell wall is made of cellulose.
Q: A bacterium is 3 µm long. Its image in a micrograph is 15 mm long. Calculate the magnification.
A: \(15 \times 1000 = 15\,000\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), then \(15\,000 \div 3 = \times 5000\).
Q: State three features that a bacterium has that an animal cell does not.
A: Any three of: a cell wall of peptidoglycan, circular DNA free in the cytoplasm, 70S ribosomes in the cytoplasm, a plasmid, a capsule, a flagellum.
Q: What is a plasmid, and why might it be important to a bacterium?
A: A small loop of circular DNA, separate from the main loop of DNA. It can carry extra genes, such as genes for resistance to antibiotics.
Q: State two reasons why antibiotics that damage the bacterial cell wall do not damage human cells.
A: Human cells have no cell wall, and certainly no peptidoglycan; the antibiotic acts on a structure that human cells lack.
Q: A virus particle is 80 nm wide. Calculate the width of its image in mm at a magnification of ×150 000.
A: \(80 \times 150\,000 = 12\,000\,000\,\mathrm{nm} = 12\,\mathrm{mm}\).
Q: Can a bacterium of 2 µm and a virus of 100 nm be seen with a light microscope? Explain.
A: The bacterium can (2 µm is greater than the resolution of about 200 nm, so it is seen as a small particle). The virus cannot: 100 nm is smaller than 200 nm, so it cannot be resolved.
Q: Explain why viruses are described as non-cellular.
A: A virus has no cytoplasm, cell surface membrane or ribosomes; it is just a nucleic acid core in a protein capsid (and an envelope in some), not a cell.
Q: Give two differences between bacterial DNA and the DNA of a human cell.
A: Bacterial DNA is circular, human DNA is linear (in chromosomes); bacterial DNA lies free in the cytoplasm with no nuclear envelope, human DNA is inside a nucleus.
Q: Which are found in the cytoplasm of a yeast cell (a fungus): 70S ribosomes or 80S ribosomes?
A: 80S ribosomes, because yeast cells are eukaryotic.
:::

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A scientist wants to see a virus of width 120 nm so that its image is 6 mm wide. (a) Calculate the magnification needed. (b) A student suggests using a light microscope with a total magnification of ×1500. Evaluate this suggestion. (c) Suggest why the virus must be dead when it is viewed in an electron microscope.
H: Convert 6 mm to nm: 1 mm = 1 000 000 nm.
A: (a) \(6\,\mathrm{mm} = 6\,000\,000\,\mathrm{nm}\), \(M = 6\,000\,000 \div 120\) [M1] \(= \times 50\,000\) [A1]
(b) At ×1500 the image would be \(120 \times 1500 = 180\,000\,\mathrm{nm} = 0.18\,\mathrm{mm}\), too small to see. [B1]
More importantly, the virus is smaller than the resolution of a light microscope (200 nm), so it cannot be seen as a separate particle however high the magnification. [B1]
(c) The inside of an electron microscope is a vacuum, and the preparation (fixing and staining) kills the specimen. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ A student states: "Viruses are living organisms because they contain DNA or RNA and they reproduce." Evaluate this statement.
H: Give a point in favour, points against, and a judgement.
A: In favour: viruses have genetic material (DNA or RNA), which can mutate and evolve, and they make copies of themselves. [B1]
Against: they can reproduce only inside a host cell, using its ribosomes, enzymes and ATP. [B1]
Against: they are non-cellular and show no metabolism, growth or response of their own outside a host. [B1]
Judgement: there is no agreed answer, but most characteristics of life are missing, so it is better to describe them as non-living particles, or on the border of life. [B1]
:::

:::recap
- A prokaryotic cell (bacterium): unicellular, generally 1–5 µm, peptidoglycan wall, circular DNA free in the cytoplasm (no nucleus), 70S ribosomes, no organelles with double membranes. Some have plasmids, a capsule, flagella and pili.
- Compared with eukaryotic cells: smaller, no nucleus, circular DNA, 70S (not 80S) ribosomes, peptidoglycan (not cellulose) wall, no mitochondria, chloroplasts, ER or Golgi body.
- Viruses are non-cellular: a nucleic acid core (DNA or RNA) in a protein capsid; some have an outer envelope of phospholipid. They are about 20–300 nm and reproduce only inside host cells.
- Antibiotics target bacterial structures (peptidoglycan wall, 70S ribosomes) so they do not act on viruses.
- 1 µm = 1000 nm: convert before comparing. Light microscope resolution about 200 nm: it cannot resolve most viruses.
:::
