## Hormones and the endocrine system

The body has two coordination systems: the nervous system and the **endocrine system**. The endocrine system uses chemical messengers that travel in the blood.

:::def Hormone
A chemical substance, made by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood, that alters the activity of one or more specific target cells.
:::

:::def Endocrine gland
A gland with no duct: it secretes its hormones directly into the blood.
:::

Every hormone follows the pattern from [Cell membranes and cell signalling](#bio.membranes): a gland secretes it into the blood, the blood carries it to every organ, and only **target cells** have **receptors** with a binding site of the right shape. In this chapter the glands are the **pancreas** (insulin and glucagon) and, from [Homeostasis and the kidney](#bio.homeostasis), the **posterior pituitary gland** (it releases ADH, a hormone made in the hypothalamus). All three hormones are peptides or small proteins, so they cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer: each binds to a receptor in the cell surface membrane.

| Hormone | Made in | Target cells | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| ADH | hypothalamus; released from the posterior pituitary gland | collecting duct cells of the kidney | more water reabsorbed, a small volume of concentrated urine |
| insulin | β cells of the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas | liver, muscle (and other) cells | blood glucose concentration falls |
| glucagon | α cells of the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas | liver cells | blood glucose concentration rises |

## Controlling blood glucose

Cells respire glucose to make ATP, so every cell needs a steady supply. The concentration of glucose in the blood of a healthy person is kept at about 4–6 mmol dm⁻³. It rises after a meal, when carbohydrate is digested and glucose is absorbed, and it falls between meals and during exercise, when cells use glucose. If it falls too low, brain cells are starved of the glucose they need for respiration. If it stays too high, it lowers the water potential of the blood, which draws water out of cells and leads to the kidney problems you met in [Homeostasis and the kidney](#bio.homeostasis).

:::def Islets of Langerhans
Groups of endocrine cells scattered through the pancreas. The **α cells** secrete the hormone glucagon and the **β cells** secrete the hormone insulin. The rest of the pancreas makes digestive enzymes.
:::

The α and β cells are the receptors and the coordinators in one: they detect the glucose concentration of the blood passing through them and secrete the right hormone. The effectors are the liver, muscle and other cells.

### Insulin: what happens when blood glucose rises

:::steps
1. After a meal, glucose is absorbed into the blood, so the **blood glucose concentration rises**.
2. The **β cells** of the islets of Langerhans detect the rise and **secrete insulin** into the blood.
3. Insulin travels to its target cells and **binds to receptors** in their cell surface membranes.
4. In **muscle cells** and other cells, insulin makes vesicles that hold **GLUT4** carrier proteins fuse with the cell surface membrane. There are now many more carriers, so **glucose enters the cells faster by facilitated diffusion**.
5. In **liver cells** and **muscle cells**, insulin activates the enzymes that **link glucose into glycogen**: the glucose concentration inside the cells falls, so even more glucose enters, and glucose is stored as glycogen. The cells also respire more glucose.
6. The **blood glucose concentration falls**, the β cells are stimulated less, and less insulin is secreted: **negative feedback**.
:::

:::def Glycogenesis
The formation of glycogen from glucose, mainly in liver and muscle cells.
:::

Notice who does what. Insulin is a **hormone**: it does not itself change glucose into glycogen. It binds to a receptor and **stimulates the cells**, and the cells' own enzymes carry out the glycogenesis.

:::eg Explain how insulin lowers the blood glucose concentration after a meal.
Insulin acts on both muscle and liver cells, so name both and give the effect on each.

After a meal the blood glucose concentration rises, and the β cells of the islets of Langerhans detect this and secrete insulin into the blood. [B1]

Insulin binds to receptors on the cell surface membranes of target cells in the muscle and liver. [B1]

In muscle cells, vesicles containing GLUT4 carrier proteins fuse with the cell surface membrane, so more glucose enters by facilitated diffusion. [B1]

In muscle and liver cells, insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis), and the cells respire more glucose. [B1]

The blood glucose concentration falls, so the β cells are stimulated less and less insulin is secreted: negative feedback. [B1]

A weaker answer says "insulin turns glucose into glycogen". Insulin is a hormone, not an enzyme; the answer misses the receptor, the extra carrier proteins and the feedback, and scores one mark at most.
:::

### Glucagon: what happens when blood glucose falls

Between meals, or in exercise, the blood glucose concentration falls and the opposite loop works.

:::steps
1. The **blood glucose concentration falls** below the set point.
2. The **α cells** of the islets of Langerhans detect the fall and **secrete glucagon** into the blood.
3. Glucagon binds to receptors on **liver cells**. (Muscle cells do **not** have glucagon receptors, so they do not respond.)
4. In liver cells, glucagon starts a signalling pathway (the next section) that causes **glycogenolysis**, the breakdown of glycogen to glucose, and **gluconeogenesis**, the making of glucose from other substances.
5. **Glucose leaves the liver cells** and enters the blood, so the **blood glucose concentration rises**.
6. The α cells are stimulated less and less glucagon is secreted: negative feedback.
:::

:::def Glycogenolysis
The breakdown of glycogen to glucose, mainly in liver cells.
:::

:::def Gluconeogenesis
The production of glucose from substances that are not carbohydrates, such as amino acids and glycerol, mainly in liver cells.
:::

Muscle cells keep glycogen too, but they use it themselves in respiration. They have no glucagon receptors and the glycogen stored in muscle is not released as glucose into the blood.

:::warn Three words that look alike
**Glycogen** is the storage polysaccharide. **Glucagon** is the hormone from the α cells. **Glycogenesis** is making glycogen (insulin stimulates it) and **glycogenolysis** is breaking it down (glucagon stimulates it). Write them carefully: a spelling that gives the wrong word loses the mark.
:::

## Cell signalling: how glucagon works inside a liver cell

Glucagon is a peptide hormone. It cannot enter a liver cell, yet it makes the cell release glucose within minutes. How does a message from outside the cell start a response deep inside it? Here is the chain, one link at a time. It is a model for the way many hormones work.

:::def Second messenger
A small molecule inside a cell that passes on a signal from a cell surface receptor to other molecules in the cell. The hormone is the **first messenger**; it stays outside the cell.
:::

:::steps
1. **Binding.** Glucagon (the first messenger) binds to its **receptor**, a protein in the cell surface membrane of the liver cell. The binding site has a shape complementary to glucagon. Binding causes a **conformational change**: the receptor changes shape on the inside of the membrane.
2. **G-protein.** The new shape of the receptor activates a **G-protein**, a protein on the inner face of the membrane.
3. **Adenylyl cyclase.** The activated G-protein **stimulates adenylyl cyclase**, an enzyme in the membrane.
4. **Second messenger.** Adenylyl cyclase converts **ATP** to **cyclic AMP (cAMP)**, the second messenger. It is made in the cytoplasm and spreads through it.
5. **Protein kinase A.** cAMP **activates protein kinase A**, an enzyme that adds phosphate groups to other proteins (**phosphorylation**).
6. **Enzyme cascade.** Protein kinase A **phosphorylates** the next enzyme and activates it; that enzyme phosphorylates and activates the next, and so on. At each step, one active enzyme activates **many** molecules of the next enzyme, so the signal is **amplified**.
7. **Response.** The **final enzyme** in the pathway is activated and **catalyses the breakdown of glycogen** to glucose. The glucose leaves the cell and enters the blood.
:::

:::note The names of the final enzymes
You do not need to name the enzymes in the cascade. In liver cells the final one is glycogen phosphorylase, which releases glucose units from glycogen as glucose phosphate; the phosphate is removed before glucose leaves the cell.
:::

Why does the cell use a cascade instead of one enzyme?

- **Amplification.** One glucagon molecule bound to one receptor can lead to the breakdown of a huge number of glycogen molecules, because each step multiplies the number of active molecules.
- **Speed.** The response begins within seconds and stops soon after the glucagon concentration falls, because the active molecules are switched off again.
- **Message into the cell.** The hormone does not need to cross the membrane. The membrane receptor and the second messenger carry the message.

:::eg A simplified model of the amplification of the glucagon signal uses invented numbers. One glucagon molecule activates one receptor. Each receptor activates 10 G-proteins, and each G-protein activates one adenylyl cyclase molecule. Each adenylyl cyclase makes 100 cAMP molecules, and each cAMP activates one protein kinase A. Each protein kinase A activates 10 molecules of the first enzyme, and each of these activates 10 molecules of the final enzyme, glycogen phosphorylase. Each active glycogen phosphorylase releases 20 glucose units in one second. Calculate the number of glucose units released per second from one glucagon molecule.
Work down the pathway, multiplying at each stage.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{G-proteins} &= 1 \times 10 = 10 \\ \text{adenylyl cyclase} &= 10 \times 1 = 10 \\ \text{cAMP} &= 10 \times 100 = 1000 \\ \text{protein kinase A} &= 1000 \times 1 = 1000 \\ \text{first enzyme} &= 1000 \times 10 = 10\,000 \\ \text{final enzyme} &= 10\,000 \times 10 = 100\,000 \end{aligned}\]

[M1]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{glucose units per second} &= 100\,000 \times 20 \\ &= 2.0 \times 10^{6} \end{aligned}\]

[A1]

So one hormone molecule leads to about two million glucose units per second. The numbers are invented, but they show why a tiny amount of hormone has a large effect: the signal is amplified at every step.
:::

:::warn Which is which?
**cAMP is not the hormone**: it is the second messenger, made inside the cell. **Adenylyl cyclase is not the receptor**: it is an enzyme that makes cAMP. The **receptor** is the protein that glucagon binds to. A mark scheme will give credit for each of these only when the right name is next to the right job.
:::

## Measuring glucose: test strips and biosensors

People with diabetes need to know their glucose concentration, and doctors test urine and blood for it. Both methods use an enzyme that is specific to glucose.

### Test strips

A **test strip** is a plastic strip with a pad that holds two enzymes and a colourless chemical, a **chromogen**, that becomes coloured when it is oxidised. Two reactions happen in turn.

:::steps
1. **Glucose oxidase** catalyses the oxidation of glucose by oxygen. The products are gluconic acid (formed as gluconolactone) and **hydrogen peroxide**.
2. **Peroxidase** catalyses a reaction in which the hydrogen peroxide oxidises the chromogen. The products are a **coloured compound** and water.
:::

\[\text{glucose} + \mathrm{O_2} + \mathrm{H_2O} \xrightarrow{\text{glucose oxidase}} \text{gluconic acid} + \mathrm{H_2O_2}\]

\[\mathrm{H_2O_2} + \text{chromogen (colourless)} \xrightarrow{\text{peroxidase}} \text{coloured product} + \mathrm{H_2O}\]

The more glucose in the sample, the more hydrogen peroxide is made, and the stronger the colour. The user compares the colour of the pad with a **colour chart** on the bottle. The test is **specific to glucose**, because the active site of glucose oxidase is complementary to glucose only: other sugars, such as fructose or galactose, do not fit, which is an advantage over Benedict's test (which reacts with any reducing sugar). It is only **semi-quantitative**: the colour chart has a few bands, and judging a colour by eye is subjective, so the result is a range, not an exact value.

### Biosensors

:::def Biosensor
A device that uses a biological molecule, such as an enzyme, to detect a substance and converts the reaction into an electrical signal that is proportional to the concentration of the substance.
:::

A glucose biosensor has **glucose oxidase immobilised** on or next to an **electrode** (immobilised enzymes are in [Enzyme kinetics, inhibitors and immobilised enzymes](#bio.enzymes2)). A drop of blood is placed on the strip.

:::steps
1. Glucose in the blood reacts at the enzyme: glucose oxidase catalyses its oxidation, and the reaction passes electrons to the electrode (or produces hydrogen peroxide that is oxidised at the electrode).
2. This produces a **small electric current**. The **greater the glucose concentration, the greater the current**.
3. A **transducer** in the meter converts the current to a signal, and the meter shows the **glucose concentration as a number**, using a calibration.
:::

:::eg Explain how a test strip detects glucose in a urine sample, and why the result is only semi-quantitative.
The strip pad holds glucose oxidase, peroxidase and a colourless chromogen.

Glucose oxidase catalyses the oxidation of glucose, producing gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide. [B1]

Peroxidase catalyses the reaction of the hydrogen peroxide with the chromogen, which forms a coloured product. [B1]

The more glucose there is, the more hydrogen peroxide and the stronger the colour; the colour is compared with a chart. [B1]

The result is semi-quantitative because the colour is judged by eye and the chart gives a range of concentrations, not an exact value. [B1]

(Also credit: the strip is specific to glucose because the active site of glucose oxidase is complementary only to glucose.)
:::

:::eg A biosensor was calibrated with solutions of known glucose concentration. A sample of blood gave a current of 3.30 µA. Use the calibration to find its glucose concentration.
| glucose / mmol dm⁻³ | current / µA |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0.30 |
| 2 | 1.50 |
| 4 | 2.70 |
| 6 | 3.90 |
| 8 | 5.10 |
| 10 | 6.30 |

**Step 1: the pattern.** The current rises by 1.20 µA for every 2 mmol dm⁻³, which is 0.60 µA for each mmol dm⁻³. The points lie on a straight line, so the current is proportional to the glucose concentration, apart from the 0.30 µA with no glucose. [B1]

**Step 2: the concentration.** Subtract the 0.30 µA and divide by the gradient:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{glucose} &= \frac{3.30 - 0.30}{0.60} \\ &= 5.0\,\mathrm{mmol\,dm^{-3}} \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

This is in the normal range of 4–6 mmol dm⁻³. You could also read 3.30 µA across to the line on a graph and down to the axis.
:::

:::eg Real context, type 1 diabetes. In a person with type 1 diabetes the β cells of the islets of Langerhans are destroyed by the person's own immune system. Explain why the person has a high blood glucose concentration, and why they are given insulin by injection made by genetically modified bacteria.
Without β cells, little or no insulin is secreted, so liver and muscle cells do not take up glucose and do not convert it to glycogen; the blood glucose concentration stays high. [B1]

Insulin is a protein, so it is injected: if swallowed, it would be hydrolysed (digested) by protease enzymes in the stomach and small intestine. [B1]

The gene for human insulin is put into bacteria (see [Genetic technology in medicine and agriculture](#bio.genetech2)), which make large amounts of human insulin cheaply, with fewer side-effects than insulin from animals. [B1]

The person tests the blood glucose concentration with a biosensor and adjusts the dose of insulin to the meals they eat. [B1]
:::

## Comparing the endocrine and nervous systems

You will meet neurones in [Neurones and action potentials](#bio.nerves). The two coordination systems differ in six main ways.

| Feature | Nervous system | Endocrine system |
|---|---|---|
| signal | electrical impulses (action potentials) along neurones | chemical (hormones) |
| route | along neurones, to a specific effector | in the blood, to every part of the body |
| speed | very fast | slower: a hormone must be made, released and carried |
| duration of effect | short-lived: stops when the impulses stop | long-lasting: the hormone stays in the blood until it is broken down |
| area affected | localised: only the cells the neurone reaches | widespread: every target cell with the receptor |
| examples | pulling a hand from a hot object | control of blood glucose by insulin and glucagon |

:::exam Compare means both sides
A "compare" question wants a similarity or difference **for each system in the same sentence**. Write "the nervous system uses electrical impulses, but the endocrine system uses hormones in the blood", not two separate lists. Both systems have receptors, coordinators and effectors, and both can use negative feedback: that is a good similarity to state.
:::

Q: State what is meant by a hormone and name the two hormones from the pancreas that control blood glucose.
A: A chemical made by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood that alters the activity of specific target cells. Insulin and glucagon.
Q: Which cells of the islets of Langerhans make insulin, and which make glucagon?
A: β cells make insulin; α cells make glucagon.
Q: Explain why muscle cells do not respond to glucagon, but liver cells do.
A: Only liver cells have receptors with a binding site complementary to glucagon. Muscle cells lack glucagon receptors, so glucagon cannot bind to them.
Q: Put these in order: adenylyl cyclase activated, cAMP made, glucagon binds to receptor, G-protein activated, protein kinase A activated.
A: Glucagon binds to receptor; G-protein activated; adenylyl cyclase activated; cAMP made; protein kinase A activated.
Q: Name the second messenger in the glucagon pathway and the molecule from which it is made.
A: Cyclic AMP (cAMP), made from ATP by adenylyl cyclase.
Q: State what is meant by amplification of a signal, and why an enzyme cascade achieves it.
A: A small signal causes a much larger response. Each enzyme in the cascade, once active, activates many molecules of the next enzyme.
Q: A test strip contains glucose oxidase and peroxidase. State the job of each.
A: Glucose oxidase catalyses the oxidation of glucose and produces hydrogen peroxide. Peroxidase catalyses the reaction between hydrogen peroxide and the chromogen, which makes the coloured product.
Q: A glucose biosensor gives a current of 4.50 µA. A second reading is 2.25 µA. The biosensor's current is proportional to the glucose concentration. If the first reading corresponds to 9.0 mmol dm⁻³, calculate the concentration for the second reading.
A: \(2.25 \div 4.50 \times 9.0 = 4.5\,\mathrm{mmol\,dm^{-3}}\).
Q: Give two ways in which a biosensor is better than a test strip for monitoring blood glucose.
A: It gives a quantitative (exact) value, and the reading is objective, because there is no colour to judge by eye. (Also: needs only a small drop of blood and is quick.)

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A marathon runner's blood glucose concentration stays near 4.5 mmol dm⁻³ in the final hour of a race, even though the muscles respire large amounts of glucose. (a) Explain how glucagon keeps the concentration steady. (b) Explain why glycogen stored in the runner's leg muscles does not help to keep the blood glucose concentration steady.
H: For (a), start with what detects the fall, then the target cells and the cascade.
H: For (b), do muscle cells have receptors for glucagon?
A: (a) As the blood glucose concentration falls, the α cells of the islets of Langerhans secrete glucagon. [B1]
Glucagon binds to receptors on liver cells, which start a cascade that causes glycogenolysis, and gluconeogenesis; glucose is released into the blood. [B1]
When the concentration rises, less glucagon is secreted: negative feedback. [B1]
(b) Muscle cells do not have glucagon receptors, so glucagon does not make them break down glycogen. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] (#bio.enzymes) ★★★ A test strip for glucose uses glucose oxidase and peroxidase. (a) Explain why a test strip with glucose oxidase gives a positive result with glucose but not with fructose. (b) The strip is dipped in a sample of urine that has been left in a warm place for a long time, and gives no colour even though the person has diabetes. Suggest two reasons. (c) State why the pad must also contain peroxidase.
H: Use the words "active site" and "complementary".
H: For (b), what could have happened to the glucose, or to the enzymes?
A: (a) The active site of glucose oxidase has a shape complementary to glucose only. [B1]
Fructose has a different shape and cannot bind to the active site, so no enzyme–substrate complexes form with it. [B1]
(b) The glucose in the sample was used up (for example by bacteria respiring it). [B1]
The enzymes in the pad were denatured by the heat (or the pad was old), so they no longer catalysed the reactions. [B1]
(c) Glucose oxidase makes only hydrogen peroxide, which is colourless; peroxidase is needed to use it to turn the chromogen into a coloured product. [B1] [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.homeostasis) ★★★ A person who has not eaten for many hours has a high concentration of ADH in the blood and a high concentration of glucagon in the blood. Compare how ADH and glucagon travel to their target cells and how they bring about their responses.
H: Give similarities as well as differences: where each is made, how it travels, what it binds to and what its target cells do.
A: Both are hormones, secreted by endocrine tissue (the posterior pituitary gland releases ADH; the α cells of the pancreas make glucagon) into the blood. [B1]
Both travel in the blood to all organs, but only target cells respond. [B1]
Both are peptides, so neither crosses the membrane: each binds to a receptor in the cell surface membrane. [B1]
ADH acts on the collecting duct cells of the kidney; glucagon acts on liver cells. [B1]
ADH makes vesicles with aquaporins fuse with the membrane, so more water is reabsorbed. [B1]
Glucagon starts a cascade through G-protein, adenylyl cyclase and cAMP, which leads to the breakdown of glycogen. [B1]

:::recap
- Hormones are made by endocrine glands, carried in the blood and act on target cells that have receptors. ADH, insulin and glucagon are examples.
- Blood glucose is kept near 4–6 mmol dm⁻³ by negative feedback. β cells (insulin) detect a rise; α cells (glucagon) detect a fall.
- Insulin: target cells take up glucose (more GLUT4 in muscle, facilitated diffusion), glycogenesis in liver and muscle, more respiration; blood glucose falls.
- Glucagon: liver cells only; glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis; glucose released; blood glucose rises.
- Glucagon cascade: binds to receptor (conformational change), G-protein activated, adenylyl cyclase stimulated, ATP to cAMP (second messenger), protein kinase A activated, enzyme cascade of phosphorylation (amplification), final enzyme catalyses glycogen breakdown.
- Test strip: glucose oxidase makes gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide; peroxidase uses the peroxide to turn a chromogen into a coloured product; semi-quantitative. Biosensor: glucose oxidase immobilised on an electrode gives a current proportional to the concentration; quantitative.
- Nervous system: electrical impulses, fast, short-lived, localised. Endocrine system: hormones in the blood, slower, long-lasting, widespread.
:::
