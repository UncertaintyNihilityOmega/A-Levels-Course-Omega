@summary Infectious diseases are caused by pathogens that can be passed from one host to another. You will learn the name and type of pathogen that causes cholera, malaria, tuberculosis (TB) and HIV/AIDS, how each is transmitted, and how to discuss the biological, social and economic factors that must be considered in preventing and controlling each one.

:::why
Infectious diseases such as cholera, malaria, TB and HIV have shaped history and still shape health and wealth in many parts of the world. To control a disease you must first know what causes it and how it spreads, because the way to stop a disease depends on how it gets from one person to the next. Exam questions ask you to name the pathogen, to explain the route of transmission, and to discuss a balanced set of measures that are biological, social and economic. Learning to write that discussion well is a skill that you will use for the whole course.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know what a bacterium and a virus are from [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes).

:::try Warm-up
Q: State two structures that a bacterium has and a virus does not. (from [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes))
A: Any two of: cell surface membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, a cell wall of peptidoglycan, its own metabolism. A virus is not a cell.
Q: Why can a virus reproduce only inside a host cell? (from [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes))
A: It has no ribosomes, no enzymes for respiration and no metabolism of its own, so it needs the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to make new virus particles.
Q: Name the type of cell that has a nucleus and the type that does not. (from [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells))
A: Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus; prokaryotic cells (bacteria) have no nucleus.
:::

## Pathogens and infectious disease

Some diseases, such as a cancer or a faulty gene, cannot be caught from another person. Others can. You catch a cold, or cholera, or TB because a tiny organism or virus gets into your body, multiplies and harms you.

:::def Pathogen
An organism or virus that causes disease.
:::

:::def Infectious disease
A disease caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, so it is transmissible.
:::

:::def Transmission
The passing of a pathogen from an infected host to a new host.
:::

Pathogens come in several types, and you must name the type for each of the four diseases in this chapter:
- **Bacteria**: prokaryotic cells with a peptidoglycan wall, for example *Vibrio cholerae* (cholera) and *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (TB).
- **Viruses**: non-cellular particles that reproduce only inside host cells, for example the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV).
- **Protoctists**: single-celled organisms whose cells have a nucleus (eukaryotic cells), for example *Plasmodium* (malaria).

(Fungi are a fourth type of pathogen, but none of the four diseases in this syllabus topic is caused by one.)

:::def Vector
An organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another, without itself being the cause of the disease. The female *Anopheles* mosquito is the vector of the malarial parasite.
:::

:::def Carrier
A person who is infected with a pathogen and can pass it on to others, but who has no symptoms, or only mild ones.
:::

Pathogens are passed on in a small number of ways. Each disease in this chapter uses one or more of them.

| Route of transmission | How it works | Example in this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| contaminated water or food | pathogens in faeces get into drinking water or food and are swallowed | cholera |
| droplets in the air | tiny drops from coughs and sneezes are breathed in | TB |
| a vector | a living organism carries the pathogen from one host to the next | malaria |
| body fluids | blood, semen or other fluids pass from an infected person to another | HIV |
| mother to child | across the placenta, at birth or in breast milk | HIV (also malaria) |
| contaminated animal products | meat or unpasteurised milk from an infected animal | TB (*Mycobacterium bovis*) |

### Writing names correctly

The scientific name of an organism has two parts: the genus (a capital letter) and the species (small letters), and the whole name is written in italics or underlined: *Vibrio cholerae*. After the first mention the genus is shortened: *V. cholerae*, *M. tuberculosis*. A disease name, like cholera or malaria, is not italic and has no capital.

:::warn Disease, pathogen, vector
"Malaria is caused by mosquitoes" is wrong. Malaria is the **disease**. It is caused by the **pathogen**, a protoctist of the genus *Plasmodium*. The mosquito is the **vector** that carries the pathogen. In the same way "HIV is AIDS" is wrong: HIV is the virus, and AIDS is the condition that may develop later.
:::

:::eg Explain the difference between the disease malaria, the pathogen that causes it and the mosquito, and state the type of each organism.
Malaria is the disease. [B1]

It is caused by the pathogen *Plasmodium* (for example *P. falciparum*), which is a protoctist, a single-celled eukaryotic organism. [B1]

The female *Anopheles* mosquito is the vector: it carries the parasite from an infected person to a healthy one when it bites, but it does not cause the disease itself. [B1]

A weaker answer says "malaria is caused by the mosquito, which is a bacterium". It confuses the vector with the pathogen and names the wrong type, and scores nothing.
:::

:::key The four diseases at a glance
| Disease | Pathogen | Type | How it is transmitted |
|---|---|---|---|
| cholera | *Vibrio cholerae* | bacterium | water or food contaminated with faeces |
| malaria | *Plasmodium falciparum*, *P. malariae*, *P. ovale*, *P. vivax* | protoctists | bite of an infected female *Anopheles* mosquito; also blood transfusion and mother to fetus |
| tuberculosis (TB) | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and *M. bovis* | bacteria | droplets from coughs and sneezes; *M. bovis* also in unpasteurised milk and meat from infected cattle |
| HIV/AIDS | human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) | virus | body fluids: unprotected sex, shared needles, blood products, mother to child |
:::

## Cholera

:::def Cholera
A disease caused by the bacterium *Vibrio cholerae*, which infects the small intestine and causes severe watery diarrhoea and dehydration.
:::

*V. cholerae* is a curved rod-shaped bacterium. Once swallowed, it multiplies in the small intestine and releases a toxin. The toxin makes the cells of the intestine lining release large volumes of water into the gut, so the person loses litres of watery diarrhoea. Without treatment, the dehydration (and loss of ions) can kill within a day or two.

### How cholera is transmitted

Cholera spreads by the **faecal–oral route**. The faeces of an infected person contain millions of bacteria. If sewage reaches rivers, wells or pumps, or if a person with unwashed hands prepares food, other people swallow the bacteria in water or food. Each new patient then releases more bacteria into the environment.

### Preventing and controlling cholera

Think of the cycle in the figure: the aim is to break it at as many points as possible.

| Type of factor | Measures |
|---|---|
| **biological** | treatment of patients with oral rehydration solution (clean water with sugar and salts) to replace lost water and ions; antibiotics for severe cases; oral vaccines, which protect only partly and for a limited time, so they are not enough on their own |
| **social** | hand-washing and food-hygiene education; boiling or chlorinating water; building latrines; keeping sewage away from drinking water; quick reporting and isolation of cases in an outbreak |
| **economic** | treated piped water and sewage works are very expensive to build and run, and many poor countries cannot afford them; but an outbreak is also costly (hospital care, lost work, lost trade) |

:::eg Real context, a flood: after floods in a city, cholera cases rise sharply a few days later. (a) Explain why. (b) Suggest three measures to reduce the spread. This is a 6-mark question.
**(a) Explanation.**

Flooding damages sewage systems and washes sewage, which contains faeces from infected people, into the water used for drinking. [B1]

The faeces contain the bacteria *V. cholerae*. People drink contaminated water, or eat food washed in it, so the bacteria enter by the mouth: the faecal–oral route. [B1]

Each infected person passes out more bacteria, so the number of cases grows quickly. [B1]

**(b) Three measures**, each linked to the cycle:

Provide clean drinking water, or tell people to boil or chlorinate it, so that the bacteria are not swallowed. [B1]

Give patients oral rehydration solution, to replace the water and ions lost, so fewer die. [B1]

Provide safe sanitation (toilets) and hygiene education (hand-washing), so faeces do not contaminate water and food. [B1]

(Also credit: vaccination of people in the affected area, though protection is partial; isolating patients.)

A weaker answer says "the floods bring cholera germs". It does not say where the bacteria come from or how they enter the body, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

:::exam Name the route, name the pathogen
For a transmission question, always give three things: the **pathogen** (with its type if the question asks), the **route** ("faecal–oral": water or food contaminated with faeces) and the **link** that explains the situation in the question (floods, crowded housing, shared needles).
:::

## Malaria

:::def Malaria
A disease caused by protoctists of the genus *Plasmodium*, which infect red blood cells and the liver, and are passed on by female *Anopheles* mosquitoes. It causes repeated bouts of fever.
:::

There are four species that infect humans: *Plasmodium falciparum*, *Plasmodium malariae*, *Plasmodium ovale* and *Plasmodium vivax*. They are protoctists: single-celled organisms whose cells have a nucleus. The details of the life cycle are not needed for the syllabus. What you need is the pathogen and how it passes from person to person.

### How malaria is transmitted

- **A vector.** A female *Anopheles* mosquito needs a meal of blood to make her eggs. When she bites an infected person she takes up *Plasmodium* with the blood. The parasite develops in the mosquito. When she bites another person later, she injects it, and that person becomes infected.
- **Blood.** Infected blood can pass the parasite on if it is used in a blood transfusion, or if needles are shared.
- **Mother to fetus.** The parasite can cross the placenta from an infected mother to the fetus.

Mosquitoes breed in standing water: the female lays her eggs on the water, and the larvae develop in it. So malaria is mostly found in warm, wet regions where *Anopheles* mosquitoes can live.

### Preventing and controlling malaria

| Type of factor | Measures |
|---|---|
| **biological** | kill or block the vector: insecticide-treated bed nets (the mosquitoes bite mostly at night), insecticide sprays, draining or covering standing water, and fish or other organisms that eat larvae; prophylactic (preventive) drugs for people at risk, which must be taken regularly; treating patients quickly with antimalarial drugs; no vaccine gives complete protection against all forms |
| **social** | education about sleeping under a net, clearing standing water, and getting treatment early; houses with screens on windows; using nets properly (not for other uses, such as fishing) |
| **economic** | nets, insecticides, drugs, spraying teams and health workers cost money that poor countries do not have; but malaria also costs a great deal in lost working days, health care and reduced income, so control is cost-effective |

A major difficulty is **resistance**. Mosquitoes can become resistant to insecticides, and *Plasmodium* can become resistant to antimalarial drugs, so a measure that worked well may lose effect over time. The idea is explained in [Antibiotics and resistance](#bio.antibiotics).

:::exam "Discuss": give balance
"Discuss" is a command word that asks for a balanced account: say what each measure does, say why it works, and say what limits it (cost, resistance, behaviour, no complete cure). A list of measures with no limits cannot score full marks.
:::

:::eg Discuss the biological, social and economic factors that need to be considered in the control of malaria. This is an 8-mark question.
Organise the answer under the three headings, and give a reason or a limit for each point.

**Biological factors**

Insecticide-treated bed nets prevent female *Anopheles* mosquitoes biting at night, so fewer people are infected. [B1]

Draining or covering standing water removes the places where mosquito larvae develop, so there are fewer mosquitoes. Insecticide sprays kill adult mosquitoes. [B1]

Prophylactic drugs kill the parasite in the body, but they must be taken regularly, and the parasite can evolve resistance to them. [B1]

Mosquitoes can become resistant to insecticides, so the measures need to be changed from time to time, and no vaccine gives complete protection. [B1]

**Social factors**

Education is needed so that people use nets correctly every night, remove standing water near their homes and seek treatment early. [B1]

Some people cannot or will not use nets (for example, it is too hot, or they have no money to replace them), so control depends on people's behaviour and culture. [B1]

**Economic factors**

Nets, insecticides, drugs and health workers are expensive, and poorer countries may not be able to afford them without help from other countries. [B1]

Malaria also costs a country money, through lost working days, care of the sick and reduced income, so spending on control can save money in the long term. [B1]

(Also credit: housing with screens; controlling mosquito larvae with fish; rapid diagnosis and treatment of cases.)

A weaker answer lists "bed nets, drugs, insecticide" and says "it costs a lot". It does not link each to the biology of the vector, it does not separate social from economic factors, and it gives no limits: about three marks out of eight.
:::

## Tuberculosis (TB)

:::def Tuberculosis (TB)
A disease caused by the bacteria *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and *Mycobacterium bovis*, which mainly infect the lungs. Symptoms include a long-lasting cough (sometimes with blood), weight loss, fever and night sweats.
:::

*M. tuberculosis* and *M. bovis* are bacteria. In the lungs, the bacteria are taken up by phagocytes (see [The immune response](#bio.immunity)) but are not always killed. The lung tissue is damaged, and the person coughs. Many people infected by TB have **latent TB**: the immune system keeps the bacteria in check, they have no symptoms and do not pass the bacteria on, but the disease can become active later, especially if the immune system becomes weak.

### How TB is transmitted

- ***M. tuberculosis*: droplets in the air.** An infected person with active TB in the lungs coughs or sneezes and releases tiny droplets containing the bacteria. Another person breathes the droplets in and the bacteria reach their lungs. The risk is greatest with close, long contact, in crowded and badly ventilated rooms.
- ***M. bovis*: animal products.** This bacterium infects cattle. People are infected by drinking unpasteurised milk, or eating meat that is not properly cooked, from infected cattle.

### Preventing and controlling TB

| Type of factor | Measures |
|---|---|
| **biological** | the BCG vaccine, which gives partial protection and is given to children in countries where TB is common; **contact tracing** (finding and testing the people who were in close contact with a patient) so that new cases are treated early; treating patients with a **combination of several antibiotics** for at least six months, so that the bacteria are killed and resistance is less likely; pasteurising milk, and testing and removing infected cattle |
| **social** | **supervised treatment**: a health worker checks that each patient takes every dose, because patients feel better after a few weeks and may stop; less overcrowding and better ventilation; education to reduce the stigma attached to the disease, so people seek tests early |
| **economic** | a long course of several drugs, tests, tracing and supervision cost a lot, and a poor family may be unable to afford time off work; but untreated TB costs more in lost work and further spread |

Two links with other topics matter:
- **Resistance.** If a patient stops taking the drugs early, the bacteria that survive may be resistant, and they spread. **MDR-TB** (multidrug-resistant TB) is TB caused by bacteria resistant to the main antibiotics used to treat it. It is much harder, longer and more expensive to treat. See [Antibiotics and resistance](#bio.antibiotics).
- **HIV.** HIV weakens the immune system, so a person who has HIV is much more likely to develop active TB. In places where both are common, the two diseases make each other worse.

:::eg Explain how TB spreads from one person to another, and why a high rate of TB is found in overcrowded housing. This is a 4-mark question.
An infected person coughs or sneezes, releasing droplets that contain the bacteria *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* into the air. [B1]

Other people breathe in the droplets, so the bacteria enter their lungs. [B1]

In overcrowded housing many people share a small, badly ventilated space for long periods, so the droplets are not diluted. [B1]

So each person breathes in more droplets, and one infected person is more likely to pass the bacteria on to many others. [B1]

(Also credit: *M. bovis* spreads in unpasteurised milk and meat from infected cattle.)

A weaker answer says "TB is spread by germs in the air". It gives no pathogen and does not say the bacteria are in droplets from coughs, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

:::warn TB is not spread in water
TB is spread by droplets in the air (and, for *M. bovis*, by milk and meat). It is not a water-borne disease like cholera. Do not mix up the two routes.
:::

## HIV and AIDS

:::def HIV (human immunodeficiency virus)
The virus that causes AIDS. It is an enveloped RNA virus that infects and destroys T-helper cells, which coordinate the immune response.
:::

:::def AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome)
The group of illnesses that develops when HIV has destroyed so many of the T-helper cells that the immune system can no longer defend the body against infections and some cancers.
:::

HIV is a virus, so it can reproduce only inside host cells. It infects **T-helper cells**, white blood cells that organise the immune response (see [The immune response](#bio.immunity)). The virus reproduces in the cell and destroys it. Over years, without treatment, the number of T-helper cells falls and the person becomes unable to fight off pathogens. TB, pneumonia and certain cancers can then take hold. These are called opportunistic infections, because they take their chance when the defences are down. The stage at which these illnesses appear is **AIDS**. People can carry HIV for many years before they develop AIDS, and effective drug treatment can prevent AIDS from developing.

### How HIV is transmitted

HIV is passed on in body fluids that contain virus particles or infected cells: blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk.
- **Unprotected sexual intercourse** (without a condom) with an infected partner.
- **Sharing needles** and syringes, which carry blood from one person to another.
- **Blood and blood products**, if the blood is not screened (for example, an infected blood transfusion).
- **Mother to child**: across the placenta, at birth (contact with the mother's blood), or in breast milk.

HIV is not passed on by touching, hugging, sharing cups or toilets, coughing, or by mosquitoes, because the virus does not survive outside the body for long and is not in sweat, saliva or tears in an amount that can infect.

### Preventing and controlling HIV

| Type of factor | Measures |
|---|---|
| **biological** | **condoms** to stop body fluids passing; **screening** of blood and blood products before use; **needle exchange** schemes that give clean needles; **antiretroviral drugs**, which slow the reproduction of the virus: they control HIV but do not cure it, they lower the amount of virus in the blood so the risk of passing it on falls, and taken by a pregnant woman they reduce the chance of the baby being infected; there is no vaccine, partly because the virus changes quickly |
| **social** | **education** about how HIV is and is not passed on; encouraging people to be tested; reducing the **stigma** that stops people being tested or telling partners; changing behaviour; support for people with HIV |
| **economic** | a person with HIV must take drugs for life, which is expensive for the person and for the country; testing and screening cost money; HIV mostly affects adults of working age, so it lowers a country's income, and children may be orphaned |

:::warn HIV is not AIDS
HIV is the **virus**. AIDS is the **syndrome** (the set of illnesses) that follows after the immune system has been badly damaged. A person can have HIV and not have AIDS. And antibiotics cannot cure HIV, because antibiotics do not act on viruses (see [Antibiotics and resistance](#bio.antibiotics)).
:::

Line 2: AIDS is a virus that destroys the immune system.
Line 3: People with AIDS get other infections, because their immune system is weak.

## Discussing prevention and control

The syllabus asks you to **discuss** the factors that need to be considered in controlling cholera, malaria, TB and HIV. The same plan works for any of them.

:::steps
1. **Biological**: break the route of transmission (clean water, vector control, vaccination, treatment, screening), and say what limits it (resistance, no vaccine, a latent infection).
2. **Social**: what people do and know: education, hygiene, using nets or condoms, finishing the treatment, stigma, housing.
3. **Economic**: who pays: the cost of water treatment, drugs, screening and staff; the cost of not acting; the need for help from richer countries.
4. Link them: a measure works only if it is affordable, understood and used.
:::

| | cholera | malaria | TB | HIV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cut the route | clean water and sewage treatment | nets, draining water, insecticide | ventilation, pasteurised milk | condoms, clean needles, screening blood |
| vaccine | partial protection | no complete protection | BCG: partial protection | none |
| treatment | oral rehydration solution | antimalarial drugs | several antibiotics for months | antiretroviral drugs (control, no cure) |
| main limit | cost of water and sewage works | resistance, behaviour | long treatment, MDR-TB | no cure, stigma |

Now see how a graph of real-looking data can be used. In the plot, the number of malaria cases per 1000 children is shown before and after a programme of bed nets and spraying. The data are invented for the example.

:::

:::eg The graph shows invented data for a malaria control programme. Describe the changes in the number of cases and calculate the percentage fall. Suggest two reasons why the number did not fall to zero.
**Describe.** The number of cases stayed at about 120 per 1000 children per year until the programme started at year 5. It then fell steeply for about three years, to about 40 cases per 1000 children per year at year 8, and stayed at about 40 after that.

**Calculate the percentage fall**, from about 120 to about 40:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{fall} &= \frac{120 - 40}{120} \times 100 \\ &= 67\% \end{aligned}\]

**Reasons the number did not reach zero** (any two suitable ideas): some people did not use their nets every night; mosquitoes survive in places the spraying does not reach, or have become resistant to the insecticide; the parasite is still present in infected people (including those without symptoms), so new infections continue; people travel in from areas without a programme.

The data show a **correlation** between the programme and the fall. Other things could have changed in those years (rainfall, new drugs), so a careful scientist says that the fall is *consistent with* the programme working.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State what is meant by the term pathogen, and name the type of pathogen that causes malaria.
A: A pathogen is an organism or virus that causes disease. Malaria is caused by protoctists (*Plasmodium*).
Q: Name the pathogen that causes cholera and state its type.
A: *Vibrio cholerae*; a bacterium.
Q: State the route by which cholera is transmitted.
A: The faecal–oral route: swallowing water or food contaminated with faeces that contain the bacteria.
Q: What is meant by a vector? Name the vector of malaria.
A: An organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without causing the disease itself. The female *Anopheles* mosquito.
Q: Name the two bacteria that cause TB and state one way in which each is transmitted.
A: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*: droplets from coughs and sneezes. *Mycobacterium bovis*: unpasteurised milk (or meat) from infected cattle.
Q: Name three ways in which HIV can be transmitted.
A: Any three of: unprotected sexual intercourse; sharing needles; infected blood or blood products (a transfusion); from mother to child across the placenta, at birth or in breast milk.
Q: Explain why antiretroviral drugs do not cure HIV infection.
A: They slow the reproduction of the virus and lower the amount of virus in the blood, but they do not remove all the virus from the body, so a person must keep taking them.
Q: Of 400 children in a village, 18 were found to have malaria. Calculate the percentage with the disease.
A: \(18 \div 400 \times 100 = 4.5\%\).
Q: Explain why people with HIV are more likely to develop TB.
A: HIV destroys T-helper cells, so the immune system is weak and cannot keep the TB bacteria in check, so latent TB becomes active and new infections are not stopped.
Q: Suggest why hospital patients with TB are asked to cover their mouths when they cough.
A: To trap the droplets that contain the bacteria, so that other patients and staff do not breathe them in.

Q: [3 marks] ★ Malaria is not passed on by touching or coughing, but it can be passed on in a blood transfusion. Explain why.
A: The parasite *Plasmodium* lives inside the body, in the liver and in red blood cells, not in the airways or on the skin. [B1]

Q: [4 marks] ★★ In one town, there were two districts. In district A, a water-treatment works and chlorination were introduced. District B kept its old supply. The table shows the number of cholera cases per 100 000 people in a year.

H: (b) Think about the route by which cholera spreads.

\(24 \div 80 \times 100 = 30\%\) [A1]
(b) The percentage of babies infected is much lower (2.5% compared with 30%) when the mother took the drugs, so the drugs reduce the risk. [B1]
(c) Across the placenta (before birth) and at birth. (Also credit: in breast milk.) [B1] [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ A person with TB lives in a small house with seven other people, and the windows are kept shut in winter. (a) Explain why the other people are at high risk of becoming infected. (b) The health service traces and tests everyone who lives in the house. Explain why. (c) State one reason for treating the patient with several antibiotics for six months.
H: For (a), name the route and say how the housing helps it.
H: For (c), what happens if the bacteria are not all killed?
A: (a) The patient releases droplets containing *M. tuberculosis* when coughing, and in a small, shut room other people breathe them in. [B1]
The air is not replaced, and contact is close and for long periods. [B1]
(b) Contact tracing finds people who are already infected, so they can be treated early, and this stops the bacteria spreading further. [B1] [B1]
(c) Using several antibiotics for a long time kills all the bacteria, so resistant bacteria do not survive and spread (and the disease does not return). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Discuss the factors that need to be considered in the prevention and control of cholera.
H: Use the three headings: biological, social, economic.
H: Give a reason, and a limit, for each measure.
A: Clean water, or boiling or chlorination, breaks the faecal–oral route, so fewer people swallow *V. cholerae*. [B1]
Treating sewage so it does not reach drinking water also breaks the route. [B1]
Oral rehydration solution replaces the water and ions lost, so patients survive; vaccines protect only partly and for a limited time. [B1]
Education about hand-washing, food hygiene and latrines changes behaviour, but people must understand and use it. [B1]
Water and sewage works are very expensive to build and run, so poor countries may need help. [B1]
An outbreak costs money (hospital care, lost work), so prevention saves money in the long term. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.prokaryotes) ★★★ HIV is a virus and *Vibrio cholerae* is a bacterium. (a) State two differences in structure between a virus and a bacterium. (b) Explain why an antibiotic that kills *V. cholerae* has no effect on HIV. (c) Explain why HIV is a serious disease even though HIV itself does not kill most of the people it infects.
H: For (c), what does HIV attack, and what does the person then become unable to do?
A: (a) A bacterium is a cell with cytoplasm and a cell wall; a virus is not a cell and has no cytoplasm or wall. [B1]
A bacterium has ribosomes and its own metabolism; a virus has neither. [B1]
(b) Antibiotics act on bacterial structures or processes, such as the cell wall or ribosomes. [B1]
HIV has none of these, since it reproduces inside host cells using the host's machinery. [B1]
(c) HIV infects and destroys T-helper cells, so the immune system weakens. [B1]
The person is then unable to fight other pathogens (such as TB), and these illnesses cause the death. [B1]

:::recap
- A pathogen is an organism or virus that causes disease; an infectious disease is transmissible. A vector carries a pathogen without causing the disease; a carrier has the pathogen without symptoms.
- Cholera: *Vibrio cholerae*, a bacterium, spread by water or food contaminated with faeces (faecal–oral). Control: clean water, sewage treatment, oral rehydration solution, hygiene, partial vaccines.
- Malaria: four *Plasmodium* species (protoctists), carried by the female *Anopheles* mosquito (also blood and mother to fetus). Control: bed nets, insecticide, draining standing water, prophylactic drugs, education. Resistance in mosquitoes and parasites is a problem; no vaccine gives complete protection.
- TB: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* and *M. bovis*, bacteria, spread by droplets from coughs and sneezes (and *M. bovis* in unpasteurised milk and meat). Control: BCG vaccine, contact tracing, several antibiotics for months with supervised treatment, pasteurisation. MDR-TB arises when treatment is incomplete; HIV makes TB more likely.
- HIV: a virus that infects T-helper cells; AIDS is the syndrome of illnesses that follows. Spread by body fluids (unprotected sex, shared needles, blood products, mother to child). Control: condoms, needle exchange, screening blood, education, testing, antiretroviral drugs (control, not cure); no vaccine.
- Discuss: biological, social and economic factors, with a reason and a limit for each. Correlation is not proof of cause.
:::
