:::why
Real papers do not announce the topic. In one hour you can meet a membrane, a codon, a heart valve and an antibody, and you must recognise each one without a chapter title to help you. This chapter mixes all eleven AS topics so that you practise the skill the exam actually tests: working out what a question is about, choosing the right ideas and writing them in mark-scheme language. Do it after you have revised the topic chapters, not instead of them.
:::

## How to use this chapter

The questions get harder as each section goes on, and **no two neighbouring questions come from the same topic**, so you cannot guess the topic from the question before. Each question shows its marks, a star rating and a link to the chapter to revise.

- ★ one idea or one step; ★★ two steps or an explanation with a cause and an effect; ★★★ several parts, or ideas from more than one topic.
- Answer on paper first, then open the answer. Mark yourself honestly, one marking point at a time.
- Keep a log. For every mark you lose, write down the reason: **knowledge** (you did not know it), **reading** (you answered a different question) or **technique** (you knew it but did not write it in mark-scheme language). Revise the chapter in the link for the first kind; practise command words for the other two.
- Timing: the AS papers give roughly one minute per mark. Fifteen quick questions should take about 30 minutes, a data-response question about 8 minutes and an extended question about 10 minutes.

:::exam Describe or explain?
"Describe" asks what happens: give the trend and quote figures with units, with no reasons. "Explain" asks why: give the biological reason, in a chain from cause to effect. A data-response question usually asks for both. Write the description first, then use words such as "because" and "so" to start the explanation.
:::

## Section 1: fifteen quick questions

Recall and short calculations. Aim for full marks on every one before you move on.

:::try Section 1: quick questions
Q: [2 marks] ★ (#bio.microscopy) A drawing of a cell is 63 mm long. The cell is 18 µm long. Calculate the magnification of the drawing.
H: Convert both lengths to the same unit first.
A: \(63\,\mathrm{mm} = 63\,000\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) [M1]. Magnification \(= \dfrac{63\,000}{18} = 3500\) (×3500) [A1]

Q: [3 marks] ★ (#bio.enzymes) Explain how a competitive inhibitor reduces the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction, and why adding more substrate reduces the effect of the inhibitor.
H: Think about which part of the enzyme the inhibitor binds to, and what the two molecules are competing for.
A: The inhibitor has a shape similar to (complementary to) the substrate, so it fits into the active site [B1]. Fewer substrate molecules can bind, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form and the rate falls [B1]. With more substrate there are more substrate molecules than inhibitor molecules, so the active sites are more likely to be occupied by substrate and the rate rises again [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★ (#bio.dna) One strand of a DNA molecule has the base sequence 3′–TACGGCAT–5′. Write the base sequence of the complementary strand, and calculate the total number of hydrogen bonds between the two strands. A–T pairs have two hydrogen bonds and C–G pairs have three.
H: Pair each base with its complement, then count the A–T and C–G pairs separately.
A: Complementary strand: 5′–ATGCCGTA–3′ [B1]. There are 4 A–T pairs and 4 C–G pairs, so \(4 \times 2 + 4 \times 3\) [M1] \(= 20\) hydrogen bonds [A1].

Q: [2 marks] ★ (#bio.movement) A cube of agar has sides of 0.6 cm. Calculate its surface area to volume ratio.
H: A cube has six equal faces.
A: Surface area \(= 6 \times 0.6^2 = 2.16\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) and volume \(= 0.6^3 = 0.216\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) [M1]. Ratio \(= 2.16 : 0.216 = 10 : 1\) [A1]

Q: [2 marks] ★ (#bio.immunity) State two ways in which the secondary immune response differs from the primary immune response.
A: It is faster (a shorter delay before antibodies appear) [B1]. More antibodies are made, reaching a higher concentration, and they stay in the blood for longer [B1]. (Also credit: memory cells are already present so more plasma cells are made.)

Q: [2 marks] ★ (#bio.planttransport) State two differences between a xylem vessel element and a sieve tube element.
A: A mature xylem vessel element is dead with no cytoplasm, but a sieve tube element is alive and contains cytoplasm [B1]. Xylem vessel walls are thickened with lignin, but sieve tube elements have thin cellulose walls [B1]. (Also credit: sieve tube elements have sieve plates at their ends and a companion cell next to them, but xylem vessel elements have no end walls.)

Q: [3 marks] ★ (#bio.heart) Name the blood vessel that carries blood from the heart to the lungs, the vessel that carries blood from the lungs to the heart, and the vessel that carries blood from the left ventricle to the body.
A: Pulmonary artery [B1]. Pulmonary vein [B1]. Aorta [B1].

Q: [2 marks] ★★ (#bio.carbs) State two differences between the structure of amylose and the structure of cellulose.
A: Amylose is made of α-glucose but cellulose is made of β-glucose [B1]. Amylose chains coil into a helix, but cellulose chains are straight (every other glucose is inverted) and are held together in microfibrils by hydrogen bonds between chains [B1]. (Also credit: both have 1,4 glycosidic bonds; amylose is not cross-linked.)

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.disease) State three ways in which HIV can be transmitted from one person to another.
A: Any three of: unprotected sexual intercourse (through body fluids) [B1]; sharing needles or other equipment contaminated with infected blood [B1]; infected blood or blood products in a transfusion [B1]. (Also credit: from an infected mother to her baby across the placenta, at birth or in breast milk.)

Q: [2 marks] ★★ (#bio.proteins) Name the type of bond that holds a polypeptide in an α-helix, and state which groups of the polypeptide form it.
A: Hydrogen bonds [B1], between the C=O group of one peptide bond and the N–H group of another peptide bond a few amino acids along the chain [B1]. (Also credit: between the oxygen and the hydrogen of the polypeptide backbone.)

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.mitosis) A root tip cell of a broad bean plant has 12 chromosomes. State the number of chromatids in the cell at prophase, the number of chromosomes in each daughter cell after mitosis, and the stage of mitosis at which the centromeres divide.
A: 24 chromatids at prophase (12 chromosomes, each of two sister chromatids) [B1]. 12 chromosomes in each daughter cell [B1]. Anaphase [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.gasexchange) State the function of cartilage, of smooth muscle and of elastic fibres in the wall of a bronchus.
A: Cartilage holds the airway open and stops it collapsing when the pressure inside falls on breathing in [B1]. Smooth muscle contracts to narrow the airway (and relaxes to widen it) [B1]. Elastic fibres stretch as the air enters and recoil to help force air out [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.foodtests) Describe how you would test a solution for protein, and state the result if protein is present.
A: Add sodium hydroxide solution and then a few drops of dilute copper(II) sulfate solution (the biuret test) and mix [B1]. Protein present: the blue solution turns purple (lilac) [B1]. No protein: the solution stays blue [B1].

Q: [2 marks] ★★ (#bio.synthesis) The template strand of part of a gene reads 3′–TAC CGA TTG–5′. Write the base sequence of the mRNA made from it, and the anticodon of the tRNA that pairs with the second codon of the mRNA.
A: mRNA: 5′–AUG GCU AAC–3′ (uracil, not thymine) [B1]. The second codon is GCU, so the anticodon is CGA [B1].

Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#bio.antibiotics) A paper disc soaked in an antibiotic was placed on an agar plate covered with bacteria. After incubation there was a clear circle of diameter 18 mm around the disc. Calculate the area of the clear circle, and state what a larger clear zone would show.
H: Area of a circle \(= \pi r^2\), and the radius is half the diameter.
A: Radius \(= 9\,\mathrm{mm}\), so area \(= \pi \times 9^2\) [M1] \(= 254\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) [A1]. A larger zone shows that the bacteria are more sensitive to that antibiotic (it stops them growing at a greater distance) [B1].
:::

## Section 2: eight data-response questions

Each question gives data. Remember: describe with figures and units, then explain with biology. Where a calculation is asked, show your working.

:::try Data response 1: onion cells in sucrose solution
Q: [7 marks] ★ (#bio.movement) A student placed pieces of onion epidermis in sucrose solutions of different concentrations. After 20 minutes, she counted the percentage of cells in which the protoplast had pulled away from the cell wall (plasmolysed cells).

| sucrose / mol dm⁻³ | plasmolysed cells / % |
|---|---|
| 0.10 | 0 |
| 0.20 | 4 |
| 0.30 | 18 |
| 0.40 | 50 |
| 0.50 | 82 |
| 0.60 | 96 |

(a) Describe the results. [2]

(b) Estimate the concentration of sucrose solution that has the same water potential as the cell sap of the onion cells, and give the reason for your answer. [2]

(c) Explain, in terms of water potential, why most cells are plasmolysed in the 0.60 mol dm⁻³ solution. [3]
H: For (a) quote the first and last values and the range where the change is steepest.
H: For (b) the cells of a tissue differ a little, so think about what half of the cells plasmolysing means.
A: (a) As the sucrose concentration increases from 0.10 to 0.60 mol dm⁻³, the percentage of plasmolysed cells increases from 0% to 96% [B1]. The change is steepest between 0.30 and 0.50 mol dm⁻³ (from 18% to 82%) [B1].

(b) 0.40 mol dm⁻³ [B1], because half of the cells (50%) are plasmolysed, so on average there is no net movement of water between the cells and the solution [B1]. (Also credit: cells are at the point of incipient plasmolysis.)

(c) The solution has a lower (more negative) water potential than the cell sap [B1]. Water leaves the cell by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, through the partially permeable cell surface membrane [B1]. The protoplast shrinks and pulls away from the cell wall [B1].
:::

:::try Data response 2: pressures in the left side of the heart
Q: [8 marks] ★ (#bio.heart) The table shows the pressures in the left ventricle and the aorta of a resting person during one cardiac cycle. The pressure in the left atrium stayed below 1 kPa throughout.

| time / s | left ventricle / kPa | aorta / kPa |
|---|---|---|
| 0.0 | 0.7 | 10.7 |
| 0.1 | 3.5 | 10.7 |
| 0.2 | 12.5 | 12.3 |
| 0.3 | 16.0 | 16.0 |
| 0.4 | 9.0 | 12.5 |
| 0.5 | 1.0 | 11.5 |
| 0.6 | 0.5 | 11.0 |
| 0.7 | 0.6 | 10.8 |

(a) State whether the atrioventricular valve is open or closed at 0.1 s, and explain your answer. [2]

(b) State the time, to the nearest 0.1 s, at which the semilunar valve first opens, and give the evidence for it. [2]

(c) One cardiac cycle lasts 0.8 s. Calculate the heart rate in beats per minute. [2]

(d) Explain why the pressure in the aorta falls only slowly between 0.4 s and 0.7 s. [2]
H: For (a) and (b) compare two pressures at the time named.
A: (a) Closed [B1], because the pressure in the left ventricle (3.5 kPa) is higher than the pressure in the left atrium (below 1 kPa), which pushes the valve shut [B1].

(b) 0.2 s [B1], because this is the first time at which the pressure in the left ventricle (12.5 kPa) is higher than the pressure in the aorta (12.3 kPa) [B1].

(c) Heart rate \(= \dfrac{60}{0.8}\) [M1] \(= 75\) beats per minute [A1]

(d) The semilunar valve closes (once the ventricular pressure falls below the aortic pressure), so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle [B1]. The elastic wall of the aorta recoils, squeezing the blood and keeping the pressure high [B1].
:::

:::try Data response 3: two protein-digesting enzymes
Q: [8 marks] ★★ (#bio.enzymes) The table shows the activity of pepsin and of trypsin, two protein-digesting enzymes, at different pH values at 37 °C. The activity is in arbitrary units (invented data).

| pH | pepsin / au | trypsin / au |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 62 | 0 |
| 2 | 100 | 0 |
| 3 | 72 | 2 |
| 4 | 30 | 6 |
| 5 | 8 | 16 |
| 6 | 0 | 42 |
| 7 | 0 | 84 |
| 8 | 0 | 100 |
| 9 | 0 | 68 |

(a) Describe the effect of pH on the activity of pepsin. [2]

(b) Explain why the activity of trypsin at pH 5 is lower than at pH 8. [3]

(c) Pepsin has no activity at pH 8 even when there is plenty of protein. Explain why. [2]

(d) State how the pH could be kept constant in an investigation of one of these enzymes. [1]
H: For (b) and (c) go from the hydrogen ions or hydroxide ions to the R groups, to the shape of the active site, to the enzyme–substrate complexes.
A: (a) As the pH rises from 1 to 2, the activity of pepsin rises from 62 to a maximum of 100 arbitrary units [B1]. Above pH 2 it falls, to 8 at pH 5 and to 0 at pH 6 and above [B1].

(b) At lower pH the high concentration of hydrogen ions changes the charges on the R groups of amino acids (breaks ionic and hydrogen bonds) [B1]. This changes the tertiary structure, so the shape of the active site changes [B1]. Fewer substrate molecules fit the active site, so fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form and the rate is lower [B1].

(c) At pH 8 the pepsin is denatured: its tertiary structure and the shape of the active site have changed [B1]. The active site is no longer complementary to the substrate, so no enzyme–substrate complexes form [B1].

(d) Use a buffer solution [B1].
:::

:::try Data response 4: antibodies in a young baby
Q: [7 marks] ★★ (#bio.antibodies) Before birth, a baby receives antibodies from its mother across the placenta. The table shows the concentration of these maternal antibodies in a baby's blood in the first eight months of life (invented data).

| age / months | maternal antibodies / au |
|---|---|
| 0 | 100 |
| 2 | 50 |
| 4 | 25 |
| 6 | 12 |
| 8 | 6 |

(a) Describe the change in the concentration of maternal antibodies. [2]

(b) Calculate the percentage decrease in the concentration between 0 and 4 months. [2]

(c) State the type of immunity that maternal antibodies give the baby. [1]

(d) Explain why this immunity does not last. [2]
H: For (a) quote figures with units and give the pattern of the fall.
H: For (d) think about what the baby's own cells have and have not done.
A: (a) The concentration falls from 100 au at birth to 6 au at 8 months [B1]. It falls by half every 2 months (the fall is fastest at the start) [B1].

(b) Decrease \(= 100 - 25 = 75\); percentage decrease \(= \dfrac{75}{100} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 75\%\) [A1]

(c) Natural passive immunity [B1].

(d) The antibodies are proteins that are broken down (hydrolysed) in the baby's body and are not replaced [B1]. The baby's own B-lymphocytes were not stimulated by the antigens, so no memory cells or plasma cells were made [B1].
:::

:::try Data response 5: a cell culture and a drug
Q: [8 marks] ★★ (#bio.mitosis) Human cells were grown in a nutrient solution. The cells in one flask had no drug. A drug that inhibits DNA polymerase was added to a second flask at the start. The number of cells in each flask was counted at intervals (invented data).

| time / h | no drug / 10⁴ cells per mL | with drug / 10⁴ cells per mL |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 2.0 | 2.0 |
| 12 | 4.1 | 2.1 |
| 24 | 7.9 | 2.0 |
| 36 | 16.2 | 2.1 |
| 48 | 31.6 | 2.0 |

(a) Describe the change in the number of cells with no drug. [2]

(b) Explain this change. [2]

(c) Calculate the percentage increase in the number of cells with no drug between 0 and 12 h. [2]

(d) Explain the results with the drug. [2]
H: For (d) say when in the cell cycle DNA polymerase works, and what the cell needs before mitosis.
A: (a) The number of cells increases from \(2.0 \times 10^4\) to \(31.6 \times 10^4\) per mL in 48 h [B1]. It roughly doubles every 12 h [B1].

(b) The cells divide by mitosis [B1]. Each division makes two genetically identical daughter cells, so the number doubles with each cell cycle [B1].

(c) Increase \(= 4.1 - 2.0 = 2.1\); percentage increase \(= \dfrac{2.1}{2.0} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 105\%\) [A1]

(d) With the drug the number stays at about \(2.0 \times 10^4\) per mL [B1], because DNA cannot be replicated in the S phase, so the cells cannot go on to divide by mitosis [B1].
:::

:::try Data response 6: water potentials in a root
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#bio.planttransport) The table shows the water potential of the soil solution and of parts of a root of a young plant (invented data).

| region | water potential / kPa |
|---|---|
| soil solution | −100 |
| root hair cell | −300 |
| cortex cell | −400 |
| endodermis cell | −550 |
| xylem | −700 |

(a) Describe how water moves from the soil to the xylem, using the data. [2]

(b) Too much fertiliser was added, which lowered the water potential of the soil solution to −500 kPa. Predict what happens to water in the root hair cells, and explain why. [2]

(c) Explain the role of the Casparian strip in the movement of water into the xylem. [2]
H: For (b) compare −500 with −300 and remember that water moves towards the more negative value.
A: (a) Water moves by osmosis [B1], down a water potential gradient, from the soil (−100 kPa) to the root hair cell (−300), cortex, endodermis and xylem (−700 kPa), through partially permeable membranes [B1].

(b) Water moves out of the root hair cells into the soil [B1], because the soil solution (−500 kPa) now has a lower (more negative) water potential than the root hair cell (−300 kPa) [B1].

(c) The Casparian strip is a waterproof band (containing suberin) in the walls of the endodermis cells, and it blocks the apoplast pathway [B1]. Water must therefore pass through the cytoplasm (the symplast), across a partially permeable membrane, which controls which substances enter the xylem [B1].
:::

:::try Data response 7: insecticide-treated bed nets
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.disease) In 2019, insecticide-treated bed nets were given to the families in one group of villages. A second group of similar villages received none. The table shows the number of cases of malaria per 1000 children each year (invented data).

| year | with nets / cases per 1000 | without nets / cases per 1000 |
|---|---|---|
| 2018 | 210 | 208 |
| 2019 | 140 | 215 |
| 2020 | 96 | 205 |
| 2021 | 70 | 212 |

(a) Describe the differences between the two groups of villages. [2]

(b) Calculate the percentage decrease in cases in the villages with nets between 2018 and 2021. [2]

(c) Explain how insecticide-treated nets reduce the number of cases of malaria. [2]

(d) Suggest one economic reason why cases did not fall to zero in the villages with nets. [2]
H: For (a) one group changes and the other does not. For (d) think about what it costs to keep nets in use.
A: (a) In the villages with nets, the cases fell steadily from 210 to 70 per 1000 children [B1]. In the villages without nets the numbers did not change much, staying between 205 and 215 per 1000 [B1].

(b) Decrease \(= 210 - 70 = 140\); percentage decrease \(= \dfrac{140}{210} \times 100\) [M1] \(= 67\%\) (66.7%) [A1]

(c) The net stops mosquitoes (the vector) from reaching people at night, so fewer people are bitten and fewer *Plasmodium* parasites are transmitted [B1]. The insecticide kills the mosquitoes that land on the net, so the number of vectors falls [B1].

(d) Nets wear out and have to be replaced, and families (or governments) may not be able to afford enough new nets [B1], so some children are left unprotected and still bitten [B1]. (Also credit: not enough money for the insecticide treatment, for education about use, or for drugs.)

:::try Data response 8: haemoglobin at altitude
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ (#bio.oxygen) Use the graph above.

(a) Compare the saturation of the two types of haemoglobin at 4 kPa. [1]

(b) State which haemoglobin has the higher affinity for oxygen, and how the graph shows this. [2]

(c) The partial pressure of oxygen in the air in the alveoli is lower at high altitude. Explain how the haemoglobin of mammal X is an advantage to it. [2]

(d) A respiring muscle has a partial pressure of oxygen of 4 kPa. Using the values at 8 kPa and 4 kPa, calculate the percentage of the haemoglobin that unloads its oxygen when the partial pressure falls from 8 kPa to 4 kPa, for each type, and explain what the difference shows. [2]
H: For (d) subtract the two saturations for each curve, then think about how easily each haemoglobin gives up oxygen.
A: (a) At 4 kPa the haemoglobin of mammal X is 83% saturated, compared with 61% for human haemoglobin [B1].

(b) Mammal X [B1]. Its curve is to the left of the human curve, so it is more saturated at any given partial pressure of oxygen [B1].

(c) At the lower partial pressure of oxygen in the alveoli, the haemoglobin of mammal X still becomes nearly saturated (97% at 8 kPa) [B1], so more oxygen is carried to the tissues than if it had human haemoglobin [B1].

(d) Human haemoglobin: \(91 - 61 = 30\%\); mammal X: \(97 - 83 = 14\%\) [M1]. The haemoglobin of mammal X gives up less of its oxygen at 4 kPa (it holds on to it more tightly), so the respiring tissue gets less oxygen unless its partial pressure of oxygen falls further [A1]. ## Section 3: six extended synoptic questions

These join topics, as the last part of a Paper 2 question often does. Plan each answer as a list of marking points before you write it.

:::try Extended 1: starch digestion and the absorption of glucose
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.enzymes) Starch in a meal is digested to glucose, which is absorbed through the cells that line the small intestine. Amylase is secreted into the gut and hydrolyses starch to maltose. Maltase is an enzyme in the cell surface membrane of the epithelial cells, and it hydrolyses maltose to glucose.

(a) Explain why amylase hydrolyses starch but does not hydrolyse protein. [2]

(b) Name the bond that is broken when maltose is hydrolysed, and state what is added to break it. [2]

(c) The cell surface membrane of the epithelial cells forms microvilli. Explain how microvilli help digestion and absorption. [2]

(d) Glucose leaves the epithelial cells and enters the blood by facilitated diffusion. Describe how glucose crosses the membrane in this way. [2]
H: For (c) there are two things on the membrane that need space. For (d) say what the glucose moves through, and which way it moves.
A: (a) The active site of amylase has a shape complementary to starch [B1] but not to protein molecules, so only starch forms enzyme–substrate complexes (the enzyme is specific) [B1].

(b) The glycosidic bond [B1], by adding water (hydrolysis) [B1].

(c) Microvilli give the membrane a larger surface area [B1], so there is room for more maltase molecules and more transport proteins to digest and absorb more glucose [B1].

(d) Glucose moves down its concentration gradient, from a higher concentration in the cell to a lower concentration in the blood [B1], through the channel or carrier proteins in the membrane, without using ATP [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 2: DNA replication and mitosis
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.dna) A skin cell divides by mitosis to make two daughter cells that must carry the same genetic information as the parent cell.

(a) Describe how semi-conservative replication makes two identical DNA molecules. [4]

(b) State the phase of the cell cycle in which DNA is replicated. [1]

(c) Explain why DNA must be replicated before mitosis. [2]

(d) State the function of the telomeres at the ends of the chromosomes. [1]
H: For (a) list four steps: the strands, the pairing, the enzyme, and the result.
A: (a) The hydrogen bonds between the bases break and the two strands separate, and each strand acts as a template [B1]. Free nucleotides pair with the exposed bases by complementary base pairing (A with T, C with G) [B1]. DNA polymerase joins the nucleotides together (by phosphodiester bonds) to make the new strand [B1]. Each new DNA molecule has one original strand and one new strand [B1].

(b) The S phase of interphase [B1].

(c) So that each daughter cell receives a complete copy of all the DNA (the same number of chromosomes) [B1] and so that each daughter cell is genetically identical to the parent cell [B1].

(d) Telomeres prevent the loss of genes from the ends of the chromosomes when the DNA is replicated [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 3: haemoglobin, from structure to oxygen transport
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.proteins) Haemoglobin is the protein that carries oxygen in red blood cells.

(a) Describe the structure of a haemoglobin molecule. [2]

(b) Explain why the oxygen dissociation curve of haemoglobin is S-shaped. [3]

(c) A sprinter's muscles produce a large amount of carbon dioxide. Explain the effect of this on the release of oxygen from haemoglobin. [3]
H: For (b) start with the first oxygen molecule and say how it changes the molecule. For (c) say what carbon dioxide does to the pH, then what the pH does to haemoglobin.
A: (a) Four polypeptide chains (two α-globin and two β-globin) make up the quaternary structure [B1]. Each chain has a haem group containing an iron ion, which binds one oxygen molecule [B1].

(b) At a low partial pressure of oxygen, the first oxygen molecule binds slowly, because the binding site is hard to reach, so the curve is shallow at first [B1]. Its binding changes the shape of the haemoglobin so that the next oxygen molecules bind more easily (cooperative binding), and the curve becomes steep [B1]. The curve flattens as it nears 100% saturation, because there are few binding sites left and the last site is hard to fill [B1].

(c) Carbon dioxide dissolves and forms an acid, lowering the pH (more hydrogen ions) [B1]. This changes the shape of the haemoglobin so that it has a lower affinity for oxygen and the curve shifts to the right (the Bohr shift) [B1]. Oxygen is released more readily to the respiring muscle [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 4: too much fertiliser
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.planttransport) A gardener added much more fertiliser than the instructions advised to the soil around some lettuce plants. Within three days the leaves of the plants wilted and growth slowed.

(a) Explain why the plants wilted. [4]

(b) Suggest why the growth of the lettuce slowed. [2]

(c) Explain why heavy watering of the soil may let the plants recover. [2]
H: For (a) use the words water potential, osmosis and turgid in a chain. For (b) think of what the leaves need to make food.
A: (a) The fertiliser dissolves, so the soil solution has a lower (more negative) water potential than the root hair cells [B1]. Water leaves the root hair cells by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, through the partially permeable membrane [B1]. The cells lose turgor (the protoplasts shrink) [B1], so the leaves become flaccid and wilt [B1].

(b) Less water reaches the leaves, so less is available for photosynthesis [B1]. The stomata close to reduce water loss, so less carbon dioxide diffuses in and less sugar is made for growth [B1].

(c) Watering dilutes the soil solution, so its water potential rises above that of the root hair cells [B1]. Water then enters the cells by osmosis and they become turgid again [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 5: antibodies and protein structure
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.antibodies) Antibodies are proteins made by plasma cells.

(a) Describe the structure of an antibody molecule. [3]

(b) Explain why an antibody against an influenza virus does not bind to a measles virus. [2]

(c) A gene mutation changes one amino acid in the variable region of an antibody. Explain how this could stop the antibody binding to its antigen. [2]

(d) Monoclonal antibodies are made by fusing B-lymphocytes with another type of cell. Name the other type of cell. [1]
H: For (c) start from the primary structure and end at the shape of the antigen-binding site.
A: (a) Four polypeptide chains: two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains [B1], held together by disulfide bonds [B1]. The ends of the two arms are the variable regions, which form the two antigen-binding sites [B1]. (Also credit: a Y shape with a constant region in the stem.)

(b) The variable region has a specific tertiary shape, set by its sequence of amino acids [B1]. This binding site is complementary only to the antigen of the influenza virus, so it does not fit the antigens of the measles virus [B1].

(c) The change in the primary structure changes the bonds between the R groups, so the tertiary structure of the variable region changes [B1]. The shape of the antigen-binding site is no longer complementary to the antigen, so the antibody cannot bind it [B1].

(d) Tumour (cancer or myeloma) cell [B1].
:::

:::try Extended 6: cholera, ions and osmosis
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#bio.disease) Cholera is a disease of the small intestine. The cholera toxin causes the epithelial cells to release chloride ions into the lumen of the gut.

(a) Name the pathogen that causes cholera and state how people become infected. [2]

(b) Explain how the release of chloride ions causes watery diarrhoea. [3]

(c) Oral rehydration solution contains water, salts and glucose. Explain why a patient is given ions as well as water. [2]

(d) State one way to prevent the spread of cholera in a town. [1]
H: For (b) chloride ions change the water potential of the lumen: say which way, and what water then does.
A: (a) *Vibrio cholerae*, a bacterium [B1]. People swallow water or food contaminated with faeces that contain the bacteria [B1].

(b) The chloride ions make the water potential of the contents of the lumen lower (more negative) than that of the cells and the blood [B1]. Water moves into the lumen by osmosis, down the water potential gradient, through the partially permeable membranes [B1]. This large volume of water in the gut is lost as watery diarrhoea [B1].

(c) The patient has lost ions as well as water, so the ions must be replaced [B1]. A solution with the right ions has a water potential similar to the body fluids, so the water is kept in the blood and tissues instead of moving out by osmosis [B1]. (Also credit: ions and glucose help the absorption of water.)

(d) Treat sewage and provide clean drinking water (or improve hygiene, or vaccinate) [B1].
:::

## Section 4: ten multiple-choice questions

Choose the one correct answer. Read all four options before you decide: each wrong option is a real mistake that students make.

:::quiz Mixed multiple choice
? Which statement about resolution is correct?
- It is the number of times an image is larger than the specimen.
- It is the brightness of the image of a specimen.
+ It is the smallest distance between two points at which they can still be seen as separate.
- It increases when the magnification of a light microscope is increased without limit.
> Resolution is about detail, not size. Magnification can be increased without limit, but once it is beyond the resolution of the microscope it only makes a bigger blur.

? Which level of protein structure is held in shape mainly by hydrogen bonds between the C=O and N–H groups of the polypeptide backbone?
- primary
+ secondary
- tertiary
- quaternary
> Primary structure is the sequence of amino acids (peptide bonds). Tertiary structure comes from bonds between R groups, and quaternary structure is how several polypeptides fit together.

? Which statement describes a non-competitive inhibitor?
- It binds to the active site in place of the substrate.
- Its effect is removed by adding a large amount of substrate.
+ It binds to a site other than the active site and changes the shape of the active site.
- It is a substrate that is converted to products more slowly than the real substrate.
> A non-competitive inhibitor does not compete for the active site, so more substrate cannot overcome it and Vmax falls.

? A plant cell is placed in a solution with a lower water potential than its cell sap. What happens?
- Water enters the cell by osmosis until it bursts.
- Sucrose enters the cell by active transport and the cell becomes turgid.
- Nothing happens, because the cell wall is fully permeable to water.
+ Water leaves the cell by osmosis and the protoplast pulls away from the cell wall.
> Water moves from the higher water potential (the cell sap) to the lower (the solution). Plant cells do not burst because of the wall, but they do plasmolyse in a strong solution.

? DNA polymerase can add nucleotides only to the 3′ end of a growing strand. What is the result of this?
- DNA replication is semi-conservative.
- The two strands of the double helix are antiparallel.
- The hydrogen bonds between the bases are broken.
+ One of the new strands is made in short fragments that are then joined.
> The lagging strand is made in fragments because it runs the opposite way to the direction in which the polymerase works. The fragments are joined by DNA ligase.

? Which statement best explains why the water in a xylem vessel does not break into separate columns?
- Companion cells pump the water up the vessel using ATP.
+ Water molecules stick to each other by hydrogen bonds (cohesion) and to the walls (adhesion).
- Water moves up by osmosis between the vessel elements.
- The thick lignin walls of xylem vessels squeeze the water upwards.
> Cohesion between water molecules and adhesion to the wall keep the column continuous under tension. Xylem vessels are dead and use no ATP.

? Which event happens at the start of ventricular systole?
- The semilunar valves close.
- The atrioventricular valves open.
+ The atrioventricular valves close, because the ventricular pressure is higher than the atrial pressure.
- The atria contract and force blood into the ventricles.
> As the ventricles start to contract, the pressure inside them rises above the pressure in the atria and the AV valves slam shut. The atria contract earlier, in atrial systole.

? Which feature maintains a steep concentration gradient of oxygen across the wall of an alveolus?
- The thickness of the alveolar wall is several cells.
+ Ventilation of the alveolus and the flow of blood through the capillaries.
- The presence of elastic fibres in the wall of the bronchiole.
- The mucus on the lining of the alveolus.
> Breathing keeps replacing the air that has lost oxygen, and the blood flow carries oxygen away. A thick wall would reduce diffusion, and mucus and elastic fibres do not set up the gradient.

? Why is the antibiotic penicillin not effective against HIV?
- HIV has a thick cell wall that penicillin cannot cross.
- HIV is a protoctist, and penicillin kills only bacteria.
- HIV breaks down penicillin using its own enzymes.
+ HIV is a virus with no cell wall and replicates inside host cells.
> Penicillin stops the cell walls of growing bacteria from forming. A virus has no cell wall and no metabolism of its own to disrupt.

? Which is an example of artificial active immunity?
- An injection of antibodies against tetanus after a deep wound.
- A baby receiving antibodies from its mother in breast milk.
- A child making antibodies after catching measles.
+ A child making antibodies after an injection of a weakened pathogen.
> Active immunity means your own cells make the antibodies, and artificial means it comes from a vaccine, not an infection. The first two are passive and the third is natural.
