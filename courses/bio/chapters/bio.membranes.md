## What a membrane does

Look at any cell and you find a **cell surface membrane** around it. Look inside a eukaryotic cell and you find more membranes: around the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts, and in the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi body and lysosomes (see [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells)). All of these have the same basic structure, so one model explains them all.

Membranes do five jobs:
- they **separate** the cell from its surroundings, and one compartment of the cell from another, so different chemical conditions can be kept in each;
- they are a **barrier** that is **partially permeable**: some substances cross easily, others not at all;
- they carry **transport proteins** that let particular substances cross;
- they carry **receptors** that detect signals from other cells;
- they carry **markers** that let cells recognise one another.

:::def Partially permeable membrane
A membrane that allows some substances to pass through but not others. Which substances cross depends on their size, charge and solubility in lipid, and on the transport proteins in the membrane. (The word "selectively" is also used, but "partially permeable" is the syllabus term.)
:::

## The phospholipid bilayer

Start from the phospholipid you already know: a polar, **hydrophilic head** containing a phosphate group, and two non-polar, **hydrophobic tails** made of fatty acids. The molecule has a water-loving end and a water-hating end.

Now put a lot of phospholipids in water. Water molecules are polar and form hydrogen bonds with each other and with the phosphate heads. They cannot form these bonds with the non-polar tails, so the tails are pushed away from the water and cling together by weak hydrophobic interactions. The result happens without any energy input: the molecules **self-assemble** into a double layer.

- In each layer the **heads face outwards, towards water**.
- The **tails face inwards, towards each other**, so they are hidden from water in the middle.
- Every cell surface membrane therefore has water on both sides: watery tissue fluid outside and watery cytoplasm inside. The bilayer is the arrangement in which all the heads can face a water layer and all the tails are hidden.

:::def Phospholipid bilayer
Two layers of phospholipid molecules arranged with their hydrophilic phosphate heads facing the water on either side and their hydrophobic fatty acid tails facing each other in the middle of the membrane.
:::

The bilayer is held together by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds with water, not by strong covalent bonds between neighbouring molecules. That is why it can bend, stretch, reseal after being pricked and let molecules move in it. A whole membrane is about **7 nm** thick, so one bilayer is far too thin to see with a light microscope.

The hydrophobic middle is also why the bilayer is a barrier. Charged particles such as sodium ions \(\ce{Na+}\) and chloride ions \(\ce{Cl-}\), and large polar molecules such as glucose, are attracted to water and cannot dissolve in the non-polar centre, so they cannot slip between the phospholipids. Small non-polar molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide dissolve in the hydrophobic centre and pass straight through. Water molecules are small and, although polar, a few cross by squeezing between the phospholipids.

:::key Why the bilayer is a barrier
1. The **tails** in the middle are **hydrophobic**, so ions and polar molecules (glucose, amino acids) cannot dissolve in them.
2. **Small, non-polar (lipid-soluble) molecules** such as oxygen, carbon dioxide and fatty acids dissolve in the tails and cross freely.
3. Everything else needs a **transport protein**.
:::

## The fluid mosaic model

The proteins of the membrane are not an extra layer. They sit in the bilayer and give each kind of membrane its own character. The syllabus model has two words in its name, and each earns a mark.

:::def Fluid mosaic model
The model of membrane structure in which a bilayer of phospholipids is fluid, because the phospholipid molecules (and many proteins) can move sideways within their own layer, and the proteins are scattered through the bilayer like the tiles of a mosaic, some spanning it and some on its surfaces.
:::

- **Fluid**: the phospholipids are not locked in place. Each molecule moves sideways within its layer, swapping places with its neighbours many times a second. Many proteins also drift sideways. (A phospholipid rarely flips from one layer to the other, because that would drag its polar head through the hydrophobic middle.)
- **Mosaic**: the proteins are **scattered** through the bilayer in a pattern that is not regular, as the tiles in a mosaic picture are. Different membranes carry different proteins.

### The arrangement of the proteins

:::def Intrinsic (integral) protein
A protein that is embedded in the phospholipid bilayer. Many span the whole bilayer and are called transmembrane proteins. Their hydrophobic R groups sit among the fatty acid tails, and their hydrophilic R groups face the water on either side or line a pore.
:::

:::def Extrinsic (peripheral) protein
A protein found on one surface of the bilayer only, not embedded in it. It is held by interactions with the hydrophilic heads or with other proteins.
:::

The way a protein sits in the membrane follows from the rule you learned for globular proteins in [Proteins](#bio.proteins): R groups that are hydrophobic turn away from water. A transmembrane protein has a band of hydrophobic amino acids around its middle, which sits among the hydrophobic tails, and hydrophilic amino acids at both ends, which stick out into the water. That is why it stays in the membrane and why it does not dissolve away. Channel proteins and carrier proteins are intrinsic proteins; so are most receptors.

:::story The fluid mosaic model
S. J. Singer and Garth Nicolson proposed the fluid mosaic model in 1972. Before it, many biologists pictured a membrane as a sandwich: a lipid layer coated on both faces with a continuous protein layer. Electron micrographs of membranes and, later, experiments in which labelled proteins were seen to drift around the surface of a cell showed that the proteins were embedded in the lipid and could move.
:::

## The components of a cell surface membrane

Each component has more than one role. Learn them as a list, because exam answers are marked as separate marking points.

### Phospholipids

- Form the **bilayer**, the basic barrier of the membrane (**permeability**): lipid-soluble molecules cross, ions and polar molecules do not.
- Because they are not bonded to each other, they can move sideways, making the membrane **fluid**. A fluid membrane can change shape, bend, and fuse with other membranes (for example in making vesicles in [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement)).
- Phospholipids whose tails contain a **double bond** (unsaturated fatty acids, see [Water and lipids](#bio.lipids)) have a kink in the tail. The kink stops neighbouring tails packing closely, so the membrane stays fluid at lower temperatures.

### Cholesterol

Cholesterol is a small lipid molecule found in the cell surface membranes of animal cells. Its small hydrophilic end lies near the phospholipid heads and the rest of the molecule lies in the hydrophobic centre, **between the phospholipid tails**.

:::def Cholesterol (in membranes)
A lipid molecule that sits between the fatty acid tails of the phospholipids in animal cell surface membranes. It regulates the fluidity of the membrane and reduces its permeability to water and ions.
:::

Cholesterol works as a buffer for fluidity: it works against whatever change a rise or fall in temperature would cause.
- **At higher temperatures** the phospholipids would move about too much and the membrane would become too fluid. Cholesterol **restricts the movement** of the phospholipids by binding the tails, so the membrane is **less fluid** and more stable.
- **At lower temperatures** the phospholipids would pack tightly together and the membrane would become rigid. Cholesterol **prevents the tails packing closely**, so the membrane stays **fluid**.
- It fills the small gaps between the tails, so it **reduces the permeability** of the bilayer to water, \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{H+}\) ions and other small polar molecules that might otherwise slip through.
- It makes the membrane **more stable**: it adds mechanical strength and helps the membrane keep its shape.

:::warn Cholesterol and fluidity: always say which temperature
"Cholesterol makes the membrane more fluid" and "cholesterol makes it less fluid" are both marked wrong. Cholesterol **regulates** fluidity: it reduces it at high temperature and stops the membrane becoming rigid at low temperature. Plants have other lipids in their membranes, so keep this answer for animal cells.
:::

### Glycolipids and glycoproteins

:::def Glycolipid
A lipid molecule with a carbohydrate chain attached. Glycolipids are part of the outer layer of the bilayer, and the carbohydrate chain points outwards into the watery surroundings.
:::

:::def Glycoprotein
A protein with a carbohydrate chain attached. The protein is embedded in the membrane and the carbohydrate chain extends out of the outer surface of the membrane into the watery surroundings.
:::

All carbohydrate chains are on the **outer surface** of the cell surface membrane, never the inner one. They form a fuzzy sugar coat called the **glycocalyx**. The chains are hydrophilic and hold water, which helps to protect and cushion the cell, and they are the key to three jobs:
- **Cell recognition.** Chains of different shapes act as **cell surface antigens** that identify the cell as part of the body ("self") or as foreign ("non-self") to the immune system (see [The immune response](#bio.immunity)). The ABO blood group of a red blood cell depends on its carbohydrate chains.
- **Receptors.** Many receptor proteins are glycoproteins that bind to a signalling molecule (below).
- **Cell adhesion.** Carbohydrate chains help cells to stick to each other and form tissues.

### Proteins

| Protein | Where it is | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| channel protein | spans the bilayer, forms a water-filled pore | lets particular ions or polar molecules diffuse through |
| carrier protein | spans the bilayer, changes shape | binds a particular molecule and carries it across |
| receptor protein | spans the bilayer, binding site on the outer surface | binds a ligand (a signalling molecule) and starts a response |
| glycoprotein (antigen) | spans the bilayer, chain on the outer surface | cell recognition, or receptor |
| extrinsic protein | on one surface of the bilayer | for example holds the membrane to the cytoskeleton inside |

The syllabus asks about channel proteins, carrier proteins and receptors, so keep them separate in your mind.

:::cols
:::col Channel protein
- Water-filled pore through the bilayer.
- Passive: no ATP is used.
- Lets through **ions or polar molecules of the right size and charge**, often with a gate that opens or closes.
- Substances move **down** their concentration gradient.
:::
:::col Carrier protein
- Binds one type of molecule at a binding site, then **changes shape** to release it on the other side.
- Passive (facilitated diffusion) or active (using ATP).
- Highly specific for the molecule it carries.
- Can move a substance **against** its concentration gradient if it uses ATP.
:::
:::

The details of how substances cross are in the next chapter. For now, remember that transport proteins give the membrane its **selective** permeability.

:::warn Receptor or carrier?
A **receptor** binds a signal (a ligand) on the outside and starts a response **inside**; the ligand itself does not cross the membrane. A **carrier** moves the substance it binds **across** the membrane. Do not write "the receptor carries glucose into the cell".
:::

:::key Roles of the components (one line each)
- **Phospholipids**: form the bilayer; permeability (barrier to ions and polar molecules); fluidity.
- **Cholesterol**: regulates fluidity (less at high temperature, stays fluid at low); reduces permeability; stability.
- **Glycolipids and glycoproteins**: carbohydrate chains on the outer surface: cell recognition (antigens), receptors, cell adhesion.
- **Channel and carrier proteins**: transport of ions and polar molecules.
- **Receptors**: cell signalling.
:::

:::analogy A busy city wall
Think of a membrane as the wall around a city. The bricks of the wall are the phospholipids: they keep most things out. The gates are the channel proteins, always open to certain visitors. The border guards are the carrier proteins, who check each visitor and let them through one at a time. The letterbox on the wall is a receptor: the post stays outside but the message is read inside. The flags on the towers are the glycoproteins that tell other cities who lives here.
:::

(Also credit: the membrane is about 7 nm thick; the heads are in contact with the water in the cytoplasm and the tissue fluid.)

A weaker answer says "it is a double layer of fat with proteins in it". It does not say which end faces which way, it does not name cholesterol or the carbohydrate chains, and "fat" is the wrong word, so it scores no more than two marks.
:::

:::eg Explain why a cell surface membrane is partially permeable.
The mark scheme looks for what crosses, what does not, and why.

Small, non-polar (lipid-soluble) molecules such as oxygen and carbon dioxide dissolve in the hydrophobic tails and pass through the bilayer. [B1]

Ions (such as \(\ce{Na+}\)) and large polar molecules (such as glucose) cannot pass through the hydrophobic centre of the bilayer. [B1]

They can cross only through channel proteins or carrier proteins, which are specific to certain substances. [B1]

Cholesterol between the tails reduces the movement of small polar molecules and ions through the bilayer, so some substances cross and some do not. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the membrane has holes in it for some things". There are no holes in the bilayer itself; transport proteins are specific, and the answer never mentions the hydrophobic centre, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## Cell signalling

Cells in a multicellular organism must coordinate. A cell in the pancreas must be able to tell liver cells that the blood glucose concentration is high. It does so with a **signal**: a chemical made by one cell that changes how another cell behaves.

:::def Ligand
A molecule that binds to a receptor. In cell signalling it is the signalling molecule, such as a hormone, released by one cell to affect another.
:::

:::def Cell surface receptor
A protein in the cell surface membrane, usually a glycoprotein, with a binding site whose shape is complementary to a particular ligand. When the ligand binds, the receptor triggers a response inside the cell.
:::

The syllabus asks for the main stages:

:::steps
1. **Secretion of the ligand.** A signalling cell makes and secretes a specific chemical (the ligand), for example a hormone.
2. **Transport to the target cells.** The ligand travels to other cells. Hormones travel in the **blood**; some ligands only **diffuse** a short distance through tissue fluid.
3. **Binding.** The ligand binds to a **cell surface receptor** on the **target cell**. The receptor's binding site has a shape **complementary** to the ligand, in the same way that an enzyme's active site is complementary to its substrate.
4. **Response.** Binding changes the shape of the receptor, which starts a series of events inside the cell, ending in a **specific response**.
:::

Two points explain why signalling is specific:
- Every cell the blood reaches is exposed to the ligand, but only **target cells** have receptors of the right shape. Other cells ignore the signal.
- Different cells have different receptors, and the same ligand can cause different responses in different target cells.

The signal does not need to enter the cell. The ligand stays outside, and the receptor, which spans the membrane, passes the message on. (Some inside-the-cell messenger molecules, called second messengers, carry the message on from the receptor to the rest of the cell. You do not need them at AS; they are met in the A Level chapter [Blood glucose, cell signalling and biosensors](#bio.glucose).)

:::eg Real context: insulin. Insulin is a hormone secreted by cells in the pancreas into the blood. Explain why insulin changes the behaviour of liver cells but not of cells that lack insulin receptors.
Insulin is secreted into the blood by pancreatic cells and carried all around the body. [B1]

It binds only to cells that have cell surface receptors with a shape complementary to insulin. [B1]

Liver cells have these insulin receptors in their cell surface membranes, so insulin binds and triggers a response; the other cells have no receptors, so there is no binding. [B1]

The binding triggers a specific response inside the liver cells, such as taking up glucose from the blood and storing it as glycogen. [B1]

(Also credit: a hormone has a specific shape; insulin is a protein, so it cannot cross the bilayer and acts through the receptor.)
:::

:::exam "Cell surface" and "complementary"
A receptor is **on the cell surface** and its binding site is **complementary** to the ligand. Write both words. "The hormone fits the receptor" without "complementary" and "cell surface" loses a mark.
:::

## Investigating membrane permeability: beetroot

Beetroot cells contain a purple-red pigment, **betalain**, stored inside the large vacuole of each cell. Pigment molecules are too large to pass through an intact membrane, so a piece of beetroot kept at 20 °C hardly colours the water around it. If the membranes are damaged, the pigment leaks out into the water. How red the water becomes is therefore a measure of how much the membranes have been damaged, which makes beetroot a simple test of **membrane permeability**.

:::lab How temperature affects the permeability of beetroot membranes
**Aim**: find how temperature affects the amount of pigment that leaks from beetroot tissue.

:::steps
1. Use a cork borer to cut cylinders of beetroot, then cut equal slices (for example 5 mm thick) from the same beetroot. Rinse them in running water until the water stays clear, to remove pigment from cut cells.
2. Put the same number of slices in each of seven test tubes containing the same volume (for example 10 cm³) of distilled water.
3. Place each tube in a water bath at one of 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70 and 80 °C. Leave all of them for the same time (for example 10 minutes), checking the temperature of the water bath with a thermometer.
4. Remove the slices (or filter the liquid) and let each tube cool to room temperature.
5. Pour each solution into a cuvette and read its **absorbance** in a colorimeter with a **green filter**. A red-purple solution absorbs green light most. Set the colorimeter to zero using a blank of distilled water.
6. Repeat three times at each temperature, calculate the mean, and plot absorbance (y-axis) against temperature (x-axis).
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is temperature. The dependent variable is the absorbance of the solution. Standardise the volume of water, the number and size of slices (same surface area), the time in the water bath, the beetroot (same plant), the filter and the cuvette.

**Control**: slices in distilled water at room temperature, which gives the leakage from intact membranes.

**Typical results**: the absorbance stays low and rises only a little up to about 40 °C, then increases steeply between 50 °C and 70 °C and levels off at high temperature (see the graph below).

**Sources of error**: slices differ in size and in the amount of pigment; cut cells at the edge leak pigment whatever the temperature; the slices cool while being moved; the colorimeter reading changes if the cuvette is not clean. Improvements: rinse the slices, use more repeats, use a bigger sample of slices, and use temperature steps of 5 °C around the steep part of the graph.

**Safety**: hot water baths can scald. Handle hot tubes with tongs or a holder, take care with the cork borer (cut on a tile, never towards your hand), and wipe up spills. Risk: low to moderate.

The same method tests other factors that damage membranes: use a range of **ethanol concentrations** (made by diluting a stock solution in a serial dilution, see [Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3)](#bio.practical)) in place of the temperatures, at one fixed temperature. Keep ethanol away from flames and use small volumes.
:::

Why does heating damage the membranes?
- The phospholipids gain **kinetic energy** and move more. The bilayer becomes **more fluid** and less ordered, so gaps open between the molecules.
- The **channel and carrier proteins** are made of polypeptides. Heat breaks the hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold their tertiary structure, so they are **denatured** and change shape. They no longer fit properly in the bilayer, which leaves gaps (see [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes) for denaturation).
- The membrane is **no longer a barrier**, so the pigment, which cannot cross an intact membrane, **leaks out** by diffusion into the water.
- Ethanol, an organic solvent, **dissolves** the phospholipids in the bilayer and disrupts the hydrophobic interactions, which also makes the membranes leaky.
- At 20–40 °C the membranes are intact, so little pigment leaks.

(Also credit: the levelling off is because most of the pigment has already left the cells; the rise from 40 °C to 50 °C is 0.13 absorbance units while the rise from 50 °C to 60 °C is 0.36.)

A weaker answer says "the pigment comes out when it is hot because the cells die". It gives no figures, does not say what is damaged in the membrane and does not link it to the absorbance, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State what is meant by the term "partially permeable membrane".
A: A membrane that allows some substances to pass through but not others.
Q: Explain why the heads of the phospholipids face outwards and the tails face inwards in a membrane.
A: The heads are hydrophilic (polar) and are attracted to the water on both sides. The tails are hydrophobic (non-polar), so they are pushed away from the water and group together in the middle.
Q: State two ways in which the fluid mosaic model gets its name.
A: "Fluid" because the phospholipids (and many proteins) can move sideways in their own layer. "Mosaic" because the proteins are scattered through the bilayer, like the tiles of a mosaic.
Q: State the difference between an intrinsic protein and an extrinsic protein.
A: An intrinsic protein is embedded in the bilayer (many span it); an extrinsic protein is on one surface of the bilayer only.
Q: Explain why oxygen can cross a cell surface membrane quickly but sodium ions cannot.
A: Oxygen is small and non-polar, so it dissolves in the hydrophobic tails and passes between the phospholipids. Sodium ions are charged, so they are repelled by the hydrophobic centre and can cross only through a channel or carrier protein.
Q: State two roles of cholesterol in a cell surface membrane.
A: Any two of: regulates fluidity (less fluid at high temperature, keeps it fluid at low temperature); reduces permeability to water and ions; makes the membrane more stable.
Q: Name the part of a glycoprotein that is on the outer surface of the membrane and state one function of it.
A: The carbohydrate chain. It acts as a cell surface antigen (cell recognition), or as part of a receptor, or helps cells stick together.
Q: A bilayer is 7 nm thick. Calculate how many bilayers placed one on top of another would be needed to make 1 µm.
A: 1 µm = 1000 nm, so \(1000 \div 7 = 143\) bilayers (to the nearest whole number).
Q: Explain why a ligand that binds to a receptor on a liver cell has no effect on a muscle cell without that receptor.
A: The ligand can bind only to a receptor with a complementary shape. The muscle cell has no such receptors, so no ligand binds and there is no response.

Many of the proteins can also move sideways (so the structure is not rigid). [B1]
Mosaic: the proteins are scattered through the phospholipid bilayer. [B1]

Q: [4 marks] ★★ Compare channel proteins and carrier proteins. Give two similarities and two differences.
H: Think about the shape, how specific they are, and whether they change shape.
A: Similarity: both are transmembrane (intrinsic) proteins that transport substances across the membrane. [B1]
Similarity: both are specific to particular substances (shape or charge), so they make the membrane selective. [B1]
Difference: a channel protein forms a water-filled pore, but a carrier protein binds the substance and changes shape. [B1]
Difference: channel proteins are only passive (down a gradient); carrier proteins can be passive or active (using ATP). [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Some bacteria make a toxin that is secreted as a soluble protein. It binds to a receptor in the cell surface membrane of a target cell, and several toxin molecules then join together and insert into the bilayer to form a water-filled pore. (a) Explain why the toxin can enter the membrane even though it began as a soluble protein. (b) Explain how the pore kills the target cell.
H: The toxin changes shape when it enters the membrane. Which R groups end up facing the bilayer, and which face the pore?
H: What does a membrane normally keep inside, and what does it keep out?
A: (a) After binding to the receptor, the toxin molecules join into a ring and change shape, so hydrophobic R groups face outwards. [B1]
These hydrophobic R groups interact with the hydrophobic fatty acid tails of the bilayer, so the toxin is held in the membrane. [B1]
Hydrophilic R groups line the inside of the ring, so the pore is filled with water. [B1]
(b) The pore lets ions and polar molecules diffuse through, so the membrane loses its partial permeability. [B1]
Substances that should stay in the cell (for example ions, small molecules) leak out and unwanted substances leak in, so the cell cannot control its internal conditions. [B1]
The cell stops working and dies. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A drug has a shape similar to a hormone and binds to the hormone's receptor on target cells, but it does not cause a response. (a) Explain how the drug reduces the response of the cells to the hormone. (b) A doctor says that a large increase in hormone concentration reduces the effect of the drug. Explain why. (c) Suggest why the drug is unlikely to affect cells that lack the receptor.
H: Compare with a competitive inhibitor of an enzyme in [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes).
A: (a) The drug binds to the receptor's binding site because its shape is similar to the hormone. [B1]
So fewer hormone molecules can bind to receptors, and fewer receptors are activated, so there is a smaller response. [B1]
(b) The hormone and the drug compete for the same binding sites. [B1]
With more hormone molecules, more of the binding sites are occupied by the hormone than by the drug, so the response increases. [B1]
(c) Cells without the receptor have no binding site complementary to the drug, so it cannot bind. [B1]
The drug is specific to the receptor, so there is no response, in the same way that other cells do not respond to the hormone. [B1]

:::recap
- A membrane is about 7 nm thick: a bilayer of phospholipids with hydrophilic heads facing the water on both sides and hydrophobic tails facing inwards. It is partially permeable.
- Fluid mosaic model: phospholipids and many proteins move sideways (fluid); proteins are scattered through the bilayer (mosaic). Intrinsic proteins are embedded (many span the bilayer); extrinsic proteins are on a surface.
- Cholesterol (between the tails) regulates fluidity (less fluid at high temperature, prevents rigidity at low temperature), reduces permeability and adds stability.
- Glycolipids and glycoproteins have carbohydrate chains on the outer surface only: cell recognition (antigens), receptors, adhesion.
- Channel proteins (pore) and carrier proteins (change shape) transport ions and polar molecules; receptors bind ligands.
- Cell signalling: a ligand is secreted, travels (in the blood or by diffusion), binds to a complementary receptor on a target cell, and causes a specific response.
- Beetroot: heat or ethanol damages membranes (phospholipids move more, proteins denatured, solvent dissolves lipids), pigment leaks, absorbance rises.
:::
