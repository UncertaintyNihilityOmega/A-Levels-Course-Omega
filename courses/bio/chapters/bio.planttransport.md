:::try Warm-up
Q: A plant cell has a water potential of −700 kPa. It is placed in a solution with a water potential of −400 kPa. In which direction does water move, and what is the process called? (from [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement))
A: Into the cell, by osmosis. Water moves from the higher (less negative) water potential, −400 kPa, to the lower (more negative) water potential, −700 kPa, through a partially permeable membrane.
Q: Explain how two water molecules form a hydrogen bond. (from [Lipids and water](#bio.lipids))
A: Water is polar. The oxygen atom of one molecule is slightly negative and a hydrogen atom of another molecule is slightly positive, and the opposite charges attract. One hydrogen bond is weak, but there are very many.
Q: Name the polysaccharide in plant cell walls and the monomer it is made from. (from [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs))
A: Cellulose, made from β-glucose. Its chains are held together in microfibrils by hydrogen bonds.
:::

## Why plants need transport systems

A single-celled alga takes in what it needs through its surface by diffusion. A tree cannot. Its roots are metres from its leaves, and diffusion over such a distance would take years. As you saw in [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement), a large organism has a small surface area to volume ratio, so its surface is far too small to supply every cell by diffusion. Large plants need **transport systems** to carry substances between the roots, where water and mineral ions enter, and the leaves, where sugars are made.

There are two:

- **Xylem** carries water and dissolved mineral ions (such as nitrate and magnesium) from the roots up to the stem and leaves.
- **Phloem** carries dissolved organic compounds, mainly sucrose and amino acids, from where they are made or stored to where they are used.

:::key What is transported
Some mineral ions and organic compounds are transported in plants **dissolved in water**. Water is the solvent in both tissues. Xylem moves water and mineral ions; phloem moves sucrose and amino acids dissolved in water.
:::

Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called **vascular bundles** (in roots, in a central core), and together with their supporting cells they are the plant's vascular tissue. This chapter covers xylem and the movement of water. Phloem is the subject of [Translocation in phloem](#bio.translocation).

## Where the tissues are: transverse sections

A herbaceous dicotyledon (a non-woody plant with two seed leaves, such as a sunflower or a bean) has the same tissues in every plant, but they are arranged differently in the stem, the root and the leaf. You must be able to describe each arrangement and draw it as a **plan diagram**.

### How to draw a plan diagram

:::def Plan diagram
A low-power drawing that shows the outlines of tissues and where they lie in relation to one another. It shows no individual cells.
:::

:::key Rules for a plan diagram
1. Use a sharp pencil and draw **clear single lines**. No shading, no colouring, no cells.
2. Draw only **tissue boundaries**: the outline of the section, and the regions of xylem, phloem and other tissues.
3. Keep **proportions** correct: if the xylem fills a quarter of the radius on the slide, it fills a quarter of the radius in your drawing.
4. Make the drawing large (fill at least half the space), with a title that says what the section is (for example "TS of a dicotyledonous stem") and the magnification or a scale bar.
5. Label with **ruled lines** that touch the tissue, do not cross, and are written horizontally to the side of the drawing.
:::

:::steps
1. Look at the whole section at low power. Find the outer edge and the vascular tissue: these decide the shape.
2. Draw the outline of the section. Then draw the boundary of each tissue region in turn, working from the outside in.
3. Mark which parts are xylem and which are phloem by labels, not shading.
4. Label, then add the title and magnification.
:::

In a stained slide, xylem vessels are recognised by their **large, empty, thick-walled** lumens, and phloem by small, thin-walled cells in groups next to them (colours depend on the stain, so do not rely on them). In a real photomicrograph the vascular tissue is often the most strongly stained part. Everything in this chapter's figures is a drawing of what you would see.

:::key Where the tissues are in a dicotyledonous stem
- The **vascular bundles** form a **ring** near the outside of the stem.
- In each bundle, **xylem is on the inside** (towards the centre) and **phloem on the outside**. A layer of **cambium**, which can divide to make new xylem and phloem, lies between them.
- The **cortex** lies outside the ring of bundles, under the epidermis; the **pith** fills the centre.
:::

## Xylem and phloem cells

You need to draw and label the cells of the vascular tissue, and to relate each to its function. We start with xylem.

### Xylem vessel elements

:::def Xylem vessel element
A long, dead cell that is joined end to end with other vessel elements to form a xylem vessel, a continuous tube. Its walls are thickened with lignin, and its end walls have broken down.

Each feature helps water travel up the plant:

| Feature | How it helps |
|---|---|
| **No end walls** between vessel elements | the vessel is one long continuous, open tube, so water moves through it without obstruction |
| **Dead cells with no cytoplasm, nucleus or organelles** | the lumen is empty, so there is nothing to slow the flow of water |
| **Narrow lumen** | a narrow column of water is strongly held together by cohesion and adhesion, so it is less likely to break (see below) |
| **Lignin** in the walls | strong, rigid and waterproof; it stops the vessel collapsing inwards when the water inside is under tension, and gives the stem support |
| **Pits**: gaps in the lignin where the wall is cellulose only | water can move sideways out of one vessel into neighbouring cells or vessels, and into the rest of the plant |

In vessel elements, lignin is laid down in rings, spirals or a nearly continuous layer. The pattern leaves the wall flexible enough to grow in a young stem (rings, spirals) or stronger in an older one (continuous with pits).

:::warn Xylem is dead
The vessel elements are dead when mature. They are not "living cells that pump water". Water moves through them by physical forces (next sections), so the xylem needs no energy from the plant. Do not say that xylem cells are "alive", "use ATP" or "have a nucleus".
:::

## Water enters the root

:::def Root hair cell
A cell of the root epidermis with a long, thin extension (the root hair) that reaches between soil particles. It takes in water by osmosis and mineral ions by active transport and diffusion.
:::

A root hair cell is adapted to absorb: the root hair gives a **very large surface area** in contact with soil water, and the cell wall is **thin**, so water crosses quickly.

- **Water** enters by **osmosis**. Soil water is a dilute solution, so its water potential is high (close to 0 kPa). The cytoplasm and vacuole of the root hair cell contain sugars, ions and other solutes, so their water potential is lower. Water moves down this water potential gradient across the partially permeable cell surface membrane.
- **Mineral ions** are often at a lower concentration in the soil than in the cell. They enter by **active transport** using carrier proteins and ATP (mitochondria are common in the cells nearby). Some enter by facilitated diffusion when the soil is richer. See [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement).

### From the root hair to the xylem

Once inside the root, water has to cross the cortex and reach the xylem in the centre. It can take two routes, and the routes are called pathways.

:::def Apoplast pathway
The pathway for water through the cell walls and the water-filled spaces between cells, outside the cell surface membranes. Water does not cross a membrane.
:::

:::def Symplast pathway
The pathway for water through the cytoplasm of cells, which are linked by plasmodesmata. Water crosses the cell surface membrane once, when it enters the first cell.
:::

**The apoplast pathway.** Plant cell walls are made of cellulose microfibrils, and there are spaces between the fibres, filled with water. Water can seep along these spaces from one cell wall to the next without ever crossing a membrane. In the root cortex this is the main route, because water is pulled along it by transpiration (below) as in a wick. In the xylem, the walls are thickened with **lignin**, which is waterproof: this is why water travels up inside the vessels (not through their walls) and leaves sideways only at the pits.

**The symplast pathway.** Water enters the cytoplasm of a root hair cell through its cell surface membrane, then passes from cell to cell through the **plasmodesmata**, narrow channels in the cell walls lined by the cell surface membrane and filled with cytoplasm. Water moves by osmosis down a water potential gradient, as each cell along the way has a slightly lower water potential than the one before. (Some water also crosses the vacuoles of the cells: this cell-to-cell route is counted as part of the symplast pathway.)

|---|---|---|
| route | cell walls and spaces between cells | cytoplasm, through plasmodesmata |
| crosses a membrane? | no (until the endodermis) | yes, the cell surface membrane at the start |
| main structure used | cellulose cell walls | plasmodesmata |

### The endodermis and the Casparian strip

Around the vascular tissue is a ring of cells, the **endodermis**. In the walls between neighbouring endodermal cells there is a band of **suberin**, called the **Casparian strip**.

:::def Casparian strip
A band of **suberin** in the cell walls of endodermis cells. Suberin is a waterproof, fatty substance, so the strip blocks the apoplast pathway.
:::

Water in the apoplast pathway cannot pass the Casparian strip. To go on, it has to **enter the cytoplasm** of an endodermal cell by crossing its **cell surface membrane**, which joins the symplast pathway. Water and ions then pass to the xylem through the cytoplasm of the endodermal cells, and so the endodermis has two jobs:

- It **forces all water and ions to pass through a membrane**. The membrane has carrier and channel proteins, and these **select which mineral ions** may enter the xylem. Harmful substances in the soil water can be stopped.
- The endodermal cells can use **active transport** to load mineral ions into the xylem, which lowers its water potential and helps water to enter.

:::warn Where are the Casparian strip and suberin?
The Casparian strip is in the **endodermis**, not in the root hair, and it is made of **suberin**, not lignin or cellulose. It blocks the **apoplast** pathway (the walls), not the symplast. A common error is "the Casparian strip stops water going through the cytoplasm".
:::

Follow a water molecule out of the plant:

:::steps
1. Water moves out of the xylem in the leaf, along the cell walls of the mesophyll cells (the apoplast pathway) and through the cells.
2. The cell walls of the mesophyll cells are wet. Water **evaporates** from their surfaces into the **air spaces** of the spongy mesophyll, so the air spaces are almost saturated with water vapour.
3. Water vapour **diffuses** through the air spaces and out through the **stomata**, down a **water potential gradient**, because the air outside the leaf has a lower water potential than the air spaces.
:::

:::warn Where does the water evaporate?
Water evaporates from the **wet surfaces of the mesophyll cell walls inside the leaf**, not from the stomata. The stomata are the gaps through which the **vapour** diffuses. Write: "evaporates from the mesophyll cell walls into the air spaces, then diffuses out through the stomata".
:::

Transpiration is not a waste. It pulls water up the xylem, which carries mineral ions to the leaves, it keeps cells turgid, and it cools the leaf, but it also means a plant on a hot day can lose water faster than its roots take it up (see the wilting example below). Stomata are surrounded by **guard cells**; their opening and closing is a topic for [Control and coordination in plants](#bio.hormones).

## How water rises: cohesion-tension

Evaporation at the leaves explains why water moves, but not how it is lifted up a stem. The answer is in the water molecules themselves, because water is held together by hydrogen bonds.

:::def Cohesion
The attraction between water molecules, because of hydrogen bonds between them. It makes water molecules stick together, so a column of water in a narrow tube does not break easily.
:::

:::def Adhesion
The attraction between water molecules and the walls of the xylem vessels, which contain cellulose (and lignin), formed by hydrogen bonds. It helps hold the water column up and stops it from breaking away from the wall.
:::

:::def Tension (transpiration pull)
The pulling force on the water in the xylem, caused by the loss of water from the leaves, which pulls the column of water up as a continuous stream.
:::

:::steps
1. Water evaporates from the mesophyll cell walls and diffuses out of the leaf (transpiration).
2. This lowers the water potential of the mesophyll cells, so water moves into them from the xylem down a water potential gradient (the symplast and apoplast pathways).
3. Water leaving the top of the xylem creates a **tension** (a pull, or negative pressure) in the xylem.
4. Water molecules are **cohesive**: hydrogen bonds between them mean the pull on the water at the top is passed down the whole column, and the water moves up as a **continuous column** from the root to the leaf.
5. Water is also **adhesive**: hydrogen bonds between water and the cellulose and lignin of the xylem walls help to hold the column up and stop it from breaking.
6. Water is pulled into the root from the soil to replace it, across the root by the pathways above.
:::

This is the **cohesion-tension** theory. The pull is passive, using the energy of the sun that evaporates the water, and the xylem needs no energy of its own. Two observations support it:

- A tree trunk measured with a sensitive gauge is **slightly narrower during the day**, when transpiration is fastest, than at night. The tension pulls the walls of the xylem inwards.
- If a xylem vessel is cut, or an air bubble enters it, the column breaks and that vessel stops carrying water, which shows that a continuous column is essential.

:::exam Explaining water movement in the xylem
These questions are marked on points in order. Use **transpiration → lower water potential in the leaf → tension in the xylem → cohesion (hydrogen bonds) → continuous column → adhesion to the walls**. Say **hydrogen bonds** for cohesion and adhesion, and name the walls (cellulose or lignin) for adhesion. "Cohesion between water and the xylem wall" scores nothing: cohesion is water to water, adhesion is water to wall.
:::

:::eg Explain how water moves from the roots to the leaves of a tall plant.
A full-mark answer gives six separate points, in order.

Water evaporates from the surfaces of the mesophyll cell walls into the air spaces and diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata (transpiration). [B1]

This lowers the water potential of the mesophyll cells, so water moves into them from the xylem by osmosis, down a water potential gradient. [B1]

The loss of water from the top of the xylem creates a tension (a pull) in the xylem. [B1]

Water molecules are cohesive because of hydrogen bonds between them, so the water is pulled up the xylem as a continuous column. [B1]

Water is also attracted to the cellulose and lignin walls of the xylem by adhesion (hydrogen bonds), which helps to hold the column up. [B1]

The lignin in the walls prevents the vessels from collapsing inwards under the tension, and the lumen is narrow and continuous, so the column does not break. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the roots pump water up" or "water evaporates and is sucked up". It never mentions tension, hydrogen bonds or continuous columns, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## Factors affecting the rate of transpiration

The rate depends on how steep the water potential gradient is between the air spaces and the air outside, and on how open the stomata are.

| Factor | Effect | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **Light** | more light, faster | stomata are open in the light and mostly closed in the dark, so more vapour can diffuse out |
| **Temperature** | higher, faster | water evaporates faster, molecules have more kinetic energy so diffusion is faster, and warm air can hold more water vapour, so the gradient is steeper |
| **Humidity** | higher humidity, slower | the air outside has a higher water potential, so the water potential gradient is shallower and less vapour diffuses out |
| **Wind** | more wind, faster | moving air carries away the water vapour near the stomata, which keeps the gradient steep |

:::steps
1. Cut a leafy shoot **under water**, at a slant, so that no air enters the xylem. Keep the cut end under water.
2. Fit the shoot into the tube while everything is under water, so that the apparatus is **full of water with no air bubbles**, and seal every joint with petroleum jelly so that it is airtight.
3. Dry the leaves, then lift the end of the capillary tube out of the water for a moment to let **one air bubble** in. Put the end back in the beaker.
4. Note the position of the bubble on the scale, start a stopwatch, and record the distance it moves in a set time (for example every 2 minutes).
5. Change **one factor** (for example switch on a fan), let the shoot settle for several minutes, and repeat. Repeat each condition three times and calculate means.

**Calculation**: the volume of water taken up is the cross-sectional area of the capillary multiplied by the distance moved, \(V = \pi r^2 \times d\), where \(r\) is the radius of the bore. The rate is \(V \div t\).

**Variables**: the independent variable is the factor you change (wind speed, light, temperature or humidity). The dependent variable is the distance moved by the bubble in a set time (or the volume per minute). Standardise the same shoot (or leaves of equal area), the other environmental factors, the time interval, and let the apparatus reach a steady rate.

**Control**: there is no simple control for the whole apparatus, but a second shoot with its leaves smeared with petroleum jelly (which blocks the stomata) shows how much water is taken up for other reasons.

**Typical results**: the bubble moves further per minute in moving air, in strong light and at higher temperature, and moves less in humid air.

**Sources of error**: air bubbles in the xylem or joints, leaks, a shoot that has been damaged, leaves that are wet at the start, and the shoot taking up water for growth or photosynthesis, not only for transpiration. Improve by repeating, by using the same shoot throughout, and by waiting for a steady rate before each measurement.

**Safety**: take care with the cutting blade (cut away from the hand, on a tile) and with glass tubing. Keep electrical equipment (a fan or lamp) dry. Risk: low.
:::

:::eg A privet shoot was set up in a potometer. The air bubble moved 24 mm in 5.0 minutes along a capillary tube of radius 0.50 mm. Calculate the volume of water taken up and the rate of uptake.
**Step 1: the volume** is the area of the bore times the distance, in mm³.

\[\begin{aligned} V &= \pi r^2 d \\ &= \pi \times 0.50^2 \times 24 \end{aligned}\]

The radius and the distance are in millimetres, so the volume is in mm³. [M1]

\[V = 19\,\mathrm{mm^3}\]

The unrounded value is 18.85 mm³, which is 19 mm³ to the 2 significant figures of the data. [A1]

**Step 2: the rate** is the volume divided by the time, using the unrounded volume.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{rate} &= \frac{18.85\,\mathrm{mm^3}}{5.0\,\mathrm{min}} \\ &= 3.8\,\mathrm{mm^3\,min^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

The rate is 3.8 mm³ per minute. [A1]

Notice that the radius is of the capillary bore, not the width of the whole tube, and that the diameter must be halved first.
:::

| conditions | distance / mm |

| still air, 20 °C | 18 |

**Step 1: the rate in still air at 20 °C.**

\[\begin{aligned} V &= \pi \times 0.60^2 \times 18 \\ &= 20.4\,\mathrm{mm^3} \\ \text{rate} &= \frac{20.36\,\mathrm{mm^3}}{10\,\mathrm{min}} \\ &= 2.0\,\mathrm{mm^3\,min^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

[M1] for the volume, [A1] for the rate with its unit.

**Step 2: describe.** Moving air more than doubles the uptake at 20 °C, from 18 mm to 41 mm in 10 minutes. A rise of 10 °C raises it from 18 mm to 33 mm in still air, and the greatest uptake, 62 mm, is in moving air at 30 °C. [B1]

**Step 3: explain.** Moving air carries away the water vapour close to the stomata, keeping the water potential gradient steep, so vapour diffuses out faster. [B1] At the higher temperature water evaporates faster, molecules move faster and warm air holds more vapour, so transpiration and so water uptake are faster. [B1]
:::

:::eg Real context, a wilting crop: a tomato plant in moist soil is limp at 14:00 on a hot, dry, windy day but is firm again the next morning. Explain why.
At 14:00 the temperature is high, the air is dry and there is wind, so the water potential gradient out of the leaf is very steep and transpiration is fast. [B1]

Water is lost from the leaf cells faster than the roots can take it up and the xylem can supply it, so the cells lose water and their volume falls. [B1]

The cells are no longer turgid: the cell surface membrane no longer presses against the cell wall, so the cells become flaccid and the leaves wilt. [B1]

At night the stomata close and the air is cooler and more humid, so little water is lost. Water uptake catches up, and the cells take in water by osmosis and become turgid again. [B1]

Farmers use drip irrigation, which delivers water close to the roots, and mulch, which reduces evaporation from the soil, so that the roots can keep up with transpiration.
:::

Every feature does the same two things: it **keeps the air next to the stomata humid**, which reduces the water potential gradient between the air spaces and the outside, or it **lengthens the diffusion pathway** or reduces the surface area. Name the feature, then say how it reduces loss of water vapour.

| Adaptation | How it reduces water loss by transpiration |
|---|---|
| **Rolled leaf** (marram grass) | the stomata are inside, in a space of still, humid air, so the water potential gradient between the air spaces of the leaf and the outside is shallower, and less vapour diffuses out |
| **Hairs** on the inside | trap water vapour and slow air movement next to the stomata, keeping the air humid, so the water potential gradient is shallower |
| **Sunken stomata** in grooves | water vapour collects in the pit and is trapped, so the gradient is shallower and the pathway for diffusion is longer |
| **Thick waxy cuticle** on the outer surface | waterproof, so no water evaporates through the epidermis |
| **Fewer stomata** | a smaller area for vapour to diffuse out |
| **Reduced leaves or spines** (cacti) | a small surface area to volume ratio of the leaf, so less area for evaporation; the green stem photosynthesises |
| **Thick, fleshy stems** that store water (cacti, succulents) | a store of water for dry periods; roots may be shallow and widespread to catch rain, or deep to reach groundwater |

:::eg Marram grass lives on sand dunes. Using the annotated drawing, explain how its leaf is adapted to reduce loss of water by transpiration.
The leaf is rolled up, so the stomata are in an enclosed space of trapped humid air. [B1]

Hairs on the inner surface and stomata in grooves (sunken stomata) also trap water vapour, so the water potential in the air next to the stomata is high. [B1]

This reduces the water potential gradient between the air spaces in the leaf and the air outside, so less water vapour diffuses out through the stomata. [B1]

The thick waxy cuticle on the outer surface is waterproof, so little water evaporates through the epidermis. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the leaf is rolled up to stop water loss". It does not say that the humid air reduces the water potential gradient, so only the first mark is gained.

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State two substances that are transported dissolved in water in the xylem and phloem of a plant.
A: In xylem: water with mineral ions (for example nitrate or magnesium ions). In phloem: sucrose and amino acids (dissolved in water).
Q: A drawing of a root TS is 96 mm across. The real root is 1.6 mm across. Calculate the magnification.
A: \(M = I \div A = 96 \div 1.6 = 60\), so \(\times 60\).
Q: State where the xylem is found in a dicotyledonous root and where it is found in a stem.
A: In a root the xylem is in the centre as a star or cross, with phloem between its arms. In a stem the xylem is on the inside of each vascular bundle, in a ring near the outside, with phloem on the outside of the bundle.
Q: State two ways in which a xylem vessel element is different from a sieve tube element.
A: Any two of: the vessel element is dead but the sieve tube element is living; the vessel has no end walls but the sieve tube has sieve plates with pores; the vessel has lignin in its walls but the sieve tube does not; the vessel has no cytoplasm but the sieve tube has a thin layer of cytoplasm.
Q: Explain why a root hair cell is adapted to absorb water.
A: The root hair gives it a large surface area in contact with the soil water. Its cell sap has a lower water potential than the soil water, so water moves in by osmosis down the water potential gradient.
Q: Name the two pathways by which water can cross the root cortex, and state which one crosses the cell surface membrane of each cortex cell.
A: The apoplast pathway (through cell walls) and the symplast pathway (through the cytoplasm via plasmodesmata). The symplast pathway crosses the cell surface membrane (at the start); the apoplast pathway does not cross a membrane.
Q: Explain why the Casparian strip is made of suberin.
A: Suberin is waterproof, so water cannot pass through it. This blocks the apoplast pathway, forcing water to go through the cytoplasm of the endodermis cells.
Q: Explain why water vapour diffuses out of a leaf through the stomata.
A: Water evaporates from the mesophyll cell walls, so the air spaces have a high water potential. The air outside has a lower water potential, so water vapour diffuses out down the water potential gradient through the open stomata.
Q: A geranium shoot in a potometer took up water so that the bubble moved 36 mm in 8.0 minutes in a capillary of radius 0.40 mm. Calculate the rate of uptake in mm³ min⁻¹.
A: \(V = \pi \times 0.40^2 \times 36 = 18.10\,\mathrm{mm^3}\). Rate \(= 18.10 \div 8.0 = 2.3\,\mathrm{mm^3\,min^{-1}}\).
Q: Explain why transpiration is faster on a dry, windy day than on a humid, still day.
A: In dry air the water potential of the air outside is lower, and wind removes the water vapour next to the stomata. Both keep the water potential gradient between the air spaces and the outside steep, so more water vapour diffuses out per unit time.

Q: [3 marks] ★ Describe the arrangement of xylem and phloem in a transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf.
A: The xylem and phloem are in the vascular bundle of the midrib and in the veins. [B1]
The xylem is on the upper side of the bundle [B1] and the phloem is on the lower side. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★ Xylem vessel elements are described as being adapted for the transport of water. Explain how the structure of the vessel elements helps them to carry water up a tall plant.
A: There are no end walls between elements, so the vessel is a continuous tube without obstruction. [B1]
The water forms one continuous column, held together by cohesion, so the pull is passed through all of the water. [B1]
Lignin in the walls makes them strong, so they do not collapse inwards under the tension. [B1]
The cells are dead and have no cytoplasm, so nothing obstructs the flow of water. [B1]
(Also credit: narrow lumen, which helps cohesion and adhesion; pits that allow water to leave sideways.)
Q: [4 marks] ★★ The table shows the mass of water lost by two shoots of the same plant, one in still air and the other in a draught, over 30 minutes at 25 °C.

| conditions | water lost / g |
|---|---|
| still air | 0.46 |
| draught | 1.15 |

(a) Calculate the percentage increase in water loss caused by the draught. (b) Explain the difference.
H: Percentage increase \(= (\text{new} - \text{old}) \div \text{old} \times 100\).
H: Think about what moving air does to the water vapour next to the stomata.
A: (a) \((1.15 - 0.46) \div 0.46 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 150\%\) [A1]
(b) The draught removes the water vapour from near the stomata, so the water potential gradient between the leaf and the air is steeper. [B1]
More water vapour diffuses out through the stomata per unit time. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ A student set up a potometer with a lilac shoot. The air bubble moved 45 mm in 6.0 minutes in a capillary of radius 0.30 mm. (a) Calculate the volume of water taken up per minute. (b) The student wanted the rate of transpiration. State two reasons why the volume of water taken up is not exactly the same as the volume lost in transpiration. (c) Describe how the student could check that there are no air bubbles in the apparatus before the experiment.
H: For (a) use \(\pi r^2 \times\) distance, then divide by the time.
A: (a) \(V = \pi \times 0.30^2 \times 45 = 12.7\,\mathrm{mm^3}\) [M1], rate \(= 12.7 \div 6.0 = 2.1\,\mathrm{mm^3\,min^{-1}}\) [A1]
(b) Some water is used in photosynthesis [B1], or is used to keep cells turgid or for growth, and some is stored in the plant [B1].
(c) Assemble the apparatus under water and look through the tubing for bubbles. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Describe and explain the pathway taken by water from the soil to the xylem in the root. Refer to the apoplast and symplast pathways.
H: Name each pathway, say what it passes through, then explain what the endodermis does.
A: Water enters the root hair cell by osmosis, down a water potential gradient. [B1]
In the apoplast pathway it moves through the cell walls and spaces between cells, without crossing a membrane. [B1]
In the symplast pathway it moves through the cytoplasm, through the plasmodesmata. [B1]
The Casparian strip of suberin in the endodermis blocks the apoplast pathway. [B1]
Water must enter the cytoplasm of the endodermis cells, crossing a membrane, so the cells control which ions pass to the xylem. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The graph earlier in this chapter shows how the loss of water from a potted plant changes during a day. (a) Describe the change in the rate of water loss between 06:00 and 13:00. (b) Explain why the rate is lowest at night and highest in the afternoon. (c) Suggest why a plant could wilt at 13:00 even though the soil is moist.
H: For (a) quote the values from the graph, with units.
A: (a) The rate rises from about 3.3 g h⁻¹ at 06:00 to a peak of 21.5 g h⁻¹ at 13:00. [B1]
(b) At night it is dark, so the stomata are closed or almost closed, and the air is cool and humid, so little vapour diffuses out. [B1]
In the afternoon it is light and warm, so the stomata are open and evaporation and diffusion are fastest, and the gradient is steepest. [B1]
(c) Water is lost from the leaves faster than the roots take it up and the xylem carries it. [B1]
The cells lose water and are no longer turgid. [B1]
The leaves become flaccid and wilt. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (a) Cacti have thick, fleshy stems and leaves reduced to spines, and they have a thick waxy cuticle. Explain how each of these features helps a cactus to survive in a desert. (b) A student suggested that a cactus must have more stomata than other plants because it is so well adapted. Explain why this suggestion is unlikely.
A: (a) Fleshy stems store water for dry periods. [B1]
Spines have a small surface area, so less water evaporates from the leaves and the stem carries out photosynthesis. [B1]
The waxy cuticle is waterproof, so little water evaporates through the epidermis. [B1]
(b) More stomata would mean more pores for water vapour to diffuse out through. [B1]
So transpiration, and the loss of water, would be faster, which a desert plant cannot afford. [B1]
A well-adapted cactus has fewer stomata (often sunken, or open only at night), so it loses less water. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.movement) ★★★ A root hair cell has a water potential of −500 kPa. The soil water around it is −80 kPa. (a) Explain how water enters the cell. (b) The soil dries out and the water potential of the soil water falls to −700 kPa. Predict what happens to the water movement and explain the effect on the plant. (c) Mineral ions are taken up by the cell even when their concentration in the soil is lower than in the cell. Name this process and explain why oxygen is needed.
H: Compare the two water potentials, then reverse the argument for the dry soil.
A: (a) Water moves by osmosis from the soil water to the cell, because the soil water has a higher water potential (−80 kPa) than the cell (−500 kPa). [B1]
(b) The soil water now has a lower water potential than the cell, so water leaves the cell by osmosis, or does not enter. [B1]
The plant cannot take up water, so it loses turgor and wilts. [B1]
(c) Active transport, against a concentration gradient, using carrier proteins. [B1]
Active transport needs ATP, which is made by aerobic respiration, which needs oxygen. [B1]
Without oxygen the supply of ATP falls, so the uptake of ions falls. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A researcher measured the diameter of a pine tree trunk with a sensitive gauge for 24 hours. The diameter was largest at 04:00 and smallest at 15:00, and was smaller by 0.20 mm at 15:00. (a) Explain how this supports the cohesion-tension theory. (b) Predict what happens to the diameter at 15:00 if the tree is sprayed with a chemical that blocks the stomata, and explain. (c) Suggest why the trunk of a tall tree is not damaged by the tension in its xylem.
H: The tension pulls the water column up. What does the column pull on?
H: For (c) think about what lignin does to the walls of the vessels.
A: (a) During the day, transpiration is fastest, so there is a large tension in the xylem. [B1]
The cohesive water column pulls on the walls of the xylem, which adhere to the water, so the trunk narrows. [B1]
At night, transpiration is slow, so the tension is small and the trunk widens again. [B1]
(b) The diameter would change very little, because with the stomata blocked less water vapour is lost, so there is less tension. [B1]
(c) The walls are strengthened with lignin, so the vessels do not collapse. [B1]
Water in a narrow vessel is held together by hydrogen bonds (cohesion), so the column does not break. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A mangrove grows in salty mud, where the water potential of the soil water is very low. The roots take up water, but most of the salt is excluded. (a) Explain why water would flow out of the root of an ordinary plant in this mud. (b) Suggest how the endodermis helps a mangrove to exclude salt. (c) The leaves of mangroves are thick and fleshy with a thick waxy cuticle. Suggest why these features are useful in salty mud.
H: In salty mud, which has the higher water potential, the soil or the root cell?

(b) The Casparian strip blocks the apoplast pathway, so ions must cross the cell surface membranes of the endodermis. [B1]

:::recap
- Large plants need transport systems. **Xylem** carries water and mineral ions up; **phloem** carries sucrose and amino acids. Both carry them dissolved in water.
- **Stem**: vascular bundles in a ring, xylem inside, phloem outside, cambium between. **Root**: xylem in the centre as a star, phloem between its arms. **Leaf**: xylem on the upper side of the midrib and veins, phloem below.
- A **plan diagram** shows tissue outlines only, no cells, in proportion, with a title and labels. \(M = I/A\).
- **Xylem vessel elements** are dead, with lignified walls, pits, no end walls and no contents: one continuous tube. **Sieve tube elements** are living, with sieve plates and no nucleus; **companion cells** have a nucleus and many mitochondria.
- Water enters a root hair cell by osmosis (higher water potential in the soil). Across the root it moves by the **apoplast** pathway (walls, no membrane) and the **symplast** pathway (cytoplasm, plasmodesmata). The **Casparian strip** (suberin) in the **endodermis** blocks the apoplast, forcing water through a membrane, which controls which ions reach the xylem.
- **Transpiration**: evaporation from the mesophyll cell walls into the air spaces, then diffusion of vapour out through the stomata down a water potential gradient.
- **Cohesion-tension**: transpiration causes tension in the xylem; hydrogen bonds between water molecules (cohesion) pull a continuous column up; adhesion to cellulose and lignin helps hold it.
- Light, temperature, humidity and wind change the rate of transpiration. A **potometer** measures uptake; \(V = \pi r^2 d\).
- **Xerophytes** reduce water loss: rolled leaves, hairs, sunken stomata, thick waxy cuticle, fewer stomata, reduced leaves, water storage. Each one either traps humid air (a shallower water potential gradient) or reduces the surface area for evaporation.
:::
