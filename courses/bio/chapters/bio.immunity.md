@summary The immune system finds and destroys pathogens. You will learn what an antigen is and how the body tells self from non-self, how phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages) engulf and digest pathogens, how macrophages, T-helper cells, B-lymphocytes, plasma cells and T-killer cells work together in the primary immune response, and how memory cells give a faster, stronger secondary response and long-term immunity.

:::why
You meet pathogens every day, yet you are ill only occasionally, because your immune system recognises and destroys them, often before you notice. The same system explains why you rarely catch the same disease twice, why a transplanted organ can be rejected, and why HIV is so dangerous. This chapter follows an infection from the first phagocyte to the memory cells that protect you for years, and it is the base for vaccines in the next chapter.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know the cell surface membrane and its glycoproteins from [Membrane structure and cell signalling](#bio.membranes), lysosomes and the route of a secreted protein from [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells), mitosis from [Chromosomes and the mitotic cell cycle](#bio.mitosis), and the idea of a pathogen from [Infectious diseases](#bio.disease).

:::try Warm-up
Q: Which molecules on the outer surface of the cell surface membrane identify a cell to other cells? (from [Membrane structure and cell signalling](#bio.membranes))
A: Glycoproteins (and glycolipids): proteins and lipids with carbohydrate chains that stick out of the outer surface. Chains of different shapes act as cell surface antigens.
Q: What does a lysosome contain, and which organelle makes lysosomes? (from [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells))
A: Hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes. Lysosomes are made by the Golgi body.
Q: How do the two daughter cells formed by mitosis compare with each other and with the parent cell? (from [Chromosomes and the mitotic cell cycle](#bio.mitosis))
A: They are genetically identical to each other and to the parent cell, because the DNA is copied and shared out exactly.
:::

## Two kinds of defence

Pathogens that get past the skin and the lining of the airways and gut meet the **immune system**: white blood cells and the molecules they make. Its defences work in two ways.

:::def Non-specific immune response
A defence that works in the same way against any pathogen, for example phagocytosis by neutrophils and macrophages. It starts within minutes or hours and does not give memory.
:::

:::def Specific immune response
A defence in which lymphocytes respond to one particular antigen. The first response is slow, but it makes memory cells, so a second exposure to the same antigen is dealt with quickly.
:::

| | Non-specific | Specific |
|---|---|---|
| cells | phagocytes: neutrophils, macrophages | lymphocytes: B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes |
| targets | any pathogen | one antigen only |
| speed | fast, within minutes to hours | slow the first time (days), faster the second time |
| memory | none | memory cells |

The two work together: macrophages are the link. They start as phagocytes, and then they show the pathogen's antigens to the lymphocytes (below).

## Antigens: self and non-self

:::def Antigen
A molecule, usually a protein or glycoprotein, that is found on the surface of a cell or virus (or is a toxin) and that stimulates an immune response. Lymphocytes recognise it by its shape.
:::

An antigen is not the whole pathogen. It is a **molecule on the pathogen**: a protein in the coat of a virus, a glycoprotein on the surface of a bacterium or protoctist, or a toxin that the pathogen releases. Each pathogen carries many different antigens. The immune system makes a separate response to each one.

You have glycoproteins on the surface of your own cells too (see [Membrane structure and cell signalling](#bio.membranes)). The immune system must not attack them.

:::def Self antigen
An antigen on the surface of the body's own cells. It does not stimulate an immune response in that person.
:::

:::def Non-self antigen
An antigen that is not part of the body: one on a pathogen, a toxin, or the cells of another person (for example a transplanted organ), which stimulates an immune response.
:::

While lymphocytes are developing, any whose receptors fit the body's own self antigens are destroyed or switched off, so the lymphocytes that remain respond only to non-self antigens. Cells of the body that have been changed by disease, for example virus-infected cells or some cancer cells, may show abnormal antigens and so be treated as non-self.

:::key Antigens in one box
- An antigen is a **molecule** (usually a protein or glycoprotein) that stimulates an immune response.
- **Self** antigens are on the body's own cells and are ignored. **Non-self** antigens are on pathogens, toxins and other people's cells and are attacked.
- The immune system recognises an antigen by its **shape**, which must be complementary to the shape of a receptor.
:::

:::warn "Antigens are the pathogens"
Pathogens are not antigens. An antigen is a molecule **on** a pathogen. In an answer, write "antigens on the surface of the bacterium", not "the antigen enters the body".
:::

:::note Not in the syllabus
When the immune system wrongly attacks self antigens, the result is an autoimmune disease. You do not need it for the exam.
:::

## Phagocytes

:::def Phagocyte
A white blood cell that engulfs pathogens and digests them. The two kinds you must know are neutrophils and macrophages.
:::

:::def Phagocytosis
The process in which a phagocyte engulfs a pathogen or other particle by endocytosis and digests it.
:::

**Neutrophils** and **macrophages** are both made in the bone marrow and carried in the blood. They differ in what they do afterwards.

| | Neutrophil | Macrophage |
|---|---|---|
| size and nucleus | smaller; nucleus with several lobes | larger; one large nucleus |
| where | in the blood, and moves out into infected tissue | in tissues and organs such as the lungs, liver and lymph nodes |
| numbers | the most numerous phagocyte | fewer |
| life span | short-lived | long-lived |
| timing | the first cells to arrive at an infection | arrive later |
| extra role | none: engulfs and digests | **presents antigens** on its surface to T-helper cells, so it starts the specific response |

### How a phagocyte destroys a pathogen

:::steps
1. **Chemotaxis.** Pathogens and damaged cells release chemicals. The phagocyte detects them with receptors and moves up the concentration gradient, towards the source.
2. **Recognition and binding.** Receptors in the cell surface membrane of the phagocyte bind to antigens on the surface of the pathogen. Antibodies attached to a pathogen make this easier, because the phagocyte has receptors for them.
3. **Engulfing.** The surface membrane of the phagocyte folds round the pathogen (endocytosis) and encloses it in a vesicle, the **phagosome**.
4. **Lysosomes fuse** with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes into it.
5. **Digestion.** The enzymes hydrolyse the pathogen to small molecules, which the phagocyte absorbs or removes.
6. **Antigen presentation (macrophages).** A macrophage places some of the pathogen's antigens in its own surface membrane, where T-helper cells can find them.
:::

:::key Phagocytosis: the marking points
1. Phagocytes move towards the pathogen (**chemotaxis**) in response to chemicals it releases.
2. Receptors on the phagocyte **bind to antigens** on the pathogen.
3. The pathogen is **engulfed** by endocytosis and held in a **phagosome**.
4. **Lysosomes fuse** with the phagosome and release **hydrolytic enzymes**.
5. The enzymes **digest** the pathogen.
:::

:::eg Describe how a neutrophil destroys a bacterium that has entered a cut in the skin.
This is a five-step sequence. Each step is one marking point.

Chemicals released by the bacterium (or by damaged cells) attract the neutrophil, which moves towards them. [B1]

Receptors in the cell surface membrane of the neutrophil bind to antigens on the surface of the bacterium. [B1]

The neutrophil engulfs the bacterium by endocytosis, so the bacterium is enclosed in a phagosome (a vacuole). [B1]

Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes into it. [B1]

The enzymes digest (hydrolyse) the bacterium. [B1]

The pus at the site of a cut is largely dead neutrophils, with the remains of the bacteria they digested. A weaker answer says "the white blood cell eats the bacteria". It names no step, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

:::exam Name the cell and the structure
Say **phagocyte** or the cell's name, not "white blood cell" alone. Say **phagosome** (or phagocytic vacuole) for the vesicle that holds the pathogen, and **lysosome** for the organelle that carries the **hydrolytic enzymes**. "The enzymes" alone loses the mark.
:::

## Lymphocytes and the specific response

Phagocytes meet any pathogen with the same response. **Lymphocytes** are the cells of the specific response: each one responds to one antigen only.

:::def Lymphocyte
A type of white blood cell with receptors on its surface that are complementary to one particular antigen. There are two main kinds, B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes.
:::

:::def B-lymphocyte
A lymphocyte that matures in the bone marrow. When it is activated it divides and forms plasma cells, which secrete antibodies, and memory cells.
:::

:::def T-lymphocyte
A lymphocyte that is made in the bone marrow but matures in the thymus (a gland in the chest). The two kinds you must know are T-helper cells and T-killer cells.
:::

All lymphocytes begin in the bone marrow. "B" stands for the place where B cells mature, the **b**one marrow, and "T" for the **t**hymus.

Each lymphocyte carries many copies of **one** kind of receptor on its surface. A B-lymphocyte's receptors are antibody molecules (see [Antibodies and vaccination](#bio.antibodies)); a T-lymphocyte has T-cell receptors. Before you ever meet a pathogen, your body already has a huge variety of lymphocytes, so for almost any antigen there is a few lymphocytes whose receptors are complementary to it. That is the starting point for the response.

:::def Clonal selection
The way a specific response begins: only the lymphocytes whose receptors are complementary to the antigen are activated ("selected"). All the others stay unchanged.
:::

:::def Clonal expansion
The division of an activated lymphocyte by mitosis to form a large number of genetically identical cells (a clone), each with the same receptor.
:::

Here are the cells that you meet in the response. Learn what each one does.

| Cell | Job |
|---|---|
| **macrophage** | engulfs a pathogen and **presents its antigens** on its surface |
| **T-helper cell** | binds to the presented antigen, divides, and releases **cytokines** that stimulate B-lymphocytes and T-killer cells |
| **B-lymphocyte** | binds to its antigen; stimulated by cytokines it divides and forms plasma cells and memory cells |
| **plasma cell** | secretes large amounts of **antibody**; lives for a short time |
| **T-killer cell** | destroys body cells that are infected by a pathogen |
| **memory cell** | long-lived; stays in the body and gives a fast response if the antigen returns |

:::def Cytokine
A small signalling protein released by a cell of the immune system that acts on other immune cells, for example to stimulate them to divide.
:::

:::def Plasma cell
A cell formed from an activated B-lymphocyte that secretes antibodies in large quantities.
:::

:::def Antibody
A protein (an immunoglobulin) made by plasma cells that binds to a specific antigen. Its structure is the topic of the next chapter.
:::

A plasma cell makes protein and sends it out of the cell, so it has a great deal of rough endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi bodies (see [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells)): the route that you met for secreted enzymes is the route of an antibody.

## The primary immune response

:::def Primary immune response
The response of the immune system the first time it meets a particular antigen. It is slow, because only a few lymphocytes have the right receptors and they must divide many times, and it produces memory cells.
:::

:::steps
1. A **macrophage** engulfs the pathogen and **presents** its antigens on its own surface.
2. A **T-helper cell** whose receptor is complementary to the antigen binds to the macrophage. It is **activated** (clonal selection).
3. The activated T-helper cell **divides by mitosis** (clonal expansion) and the clone **releases cytokines**.
4. The cytokines stimulate a **B-lymphocyte** that has bound the same antigen with its antibody receptor. The B cell **divides by mitosis** to form a clone.
5. Cells of this clone **differentiate** into **plasma cells**, which **secrete antibodies** complementary to the antigen, and into **memory cells**.
6. The cytokines also stimulate **T-killer cells** with the right receptor. They divide by mitosis and **destroy body cells that are infected** by the pathogen.
7. The antibodies and phagocytes remove the pathogen. Plasma cells and T-killer cells die when the infection is over, but **memory cells** stay.
:::

:::key The primary response: the marking points
1. **Macrophage** engulfs the pathogen and **presents** the antigen.
2. **T-helper cell** with a **complementary receptor** binds, is activated and **releases cytokines**.
3. Cytokines **stimulate B-lymphocytes** (with complementary receptors) to **divide by mitosis**.
4. B cells become **plasma cells** that **secrete antibodies**.
5. **T-killer cells** are activated and **destroy infected cells**.
6. Some cells become **memory cells**.
:::

Why is the first response slow? At the start, only a few lymphocytes have a receptor that fits the antigen. They must find the antigen, be activated and divide many times to build up enough plasma cells and T-killer cells, and that takes several days. During this time the pathogen reproduces and you may have symptoms.

### What the antibodies do

Antibodies do **not** kill pathogens directly. They bind to the antigens and so they:
- **neutralise** toxins and viruses, because the bound antibody stops them attaching to body cells;
- **agglutinate** pathogens (clump them together), so phagocytes can engulf many at once;
- **label** the pathogen, so phagocytes engulf it more easily.

The structure behind each of these is in [Antibodies and vaccination](#bio.antibodies).

### What T-killer cells do

Antibodies cannot get inside cells. A virus that is inside a body cell is safe from them. But the infected cell shows viral antigens on its own surface, and a T-killer cell with a complementary receptor binds to it. The T-killer cell destroys the infected cell, so that the virus cannot reproduce in it. The same idea applies to some cancer cells.

:::warn Four mix-ups to avoid
- **T-helper cells do not make antibodies** and do not kill anything. They release cytokines that stimulate other cells.
- **Plasma cells do not turn into memory cells.** B cells divide and then differentiate into both: some into plasma cells, some into memory cells.
- **Antibodies do not "kill" pathogens.** They bind, neutralise, clump and label.
- **A lymphocyte is activated by its own antigen only.** Every lymphocyte has one kind of receptor, so only those with the right receptor respond.
:::

:::eg Describe the sequence of events in the primary immune response after a virus enters the body.
Six marking points, in order. Name each cell and what it does.

A macrophage engulfs the virus and presents its antigens on its surface. [B1]

A T-helper cell with a complementary receptor binds to the antigen and is activated; it divides by mitosis and releases cytokines. [B1]

The cytokines stimulate B-lymphocytes that have bound the antigen to divide by mitosis (clonal expansion). [B1]

The B cells differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete antibodies that bind to the antigen. [B1]

T-killer cells are activated and destroy body cells that are infected with the virus. [B1]

Some of the activated lymphocytes become memory cells. [B1]

(Also credit: only lymphocytes with receptors complementary to the antigen respond; the antibodies neutralise the virus or make it easier for phagocytes to engulf.)

A weaker answer says "the white blood cells make antibodies that kill the virus". It names no cell, has no sequence and uses "kill" for antibodies, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## Memory cells and the secondary response

:::def Memory cell
A long-lived lymphocyte, made during the primary response, that has a receptor complementary to one antigen. If that antigen returns, memory cells divide quickly and form plasma cells (or T-killer cells).
:::

:::def Secondary immune response
The faster and stronger response when the immune system meets the same antigen again. It is brought about by memory cells.
:::

After the primary response, there are far more cells with the right receptor than before the infection: the memory B and T cells. If the same antigen enters again:
- memory cells **recognise** it at once, because their receptors are complementary to it;
- they **divide by mitosis** very quickly and **differentiate into plasma cells** (and T-killer cells);
- many plasma cells secrete **antibody faster and in larger amounts**;
- the pathogen is destroyed before it has reproduced enough to cause symptoms.

Memory cells are not plasma cells and do not secrete antibody themselves; they are a store of cells that can respond. Because they live for years, they give **long-term immunity** to that pathogen.

:::eg Explain why a person who has recovered from chickenpox does not usually show symptoms when exposed to the same virus again.
Four marking points, as a chain from cause to effect.

The first infection produced memory cells that have receptors complementary to the antigens of the virus, and they are still in the body. [B1]

On meeting the virus again, the memory cells recognise the antigens and divide rapidly by mitosis. [B1]

They differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete large amounts of antibody more quickly than in the first infection. [B1]

The virus is destroyed before it can reproduce enough to cause symptoms. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the person has antibodies so they cannot get ill". It does not mention memory cells, which are the reason for the fast response.

## HIV and the immune system

HIV (see [Infectious diseases](#bio.disease)) infects and destroys **T-helper cells**. Without them the specific response cannot be organised, so the immune system fails slowly.

**(a)** The number of T-helper cells decreases, from 1000 to 160 cells mm⁻³ over 10 years. [B1]

The fall is slow at first (120 in the first 2 years) and fastest between 6 and 8 years (a fall of 240). [B1]

**(b)** Percentage decrease is the change divided by the starting value.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{decrease} &= \frac{1000 - 160}{1000} \times 100 \\ &= 84\% \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

**(c)** HIV infects and destroys T-helper cells. [B1]

T-helper cells release the cytokines that stimulate B-lymphocytes and T-killer cells, so fewer plasma cells (and so less antibody) and fewer T-killer cells are made. [B1]

The immune response is weak, so infections that a healthy person would defeat (opportunistic infections) take hold; this stage is AIDS. [B1]

## Spot the error

:::eg Spot the error: explain why the secondary immune response is faster than the primary immune response. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Memory cells made in the first infection are still in the body.
Line 2: When the pathogen returns, the memory cells secrete antibodies at once.
Line 3: There are many more cells with the right receptor than at the first infection, so many plasma cells form quickly.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: memory cells do not secrete antibodies. They divide by mitosis and differentiate into plasma cells, and the plasma cells secrete the antibodies. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct Line 2: "When the pathogen returns, the memory cells divide quickly and differentiate into plasma cells, which secrete antibodies." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: Define the term antigen.
A: A molecule, usually a protein or glycoprotein, on the surface of a cell or virus (or a toxin) that stimulates an immune response.
Q: State two differences between a neutrophil and a macrophage.
A: Any two of: a neutrophil is smaller, a macrophage larger; a neutrophil has a lobed nucleus, a macrophage one large nucleus; neutrophils are short-lived, macrophages long-lived; neutrophils arrive first; only macrophages present antigens to T-helper cells.
Q: Name the organelle that fuses with a phagosome and name the type of enzyme that it releases.
A: A lysosome; it releases hydrolytic enzymes.
Q: State where B-lymphocytes and T-lymphocytes mature.
A: B-lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow; T-lymphocytes mature in the thymus.
Q: A kidney from an unrelated donor is transplanted into a patient. Explain why the patient's immune system attacks it unless the patient is given drugs that reduce the immune response.
A: The cells of the kidney have surface antigens that are different from the patient's own, so they are non-self antigens. They stimulate lymphocytes whose receptors are complementary to them, and the patient's T-killer cells and antibodies attack the kidney cells.
Q: Explain why only a small proportion of a person's lymphocytes respond to a particular pathogen.
A: Each lymphocyte has receptors for one antigen only. Only the lymphocytes whose receptors are complementary to the antigens on that pathogen are selected and activated; the rest do not respond.
Q: Explain why the first immune response to a pathogen is slow.
A: Very few lymphocytes have receptors complementary to the antigen. They must be activated and divide by mitosis many times to make enough plasma cells and T-killer cells, and that takes days.
Q: Explain what is meant by clonal expansion and why it is needed in an immune response.
A: An activated lymphocyte divides by mitosis to form a clone of genetically identical cells, each with the same receptor. It is needed because only a few lymphocytes have a receptor complementary to the antigen, so many cells are needed to make enough antibody or T-killer cells.
Q: Antibodies attached to a bacterium make it easier for a phagocyte to engulf the bacterium. Suggest why.
A: Phagocytes have receptors in their surface membrane that bind to the antibodies, so the bacterium is held to the phagocyte and is engulfed more readily.
Q: A student says "plasma cells divide to become memory cells". Explain what is wrong with this statement.
A: Plasma cells do not divide to make memory cells. An activated B-lymphocyte divides by mitosis and its clone differentiates into plasma cells and into memory cells, which are different cell types.
Q: Describe two differences between the primary and the secondary immune response.
A: Any two of: the secondary response has a shorter lag; the rise in antibody concentration is faster; the peak is higher; the antibody stays high for longer.

Q: [2 marks] ★ Distinguish between a self antigen and a non-self antigen.
A: A self antigen is an antigen on the surface of the body's own cells, and does not stimulate an immune response. [B1]
A non-self antigen is an antigen on a pathogen, toxin or the cells of another individual, and stimulates an immune response. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ Compare the actions of a neutrophil and a macrophage. Give two differences and one similarity.
A: A macrophage presents antigens to T-helper cells; a neutrophil does not. [B1]
A neutrophil is short-lived and a macrophage is long-lived (or a neutrophil arrives first, or neutrophils are smaller). [B1]
Both engulf pathogens by phagocytosis and digest them using enzymes from lysosomes. [B1]

Q: [4 marks] ★★ A mouse is treated so that it has no macrophages, but all its other immune cells are normal. It makes very little antibody when it is injected with a protein antigen. Explain why.
H: What does a T-helper cell need before it can be activated?
A: Macrophages engulf the antigen and present it on their surface. [B1]
A T-helper cell is activated only when its receptor binds to the presented antigen, so without macrophages it is not activated. [B1]
So no cytokines are released to stimulate the B-lymphocytes. [B1]
The B cells do not divide and form plasma cells, so very little antibody is made. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ A skin graft was taken from one mouse and placed on an unrelated mouse. It was rejected after 10 days. A second graft from the same donor was rejected after 3 days. Explain these results.
H: Think about what antigens are on the graft cells, and what the first graft left behind in the recipient.
A: The donor cells carry antigens that are non-self to the recipient, so they stimulate an immune response. [B1]
The first rejection is slow because few lymphocytes have receptors complementary to the antigens, and they must be activated and divide (a primary response). [B1]
T-killer cells destroy the graft cells. [B1]
The first graft produced memory cells with receptors for these antigens. [B1]
At the second graft the memory cells divide quickly and form many T-killer cells, so rejection is faster (a secondary response). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.cells) ★★★ (a) Describe the pathway taken by an antibody molecule through a plasma cell, from its synthesis to its release. (b) A plasma cell has much more rough endoplasmic reticulum than a memory cell. Suggest why.
H: Use the route that a secreted protein takes: ribosome, ER, Golgi body, vesicle, cell surface membrane.
A: (a) The polypeptides are made by ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum, and fold inside it. [B1]
Vesicles carry the protein from the ER to the Golgi body. [B1]
The Golgi body modifies the protein and packages it into secretory vesicles. [B1]
The vesicles fuse with the cell surface membrane and release the antibody by exocytosis. [B1]
(b) A plasma cell secretes large amounts of antibody, which is a protein, so it needs much rough ER (with ribosomes) to make it. [B1]
A memory cell does not secrete antibody, so it needs much less. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ T-killer cells can destroy some cancer cells. (a) Suggest why a cancer cell may be recognised as non-self. (b) Describe how a T-killer cell destroys a cancer cell and explain why only some T-killer cells respond to a particular cancer cell.
H: Think about what a mutation does to a protein, then to the antigens on the surface of the cell.
A: (a) A mutation can change a protein so that the antigens on the surface of the cancer cell are abnormal (different from the body's self antigens). [B1]
So lymphocytes see them as non-self antigens. [B1]
(b) The receptor of a T-killer cell is complementary to the abnormal antigen, so it binds to the cancer cell and the T-killer cell is activated. [B1]
It destroys the cancer cell, for example by releasing chemicals that kill it. [B1]
Only T-killer cells with a receptor complementary to this antigen respond, because each lymphocyte has one kind of receptor. [B1]

:::recap
- Non-specific response: phagocytes (neutrophils, macrophages), any pathogen, fast, no memory. Specific response: lymphocytes, one antigen, slow the first time, makes memory cells.
- An **antigen** is a molecule (often a protein or glycoprotein) that stimulates an immune response. **Self** antigens are ignored; **non-self** antigens are attacked.
- Phagocytosis: chemotaxis, receptors bind antigens, engulfed into a phagosome, lysosomes fuse, hydrolytic enzymes digest. Macrophages present antigens.
- B-lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow, T-lymphocytes in the thymus. Each lymphocyte has receptors for one antigen: clonal selection, then clonal expansion by mitosis.
- Primary response: macrophage presents antigen, T-helper cell activated and releases cytokines, B cells divide and form plasma cells (secrete antibodies) and memory cells, T-killer cells destroy infected cells.
- Memory cells give a faster, larger, longer secondary response by dividing and forming plasma cells, and give long-term immunity.
- HIV destroys T-helper cells, so the specific immune response fails and opportunistic infections take hold.
:::
