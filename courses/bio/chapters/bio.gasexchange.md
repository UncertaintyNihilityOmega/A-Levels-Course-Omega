## Why a mammal needs a gas exchange system

Cells release energy from glucose by respiration, which uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. A single-celled organism can take in oxygen through its surface by diffusion because it is tiny: its surface area is large compared with its volume. A mammal is not tiny. Its surface area to volume ratio is small (see [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement)), its skin is covered with a waterproof layer, and most of its cells are many millimetres from the air. So a mammal needs a special **exchange surface** in the lungs, and a transport system (the blood) to carry gases between the lungs and the cells.

:::def Gas exchange
The movement of oxygen from the air into the blood and of carbon dioxide from the blood into the air, by diffusion across an exchange surface.
:::

A good exchange surface has the same features wherever you meet it in biology:

:::key What a good exchange surface needs
1. A **large surface area**, so many molecules can cross at once.
2. A **short diffusion distance**: a thin surface, one or two cells thick.
3. A **steep concentration gradient**, kept steep by moving the air (ventilation) on one side and the blood on the other.
4. A **moist surface**, so that gases can dissolve before they diffuse.
:::

In the lungs, ventilation means breathing in and out. It keeps the air in the alveoli rich in oxygen and low in carbon dioxide compared with the blood. This chapter is about the **structure** of the system; the details of how the chest moves are not needed.

:::def Trachea
The wide airway that carries air from the throat towards the lungs. It is held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage and divides into the two bronchi.
:::

:::def Bronchus (plural bronchi)
One of the two airways formed when the trachea divides, one entering each lung. Each bronchus divides into smaller and smaller branches.
:::

:::def Bronchiole
A narrow airway, less than about a millimetre across, with no cartilage in its wall. Bronchioles lead to the alveoli.
:::

:::def Alveolus (plural alveoli)
A tiny air sac at the end of a bronchiole, lined by a single layer of thin, flat (squamous) cells, where gas exchange with the blood takes place.
:::

:::steps
1. **Trachea**: one tube, wide enough for a large volume of air, running from the throat down the neck and into the chest.
2. **Bronchi**: the trachea divides into a left and a right bronchus, one for each lung.
3. **Bronchioles**: each bronchus divides many times. The narrow branches at the end of the tree are the bronchioles.
4. **Alveoli**: each bronchiole ends in a cluster of alveoli. Each lung contains hundreds of millions of them.
5. **Capillary network**: every alveolus is wrapped in a dense network of blood capillaries. Blood from the heart (low in oxygen) arrives in the network, gains oxygen and loses carbon dioxide, and flows back to the heart. The route of the blood is in [Blood vessels, blood and tissue fluid](#bio.mammaltransport).
:::

Only the alveoli are exchange surfaces. The trachea, bronchi and bronchioles are tubes that carry air to them: their walls are too thick, and too far from the blood, for much gas to cross.

:::key The airway tree in one box
- Path of air: trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli.
- Each branch is narrower than the one before, but together the branches give a huge number of alveoli.
- The **lungs** are made of the bronchi and bronchioles, the alveoli and the capillary networks around them.
- Gas exchange happens **only in the alveoli**.
:::

:::warn Where gas exchange happens
"Gas exchange takes place in the bronchi" and "oxygen diffuses into the alveoli" are both wrong. The bronchi only carry air. Oxygen diffuses from the air **in** the alveoli **into the blood** in the capillaries.
:::

## The tissues in the walls of the airways

You need to know six tissues, and also where each is found. First, what each one is.

:::def Cartilage
A firm but flexible connective tissue made of a smooth, glassy matrix (with collagen fibres in it) with a few cells in small spaces. In the airways it supports the wall and keeps the tube open.
:::

:::def Ciliated epithelium
A lining of tall cells, each with many tiny hair-like projections called cilia on the surface facing the airway. The cilia beat in a coordinated way and move the layer of mucus along.
:::

:::def Goblet cell
A cell shaped like a wine glass that is found among the ciliated cells in the lining of the airways. It makes and secretes mucus.
:::

:::def Squamous epithelium
A lining made of a single layer of very thin, flat cells. Squamous means "flat". The alveoli are lined with squamous epithelium, and so is the wall of a capillary (the endothelium).
:::

:::def Smooth muscle
Muscle made of long, thin cells with one nucleus each and no stripes, found in the walls of the airways. When it contracts it narrows the airway; when it relaxes the airway widens. It is not under conscious control.
:::

:::def Elastic fibres
Fibres of the protein elastin in the walls of the airways and around the alveoli. They stretch when pulled and recoil to their original length when released.
:::

Two more structures are in the airway walls. **Mucous glands** are small clusters of secretory cells in the layer under the epithelium (the submucosa) that also make mucus. **Capillaries** in the wall supply the tissues with blood; around the alveoli they form the capillary network where gas exchange occurs.

### Where each tissue is found

The tissues are not the same all the way down the tree. The wall gets thinner and simpler as the tubes get narrower, and the alveoli have only the thinnest layers.

| Tissue | Trachea | Bronchus | Bronchiole | Alveolus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cartilage | C-shaped rings | irregular plates | none | none |
| ciliated epithelium | yes | yes | yes, in the larger ones | none |
| goblet cells | yes | yes | a few in the larger ones | none |
| mucous glands | yes | yes | none | none |
| smooth muscle | across the gap in the C | a ring inside the cartilage | a thick layer (the most, compared with the size of the wall) | none |
| elastic fibres | yes | yes | yes | yes, around the walls |
| squamous epithelium | none | none | none | yes, lines the alveolus |
| capillaries | a few, in the wall | a few, in the wall | a few, in the wall | a dense network |

As the tubes get narrower, the cartilage gets smaller and then disappears, the goblet cells and glands disappear, and the muscle takes over the job of controlling the width. That is why bronchioles can be squeezed narrow, which matters in asthma (see below).

## What each tissue does

**Keeping the airway clean and healthy.** The air you breathe contains dust, pollen, fungal spores and bacteria. Three structures work together to stop them reaching the alveoli:

:::steps
1. **Goblet cells and mucous glands** secrete mucus, a sticky fluid.
2. The mucus **traps** dust particles and pathogens such as bacteria.
3. The **cilia** of the ciliated epithelial cells beat in the same direction and sweep the mucus, with what it has trapped, up towards the throat.
4. The mucus is swallowed (stomach acid kills many of the bacteria in it) or coughed out.
:::

**Keeping the airway open and controlling its width.**

| Tissue | Function |
|---|---|
| cartilage | supports the wall and keeps the trachea and bronchi open, so they do not collapse when the pressure inside falls as you breathe in; the gap in the C lets the food tube behind the trachea bulge when you swallow |
| smooth muscle | contracts to narrow the airway and relaxes to widen it, which controls how much air reaches the alveoli |
| elastic fibres | stretch as the lungs fill when you breathe in, and recoil as you breathe out, which helps push the air out |
| squamous epithelium | forms a wall one cell thick in the alveoli, so gases have only a very short distance to diffuse |

:::warn Elastic fibres do not contract
Only muscle contracts. Elastic fibres are not muscle: they are stretched and then **recoil** passively. Write "the elastic fibres recoil", never "the elastic fibres contract". Also remember that only the trachea and bronchi have cartilage, and the alveoli have no cilia and no cartilage.
:::

:::key Which tissue does which job
- **Cilia + goblet cells + mucous glands**: trap and remove dust and pathogens.
- **Cartilage**: keeps the larger airways open.
- **Smooth muscle**: narrows or widens the airway.
- **Elastic fibres**: stretch on breathing in, recoil on breathing out.
- **Squamous epithelium**: a very thin wall for gas exchange.
:::

:::eg A student is shown two sections cut across airways. Section X has a wide lumen, a lining with tall ciliated cells and goblet cells, and a C-shaped piece of cartilage with muscle across the gap. Section Y has a narrow lumen with a folded wall, a thick layer of smooth muscle, a lining with few or no goblet cells and no cartilage. Identify each airway and give the evidence.
**Section X is the trachea.**

The C-shaped cartilage, with smooth muscle across the gap in the C, is found only in the trachea. [B1]

There is no cartilage in the wall, which tells you it is a bronchiole (a bronchus would have plates of cartilage). [B1]

The wall has a relatively thick layer of smooth muscle and few or no goblet cells, and the narrow lumen and folds show that the muscle has contracted. [B1]

A weaker answer says "X is a bronchus because it is big". It does not use a feature of the wall, so it scores nothing: always name the tissue you can see.

:::key How the alveoli are adapted for gas exchange
| Feature | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| hundreds of millions of tiny alveoli | a very large total surface area for gas exchange |
| wall of squamous epithelium, one cell thick | a very short diffusion distance |
| capillary wall of squamous endothelium, one cell thick, with the capillary very close to the alveolus | a second very short distance; red blood cells are pressed close to the air |
| a dense network of capillaries around each alveolus | a large blood supply; the blood carries oxygen away and brings carbon dioxide, which keeps the gradient steep |
| ventilation brings fresh air into the alveoli and removes air rich in carbon dioxide | the gradient for oxygen and for carbon dioxide stays steep |
| a thin film of water on the inside of the wall | oxygen dissolves in it before it diffuses into the cells |
| elastic fibres in the wall | the alveolus stretches as it fills and recoils to push air out |

**The route of oxygen.** Oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveolus, down its concentration gradient, through:
1. the **squamous epithelial cell** of the alveolus,
2. the fused basement membranes between the two layers,
3. the **squamous endothelial cell** of the capillary,
4. the **plasma**, and then into a **red blood cell**, where it binds to haemoglobin (see [Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide](#bio.oxygen)).

**The route of carbon dioxide** is the same, but in reverse. Carbon dioxide diffuses from the blood in the capillary, through the same two thin layers, into the air in the alveolus, and is breathed out.

The concentration gradient is kept steep by two movements. Ventilation replaces the air in the alveoli, so the oxygen concentration there stays high and the carbon dioxide concentration stays low. Blood flow keeps replacing the blood in the capillaries: blood arriving at the alveolus has less oxygen and more carbon dioxide than the air in it, and haemoglobin removes oxygen from the plasma at once.

:::warn Say "into the blood", and say "diffusion"
Gases cross the alveolus wall by **diffusion**, a passive process: no ATP is needed. Write that oxygen diffuses from the alveolus **into the blood** (not "into the alveoli" or "into the lungs"). The gradient is **kept steep** by ventilation and blood flow. Saying "the alveoli have a large surface area" earns a mark only if you add what it does ("so more oxygen can diffuse at the same time").
:::

:::exam Adaptation questions: feature, then reason
For "explain how the alveoli are adapted", each mark is a **feature** linked to a **reason** that uses the rate of diffusion: "one cell thick, so a short diffusion distance". A list of features with no reasons scores about half the marks.
:::

The effect of each factor can be reasoned from simple proportion. Other things staying the same, the rate of diffusion across an exchange surface is directly proportional to the surface area and to the concentration difference, and inversely proportional to the thickness of the barrier. Doubling the thickness halves the rate.

:::plot A model of an exchange surface: the relative rate of diffusion of oxygen falls as the barrier gets thicker (surface area and concentration difference kept the same; thickness in arbitrary units).

At a thickness of 0.5 the relative rate is 200, at 1 it is 100 and at 3 it is about 33. Halving the thickness doubles the rate."
:::

:::eg Use the graph to describe how the thickness of the barrier affects the rate of diffusion, and calculate the percentage fall in rate when the barrier thickens from 1 to 3 units. Explain why a thin barrier matters in the alveolus.
**Describe**: as the thickness increases from 1 to 3 units the relative rate falls from 100 to about 33. The curve is steepest at small thickness, and doubling the thickness (1 to 2) halves the rate (100 to 50).

**Percentage fall**: the rate at a thickness of 3 is \(100 \div 3 = 33.3\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{fall} &= \frac{100 - 33.3}{100} \times 100 \\ &= 67\% \end{aligned}\]

**Explain**: the thicker the barrier, the longer the diffusion distance. Each oxygen molecule takes longer to cross, so fewer cross each second. The alveolus wall is only one cell thick (and so is the capillary wall), so oxygen crosses quickly and the blood can take up enough oxygen to meet the needs of respiration.
:::

:::eg Explain how the alveoli are adapted for gas exchange. This is a 5-mark question.
Each mark is a feature, with its reason.

The lungs have millions of alveoli, giving a very large surface area for the diffusion of oxygen and carbon dioxide. [B1]

The wall of an alveolus is one cell thick (squamous epithelium), and so is the capillary wall, so the diffusion distance is very short. [B1]

Each alveolus is surrounded by a dense network of capillaries, so there is a large blood supply and blood carries oxygen away, which keeps the concentration gradient steep. [B1]

Ventilation brings fresh air into the alveoli and removes carbon dioxide, which keeps the concentration gradients steep. [B1]

A thin film of moisture lines the alveolus, so oxygen dissolves in it and can diffuse into the blood. [B1]

(Also credit: elastic fibres allow the alveoli to stretch and recoil; red blood cells are squeezed close to the wall.)

A weaker answer lists "large surface area, thin walls, good blood supply" with no reasons, and scores about two marks.
:::

## Looking at the airways under the microscope

You may be asked to recognise these tissues on a slide, a photomicrograph or an electron micrograph, and to make a plan diagram. The pictures in this guide are drawings, so use the cues in the table: in a real photomicrograph the colours depend on the stain, so look for **shape and pattern**.

| What you see | It is |
|---|---|
| a round, wide hole with a C-shaped piece of smooth, glassy tissue in the wall | trachea |
| a smaller hole, with several separate plates of the same glassy tissue and a ring of muscle inside them | bronchus |
| a narrow, often folded wall with thick muscle and no glassy tissue | bronchiole |
| many tiny holes (spaces) like the bubbles in foam, separated by very thin walls | alveoli |
| a smooth, glassy matrix with a few cells sitting in small spaces | cartilage |
| tall cells in a row, with fine hairs on the free surface | ciliated epithelium |
| pale, swollen, empty-looking cells among the tall cells (the mucus does not stain) | goblet cells |
| bundles of long, thin cells with a rod-shaped nucleus, cut as spindles | smooth muscle |
| a very thin line with a flattened nucleus that bulges out | squamous epithelium |
| a tiny tube with red blood cells inside it, wall one cell thick | capillary |

Under the electron microscope you also see the detail inside the cells. In a ciliated cell, the cilia are cut along their length as fine hairs, or across as small circles. In a goblet cell you see many vesicles of mucus near the top. The squamous cells of the alveolus and the capillary wall are so thin that the two layers, with the basement membrane between, look like one line.

:::lab Making plan diagrams of a transverse section of the trachea and a bronchus
**Aim**: draw plan diagrams of the walls of the trachea and of a bronchus from prepared slides, and recognise the tissues.

:::steps
1. Put the slide on the stage and focus at **low power** (the ×4 or ×10 objective), so you can see the whole section.
2. Use a sharp pencil, and draw a **large** diagram, taking up about half the page, with the proportions of the layers right.
3. Draw **only the outlines** of the tissue layers, with single, continuous lines. Do not draw individual cells (a plan diagram shows tissues, not cells).
4. Show the lumen, then the layers from the lumen outwards: the lining epithelium, the submucosa (with mucous glands), the smooth muscle, and the cartilage (a C-shape in the trachea, separate plates in a bronchus).
5. Label each layer with a ruled line that does not cross another line. Add a title, the magnification, and the scale.
6. Repeat for the second slide and compare the two diagrams.
:::

**Variables**: this is an observational task, so there is no independent variable. The skills assessed are accurate observation, drawing and labelling.

**Typical results**: the trachea has a wide round lumen, a C-shaped cartilage ring and a muscle band across the gap in the C; a bronchus has a smaller lumen, a continuous ring of smooth muscle inside several cartilage plates.

**Sources of error**: a section cut at an angle makes the wall look thicker or the lumen oval; a fold or tear can look like a layer; mucus and tissue can shrink when the slide is made. **Safety**: glass slides and cover slips break and can cut; carry slides flat and handle them carefully.

Calculating the magnification of a drawing, and using a scale bar, are developed in [Practical skills: microscopy and drawing (Paper 3)](#bio.practical2).
:::

:::exam Plan diagrams
A plan diagram earns marks for: a large drawing, sharp single lines, no cells drawn, the right proportions between layers, clear labels with ruled lines that do not cross, and a title with magnification. Drawing cells, shading, or labelling in the middle of a layer without a line all lose marks.
:::

:::eg A student made a plan diagram of the wall of the trachea. The drawing had a scale bar 25 mm long that stood for 0.5 mm on the slide. The distance across the whole wall (lumen edge to outside) in the drawing was 36 mm. Calculate the magnification of the drawing and the actual thickness of the wall in µm.
The scale bar gives the magnification, because it is an image length for an actual length.

\[\begin{aligned} M &= \frac{I}{A} \\ &= \frac{25\,\mathrm{mm}}{0.5\,\mathrm{mm}} \\ &= 50 \end{aligned}\]

So the drawing is at ×50. Now rearrange \(M = I/A\) to \(A = I/M\), with both lengths in the same units:

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{36\,\mathrm{mm}}{50} \\ &= 0.72\,\mathrm{mm} \\ &= 720\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

The unit conversion is the step students forget: 1 mm = 1000 µm, so \(0.72 \times 1000 = 720\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).
:::

## When the muscle narrows the airway: asthma

The smooth muscle in the bronchioles matters in everyday life. In asthma, the muscle in the walls of the bronchioles contracts too easily in response to triggers such as pollen or cold air. The lining also becomes inflamed and makes extra mucus. The lumen of each bronchiole becomes narrower, and so air flows in and out less easily.

A **reliever inhaler** contains a drug that makes the smooth muscle relax, so the bronchioles widen again within minutes.

:::eg Real context, asthma: explain why an asthma attack makes breathing difficult and reduces the oxygen reaching the blood, and why a reliever inhaler helps.
During an attack the smooth muscle in the walls of the bronchioles contracts, so the lumen becomes narrower. [B1]

Extra mucus also partly blocks the airways. [B1]

So less air flows into and out of the alveoli, and the air in the alveoli is not replaced quickly. The oxygen concentration in the alveoli falls and the carbon dioxide concentration rises, so the concentration gradients between alveolar air and blood are less steep and less oxygen diffuses into the blood. [B1]

A reliever contains a drug that makes the smooth muscle relax, so the bronchioles widen and air flows more easily again. [B1]

The cartilage cannot be responsible for the narrowing: bronchioles have none. That is why the width is controlled by muscle.
:::

:::recap
- Path of air: trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli, each alveolus wrapped in a capillary network. Gas exchange takes place only in the alveoli.
- Trachea: C-shaped cartilage, ciliated epithelium, goblet cells, mucous glands, smooth muscle across the gap. Bronchus: plates of cartilage and a ring of muscle. Bronchiole: no cartilage, a thick layer of smooth muscle. Alveolus: squamous epithelium only.
- Goblet cells and mucous glands make mucus that traps dust and pathogens; cilia sweep it to the throat.
- Cartilage keeps airways open; smooth muscle narrows or widens them; elastic fibres stretch on breathing in and recoil on breathing out (they do not contract); squamous epithelium gives a very thin wall.
- Alveoli: very large total surface area, walls one cell thick, a capillary network, ventilation and blood flow to keep gradients steep, a moist lining.
- Oxygen diffuses from the alveolus through the squamous epithelium and the capillary endothelium into the plasma and then the red blood cell; carbon dioxide goes the other way.
- Plan diagram: low power, large, outlines only, no cells, correct proportions, ruled labels.
- Asthma: smooth muscle in the bronchioles contracts and more mucus is made, narrowing the airways.
