@summary Monohybrid inheritance is the inheritance of one gene. You will learn the vocabulary of genetics (gene, locus, allele, dominant, recessive, codominant, linkage, test cross, F1, F2, genotype, phenotype, homozygous, heterozygous), how to write a full genetic diagram with a Punnett square, and how to predict crosses involving dominance, codominance, multiple alleles (the ABO blood groups) and sex linkage, test crosses, pedigrees and probabilities.

:::why
Will a child inherit a family illness? What are the chances of a particular blood group? Why do some conditions affect boys far more than girls? Genetics answers these questions with a simple method: write the parents' alleles, work out their gametes, and combine them. Exam questions on inheritance reward a tidy, complete genetic diagram and a ratio or probability stated with its phenotype names, so this chapter teaches the method and the exact layout examiners expect.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know how meiosis halves the chromosome number and shuffles alleles from [Meiosis and genetic variation](#bio.meiosis), and that a gene codes for a polypeptide from [Protein synthesis and gene mutation](#bio.synthesis).

:::try Warm-up
Q: How many chromosomes are in a human sperm cell, and why that number? (from [Meiosis and genetic variation](#bio.meiosis))
A: 23, because meiosis halves the chromosome number from 46, so that fertilisation restores 46.
Q: How many different alleles of one gene can a single diploid cell carry? (from [Meiosis and genetic variation](#bio.meiosis))
A: Two at most: one on each chromosome of a homologous pair (they may be the same allele or two different ones).
Q: A gene codes for the sequence of amino acids of a polypeptide. What can a change in the gene sequence do? (from [Protein synthesis and gene mutation](#bio.synthesis))
A: It can change the amino acids, and so the polypeptide's shape, so a different allele can make a protein that works differently or not at all.
:::

## The language of genetics

Genetics has its own vocabulary, and exam answers must use it exactly. Learn these terms first.

:::def Gene
A length of DNA (a sequence of nucleotides forming part of a DNA molecule) that codes for a polypeptide.
:::

:::def Locus
The position of a gene on a chromosome (on a DNA molecule). The two chromosomes of a homologous pair carry the same gene at the same locus.
:::

:::def Allele
One of the different forms of a gene. Alleles of one gene differ slightly in their base sequence, so they can code for slightly different polypeptides.
:::

:::def Genotype
The alleles of the genes that an organism carries.
:::

:::def Phenotype
The observable features of an organism, caused by its genotype and often by the environment as well.
:::

:::def Homozygous
Having two identical alleles of a gene, for example TT or tt.
:::

:::def Heterozygous
Having two different alleles of a gene, for example Tt.
:::

:::def Dominant allele
An allele that is expressed in the phenotype when it is present with a different allele. It has the same effect in a heterozygote (Tt) and in a homozygote (TT).
:::

:::def Recessive allele
An allele that is expressed in the phenotype only when no dominant allele is present, that is, when it is homozygous (tt).
:::

:::def Codominant alleles
Alleles that both contribute to the phenotype of a heterozygote. Neither is dominant over the other, so both are expressed.
:::

:::def Multiple alleles
Three or more alleles of one gene in a population. Any one individual still carries only two of them.
:::

A heterozygous person who does not show a recessive condition but could pass the recessive allele on is called a **carrier**.

### Crosses and generations

Biologists name the generations of a cross so that results are easy to compare.

:::def F1 and F2
The F1 (first filial) generation is the offspring of a cross between two parents, the P generation. The F2 (second filial) generation is the offspring of a cross between two F1 individuals.
:::

:::def Test cross
A cross between an organism showing the dominant phenotype, whose genotype is unknown, and a homozygous recessive organism, to find out whether the unknown genotype is homozygous or heterozygous.
:::

:::def Linkage
The presence of two or more genes on the same chromosome, so that they tend to be inherited together and do not assort independently. (You will use this in [Dihybrid crosses and the chi-squared test](#bio.dihybrid).)
:::

:::warn Letters, cases and consistency
Use **one letter** for the two alleles of a gene: a capital for the dominant allele and the same letter in lower case for the recessive allele (T and t). Do not use two different letters (T and D) for one gene. Pick a letter whose capital and small shapes are easy to tell apart (use Rr rather than Cc if you write fast), and always say what each allele means in a key.
:::

### Why one allele is dominant

An allele works by coding for a polypeptide. Many recessive alleles code for a polypeptide that does not work, such as an enzyme with a changed active site. A heterozygote has one allele that makes a working polypeptide and one that does not; the working allele usually makes enough protein for the cell, so the phenotype looks like the homozygous dominant one. Only in a homozygous recessive are all copies of the polypeptide faulty. How genes become phenotypes is the topic of [Genes, phenotype and gene control](#bio.genecontrol).

## Writing a genetic diagram

A genetic diagram is a short, standard piece of working that shows how the parents' alleles combine in their offspring. Examiners give a mark for each part, so write every part, in this order.

:::key The layout of a genetic diagram
0. **Key**: which letter stands for which allele and phenotype.
1. **Parental phenotypes**, linked by ×.
2. **Parental genotypes**.
3. **Gametes**: one allele per gamete, each written in a **circle**. (In this guide round brackets stand for the circles.)
4. **A Punnett square**, with the male gametes along the top and the female gametes down the side (or a labelled set of lines joining gametes). Each box is the zygote made when two gametes fuse.
5. **Offspring genotypes**.
6. **Offspring phenotypes**.
7. **The ratio** of phenotypes, using the names of the phenotypes (for example 3 tall : 1 dwarf).
:::

Each gamete carries **one** allele of each gene, because meiosis separates the two chromosomes of a homologous pair. A parent Tt makes two kinds of gamete, (T) and (t), in equal numbers. A parent TT makes only (T).

### A cross carried to the F2

:::eg Pea plants are tall or dwarf. A true-breeding tall plant was crossed with a true-breeding dwarf plant. The F1 plants were allowed to pollinate each other. Draw genetic diagrams to show the F1 and the F2 generations.
**Key**: T = allele for tall, t = allele for dwarf. Tall is dominant, because all of the F1 are tall. "True-breeding" means homozygous, so the parents are TT and tt.

**Cross 1, parents to F1.** Parental phenotypes: tall × dwarf. Parental genotypes: TT × tt. [B1]

Gametes: (T) and (t). Each parent makes only one kind of gamete. [B1]

F1 genotype: all Tt. F1 phenotype: all tall. [B1]

**Cross 2, F1 × F1.** Parental genotypes: Tt × Tt. Gametes: (T) and (t) from each parent, in equal numbers. The Punnett square:

| | (T) | (t) |
|---|---|---|
| **(T)** | TT | Tt |
| **(t)** | Tt | tt |

F2 genotypes: TT, Tt, Tt and tt, in the ratio 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt. [B1]

F2 phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 dwarf. [B1]

Each F2 plant has a 3/4 chance of being tall and a 1/4 chance of being dwarf. The dwarf trait disappeared in the F1 and came back in the F2, because it was hidden by the dominant allele in the heterozygous F1.
:::

:::tip Four crosses to know by heart
- **homozygous dominant × homozygous recessive** (AA × aa): all heterozygous, all dominant phenotype.
- **heterozygote × heterozygote** (Aa × Aa): 3 dominant : 1 recessive, genotypes 1 : 2 : 1.
- **heterozygote × homozygous recessive** (Aa × aa): 1 dominant : 1 recessive.
- **homozygous dominant × anything**: all offspring show the dominant phenotype.

:::warn A gamete never has two alleles of one gene
Write the gametes of Tt as (T) and (t), never (Tt). A gamete is haploid, so it carries one chromosome of each pair and so one allele of each gene. Also give the ratio with phenotype names: "3 : 1" alone loses a mark.
:::

## Codominance and multiple alleles

### Codominance

When two alleles are codominant, both show in the heterozygote. Write codominant alleles as a capital letter (for the gene) with a superscript for each allele. In shorthorn cattle, the gene for coat colour has two alleles: \(C^R\) (red hairs) and \(C^W\) (white hairs). A cow with \(C^R C^W\) has both red and white hairs mixed together, called roan.

| genotype | phenotype |
|---|---|
| \(C^R C^R\) | red |
| \(C^R C^W\) | roan (red and white hairs) |
| \(C^W C^W\) | white |

:::eg Two roan shorthorn cattle are crossed. Then a roan cow is crossed with a white bull. State the genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring of each cross.
**Key**: \(C^R\) = allele for red hairs, \(C^W\) = allele for white hairs. The two alleles are codominant.

**Cross 1.** Roan × roan: \(C^R C^W\) × \(C^R C^W\). [B1]

Gametes from each parent: (\(C^R\)) and (\(C^W\)).

| | (\(C^R\)) | (\(C^W\)) |
|---|---|---|
| **(\(C^R\))** | \(C^R C^R\) | \(C^R C^W\) |
| **(\(C^W\))** | \(C^R C^W\) | \(C^W C^W\) |

Offspring: 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white. [B1]

**Cross 2.** Roan × white: \(C^R C^W\) × \(C^W C^W\). Gametes: (\(C^R\)) and (\(C^W\)) from the cow, only (\(C^W\)) from the bull. Offspring genotypes: \(C^R C^W\) and \(C^W C^W\). [B1]

Offspring: 1 roan : 1 white. [B1]

With codominance the ratio of phenotypes equals the ratio of genotypes (1 : 2 : 1), because every genotype has its own phenotype. Compare this with the 3 : 1 ratio from dominance.
:::

### Multiple alleles: the ABO blood groups

The gene for the ABO blood group has three alleles: \(I^A\), \(I^B\) and \(I^o\). \(I^A\) and \(I^B\) are codominant, and both are dominant to \(I^o\). Each person has two of the three. The alleles code for an enzyme that adds a sugar to the surface of red blood cells: \(I^A\) and \(I^B\) code for slightly different enzymes (so different surface antigens), while \(I^o\) codes for a non-functional enzyme.

| genotype | blood group (phenotype) |
|---|---|
| \(I^A I^A\) or \(I^A I^o\) | A |
| \(I^B I^B\) or \(I^B I^o\) | B |
| \(I^A I^B\) | AB |
| \(I^o I^o\) | O |

Group A is possible with two genotypes, so a person of group A who has a group O child must be \(I^A I^o\): the child gets an \(I^o\) from each parent.

:::eg A woman of blood group A and a man of blood group B have a child of blood group O. State the parents' genotypes and the possible blood groups of their children, and calculate the probability that the next child is group AB.
**Key**: \(I^A\), \(I^B\) (codominant), \(I^o\) (recessive to both).

The child is group O, so its genotype is \(I^o I^o\). It received one \(I^o\) from each parent. [B1]

The mother (group A) is therefore \(I^A I^o\) and the father (group B) is \(I^B I^o\). [B1]

Gametes: mother (\(I^A\)) and (\(I^o\)); father (\(I^B\)) and (\(I^o\)).

| | (\(I^A\)) | (\(I^o\)) |
|---|---|---|
| **(\(I^B\))** | \(I^A I^B\) | \(I^B I^o\) |
| **(\(I^o\))** | \(I^A I^o\) | \(I^o I^o\) |

Possible blood groups: AB, B, A and O, in the ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. [B1]

Probability that the next child is group AB \(= \tfrac14\) (0.25 or 25%). [B1]
:::

## Sex linkage

Sex in humans is decided by two sex chromosomes. A female has two X chromosomes (XX) and a male has an X and a Y (XY). All eggs carry an X. Half of the sperm carry an X and half carry a Y, so the chance that a child is a boy is 1/2.

The X chromosome is large and carries many genes, most with nothing to do with sex, such as the genes for blood clotting and for colour vision. The Y chromosome is much smaller and carries few genes. A gene on the non-homologous part of the X chromosome has no matching locus on the Y chromosome. This is **sex linkage**.

:::def Sex linkage
The presence of a gene on a sex chromosome. For an X-linked gene, a male has only one allele (on his X chromosome), so a recessive allele is always expressed in a male.
:::

Write X-linked alleles as a superscript on the X: \(X^H\) is the allele for normal blood clotting and \(X^h\) is the allele for haemophilia (a recessive condition in which blood clots very slowly). The Y chromosome carries no allele of the gene, so it is written Y with no superscript.

| genotype | phenotype |
|---|---|
| \(X^H X^H\) | female, no haemophilia |
| \(X^H X^h\) | female, no haemophilia (a carrier) |
| \(X^h X^h\) | female with haemophilia |
| \(X^H Y\) | male, no haemophilia |
| \(X^h Y\) | male with haemophilia |

Three results follow from the fact that a man has only one X:
- A man cannot be a carrier: he either has the allele and shows the condition, or he does not.
- A man inherits his X from his mother and his Y from his father. So he **cannot inherit an X-linked allele from his father**, and a father passes his X-linked allele only to his daughters.
- A woman shows the condition only if she is homozygous recessive, so it is much rarer in women than in men.

The gene for haemophilia is F8, which codes for blood clotting factor VIII; the link between gene, protein and condition is in [Genes, phenotype and gene control](#bio.genecontrol).

:::story Haemophilia in the royal families of Europe
Queen Victoria was a carrier of haemophilia. Her son Leopold had the condition, and several of her daughters were carriers who took the allele into the royal families of Spain, Germany and Russia. In each family, the affected people were boys, which is what the X-linked pattern predicts.
:::

:::eg A woman who is a carrier of haemophilia and a man who does not have haemophilia have children. Draw a genetic diagram, and state the probability that a son has haemophilia and that a daughter has haemophilia.
**Key**: \(X^H\) = allele for normal clotting, \(X^h\) = allele for haemophilia.

Parental genotypes: woman \(X^H X^h\), man \(X^H Y\). [B1]

Gametes: woman (\(X^H\)) and (\(X^h\)); man (\(X^H\)) and (Y). [B1]

| | (\(X^H\)) | (Y) |
|---|---|---|
| **(\(X^H\))** | \(X^H X^H\) | \(X^H Y\) |
| **(\(X^h\))** | \(X^H X^h\) | \(X^h Y\) |

Offspring: \(X^H X^H\) normal girl, \(X^H X^h\) carrier girl, \(X^H Y\) normal boy, \(X^h Y\) boy with haemophilia, in the ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. [B1]

Probability that a son has haemophilia: one of the two sons is \(X^h Y\), so \(\tfrac12\) (50%). [B1]

Probability that a daughter has haemophilia: 0, because every daughter receives \(X^H\) from her father. Half of the daughters are carriers. [B1]

The probability that any one child has haemophilia is 1/4, but this is made up of 0 for girls and 1/2 for boys. Answer the question about sons and daughters separately when it asks for them.
:::

## Test crosses

A dominant phenotype can come from two genotypes (TT or Tt). To find out which one an organism has, do a test cross: cross it with a homozygous recessive (tt).
- If the unknown is **TT**, it makes only (T) gametes, so all the offspring are Tt and show the dominant phenotype.
- If the unknown is **Tt**, half its gametes are (t), so about half the offspring are tt and show the recessive phenotype.

The homozygous recessive is used because it makes only (t) gametes. The offspring then show the alleles in the unknown parent's gametes: each offspring's phenotype reveals which allele it received from the unknown parent.

:::eg In fruit flies, long wings are dominant to vestigial (very short) wings. A long-winged fly of unknown genotype was crossed with a vestigial-winged fly. The offspring were 53 long-winged and 49 vestigial-winged. Deduce the genotype of the long-winged fly and explain your reasoning.
**Key**: L = allele for long wings, l = allele for vestigial wings.

The vestigial-winged fly is homozygous recessive, ll, and makes only (l) gametes. [B1]

The offspring include vestigial-winged flies (ll). Each received an l from the vestigial parent, so each must also have received an l from the long-winged parent. The long-winged fly must make (l) gametes, so it is heterozygous, Ll. [B1]

Check with a diagram: Ll × ll gives gametes (L), (l) and (l); offspring Ll (long) and ll (vestigial) in a 1 : 1 ratio. The observed 53 : 49 is close to 1 : 1. [B1]

If the fly had been LL, every offspring would have been Ll and long-winged. A test cross needs many offspring: if the unknown is Ll, each offspring has a 1/2 chance of being long-winged, so a small litter that happens to be all long-winged could mislead.
:::

:::key Reading a pedigree
1. **Two unaffected parents have an affected child**: the condition is caused by a **recessive** allele, and both parents are carriers (heterozygous).
2. **Two affected parents have an unaffected child**: the condition is caused by a **dominant** allele, and both parents are heterozygous.
3. **An affected female whose father is unaffected**: the condition cannot be X-linked recessive. (A daughter of an X-linked recessive condition gets her father's X chromosome, so he must be affected too.) So it is **autosomal**.
4. **An affected father with an affected son** is not X-linked: a son gets his Y from his father.
5. If many more males than females are affected, and affected males are born to unaffected parents, **X-linked recessive** inheritance is likely.
:::

Notice that a pedigree may show only that a pattern is possible, not that it is certain. Where the options differ, a good answer names the evidence and the rule.

:::eg Use the pedigree above (shaded symbols have the condition). (a) Explain how the pedigree shows that the condition is caused by a recessive allele. (b) Explain how the pedigree shows that the allele is not on the X chromosome. (c) Use H for the dominant allele and h for the recessive allele. State the genotype of II-3 and calculate the probability that III-1 is a carrier.
(a) I-1 and I-2 are unaffected, but their son II-2 is affected. [B1] An unaffected person can have an affected child only if the allele is recessive and masked by a dominant allele in the parents, who are carriers. [B1]

(b) III-2 is an affected daughter, so she is hh. [B1] She must have received an h from her father II-4, who is unaffected. A man with an X-linked recessive allele would be affected, so the allele is not on the X chromosome (it is autosomal). [B1]

(c) III-2 is hh, so she received an h from each parent. II-3 is therefore **Hh**, a carrier (and so is II-4). [B1]

II-3 and II-4 are both Hh, so their children are HH, Hh, Hh or hh. III-1 is unaffected, which rules out hh, leaving three equally likely genotypes: HH, Hh and Hh. [M1]

Probability that III-1 is a carrier \(= \tfrac23\). [A1]

The question is conditional: once you know III-1 is unaffected, the chance is out of the three unaffected possibilities, not out of four.
:::

## Genetic ratios are probabilities

A genetic ratio such as 3 : 1 is a statement of probability. Each child of two Aa parents has a probability of 3/4 of showing the dominant phenotype and 1/4 of showing the recessive phenotype. It does not mean that exactly one in four children will show it.

The chance for each child is **independent**: the earlier children do not change the later ones. A couple whose first child is affected still have the same 1/4 chance for the second. Two rules combine probabilities:
- **and**: the probability that two independent events both happen is the product of their probabilities.
- **or**: the probability that one or the other of two exclusive outcomes happens is the sum of their probabilities.

:::eg Real context, genetic counselling: a man and a woman are both carriers of an autosomal recessive condition, cystic fibrosis. Calculate the probability that (a) a child is affected, (b) a child is an unaffected carrier, (c) their first two children are both affected, (d) at least one of their first three children is affected.
Let C = normal allele and c = cystic fibrosis allele. Both parents are Cc, so their children are CC, Cc, Cc or cc.

(a) P(affected) = P(cc) = \(\tfrac14\). [B1]

(b) P(unaffected carrier) = P(Cc) = \(\tfrac24 = \tfrac12\). [B1]

(c) Each child is independent, so multiply the probabilities:

\[\begin{aligned} P(\text{two affected}) &= \tfrac14 \times \tfrac14 \\ &= \tfrac{1}{16} \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

(d) It is easier to work from the opposite event. The probability that a child is not affected is \(\tfrac34\), so the probability that all three are not affected is \(\left(\tfrac34\right)^3 = \tfrac{27}{64}\). Therefore:

\[\begin{aligned} P(\text{at least one affected}) &= 1 - \tfrac{27}{64} \\ &= \tfrac{37}{64} \\ &\approx 0.58 \end{aligned}\]

[M1] [A1]

So even with a 1 in 4 chance each time, a family of three has a 58% chance that at least one child is affected. A genetic counsellor explains probabilities like these to a couple, but cannot say what will happen in a particular pregnancy.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: carry out a test cross of a heterozygous tall plant (Tt) and show the offspring. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Parental genotypes: Tt × tt.
Line 2: Gametes: (Tt) and (T) from the first parent, (t) and (t) from the second.
Line 3: Offspring: Tt and tt in the ratio 1 tall : 1 dwarf.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: (Tt) is not a gamete. A gamete carries one allele of a gene, so Tt makes the gametes (T) and (t). The student has written the genotype of the parent as a gamete. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct Line 2: "Gametes: (T) and (t) from the first parent; (t) from the second." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State the difference between a gene and an allele.
A: A gene is a length of DNA that codes for a polypeptide. Alleles are the different forms of the same gene, with slightly different base sequences.
Q: A pea plant has the genotype Rr. State whether it is homozygous or heterozygous and list its gametes.
A: Heterozygous. It makes two kinds of gamete, (R) and (r), in equal numbers.
Q: In rabbits, black fur (B) is dominant to brown fur (b). State the phenotypes of BB, Bb and bb rabbits.
A: BB is black, Bb is black and bb is brown.
Q: A black rabbit (Bb) is crossed with a brown rabbit. State the expected ratio of offspring and the probability that a kitten is brown.
A: Gametes (B), (b) and (b). Offspring Bb and bb, so 1 black : 1 brown. The probability that a kitten is brown is \(\tfrac12\).
Q: Explain why a recessive allele has an effect on the phenotype only in a homozygote.
A: In a heterozygote the dominant allele is expressed and codes for a working polypeptide, which masks the recessive allele. Only with two copies of the recessive allele is no dominant allele present to be expressed.
Q: A person has the blood group AB. State the genotype and explain why this shows codominance.
A: \(I^A I^B\). Both alleles are expressed in the phenotype (the person has both antigens), and neither is dominant over the other.
Q: Explain why a father cannot pass an X-linked allele to his son.
A: A son inherits his father's Y chromosome, which carries no allele of that gene. He inherits his X chromosome from his mother.
Q: A colour-blind woman (\(X^b X^b\)) and a man with normal colour vision (\(X^B Y\)) have a son. State the phenotype of the son and explain your answer.
A: He is colour-blind. He gets his only X from his mother, which carries the recessive allele \(X^b\), and a Y from his father, which carries no allele, so \(X^b\) is expressed.
Q: Two carriers of a recessive disorder have a child. Calculate the probability that the child is a carrier but is not affected.
A: The cross Aa × Aa gives AA, Aa, Aa and aa, so the probability of Aa is \(\tfrac24 = \tfrac12\).
Q: State what a test cross is and explain why the second parent is homozygous recessive.
A: A test cross is a cross of an organism with the dominant phenotype and an unknown genotype with a homozygous recessive organism. The homozygous recessive makes only recessive gametes, so the phenotypes of the offspring show which alleles the unknown parent passed on.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [4 marks] ★ Explain the meaning of each pair of terms: (a) genotype and phenotype; (b) homozygous and heterozygous.
A: (a) The genotype is the alleles of the genes that an organism has, [B1] and the phenotype is its observable features. [B1]
(b) A homozygous organism has two identical alleles of a gene, [B1] but a heterozygous organism has two different alleles. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★ In guinea pigs, black fur (B) is dominant to white fur (b). Two black guinea pigs had a white offspring. Draw a genetic diagram to show the genotypes of the parents and the probability that the next offspring is white.
H: The white offspring is bb. Where did its two b alleles come from?
A: Both parents must be Bb, because each passed on a b allele. [B1]
Gametes: (B) and (b) from each parent. [B1]
Offspring genotypes: BB, Bb, Bb and bb. [B1]
Phenotypes: 3 black : 1 white. [B1]
The probability that the next offspring is white is \(\tfrac14\) (25%). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ The MN blood group system has two codominant alleles, \(L^M\) and \(L^N\). A person with \(L^M L^M\) has group M, \(L^N L^N\) has group N and \(L^M L^N\) has group MN. (a) Two people of group MN have a child. State the possible genotypes and groups of the child, with their ratio. (b) State the probability that the child has group MN. (c) Explain why the alleles are described as codominant.
A: (a) Genotypes \(L^M L^M\), \(L^M L^N\) and \(L^N L^N\), in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1. [B1] Groups M, MN and N in the ratio 1 : 2 : 1. [B1]
(b) \(\tfrac24 = \tfrac12\). [B1]
(c) Both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote, and neither masks the other. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A man of blood group AB and a woman of blood group O have children. (a) State the gametes each can make. (b) State the possible genotypes and blood groups of the children. (c) State the probability that a child has blood group A. (d) Explain why none of their children can have blood group O.
A: (a) The man makes (\(I^A\)) and (\(I^B\)); the woman makes only (\(I^o\)). [B1]
(b) \(I^A I^o\) (group A) and \(I^B I^o\) (group B). [B1]
(c) \(\tfrac12\). [B1]
(d) Group O is \(I^o I^o\), but the father has no \(I^o\) allele to pass on. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ A man with red-green colour blindness (\(X^b Y\); \(X^b\) is recessive) has children with a woman who is homozygous for normal colour vision. (a) State the genotypes and phenotypes of their children. (b) One of their daughters has children with a man with normal colour vision. Draw a genetic diagram for this second cross and state the probability that a child is a colour-blind boy. (c) Suggest why colour blindness is more common in men than in women.
H: Work out the genotype of the daughter first: which X does she get from her father?
A: (a) Daughters \(X^B X^b\) (carriers with normal vision) and sons \(X^B Y\) (normal vision). [B1]
(b) The daughter \(X^B X^b\) makes gametes (\(X^B\)) and (\(X^b\)); her partner \(X^B Y\) makes (\(X^B\)) and (Y). [B1] Offspring: \(X^B X^B\), \(X^B X^b\), \(X^B Y\) and \(X^b Y\). [B1] The probability of a colour-blind boy is \(\tfrac14\). [B1]
(c) A man has only one X, so one copy of the recessive allele makes him colour-blind. A woman needs two copies. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ The second pedigree (shown above the practice questions) shows a condition in three generations of a family. (a) Explain why the allele is recessive. (b) Give two features that suggest that the condition is X-linked recessive. (c) Using \(X^H\) and \(X^h\), give the genotype of II-3 and calculate the probability that the next child of II-3 and II-4 is a boy with the condition.
H: For (c), III-1 is affected. Which parent gave him his X chromosome?

A: (a) Both parents of II-1 (I-1 and I-2) are unaffected but II-1 is affected, so the allele is recessive. [B1]
(b) Only males are affected. [B1] Affected males are born to unaffected parents, and the mother is a carrier. [B1]
(c) II-3 is \(X^H X^h\): her son III-1 is affected (\(X^h Y\)) and must have received \(X^h\) from her, since II-4 passed on his Y. [B1] II-3 \(X^H X^h\) × II-4 \(X^H Y\): the probability of a boy with the condition is \(\tfrac14\). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ In tomato plants, red fruit (R) is dominant to yellow fruit (r). A cross of two F1 plants, both heterozygous, gave 146 plants with red fruit and 54 with yellow fruit. (a) Give the genotypes of the F1 plants and state the expected numbers of each phenotype in 200 plants. (b) Calculate the ratio of red to yellow plants observed, to one decimal place, and compare it with the expected ratio. (c) Suggest why the observed numbers are not exactly the expected numbers.
H: Expected numbers come from the 3 : 1 ratio and the total of 200.
A: (a) Rr × Rr. [B1] Expected: \(\tfrac34 \times 200 = 150\) red and \(\tfrac14 \times 200 = 50\) yellow. [B1]
(b) \(146 \div 54 = 2.7\), so the ratio is about 2.7 : 1, close to the expected 3 : 1. [B1]
(c) Fertilisation is random, so the numbers vary by chance around the expected values. [B1] A statistical test (chi-squared, in the next chapter) can show whether the difference is only due to chance. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Huntington's disease is caused by a dominant allele, H. A man with the disease, whose mother did not have it, is heterozygous. He has children with a woman who does not have the disease. (a) Draw a genetic diagram to show the possible genotypes of their children and state the probability that a child inherits the disease. (b) Calculate the probability that their first two children both do not have the disease. (c) The symptoms usually start between the ages of 30 and 50. Suggest why the dominant allele has not disappeared from the population.
H: A child who does not have the disease is hh.
A: (a) Parents Hh × hh. [B1] Offspring Hh and hh in the ratio 1 : 1; the probability that a child has the disease is \(\tfrac12\). [B1] [B1]
(b) \(\tfrac12 \times \tfrac12\) [M1] \(= \tfrac14\) [A1]
(c) The symptoms start after many people have already had children, so the allele is passed on before it affects them. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Manx cats have no tails, because of a dominant allele M. Cats with genotype MM die before birth, so they are never seen. Cats with the genotype mm have normal tails. Two Manx cats are crossed. (a) Draw a genetic diagram and state the ratio of live-born kittens with and without tails. (b) State the probability that a live-born kitten from this cross has no tail. (c) Explain why Manx cats cannot be bred to form a true-breeding line.
H: The MM kittens are not born, so remove them from the ratio.
H: For (c), what is the genotype of every tailless cat that is born?
A: (a) Parents Mm × Mm; gametes (M) and (m) from each; offspring MM, Mm, Mm and mm. [B1] MM die before birth, so the live-born kittens are Mm (no tail) and mm (tail) in the ratio 2 : 1. [B1] [B1]
(b) \(\tfrac23\). [B1]
(c) Every tailless cat that is born is Mm, because MM kittens die. [B1] Crossing two Manx cats always gives some mm kittens (with tails) and some MM kittens that die, so no tailless line can be homozygous and breed true. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ In cats, a gene on the X chromosome has an allele \(X^B\) for black fur and an allele \(X^O\) for orange fur. A female with \(X^B X^O\) has patches of black and orange fur and is called tortoiseshell. (a) State the term for the relationship between the alleles. (b) Explain why almost all tortoiseshell cats are female. (c) A tortoiseshell female is crossed with an orange male. Draw a genetic diagram and state the phenotypes of the kittens, with their ratio. (d) State the probability that a kitten is a black male.
H: A male has only one X chromosome.
A: (a) Codominance: both alleles are expressed in the heterozygote. [B1]
(b) A male has only one X chromosome, so he has only one allele of the gene and cannot be heterozygous. He can be tortoiseshell only if he has an extra X chromosome. [B1]
(c) Parents \(X^B X^O\) × \(X^O Y\); gametes (\(X^B\)), (\(X^O\)) and (\(X^O\)), (Y). [B1] Offspring \(X^B X^O\), \(X^O X^O\), \(X^B Y\) and \(X^O Y\). [B1] Phenotypes: tortoiseshell female, orange female, black male and orange male, in the ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. [B1]
(d) \(\tfrac14\). [B1]

:::quiz
? A heterozygous plant Gg is allowed to make gametes. Which is a possible gamete?
+ g
- Gg
- GG
- gg
> A gamete is haploid and carries one allele of each gene. The genotype Gg is written for the parent, and GG and gg are homozygous genotypes of the zygote.
? A tall pea plant of unknown genotype is crossed with a dwarf plant. About half of the offspring are dwarf. What is the genotype of the tall plant?
+ heterozygous
- homozygous dominant
- homozygous recessive
- it cannot be deduced
> A homozygous dominant parent would give only tall offspring. Dwarf offspring (tt) must have received a t from the tall parent, which is therefore Tt.
? A man with haemophilia (\(X^h Y\)) and a woman with no haemophilia allele (\(X^H X^H\)) have children. What are their children?
+ all sons without haemophilia; all daughters carriers
- all sons with haemophilia; all daughters carriers
- all sons without haemophilia; all daughters with haemophilia
- half the sons with haemophilia; no carrier daughters
> The sons get the father's Y and the mother's \(X^H\), so they are not affected. Every daughter gets her father's \(X^h\) and her mother's \(X^H\), so each is a carrier.
? Which is the correct way to write codominant alleles for flower colour, red (R) and white (W)?
+ \(C^R\) and \(C^W\)
- R and r
- W and w
- \(C^R\) and c
> Codominant alleles are written as a capital letter for the gene with a superscript for each allele. Upper and lower case of one letter show dominance and recessiveness.
? Two parents are both Aa for a recessive disorder. What is the probability that their first two children are both unaffected?
+ \(\tfrac{9}{16}\)
- \(\tfrac{3}{4}\)
- \(\tfrac{1}{16}\)
- \(\tfrac{3}{8}\)
> Each child is unaffected with probability 3/4, and children are independent, so \(\tfrac34 \times \tfrac34 = \tfrac{9}{16}\). Three-quarters is the answer for one child, and 1/16 is the answer for two affected children.
? In a pedigree, two affected parents have an unaffected child. What does this show?
+ the condition is caused by a dominant allele, and both parents are heterozygous
- the condition is caused by a recessive allele
- the condition is X-linked recessive
- one parent must be homozygous dominant
> The unaffected child is homozygous recessive, so each parent carries a recessive allele as well as the dominant one that makes them affected. If the condition were recessive or X-linked recessive, two affected parents could have only affected children.
? Which genotype cannot give blood group A?
+ \(I^B I^o\)
- \(I^A I^o\)
- \(I^A I^A\)
- all three can give group A
> \(I^A\) is needed for group A. \(I^B I^o\) gives group B. Both \(I^A I^A\) and \(I^A I^o\) are group A.
? Two heterozygous plants Aa are crossed. What is the expected ratio of genotypes AA : Aa : aa in the offspring?
+ 1 : 2 : 1
- 3 : 1
- 1 : 1 : 1
- 9 : 3 : 3
> The Punnett square gives one AA, two Aa and one aa. The ratio 3 : 1 is of phenotypes, when A is dominant.

:::recap
- A gene is a length of DNA coding for a polypeptide; an allele is one form of it; a locus is its position on a chromosome.
- Genotype is the alleles; phenotype is the observable feature. Homozygous: two identical alleles. Heterozygous: two different alleles.
- A dominant allele is expressed in a heterozygote; a recessive allele is expressed only in a homozygote; codominant alleles are both expressed.
- A genetic diagram: key, parental phenotypes, parental genotypes, gametes (one allele each, circled), Punnett square, offspring genotypes, phenotypes, ratio with names.
- Aa × Aa gives 3 dominant : 1 recessive (genotypes 1 : 2 : 1); Aa × aa gives 1 : 1; codominance gives 1 : 2 : 1 phenotypes.
- ABO: alleles \(I^A\), \(I^B\) (codominant) and \(I^o\) (recessive); group A, B, AB or O.
- Sex linkage: genes on the X chromosome; a male has one allele so shows a recessive allele; fathers pass X-linked alleles only to daughters; males cannot be carriers.
- A test cross with a homozygous recessive shows whether the unknown is homozygous (all dominant offspring) or heterozygous (about 1 : 1).
- Pedigrees: unaffected parents with an affected child means recessive; affected parents with an unaffected child means dominant.
- Ratios are probabilities for each child: multiply for "and", add for "or".
:::
