Q: What is the difference between a channel protein and a carrier protein? (from [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement))
A: A channel protein has a water-filled pore through which ions diffuse down their concentration gradient, with no energy needed. A carrier protein binds the substance, changes shape and moves it across; in active transport it uses energy from ATP.

## The nervous system and the reflex arc

Your body must detect changes, decide what to do and act. The nervous system does this with cells called **neurones** (nerve cells) that carry signals as electrical impulses. A bundle of neurones is a nerve. The brain and spinal cord form the central nervous system (CNS); the nerves that run between the CNS and the rest of the body are the peripheral nervous system.

The simplest complete pathway is a **reflex arc**: a fast, automatic response that does not need thought, such as pulling your hand away from a hot pan. Every pathway in the nervous system has the same parts in the same order.

:::steps
1. A **stimulus** (a change in the environment, such as heat) is detected by a **sensory receptor**.
2. The receptor starts impulses in a **sensory neurone**, which carries them towards the CNS.
3. In the CNS an **intermediate neurone** (also called a **relay neurone**) connects the sensory neurone to a motor neurone.
4. The **motor neurone** carries impulses away from the CNS.
5. An **effector** (a muscle that contracts, or a gland that secretes) carries out the response.
:::

:::def Sensory neurone
A neurone that carries impulses from a sensory receptor towards the central nervous system.
:::

:::def Intermediate neurone
A neurone, found within the central nervous system, that connects sensory neurones to motor neurones. Also called a relay neurone.
:::

:::def Motor neurone
A neurone that carries impulses from the central nervous system to an effector (a muscle or a gland).
:::

:::def Effector
A part of the body, a muscle or a gland, that carries out a response when it receives impulses from a motor neurone.
:::

In a reflex there may be only one intermediate neurone, so the delay is very short. In a response you think about, the intermediate neurones pass the signal on to the brain as well.

## The structure of neurones

All neurones have a **cell body** containing the nucleus, with the usual organelles (a lot of rough endoplasmic reticulum and mitochondria, because the cell makes neurotransmitter proteins and uses much ATP). Thin extensions carry impulses to and from the cell body. The fibre that carries impulses *away* from the cell body is the **axon**; fibres that carry impulses *towards* it are **dendrites**. A very long dendrite, as in a sensory neurone, is called a **dendron**.

Many axons are wrapped in a **myelin sheath**. It is made by **Schwann cells**, which wind round the axon many times so that it is covered by layers of their cell surface membranes. Membranes are rich in lipid, so the sheath is a fatty insulating layer. It is not continuous: between neighbouring Schwann cells are small gaps of bare axon, the **nodes of Ranvier**. You will see in a later section that the nodes are where the impulse is regenerated.

| | Sensory neurone | Motor neurone |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | receptor → CNS | CNS → effector |
| Cell body | off to one side, in the middle of the neurone | at one end, inside the CNS |
| Dendrites | one long dendron, branched endings at the receptor | many short dendrites round the cell body |
| Axon | short | very long (up to about a metre) |
| Ends at | a synapse in the CNS | a synapse with a muscle fibre or gland cell |

An intermediate neurone is short, lies entirely inside the CNS, and has many branched dendrites and axon endings that make synapses with other neurones.

Label myelin sheath, node of Ranvier, axon terminals (or synaptic knobs) and dendrites where they are shown.
:::

## Sensory receptors: turning stimuli into impulses

A sensory receptor cell is specific to one kind of stimulus: light, pressure, temperature, a chemical. Each is a **transducer**: it changes the energy of the stimulus into electrical energy, a change in the potential difference across its membrane.

:::def Sensory receptor
A cell or the ending of a neurone that detects a stimulus and starts the transmission of impulses in a sensory neurone. A receptor acts as a transducer, converting the energy of a stimulus into electrical energy.
:::

:::def Receptor potential
A change in the potential difference across the membrane of a receptor cell, caused by a stimulus. Its size increases with the strength of the stimulus: it is graded, not all-or-nothing.
:::

### A chemoreceptor: salt in a taste bud

A taste bud contains chemoreceptor cells with tiny hairs that poke into the saliva. A chemoreceptor responds to the chemical that binds to it or enters it. Salty taste is a good example, because the stimulus is sodium ions.

:::steps
1. Sodium ions in the food dissolve in the saliva. The concentration outside the receptor cell is higher than inside.
2. Sodium ions **diffuse through channel proteins** into the receptor cell, down their concentration gradient.
3. The inside of the cell becomes less negative: the membrane is **depolarised**. This change is the **receptor potential**.
4. The bigger the sodium concentration in the saliva, the more ions enter and the larger the receptor potential.
5. If the receptor potential reaches the **threshold**, the receptor cell releases **neurotransmitter** by exocytosis across a synapse.
6. Neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the sensory neurone and depolarises it. If its threshold is reached, an **action potential** starts in the sensory neurone and travels to the brain.
:::

:::eg Describe how a sodium chloride solution on the tongue leads to an action potential in a sensory neurone.
A full-mark answer follows the chain from stimulus to impulse.

Sodium ions diffuse into the chemoreceptor cell through channel proteins, down their concentration gradient. [B1]

This depolarises the membrane: the inside becomes less negative, giving a receptor potential. [B1]

If the receptor potential reaches the threshold, the receptor cell releases neurotransmitter. [B1]

The neurotransmitter binds to receptors on the sensory neurone and causes depolarisation of its membrane. [B1]

If this reaches the threshold, an action potential is produced in the sensory neurone. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the receptor detects salt and sends a message to the brain". It names no ion movement, no depolarisation, no threshold and no neurotransmitter, so it scores at most one mark.
:::

:::warn A receptor potential is not an action potential
A receptor potential is **graded** and local: a bigger stimulus gives a bigger change, and it fades with distance. An action potential is **all-or-nothing** and travels without fading. Receptors turn a graded change into impulses only when the threshold is reached.
:::

## The resting potential

A neurone that is not carrying an impulse has a steady voltage across its membrane. Because the inside is negative compared with the outside, the membrane is **polarised**.

:::def Resting potential
The potential difference across the membrane of a neurone (or other cell) that is not being stimulated, about −70 mV. The inside of the cell is negative relative to the outside.
:::

(Potential difference is a voltage; a millivolt, mV, is a thousandth of a volt. The minus sign says the inside is the more negative side.) Three things set up this voltage.

1. **The sodium–potassium pump.** Using ATP, it moves **3 Na⁺ out** and **2 K⁺ in** each cycle. So there is a high concentration of Na⁺ outside and of K⁺ inside, and a net loss of one positive charge from the inside each cycle.
2. **Potassium leak channels.** At rest the membrane has many open K⁺ channels and very few open Na⁺ channels, so it is **far more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺**. K⁺ diffuse out through these channels, down their concentration gradient, carrying positive charge with them.
3. **Large negatively charged molecules** (proteins and phosphate-containing ions) stay inside the cell because they cannot cross the membrane.

The outward leak of K⁺ is what builds the voltage. As positive charge leaves, the inside becomes more negative and attracts K⁺ back in. At about −70 mV the outward push from the concentration gradient is balanced by the inward pull of the negative charge, and the voltage stays steady. The pump is working all the time to restore the gradients that leaking and, in the next section, impulses use up.

:::warn Potassium ions are not "pumped out"
The pump moves K⁺ **in**. The loss of K⁺ from the cell is by **diffusion through channels**. Similarly, the resting potential does not need a flow of electrons: the signal in a neurone is a movement of **ions** across the membrane.
:::

## The action potential

When a neurone is stimulated enough, the voltage across its membrane changes for about a millisecond in a set pattern, the **action potential**. The change happens because **voltage-gated channels** open and close. These are channel proteins whose shape changes when the voltage across the membrane changes. A voltage-gated channel is closed at rest and opens when the membrane becomes less negative; there are two kinds, one for Na⁺ and one for K⁺.

:::def Action potential
A rapid, temporary reversal of the potential difference across the membrane of a neurone, from negative inside to positive inside and back, caused by the opening and closing of voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels. It is an all-or-nothing event that travels along the neurone.
:::

:::def Threshold potential
The membrane potential, about −50 mV, that must be reached for enough voltage-gated sodium channels to open to start an action potential.
:::

Follow the numbers on the graph. The sequence is the same every time.

:::steps
1. **Resting potential** (about −70 mV). Voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels are closed.
2. **Stimulus.** A stimulus makes the membrane a little less negative. A few voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ diffuse in, which makes the inside less negative still, which opens more Na⁺ channels. If the potential reaches the **threshold** (about −50 mV) this positive feedback runs away.
3. **Depolarisation.** Many Na⁺ channels are open. Na⁺ rush in down their electrochemical gradient (the combined concentration gradient and the attraction to the negative inside). The inside becomes positive, reaching about +35 mV.
4. **Repolarisation.** At about +35 mV the Na⁺ channels close, and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open. K⁺ diffuse out through them, down their concentration gradient. The inside becomes negative again.
5. **Hyperpolarisation.** The K⁺ channels are slow to close, so too many K⁺ leave. The potential falls below the resting potential, to about −80 mV.
6. **Return to rest.** The K⁺ channels close. The sodium–potassium pump and the leak channels restore the resting potential (−70 mV) and the ion concentrations.
:::

The key words matter in the exam: **depolarisation** means the inside becomes less negative (the membrane potential moves towards 0 and above); **repolarisation** means it becomes negative again.

:::key The action potential in one table
| Phase | Channels | Ion movement | Membrane potential |
|---|---|---|---|
| Resting | most voltage-gated channels closed; K⁺ leak channels open | K⁺ leak out; pump keeps gradients | about −70 mV |
| Depolarisation | voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open | Na⁺ diffuse in | rises past −50 mV (threshold) to about +35 mV |
| Repolarisation | Na⁺ channels close; voltage-gated K⁺ channels open | K⁺ diffuse out | falls back to about −70 mV |
| Hyperpolarisation | K⁺ channels slow to close | too many K⁺ leave | below −70 mV, to about −80 mV |
| Recovery | K⁺ channels closed | pump and leak channels restore gradients | back to −70 mV |
:::

:::analogy A row of dominoes
A domino falls only if it is pushed hard enough (the threshold). Once it falls it falls fully, in the same way, however hard it was pushed (all-or-nothing). It cannot fall again until it has been stood up (the refractory period), and it can only knock over the domino in front, not the one that has already fallen. The energy is stored in each domino, as the ion gradients are stored across the membrane, not supplied by the push.
:::

### All-or-nothing and the threshold

If a stimulus depolarises the membrane but does not reach the threshold, too few Na⁺ channels open. The Na⁺ that enter are balanced by K⁺ leaving, and the potential drifts back to rest. There is no action potential. If the threshold is reached, the action potential happens and always has the **same size** (about +35 mV at its peak) however strong the stimulus. A stronger stimulus does not make a bigger action potential.

### The refractory period

:::def Refractory period
The short time after an action potential during which the membrane cannot be stimulated to produce another one, because the voltage-gated sodium channels are closed and cannot open again until the membrane has returned to near its resting potential.
:::

The refractory period matters for three reasons.

1. **One-way transmission.** The part of the axon the impulse has just passed is in its refractory period, so the impulse can only spread forwards, to the region that has not yet been depolarised.
2. **Separate impulses.** Each action potential is a distinct event. They cannot merge or overlap.
3. **A maximum frequency.** A new action potential cannot start until the previous refractory period is over, so the number of impulses per second is limited.

\[\text{maximum frequency} = \frac{1}{\text{refractory period}}\]

:::eg A neurone has a refractory period of 2 ms. Calculate the maximum number of impulses it can carry per second.
Convert to seconds first: \(2\,\mathrm{ms} = 0.002\,\mathrm{s}\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{maximum frequency} &= \frac{1}{0.002\,\mathrm{s}} \\ &= 500\,\mathrm{s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

Take the reciprocal of the refractory period in seconds. [M1]

The neurone cannot fire more than 500 times a second, however strong the stimulus. [A1]
:::

### How strength is coded

Since every action potential is the same size, the brain finds the strength of a stimulus in two ways. First, a stronger stimulus makes the receptor produce a bigger receptor potential, so the sensory neurone reaches threshold more often per second: **more impulses per second**. Second, a strong stimulus stimulates **more receptors**, which use more sensory neurones, and some neurones have higher thresholds so they respond only to strong stimuli. The *type* of stimulus is known from *which* neurone the impulse arrives along.

## How an impulse travels along an axon

An action potential is not a single event at one place: it moves. It is a wave of depolarisation along the axon.

When part of the axon is depolarised by an action potential, Na⁺ have entered there. These ions diffuse sideways inside the axon, towards the neighbouring region, which is still at rest and more negative. Outside the axon, positive charge is drawn towards the depolarised region. These movements of ions are **local circuits**. They make the neighbouring region less negative. If its potential reaches the threshold, its voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and it has an action potential too. This repeats along the axon.

The region behind the impulse is in its refractory period, so the impulse cannot go backwards. The action potential is regenerated at each place, so it does **not** get smaller as it travels.

### Unmyelinated and myelinated axons

In an **unmyelinated** axon the whole length of the membrane has voltage-gated channels. Every small stretch must be depolarised in turn, so the impulse is slow. In a **myelinated** axon the myelin sheath is a good insulator: ions cannot cross it. Voltage-gated channels are concentrated at the **nodes of Ranvier**, so depolarisation can happen only at the nodes. Local circuits carry the depolarisation from one node to the next, and the action potential appears to jump along the axon.

:::def Saltatory conduction
The jumping of an action potential from one node of Ranvier to the next in a myelinated neurone, because the myelin sheath insulates the axon and depolarisation occurs only at the nodes. (Latin saltare means to jump.)
:::

This is much faster because the impulse is regenerated only at the nodes, so there are far fewer depolarisations along the axon, and the local circuits between nodes spread very quickly. Two other factors raise the speed: a **larger axon diameter** (less resistance to the flow of ions inside the axon) and a **higher temperature** (ions diffuse faster, and the channels open and close faster).

### Calculating the speed of conduction

\[\text{speed} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}\]

:::eg Two stimulating and recording experiments were done. In a myelinated motor neurone an impulse took 1.5 ms to travel 0.12 m between two recording electrodes. In an unmyelinated neurone an impulse took 12 ms to travel 0.012 m. Calculate the speed of conduction in each and compare them.
Use speed = distance ÷ time, with distance in metres and time in seconds.

Myelinated: \(1.5\,\mathrm{ms} = 0.0015\,\mathrm{s}\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{speed} &= \frac{0.12\,\mathrm{m}}{0.0015\,\mathrm{s}} \\ &= 80\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

Unmyelinated: \(12\,\mathrm{ms} = 0.012\,\mathrm{s}\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{speed} &= \frac{0.012\,\mathrm{m}}{0.012\,\mathrm{s}} \\ &= 1.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

The myelinated neurone conducts 80 times faster: \(80 \div 1.0 = 80\).
:::

:::eg Explain why an impulse travels faster along a myelinated neurone than along an unmyelinated neurone of the same diameter. [4]
The myelin sheath is made of Schwann cell membranes, rich in lipid, and it is an electrical insulator. [B1]

Ions cannot cross the sheath, so depolarisation (the entry of sodium ions) can occur only at the nodes of Ranvier. [B1]

Local circuits carry the depolarisation from one node to the next, so the action potential jumps between nodes (saltatory conduction). [B1]

So action potentials are generated at far fewer places along the axon, and the impulse travels much more quickly than in an unmyelinated axon, where the whole membrane must be depolarised in turn. [B1]

A weaker answer says "myelin conducts the electricity so the impulse is faster". Myelin is an insulator, and it does not conduct: this answer scores nothing for the key idea.
:::

:::eg Real context: multiple sclerosis (MS) is a disease in which the body's own immune system damages the myelin sheath of neurones in the brain and spinal cord. Explain why people with MS may have slow or poor control of their muscles.
Where myelin is lost, the axon membrane is exposed along its length. [B1]

Local circuits can leak from the exposed axon and may not reach the next node with a depolarisation big enough to reach the threshold, so impulses are slowed or fail to arrive. [B1]

Impulses along motor neurones to the muscles are delayed, weaker or missing, so muscle contraction is slow, weak or uncoordinated. [B1]

(Also credit: the signs of MS depend on which neurones lose myelin, so symptoms differ between people.)
:::

:::warn Myelin does not conduct
"The impulse travels along the myelin" and "the electrical signal jumps across the insulation" are both wrong. The myelin **insulates**. The action potential is regenerated at the **nodes**, where the axon membrane is exposed. The speed of an impulse depends on the neurone, not on the strength of the stimulus.
:::

Q: Name the structure that makes the myelin sheath, and name the gaps in it.
A: Schwann cells make the myelin sheath. The gaps are the nodes of Ranvier.

A: Any two of: the cell body of a motor neurone is at one end (a sensory neurone's is off to the side); a motor neurone has many dendrites round the cell body (a sensory neurone has one long dendron); a motor neurone has a very long axon (a sensory neurone has a short axon).

Q: A neurone has a refractory period of 4 ms. Calculate the maximum number of impulses it could carry in one second.
A: \(4\,\mathrm{ms} = 0.004\,\mathrm{s}\), so the maximum is \(1 \div 0.004 = 250\) impulses per second.
Q: State what is meant by hyperpolarisation and explain why it happens at the end of an action potential.
A: The potential falls below the resting potential (to about −80 mV). The voltage-gated potassium channels are slow to close, so more potassium ions than needed diffuse out.

A: The region of axon just behind the impulse is in its refractory period: its voltage-gated sodium channels are closed and cannot open, so it cannot be depolarised again.

Q: Explain how a bright light is distinguished from a dim light, given that all action potentials have the same size.

H: Compare the peaks of the three action potentials. Do they differ?
H: What does change with stimulus strength?
A: The conclusion is not supported: the peak height was +34, +35 and +34 mV, so the size of the impulse does not depend on stimulus strength. [B1]
This is the all-or-nothing principle: once the threshold is reached, the same voltage-gated channels open and the action potential is the same size. [B1]
What changed was the frequency: 4, 9 and 22 impulses in 100 ms (40, 90 and 220 impulses per second). [B1]
A stronger stimulus makes a larger receptor potential, so the threshold is reached again sooner after each refractory period. [B1]
The brain uses the frequency of impulses to judge strength. [B1]
It also uses the number of neurones carrying impulses (a stronger stimulus excites more receptors). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A local anaesthetic blocks the voltage-gated sodium channels of neurones. It is injected near a tooth before drilling. (a) Explain why the sensory neurones from the tooth cannot carry impulses to the brain. (b) The anaesthetic does not stop the resting potential. Explain why. (c) Suggest why a dose that blocks only half of the sodium channels may not stop the pain completely.
H: What is needed to reach the threshold?
A: (a) With the sodium channels blocked, sodium ions cannot diffuse in, so there is no depolarisation, and no action potential is started or passed on. [B1]
(b) The resting potential depends on the pump and on the open potassium leak channels, which are not blocked. [B1]
So the potassium ions still diffuse out and the inside stays negative. [B1]
(c) The sodium ions entering through the channels that are not blocked may still depolarise the membrane to the threshold. [B1]
The remaining channels open in positive feedback, and an action potential would be produced, so impulses could still pass. [B1]
:::

:::quiz
? What happens to the potential across an axon membrane during repolarisation?
+ Voltage-gated potassium channels open and potassium ions diffuse out.
- The sodium–potassium pump moves sodium ions out.
- Voltage-gated sodium channels open and sodium ions diffuse in.
- Potassium ions are pumped out by active transport.
> Repolarisation is caused by K⁺ diffusing out through voltage-gated K⁺ channels. The pump is too slow to produce it and moves K⁺ in, not out. Na⁺ entering causes depolarisation.
? What is the main reason that the resting potential is negative inside?
+ The membrane is much more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺, and K⁺ leak out.
- The sodium–potassium pump moves more positive ions into the cell than out.
- Electrons flow along the inside of the axon.
- Sodium ions are trapped inside by large proteins.
> K⁺ diffuse out through leak channels, taking positive charge with them. The pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, so it moves more positive charge out, which helps slightly, but the main cause is the K⁺ leak. Nothing in a neurone is carried by electrons.
? An axon is given a stimulus that raises the potential to −60 mV, below the threshold. What happens?
+ The potential returns to −70 mV and no action potential occurs.
- A smaller action potential occurs.
- An action potential occurs after a delay.
- The sodium channels close and stay closed.
> The action potential is all-or-nothing. If the threshold is not reached there is no action potential at all, not a small one.
? What causes the refractory period?
+ Voltage-gated sodium channels are closed and cannot open again straight away.
- The nodes of Ranvier run out of sodium ions.
- The sodium–potassium pump has stopped.
- The myelin sheath is repaired.
> After depolarisation the Na⁺ channels close and cannot open until the membrane has returned to near its resting potential. The ions are not used up in a single action potential.
? A neurone has a refractory period of 5 ms. What is the maximum frequency of impulses?
+ 200 per second
- 5 per second
- 20 per second
- 500 per second
> Maximum frequency \(= 1 \div 0.005\,\mathrm{s} = 200\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\). 5 ignores the conversion from ms, 20 muddles the digits, and 500 is the answer for a refractory period of 2 ms.
? Why does an impulse travel faster along a myelinated neurone?
+ Depolarisation occurs only at the nodes of Ranvier, so the impulse jumps from node to node.
- The myelin sheath conducts the impulse.
- Myelinated neurones have more sodium channels along the whole axon.
- The sheath makes the axon wider.
> The sheath is an insulator and ions cannot cross it. Voltage-gated channels are at the nodes, which is why the impulse jumps.
? A stronger stimulus is detected by a sensory neurone. Which change occurs?
+ The frequency of action potentials increases.
- The size of each action potential increases.
- The speed of conduction increases.
- The resting potential becomes more negative.
> The size of the action potentials and the speed of conduction depend on the neurone, not on the stimulus. Strength is coded by frequency (and by the number of neurones active).
? A sensory receptor is described as a transducer. What does this mean?
+ It changes the energy of a stimulus into an electrical change in the membrane potential.
- It amplifies the stimulus so that an action potential is always produced.
- It carries impulses from the CNS to an effector.
- It changes an action potential into a chemical signal.
> A receptor converts the energy of one kind of stimulus (light, pressure, a chemical) into a change in membrane potential, the receptor potential. Whether an action potential follows depends on the threshold being reached.
? Which statement about a motor neurone is correct?
+ Its cell body is at one end and it has a long axon to an effector.
- Its cell body is in the middle of a long dendron.
- It carries impulses from the receptors to the CNS.
- It lies entirely within the CNS.
> A motor neurone carries impulses from the CNS to an effector, with its cell body at one end. The description of a cell body on a side branch fits a sensory neurone.

:::recap
- A reflex arc: stimulus → receptor → sensory neurone → intermediate neurone → motor neurone → effector.
- Sensory neurone: one long dendron, cell body off to one side. Motor neurone: cell body at one end, long axon. Myelin from Schwann cells, with nodes of Ranvier.
- A receptor cell is a transducer. In a taste bud, Na⁺ diffuse in, the membrane depolarises (receptor potential), and if the threshold is reached neurotransmitter is released and an action potential starts in the sensory neurone.
- Resting potential about −70 mV: pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in; K⁺ leak out through channels; the membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺.
- Action potential: threshold (about −50 mV), voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ in, up to about +35 mV; Na⁺ channels close, K⁺ channels open, K⁺ out; hyperpolarisation; recovery. All-or-nothing.
- The refractory period gives one-way transmission, separate impulses and a maximum frequency (1 ÷ refractory period).
- Strength is coded by frequency and number of neurones.
- Impulses spread by local circuits. Saltatory conduction: myelin insulates, depolarisation only at nodes, so faster. Speed = distance ÷ time.
:::
