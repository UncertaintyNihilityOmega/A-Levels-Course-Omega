## The big picture

Glucose holds a lot of energy, but the cell cannot use it directly. **Aerobic respiration** takes glucose to carbon dioxide and water in four stages, passing small amounts of energy to ATP along the way:

\[\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}\]

:::def Aerobic respiration
The complete oxidation of an organic substrate, such as glucose, to carbon dioxide and water, using oxygen, with the release of energy that is used to make ATP.
:::

## Words you need

Respiration is a chain of oxidation and reduction reactions, and a few words make every later sentence easier.

:::def Coenzyme
A small organic molecule that is not a protein and helps an enzyme by carrying atoms or groups from one reaction to another. NAD, FAD and coenzyme A are coenzymes.
:::

:::def NAD and FAD
Coenzymes that carry hydrogen. When they pick up hydrogen they become **reduced NAD** and **reduced FAD**, which carry the hydrogen to the electron transport chain, where it is released and the coenzymes are used again.
:::

Never write NADH or NADPH in this course: write **reduced NAD**.

- **Oxidation** is the loss of electrons or of hydrogen (or the gain of oxygen). **Reduction** is the gain of electrons or of hydrogen. They always happen together: when one molecule loses hydrogen, another gains it.
- **Dehydrogenation** is the removal of hydrogen from a molecule. It is an oxidation of that molecule, and the hydrogen is picked up by NAD or FAD, which are reduced.
- **Decarboxylation** is the removal of a carbon atom from a molecule as carbon dioxide.
- **Phosphorylation** is the addition of a phosphate group to a molecule, such as ADP to make ATP, or glucose to make glucose phosphate.

## Stage 1: glycolysis

Glycolysis means "splitting sugar". It happens in the cytoplasm, it does not need oxygen, and it is the only stage that anaerobic respiration uses too. It has three phases.

1. **Phosphorylation.** Two ATP molecules each give a phosphate group to glucose, which becomes fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (6C). The cell has spent 2 ATP, but the phosphorylated sugar is more reactive and can no longer leave the cell.
2. **Splitting.** The fructose 1,6-bisphosphate splits into **two** molecules of triose phosphate (3C).
3. **Oxidation.** Each triose phosphate is oxidised (dehydrogenated) to pyruvate (3C). The hydrogen reduces NAD to reduced NAD. Two ATP are made from each triose phosphate by substrate-linked phosphorylation, so 4 ATP are made in all.

:::key Glycolysis, per molecule of glucose
- Location: **cytoplasm**. No oxygen needed.
- 2 ATP used, 4 ATP made: a **net gain of 2 ATP** (by substrate-linked phosphorylation).
- **2 reduced NAD** are formed.
- Products: **2 pyruvate** (3C each).
:::

:::warn Glycolysis is not in the mitochondria
Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm. Only the pyruvate enters the mitochondrion, and only if oxygen is available.
:::

## Stage 2: the link reaction

When oxygen is available, pyruvate is transported into the mitochondrion, into the matrix. Here the **link reaction** joins glycolysis to the Krebs cycle.

:::def Link reaction
The reaction that converts pyruvate (3C) to acetyl coenzyme A (2C): pyruvate is decarboxylated and dehydrogenated, and the remaining acetyl group (2C) is carried by coenzyme A to the Krebs cycle.
:::

In the matrix, for each pyruvate:
1. A carbon atom is removed as **carbon dioxide** (decarboxylation).
2. Hydrogen is removed and given to **NAD**, which becomes reduced NAD (dehydrogenation).
3. The two-carbon **acetyl** group that is left joins **coenzyme A** to make **acetyl coenzyme A** (acetyl CoA).

No ATP is made in this stage. Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group to the Krebs cycle and is then free to pick up another one. Because each glucose gave two pyruvate, the link reaction happens **twice** per glucose: 2 CO₂, 2 reduced NAD and 2 acetyl CoA.

## Stage 3: the Krebs cycle

The Krebs cycle is a loop of enzyme-catalysed reactions in the matrix. It starts and ends with the same molecule, **oxaloacetate**, which is why it is called a cycle.

1. The acetyl group (2C) from acetyl CoA joins **oxaloacetate** (4C) to form **citrate** (6C). Coenzyme A is released and used again.
2. In a series of small steps, citrate (6C) is **decarboxylated** twice, losing two carbon atoms as carbon dioxide, so the carbon number falls from 6 to 5 and then to 4.
3. At the same time, there are **dehydrogenations**: hydrogen is removed and reduces NAD and FAD.
4. One ATP is made directly by substrate-linked phosphorylation.
5. The remaining four-carbon compound is converted, in more small steps, back to oxaloacetate, ready to accept the next acetyl group.

:::key One turn of the Krebs cycle
- **2 CO₂** are released (two decarboxylations).
- **3 reduced NAD** and **1 reduced FAD** are formed (dehydrogenations).
- **1 ATP** is made by substrate-linked phosphorylation.
- Oxaloacetate (4C) is regenerated. There are **two turns** per glucose, because each glucose gives two acetyl CoA.
:::

:::warn Where does the carbon dioxide come from?
Carbon dioxide comes from **decarboxylation** of the substrate in the link reaction and the Krebs cycle. It does not come from oxygen "combining with carbon". Oxygen is used only at the very end, in stage 4, where it forms water.
:::

### Counting up the products

Add up the stages for **one molecule of glucose**, remembering that the link reaction and the Krebs cycle each run twice.

| Stage | CO₂ | reduced NAD | reduced FAD | ATP made directly (net) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Glycolysis | 0 | 2 | 0 | 2 |
| Link reaction (×2) | 2 | 2 | 0 | 0 |
| Krebs cycle (×2) | 4 | 6 | 2 | 2 |
| **Total** | **6** | **10** | **2** | **4** |

The six carbon dioxide molecules account for all six carbon atoms of glucose, so the glucose has been completely oxidised. Most of the energy is still in the 10 reduced NAD and 2 reduced FAD, which pass it to stage 4. Only 4 ATP come from substrate-linked phosphorylation.

:::note The ATP total is not required
The syllabus does not ask for the total ATP made per glucose, because the value depends on how reduced coenzymes are shuttled and on how many protons ATP synthase needs. Textbooks give about 30. Learn the stage-by-stage table, not the total.
:::

:::eg For one molecule of glucose, state the number of carbon dioxide, reduced NAD, reduced FAD and ATP molecules made directly (net) by glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle together.
Work stage by stage, doubling the link reaction and the Krebs cycle because one glucose gives two pyruvate.

Carbon dioxide: the link reaction gives 1 per pyruvate, so 2, and the Krebs cycle gives 2 per turn, so 4. Total 6. [B1]

Reduced NAD: glycolysis 2, link reaction \(2 \times 1 = 2\), Krebs cycle \(2 \times 3 = 6\). Total 10. [B1]

Reduced FAD: only the Krebs cycle, \(2 \times 1 = 2\). [B1]

ATP made directly: glycolysis nets \(4 - 2 = 2\), the link reaction none, the Krebs cycle \(2 \times 1 = 2\). Total 4. [B1]

A common error is to give the figures per turn or per pyruvate, such as "3 reduced NAD", or to forget that glycolysis uses 2 ATP so the net gain is 2, not 4.
:::

## Stage 4: oxidative phosphorylation

Most of the ATP is made here, on the **inner membrane** of the mitochondrion. It is called **oxidative** because it needs oxygen, and **phosphorylation** because ADP is phosphorylated to ATP.

:::def Oxidative phosphorylation
The synthesis of ATP using energy released as electrons pass along the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane, with oxygen as the final electron acceptor.
:::

The steps, in order:

1. **Reduced NAD and reduced FAD** deliver hydrogen to carriers in the inner membrane. They become NAD and FAD again and go back to take part in earlier stages.
2. Each **hydrogen atom splits** into a proton (\(\ce{H+}\)) and an energetic **electron**.
3. The electrons pass along the **electron transport chain**, a series of carrier molecules in the membrane. At each step they lose a little energy. (You do not need the names of the carriers.)
4. The energy released is used to **pump protons** across the inner membrane from the **matrix into the intermembrane space**. The protons build up in the intermembrane space, a **proton gradient**.
5. The protons **flow back into the matrix** down their gradient through the channel in the enzyme **ATP synthase**, by **facilitated diffusion**. The energy released makes ATP from ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\). This is **chemiosmosis**.
6. At the end of the chain, **oxygen** is the **final electron acceptor**: it takes the electrons, joins protons from the matrix and forms **water**.

\[\mathrm{\tfrac12 O_2 + 2H^+ + 2e^- \rightarrow H_2O}\]

:::analogy A hydroelectric dam
The electron transport chain is the pump that carries water uphill into a reservoir (protons into the intermembrane space). The reservoir stores energy as a difference in height (the proton gradient). ATP synthase is the turbine: water flowing back down through it turns the turbine and makes electricity (ATP). Without a pump, the reservoir empties. Without a turbine, the water runs away and no electricity is made.
:::

:::warn Three slips to avoid
1. Protons are pumped **out of the matrix into the intermembrane space**, not into the matrix.
2. ATP synthase does not pump protons. It lets them **flow back down** the gradient, and the flow makes ATP.
3. The ATP is not made by the electron transport chain directly: the chain makes the gradient, and the gradient makes the ATP.
:::

The same idea makes ATP in chloroplasts, where the thylakoid membranes play the part of the inner membrane (see [Photosynthesis](#bio.photosynthesis)).

### Why oxygen is essential

Oxygen is not used in glycolysis, the link reaction or the Krebs cycle, yet they all stop without it. The reason is the end of the chain. If there is no oxygen, the electrons have nowhere to go and the carriers fill up with electrons, so the chain stops. Then reduced NAD and reduced FAD cannot give up their hydrogen, and no NAD or FAD is available to accept hydrogen in earlier stages. The link reaction and the Krebs cycle stop, and no proton gradient is made.

:::eg Explain the role of oxygen in aerobic respiration.
A full-mark answer follows the electrons.

Oxygen is the final electron acceptor at the end of the electron transport chain. [B1]

It combines with electrons and protons (from the hydrogen) to form water. [B1]

This keeps the chain moving, so reduced NAD and reduced FAD can give up their hydrogen and be converted back to NAD and FAD. [B1]

So NAD and FAD are available for glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle to continue, and a proton gradient is maintained for ATP synthesis. [B1]

A weaker answer says "oxygen is needed to release energy from glucose" or "oxygen combines with carbon to make carbon dioxide". The first gives no mechanism and the second is wrong, so neither earns a mark.
:::

## The mitochondrion: structure and function

You can now see why a mitochondrion is built the way it is. Each part has a job in the four stages.

| Structure | Function |
|---|---|
| **Outer membrane** | smooth and permeable to small molecules; separates the mitochondrion from the cytoplasm |
| **Intermembrane space** | a narrow gap with a small volume, so a small number of pumped protons raises the proton concentration quickly and builds a steep gradient |
| **Inner membrane** | folded into **cristae**; holds the electron transport chain and ATP synthase; only some molecules cross it, so the proton gradient can be kept |
| **Cristae** | the folds give the inner membrane a large surface area, with room for many electron transport chains and ATP synthase molecules |
| **Matrix** | the fluid containing the enzymes of the link reaction and the Krebs cycle, with coenzymes and phosphates |
| **Circular DNA and 70S ribosomes** (in the matrix) | let the mitochondrion make some of its own proteins, including some of those of the electron transport chain |

In an electron micrograph, a mitochondrion is an oval or rod-shaped structure with a double membrane. The inner membrane's cristae look like shelves or fingers across the inside, and the matrix is a grey material between them. A mitochondrion cut across looks round. Cells that use a lot of ATP, such as muscle, liver and the cells lining the airways, have many mitochondria with many cristae.

:::eg Describe how you would recognise a mitochondrion in an electron micrograph and explain how its structure is related to its function in aerobic respiration.
Two parts: recognise it, and link structure to function.

A mitochondrion is oval or rod shaped and surrounded by two membranes (an envelope). [B1]

The inner membrane is folded into cristae, which are visible as shelf-like folds across the inside. [B1]

The cristae give a large surface area for the electron transport chains and ATP synthase, so more ATP can be made by oxidative phosphorylation. [B1]

The matrix contains the enzymes of the link reaction and the Krebs cycle. [B1]

The intermembrane space is small, so protons pumped into it quickly build up a steep proton gradient. [B1]

(Also credit: the matrix contains circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, so the mitochondrion can make some of its own proteins.)
:::

:::eg A mitochondrion in an electron micrograph is 62 mm long on the image. The magnification of the micrograph is ×20 000. Calculate the actual length of the mitochondrion in µm.
**Step 1: use \(M = I/A\)**, so \(A = I/M\), keeping image and actual size in the same units.

\[\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{62\,\mathrm{mm}}{20\,000} \\ &= 0.0031\,\mathrm{mm} \end{aligned}\]

**Step 2: convert to micrometres.** \(1\,\mathrm{mm} = 1000\,\mathrm{\mu m}\), so \(0.0031\,\mathrm{mm} = 3.1\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).

This is a sensible size: a mitochondrion is a few micrometres long, much smaller than a typical animal cell and about the size of a bacterium.
:::

## A poison that damages the inner membrane

Data about a poison tests whether you understand the proton gradient. Here is a typical question.

:::eg Isolated mitochondria were given pyruvate and oxygen. The table shows the oxygen taken in and the ATP made per minute by a sample of mitochondria, without and with a poison that makes the inner membrane leaky to protons. Describe the effect of the poison and explain it.
The units are µmol per minute per mg of mitochondrial protein.

| | oxygen taken in | ATP made |
|---|---|---|
| no poison | 4.0 | 10.0 |
| with poison | 5.0 | 1.5 |

**Describe.** With the poison, the ATP made falls from 10.0 to 1.5 µmol min⁻¹ per mg, a decrease of 85%, but the oxygen taken in does not fall: it rises from 4.0 to 5.0, an increase of 25%. [B1]

**Explain.** The electron transport chain still works: electrons still pass to oxygen, and protons are still pumped into the intermembrane space, so oxygen is still used. [B1]

Because the membrane is leaky, the protons flow back into the matrix through the membrane, not through ATP synthase. [B1]

So the proton gradient cannot be kept and there is far less flow of protons through ATP synthase, so much less ATP is made by chemiosmosis. [B1]

(Also credit: the energy released is lost as heat; the oxygen uptake rises because the chain is not slowed by a build-up of protons.)

The calculations: \((10.0 - 1.5) \div 10.0 \times 100 = 85\%\) decrease, and \((5.0 - 4.0) \div 4.0 \times 100 = 25\%\) increase.
:::

## Why aerobic respiration yields so much more than anaerobic

In aerobic respiration, the energy in glucose is released stage by stage and most of it is passed to ATP by chemiosmosis. In anaerobic conditions there is no final electron acceptor, so oxidative phosphorylation cannot happen, and the link reaction and Krebs cycle stop. Only glycolysis is left, with a **net gain of 2 ATP per glucose** by substrate-linked phosphorylation. The pyruvate is not completely oxidised, so most of the energy of glucose is still in the products (lactate or ethanol, see [Anaerobic respiration and investigating respiration](#bio.anaerobic)). Aerobic respiration makes many times more ATP from each glucose, and that is why large, active, warm-blooded animals depend on oxygen.

:::key Aerobic v anaerobic yield
1. Aerobic: the electron transport chain works because **oxygen is the final electron acceptor**, so chemiosmosis makes most of the ATP.
2. Anaerobic: only **glycolysis**, so a net gain of **2 ATP** per glucose.
3. In anaerobic respiration the substrate is only partly oxidised, so much energy stays in the products.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: explain how the proton gradient is made and used to make ATP. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Energy released as electrons pass along the electron transport chain is used to pump protons into the matrix.
Line 2: The protons then flow back down the gradient through ATP synthase by facilitated diffusion.
Line 3: The energy released as they flow is used to join ADP and phosphate to make ATP.

**Line 1 is wrong: the protons are pumped out of the matrix into the intermembrane space, which is how a gradient forms across the membrane. Pumping them into the matrix would make no gradient to flow down. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 2 is correct [B1]. Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct Line 1: "…to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State where in a eukaryotic cell each of the four stages of aerobic respiration takes place.
A: Glycolysis: cytoplasm. Link reaction: mitochondrial matrix. Krebs cycle: mitochondrial matrix. Oxidative phosphorylation: inner mitochondrial membrane.
Q: Glycolysis uses 2 ATP and makes 4 ATP for each glucose. State the net gain, and the type of phosphorylation involved.
A: A net gain of 2 ATP, by substrate-linked phosphorylation.
Q: Name the three products of the link reaction, per pyruvate molecule.
A: Carbon dioxide (1 molecule), reduced NAD (1 molecule) and acetyl coenzyme A (the 2C acetyl group carried by coenzyme A).
Q: How many molecules of reduced NAD are made by the Krebs cycle from one glucose molecule? Explain your answer.
A: 6. Each turn gives 3 reduced NAD, and there are two turns per glucose, because glycolysis gives two pyruvate and so two acetyl CoA.
Q: State the role of coenzyme A in aerobic respiration.
A: It carries the acetyl group (2C) from the link reaction to the Krebs cycle, where the acetyl group joins oxaloacetate.
Q: A mitochondrion in a heart muscle cell is 4.2 µm long. In a drawing of an electron micrograph it is 63 mm long. Calculate the magnification of the drawing.
A: Magnification \(= I \div A = 63\,\mathrm{mm} \div 0.0042\,\mathrm{mm} = 15\,000\), so the drawing is ×15 000.
Q: Give two ways in which the cristae help the mitochondrion make ATP.
A: They give the inner membrane a large surface area. So more electron transport chains and more ATP synthase molecules fit into the membrane.
Q: Which stage of aerobic respiration releases most of the carbon dioxide, and what is the process by which the carbon dioxide is released?
A: The Krebs cycle releases 4 of the 6 CO₂ per glucose (the link reaction releases 2). The process is decarboxylation.

Q: [4 marks] ★ State where each of the four stages of aerobic respiration (glycolysis, the link reaction, the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation) takes place in a eukaryotic cell.
A: Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm. [B1]
The link reaction takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. [B1]
The Krebs cycle takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. [B1]
Oxidative phosphorylation takes place on the inner mitochondrial membrane. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Outline glycolysis.
H: Phosphorylation, splitting, oxidation.
A: Glucose is phosphorylated using 2 ATP to form fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (6C). [B1]
This splits into two molecules of triose phosphate (3C). [B1]
Each triose phosphate is oxidised (dehydrogenated) to pyruvate (3C), and NAD is reduced to reduced NAD (2 per glucose). [B1]
4 ATP are made by substrate-linked phosphorylation, so there is a net gain of 2 ATP. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Describe the link reaction, including the role of coenzyme A.
A: Pyruvate is decarboxylated: a carbon atom is removed as carbon dioxide. [B1]
Pyruvate is dehydrogenated: hydrogen is removed and NAD is reduced to reduced NAD. [B1]
The remaining acetyl group (2C) combines with coenzyme A to form acetyl coenzyme A. [B1]
Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group to the Krebs cycle. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Outline the Krebs cycle.
H: Say what joins what, what is released, and what is regenerated.
A: Acetyl coenzyme A (2C) combines with oxaloacetate (4C) to form citrate (6C). [B1]
Citrate is decarboxylated twice, releasing carbon dioxide, so the carbon number falls from 6 to 4. [B1]
Dehydrogenation reduces NAD and FAD. [B1]
One ATP is made by substrate-linked phosphorylation. [B1]
The 4C compound is converted back to oxaloacetate through a series of small steps. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ A mitochondrion in an electron micrograph is drawn at the scale shown by a 2 µm bar that is 18 mm long. (a) Calculate the magnification. (b) The mitochondrion in the micrograph is 33 mm long. Calculate its actual length in µm. (c) Explain how the cristae increase the rate of ATP production.
H: Magnification \(=\) image size \(\div\) actual size, in the same units.
H: For (c), think of the electron transport chain and ATP synthase.
A: (a) \(18\,\mathrm{mm} \div 0.002\,\mathrm{mm}\) [M1] \(= 9000\) [A1]
(b) \(33 \div 9000 = 0.00367\,\mathrm{mm}\) [M1] \(= 3.7\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) [A1]
(c) The cristae give the inner membrane a large surface area. [B1]
So more electron transport chains and more ATP synthase molecules fit in, so more ATP is made. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Explain why the yield of ATP from respiration is much greater in aerobic conditions than in anaerobic conditions.
A: Aerobic: oxygen is the final electron acceptor, so the electron transport chain keeps working and oxidative phosphorylation makes ATP by chemiosmosis. [B1]
Anaerobic: no oxygen, so the chain stops and reduced NAD cannot be reoxidised by it. [B1]
So the link reaction and Krebs cycle also stop, and only glycolysis can make ATP, a net gain of 2 ATP per glucose. [B1]
The substrate is only partly oxidised, so most of the energy of the glucose remains in the products. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] (#bio.movement) ★★★ Roots in waterlogged soil take up mineral ions more slowly than roots in well-aerated soil. Explain why, in terms of respiration.
H: Which type of transport carries mineral ions into root hairs against a concentration gradient?
A: Mineral ions enter root hair cells by active transport, which needs ATP. [B1]
In waterlogged soil there is little oxygen. [B1]
So oxygen is not available as the final electron acceptor, the electron transport chain stops and little ATP is made by oxidative phosphorylation. [B1]
Only glycolysis makes ATP, with a net gain of 2 per glucose. [B1]
So carrier proteins in the membranes of the root hair cells receive less energy and less uptake of ions takes place. [B1]

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Cyanide binds to the last carrier of the electron transport chain and stops it passing electrons to oxygen. A cell that uses only aerobic respiration is exposed to cyanide. Predict and explain what happens to (i) the oxygen taken in by the cell, (ii) the amount of reduced NAD in the mitochondria, and (iii) the ATP made by the Krebs cycle and oxidative phosphorylation.
H: What happens to the carriers when electrons cannot be passed on?
H: Which coenzyme is needed in every dehydrogenation?
A: (i) The oxygen taken in falls to almost zero, because electrons can no longer be passed to oxygen. [B1]
(ii) Reduced NAD builds up, because it cannot give up its hydrogen to the carriers, so NAD is not regenerated. [B1]
Without NAD (and FAD), the dehydrogenations of the link reaction and Krebs cycle cannot go on, so those reactions stop, and so does the ATP made by substrate-linked phosphorylation in the Krebs cycle. [B1]
(iii) The proton pump stops, so no proton gradient is maintained and ATP synthase makes almost no ATP by oxidative phosphorylation. [B1]
The cell can make ATP only by glycolysis (net 2 ATP per glucose), as long as NAD can be regenerated by another route. [B1]
So the cell is quickly short of ATP, and the processes that need it, such as active transport and movement, fail. [B1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ Mitochondria were isolated from liver cells and placed in a chamber with pyruvate, phosphate and oxygen, but no ADP. The concentration of oxygen in the chamber was measured. ADP was added at 120 s.

| time / s | oxygen / µmol dm⁻³ |
|---|---|
| 0 | 250 |
| 60 | 247 |
| 120 | 244 |
| 150 | 214 |
| 180 | 184 |
| 210 | 154 |
| 240 | 152 |
| 300 | 149 |

(a) Calculate the rate of oxygen uptake before ADP was added and between 120 s and 210 s. (b) Explain why the oxygen uptake increased when ADP was added. (c) Explain why it slowed again after 210 s.
H: ATP synthase needs ADP and phosphate. What happens to the protons if it cannot work?
H: What happens to the proton pump if the gradient is already steep?
A: (a) Before ADP: \((250 - 244) \div 120\) [M1] \(= 0.050\,\mathrm{\mu mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}\) [A1]
With ADP: \((244 - 154) \div 90 = 1.0\,\mathrm{\mu mol\,dm^{-3}\,s^{-1}}\) [A1]
(b) With ADP present, ATP synthase can work, so protons flow back into the matrix through it and the proton gradient falls. [B1]
So the chain can pump more protons and pass more electrons to oxygen, so oxygen is used faster. [B1]
(c) After about 210 s all the ADP had been converted to ATP, so ATP synthase stopped and the protons could not flow back. [B1]
The gradient built up and slowed the pumping and the electron flow, so less oxygen was used. [B1]

:::recap
- Four stages: glycolysis (cytoplasm), link reaction (matrix), Krebs cycle (matrix), oxidative phosphorylation (inner membrane).
- Glycolysis: 2 ATP used to phosphorylate glucose, fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (6C) splits into 2 triose phosphate (3C), oxidised to 2 pyruvate; 4 ATP made, net 2; 2 reduced NAD.
- Link reaction: pyruvate is decarboxylated and dehydrogenated; the acetyl group (2C) joins coenzyme A. Per glucose: 2 CO₂, 2 reduced NAD.
- Krebs cycle: acetyl (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C) → two decarboxylations and dehydrogenations → oxaloacetate. Per turn: 2 CO₂, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP; two turns per glucose.
- Per glucose in total: 6 CO₂, 10 reduced NAD, 2 reduced FAD, 4 ATP made directly (net).
- Oxidative phosphorylation: reduced NAD and FAD give hydrogen to carriers; hydrogen splits into protons and electrons; electrons pass along the chain; the energy pumps protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space; protons flow back through ATP synthase by facilitated diffusion, making ATP; oxygen is the final electron acceptor, forming water.
- Mitochondrion: double membrane, cristae (large surface area), narrow intermembrane space (steep gradient), matrix (enzymes, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes).
- Aerobic yield is much greater than anaerobic because oxygen allows the chain to work; without it only the net 2 ATP of glycolysis remain.
:::
