## The carbohydrate family

Carbohydrates contain only three elements: carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. The name means "hydrated carbon", because in the simplest ones there are two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom, just as in water. The simplest carbohydrates are sugars, and the big ones are chains of sugars. To describe the family you need six terms.

:::def Monomer
A smaller unit from which larger molecules are made.
:::

:::def Polymer
A molecule made from a large number of monomer units joined together.
:::

:::def Macromolecule
A very large molecule. Polysaccharides, proteins and nucleic acids are macromolecules.
:::

:::def Monosaccharide
A single sugar unit. It is the monomer from which disaccharides and polysaccharides are made. Examples: glucose, fructose, galactose.
:::

:::def Disaccharide
A sugar made of two monosaccharides joined together by a glycosidic bond. Examples: maltose, sucrose, lactose.
:::

:::def Polysaccharide
A polymer made of many monosaccharides joined together by glycosidic bonds. Examples: starch, glycogen, cellulose.
:::

Every polysaccharide is a polymer and a macromolecule, but not every macromolecule is a polymer: you will meet one in [Lipids and water](#bio.lipids). Do not mix up the size words. A *monosaccharide* is one unit, a *disaccharide* is two, and a *polysaccharide* is many (hundreds to thousands).

### Monosaccharides

Monosaccharides are named by the number of carbon atoms. Their formula follows a pattern:

\[(\ce{CH2O})_n\]

with \(n\) from 3 upwards.

| Type | Carbons (n) | Formula | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| triose | 3 | \(\ce{C3H6O3}\) | triose phosphate (in photosynthesis) |
| pentose | 5 | \(\ce{C5H10O5}\) | ribose (in RNA and ATP) |
| hexose | 6 | \(\ce{C6H12O6}\) | glucose, fructose, galactose |

Glucose, fructose and galactose have the same molecular formula, \(\ce{C6H12O6}\), but their atoms are arranged differently, so they are different molecules with different properties. Glucose is the most important: it is the main substrate for respiration (see [Aerobic respiration](#bio.respiration)), and it is the building block of starch, glycogen and cellulose.

:::key Carbohydrates in one box
- Carbohydrates contain **C, H and O only**.
- Monosaccharides have the formula \((\ce{CH2O})_n\): triose \(n = 3\), pentose \(n = 5\), hexose \(n = 6\).
- Glucose, fructose and galactose are all hexoses with the formula \(\ce{C6H12O6}\).
:::

## The ring forms of glucose

In a cell, glucose is almost never a straight chain. The chain curls round and the carbon at one end bonds to an oxygen near the other end, closing a ring of five carbons and one oxygen. The carbons are numbered 1 to 6 so that every chemist can point to the same atom. In the drawing, carbon 1 is the carbon on the right, just next to the oxygen of the ring, and you count clockwise. Carbon 6 is not in the ring: it is the \(\ce{CH2OH}\) group that hangs above carbon 5.

When the ring closes, the OH group on carbon 1 can end up on either side of the ring. That gives two forms of glucose, called alpha (α) and beta (β).

:::key How to draw the glucose ring (the order that works)
1. Draw a six-sided ring with the oxygen at the **top right**. The thick edge is at the front (bottom).
2. Write \(\ce{CH2OH}\) **above** the carbon at the top left (carbon 5). That is carbon 6.
3. Carbon 1 is on the right. Write **OH below** the ring for **α**, and **OH above** the ring for **β**. The H goes on the other side.
4. Carbon 2: OH **below**. Carbon 3: OH **above**. Carbon 4: OH **below**. The H goes on the opposite side each time.
5. Check: **α = OH on carbon 1 down**, **β = OH on carbon 1 up**. Carbons 2, 3 and 4 are identical in both.
:::

:::warn Do not draw the wrong way round
The commonest lost mark in this topic is α and β swapped. Pick one rule and use it every time: for α the OH on carbon 1 points **down**, for β it points **up**. Tie it to what each makes: α-glucose builds starch and glycogen, and β-glucose builds cellulose.
:::

:::eg Draw the ring structure of α-glucose and of β-glucose, and state how they differ.
Build each drawing in the order of the key box. A full-mark answer has four separate marking points.

A six-sided ring of five carbon atoms and one oxygen atom, with the oxygen at the top right. [B1]

A \(\ce{CH2OH}\) group above the ring on carbon 5 (carbon 6). [B1]

OH groups below the ring on carbons 2 and 4 and above the ring on carbon 3, in both drawings. [B1]

On carbon 1 the OH is below the ring in α-glucose and above the ring in β-glucose: this is the only difference. [B1]

A weaker answer draws the rings but puts the OH on carbon 1 the wrong way round for one of them, or says that the two forms have "different numbers of atoms". Both have the formula \(\ce{C6H12O6}\); only the position of one OH group differs.
:::

:::story Haworth and the ring
The way of drawing sugars as flat rings with groups "up" and "down" is called a Haworth projection after the British chemist Norman Haworth, who worked out the ring structure of sugars and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1937. A real ring is not flat, but the flat drawing shows which groups are on which side, and that is all you need.
:::

## Joining sugars: condensation and the glycosidic bond

Small molecules are joined into big ones by **covalent bonds**: pairs of electrons shared between atoms. A covalent bond is strong, so a polymer is stable, and a cell can build a chain of thousands of units without it falling apart. The covalent bond between two sugar units has its own name.

:::def Glycosidic bond
A covalent bond between two monosaccharides, formed by a condensation reaction, with an oxygen atom between the two rings (a C–O–C link).
:::

:::key The role of covalent bonds
Covalent bonds join monomers into polymers (here, monosaccharides into disaccharides and polysaccharides). They are strong, so the polymer is stable; they can be broken, one at a time, by hydrolysis.
:::

### Condensation

Two glucose molecules join when the OH on carbon 1 of one molecule reacts with the OH on carbon 4 of the other. A molecule of water (one OH and one H) is removed, and the oxygen left behind links the two rings. When both molecules are α-glucose, the product is **maltose** and the bond is an **α-1,4 glycosidic bond**: the numbers say that carbon 1 of one ring is joined to carbon 4 of the other.

In words and symbols:

\[\ce{C6H12O6 + C6H12O6 -> C12H22O11 + H2O}\]

Notice that the water is a **product**: this is a condensation reaction, and the number of atoms balances (12 C, 24 H, 12 O on each side). The same reaction adds a third glucose to maltose, then a fourth, and so on. A polysaccharide of \(n\) glucose units has \(n - 1\) glycosidic bonds, and \(n - 1\) molecules of water were removed to make it.

:::eg A starch molecule contains a chain of 20 glucose units. State how many glycosidic bonds it contains and how many water molecules were removed when it was made. Calculate its relative molecular mass if glucose has a relative molecular mass of 180 and water has 18.
A chain is built by adding one unit at a time, and each addition makes one bond and removes one water molecule.

Number of bonds \(= 20 - 1 = 19\), and 19 water molecules were removed. [B1]

Mass of the 20 glucose units before joining \(= 20 \times 180 = 3600\), and mass of water removed \(= 19 \times 18 = 342\). [M1]

\[\begin{aligned} M_r &= 3600 - 342 \\ &= 3258 \end{aligned}\]

The relative molecular mass is 3258. [A1]

A common mistake is to subtract 20 water molecules, as if every unit lost water. The first glucose has nothing to join to, so 20 units make only 19 bonds.
:::

### Disaccharides

Three disaccharides appear in the syllabus.

| Disaccharide | Made from | Reducing sugar? | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|---|
| maltose | α-glucose + α-glucose | yes | made when amylase digests starch |
| sucrose | α-glucose + fructose | **no** | table sugar; the sugar plants transport |
| lactose | β-galactose + glucose | yes | the sugar in milk (optional) |

:::note Lactose
Lactose is the sugar in milk. It is hydrolysed by the enzyme lactase into glucose and galactose, as in the lactose-free milk example in [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes). Some adults make little lactase, so lactose passes undigested to the large intestine. You do not need its structure.
:::

### Reducing and non-reducing sugars

:::def Reducing sugar
A sugar that can donate electrons to (reduce) another substance. In Benedict's test, a reducing sugar reduces blue copper(II) ions to give a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide.
:::

All monosaccharides (including glucose and fructose) are reducing sugars, and so is maltose. **Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar**: the groups on its two rings that do the reducing are the very ones that joined to form its glycosidic bond, so none is free to react. Polysaccharides such as starch give a negative result with Benedict's solution too. The test itself is in [Testing for biological molecules](#bio.foodtests).

:::key Reducing or non-reducing?
- **Reducing**: glucose, fructose, galactose, maltose (and lactose).
- **Non-reducing**: sucrose, and the polysaccharides starch, glycogen and cellulose.
- Benedict's test: blue stays blue (negative) for a non-reducing sugar; brick-red precipitate (positive) for a reducing sugar.
:::

### Hydrolysis

Hydrolysis is the reverse of condensation. A molecule of water is used to break a glycosidic bond: the \(\ce{OH}\) goes to one sugar, the \(\ce{H}\) to the other, and the chain splits in two.

\[\ce{C12H22O11 + H2O -> C6H12O6 + C6H12O6}\]

In living things the reaction is catalysed by enzymes: amylase hydrolyses starch to maltose, maltase hydrolyses maltose to glucose, and sucrase hydrolyses sucrose to glucose and fructose. In the laboratory, boiling with dilute acid does the same thing, and this is the key to the **non-reducing sugar test**.

:::steps
1. Add Benedict's solution to a sample and heat. If it stays blue, there is **no reducing sugar**.
2. Take a fresh sample, boil it with dilute hydrochloric acid for a few minutes. Acid breaks (hydrolyses) any glycosidic bonds.
3. Neutralise the acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate (Benedict's solution only works in alkaline conditions).
4. Add Benedict's solution and heat. A brick-red precipitate now means that a **non-reducing sugar** was present: hydrolysis freed reducing monosaccharides.
:::

:::eg A sample was tested with Benedict's solution before and after boiling with dilute acid. Interpret the results in the table.
The samples were boiled with acid, neutralised, then heated with Benedict's solution.

| sample | before acid | after acid |
|---|---|---|
| A | blue | blue |
| B | blue | brick-red |
| C | brick-red | brick-red |

Sample A stays blue both times, so it contains no reducing sugar and no sugar that acid could hydrolyse to one. [B1]

Sample B was blue first, so it had no reducing sugar. After acid it gave a brick-red precipitate, so hydrolysis produced reducing sugars: B contains a non-reducing sugar, such as sucrose. [B1]

Sample C was already positive, so it contains a reducing sugar, such as glucose or maltose. [B1]

The acid does not change the result for C, because it was positive first; a result that changes only after acid is the evidence for a non-reducing sugar. [B1]

A weaker answer says "B has a reducing sugar", which is wrong: the first test shows that it had none. It must name the idea of hydrolysis releasing reducing sugars.
:::

:::exam State, then explain
"State that sucrose is a non-reducing sugar" earns a mark. If the question says "describe the test for a non-reducing sugar", give both parts in order: the negative Benedict's result first, then boiling with dilute acid, neutralising, and a positive Benedict's test, with the reason (the acid hydrolysed the glycosidic bond).
:::

## Starch and glycogen: energy stores

Cells need glucose all the time for respiration, but storing glucose itself would be a disaster. Glucose is small and soluble, so a store of it would pull water into the cell by osmosis (see [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement)) and the glucose would diffuse out. Plants and animals instead store glucose as **polysaccharides** that are large, compact and insoluble. They are made of α-glucose, so enzymes can hydrolyse them to glucose when it is needed.

:::def Starch
A storage polysaccharide in plants, made of two polymers of α-glucose: amylose (unbranched) and amylopectin (branched). It is stored in plant cells as grains, for example in chloroplasts and in potato tubers and seeds.
:::

:::def Glycogen
A storage polysaccharide in animals and fungi, made of α-glucose and more highly branched than amylopectin. It is stored as granules, especially in liver and muscle cells.
:::

### Amylose

Amylose is a long, **unbranched** chain of α-glucose joined only by **α-1,4 glycosidic bonds**. The chain is not straight. The α-1,4 bond makes each glucose sit at an angle to the next, so the chain coils into a **helix**, held in shape by hydrogen bonds. A coil is very **compact**, so a lot of glucose is packed into a small space.

### Amylopectin

Amylopectin is also a polymer of α-glucose with α-1,4 bonds, but at intervals it has **branches**: a different carbon (carbon 6) of a glucose unit forms an **α-1,6 glycosidic bond** with the first glucose of a new chain. A branch point occurs roughly every 24 to 30 glucose units. A branched molecule has many **free ends**, where the enzymes that hydrolyse starch (such as amylase) can remove glucose units.

### Glycogen

Glycogen is similar to amylopectin but **more highly branched**, with α-1,6 bonds roughly every 8 to 12 glucose units. That gives it even more free ends, so enzymes can remove glucose from many ends at the same time, and glucose is released **very quickly**. This suits animals, whose muscles can suddenly need a lot of energy.

:::analogy A twig versus a tree
Picture one long rope (amylose) and a tree (glycogen). A hungry enzyme can only start at an end. The rope has two ends, so only two enzymes can work at once. The tree has hundreds of twig tips, so hundreds of enzymes can start at the same time. More ends mean faster release of glucose.
:::

| Feature | Amylose | Amylopectin | Glycogen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monomer | α-glucose | α-glucose | α-glucose |
| Bonds | α-1,4 | α-1,4 and α-1,6 | α-1,4 and α-1,6 |
| Branching | none | some | many more |
| Shape | helix (coil) | branched | very branched, compact |
| Found in | plants | plants | animals and fungi |

:::key Why starch and glycogen are good stores
- **Compact** (coiled or branched): a lot of glucose in a small space.
- **Insoluble** and **large**: they do not dissolve in the cytoplasm and cannot diffuse out of the cell, and they have **no osmotic effect** (they do not lower the water potential of the cell, unlike many separate glucose molecules).
- A polymer of **α-glucose**: enzymes hydrolyse the glycosidic bonds to release glucose for respiration.
- **Branched** (amylopectin, glycogen): many **free ends**, so glucose is released rapidly.
:::

:::eg Explain why glycogen is a suitable energy store in the cells of an animal.

It is highly branched, so it has many free ends where enzymes can act at the same time. [B1]

A weaker answer says "glycogen stores energy" and stops. It does not name the structure (branched, α-glucose) or link each feature to its effect, so it scores one mark at most.

## Cellulose: the strong cell wall

Plants make more cellulose than any other organic compound. It is the main substance of the **cell wall**, which you met in [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells). A plant cell takes in water by osmosis and swells, pushing against its wall. The wall must be strong enough to stop the cell bursting, and yet let water and dissolved substances through.

:::def Cellulose
A structural polysaccharide made of long, unbranched chains of β-glucose joined by β-1,4 glycosidic bonds. It is the main component of plant cell walls.
:::

The difference from starch is small on paper and huge in effect. In β-glucose the OH on carbon 1 points up. To make a glycosidic bond, every second β-glucose has to be **turned through 180°**, upside down, so that its carbon 4 and carbon 1 line up with its neighbours. The result is a chain that is **perfectly straight**, not coiled. In amylose, by contrast, every glucose faces the same way and the chain curls.

Because the chains are straight, they can lie side by side. The many OH groups sticking out of each chain form **hydrogen bonds** with OH groups on the neighbouring chains. One hydrogen bond is weak, but each chain makes huge numbers of them, so together they are strong.

:::steps
1. Glucose units (β) are joined by β-1,4 bonds into long, straight, unbranched **cellulose molecules**.
2. Many cellulose molecules lie side by side, held by **hydrogen bonds between the chains**, forming a **microfibril**.
3. Microfibrils are bundled and wound into **cellulose fibres**.
4. Layers of fibres, running in different directions, make up the cell wall.
:::

How does this structure suit the wall?

- **High tensile strength.** The covalent bonds along each chain and the enormous number of hydrogen bonds between chains make microfibrils very hard to stretch or pull apart. The wall resists the **turgor pressure** of the cell as water enters by osmosis, so the cell does not burst (see [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement)).
- **Fibres in layers** cross one another, so the wall resists forces from many directions and a cell can be firm and hold its shape.
- **Freely permeable.** There are spaces between the fibres, so water and dissolved substances pass through the wall easily. The partially permeable membrane inside the wall, not the wall itself, controls what enters the cytoplasm.
- **Cannot be hydrolysed by most animals.** The wall is not digested by the cell's own enzymes, so it stays in place.

:::warn Hydrogen bonds: within or between?
Cellulose chains are held together by hydrogen bonds **between** chains, not "within" a chain. The strength of a microfibril comes from the very large number of those hydrogen bonds. Also, a single hydrogen bond is weak: say "many hydrogen bonds", never "strong hydrogen bonds". And remember the covalent glycosidic bonds along the chain; they are not hydrogen bonds.
:::

:::eg Compare the structures and functions of glycogen and cellulose.
Both are polysaccharides of glucose, so begin with a similarity, then give differences, each with a function. A good answer has five marking points.

Both are polymers of glucose, joined by 1,4 glycosidic bonds (formed by condensation). [B1]

Glycogen is made of α-glucose, but cellulose is made of β-glucose, with every second unit turned through 180°. [B1]

Glycogen is branched, with α-1,6 bonds as well as α-1,4, but cellulose is unbranched, with only β-1,4 bonds. [B1]

Cellulose chains are straight and form many hydrogen bonds with neighbouring chains, making microfibrils with high tensile strength; glycogen is compact and has no such bonding between molecules. [B1]

So glycogen is an energy store (many free ends, quickly hydrolysed to glucose), while cellulose is structural, strengthening the plant cell wall. [B1]

A weaker answer lists facts about one molecule at a time ("glycogen is branched ... cellulose is straight") without saying how they differ in the same feature, or leaves out the function. A comparison needs both molecules mentioned for each point.
:::

:::eg Real context: humans cannot digest cellulose, but cows live on grass. Explain.
Eating grass supplies large amounts of cellulose, but a cow gains energy from it.

Humans and cows do not make an enzyme (cellulase) that can hydrolyse the β-1,4 glycosidic bonds in cellulose. [B1]

The shape of cellulose, with its β-glucose units, is not complementary to the active site of amylase, which only hydrolyses α-1,4 bonds (enzymes are specific). [B1]

A cow has microorganisms in its stomach (the rumen) that make cellulase; they hydrolyse the cellulose to glucose, which the cow uses. [B1]

Humans cannot do this, so cellulose passes through the gut as dietary fibre. It is not useless: fibre adds bulk that helps the movement of food through the gut.
:::

Q: Define the term monosaccharide and give two examples.
A: A single sugar unit (the monomer from which larger carbohydrates are made). Examples: glucose, fructose, galactose (any two).
Q: State the general formula of a monosaccharide, and give the molecular formula of a pentose.
A: \((\ce{CH2O})_n\). For a pentose \(n = 5\), so \(\ce{C5H10O5}\).
Q: State one structural difference between α-glucose and β-glucose.
A: On carbon 1 the OH group is below the ring in α-glucose and above the ring in β-glucose.
Q: A student wrote "glucose is a polymer". Correct this statement.
A: Glucose is a monomer (a monosaccharide). Polymers of glucose include starch, glycogen and cellulose.
Q: A chain of 250 glucose units is built. How many glycosidic bonds does it contain, and how many water molecules were removed?
A: \(250 - 1 = 249\) bonds, and 249 water molecules removed.
Q: Glucose has a relative molecular mass of 180 and water of 18. Calculate the relative molecular mass of maltose.
A: \(2 \times 180 - 18 = 342\).
Q: Name the bond that joins two glucose units in maltose, the type of reaction that forms it and the type of reaction that breaks it.
A: A glycosidic bond (1,4); formed by condensation (water removed) and broken by hydrolysis (water added).
Q: A solution of sugar gives no colour change when heated with Benedict's solution, but a brick-red precipitate after boiling with acid. Name the type of sugar and explain the result.
A: A non-reducing sugar, such as sucrose. Boiling with acid hydrolysed the glycosidic bond, releasing reducing monosaccharides (glucose and fructose) that gave a positive Benedict's test.
Q: Name the two polysaccharides found in starch and state which one is branched.
A: Amylose and amylopectin. Amylopectin is branched.
Q: Give two differences between amylopectin and glycogen.
A: Glycogen is more highly branched (a branch about every 8 to 12 glucose units compared with every 24 to 30), so it has more free ends; and glycogen is found in animals (and fungi), amylopectin in plants.
Q: Cotton fabric is mostly cellulose. Explain why a cotton thread is strong.
A: Cellulose chains are straight and unbranched and form many hydrogen bonds with neighbouring chains, making microfibrils and fibres with high tensile strength.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [3 marks] ★ Define the terms monomer, polymer and macromolecule.
A: A monomer is a smaller unit from which larger molecules are made. [B1]
A polymer is a molecule made from a large number of monomer units joined together. [B1]
A macromolecule is a very large molecule (for example a polysaccharide, a protein or a nucleic acid). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Draw the ring structure of β-glucose. Your drawing must show the position of the oxygen in the ring, carbon 6, and the groups on carbon 1.
H: Start with the hexagon, oxygen at the top right, then add the CH₂OH group above the ring.
H: β means the OH on carbon 1 points up.
A: A ring of five carbons and one oxygen, with the oxygen at the top right. [B1]
A CH₂OH group (carbon 6) above carbon 5, at the top left. [B1]
On carbon 1 the OH is above the ring (and the H below). [B1]
The groups on the other carbons are correct: OH below the ring on carbons 2 and 4, and above on carbon 3. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Equal masses of amylose and amylopectin were hydrolysed by an enzyme that removes glucose from the free ends of chains. The table shows the mass of sugar released.

| time / min | amylose / mg | amylopectin / mg |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 2 | 4 | 11 |
| 4 | 7 | 19 |
| 6 | 9 | 25 |
| 8 | 10 | 29 |

(a) Describe the differences between the two sets of results. (b) Suggest an explanation for the difference.
H: Quote figures from the table with units, and compare at the same time.
H: Compare the number of free ends in the two molecules.
A: (a) The mass of sugar released increases with time for both, but the rate slows down over the 8 minutes. [B1]
More sugar is released from amylopectin at every time: at 8 minutes 29 mg compared with 10 mg for amylose. [B1]
(b) Amylopectin is branched, so it has many more free ends than the unbranched amylose. [B1]
The enzyme acts at free ends, so more enzyme molecules can work at the same time on amylopectin. [B1]
So amylopectin is hydrolysed faster and more sugar is released. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Cellulose and starch are both polymers of glucose. Explain why humans can digest starch but not cellulose.
A: Starch is a polymer of α-glucose joined by α-1,4 (and α-1,6) glycosidic bonds, whereas cellulose is a polymer of β-glucose joined by β-1,4 bonds. [B1]
Humans make amylase, which has an active site complementary to the α-1,4 bonds in starch, so it forms enzyme–substrate complexes with starch. [B1]
Cellulose has a different shape (every second glucose is turned over), which is not complementary to the active site of amylase. [B1]
Humans do not make cellulase, the enzyme that hydrolyses β-1,4 bonds. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ A cellulose molecule contains 3000 glucose units. State the number of water molecules removed when it was made. Calculate its relative molecular mass, if glucose is 180 and water is 18.
A: Number of water molecules \(= 3000 - 1 = 2999\). [B1]
\(M_r = 3000 \times 180 - 2999 \times 18\) [M1]
\(= 540\,000 - 53\,982 = 486\,018\) [A1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Starch and cellulose are both made of glucose, but starch is used for storage and cellulose for support. Explain how the structure of each is suited to its function.
H: For each polysaccharide give the monomer, the shape of the molecule, and what that does.
A: Starch is a polymer of α-glucose, which can be hydrolysed to release glucose for respiration. [B1]
Amylose coils into a helix and amylopectin is branched, so starch is compact and stores a lot of glucose in a small space. [B1]
Starch is insoluble and a large molecule, so it does not diffuse out of the cell and has no osmotic effect on the cell. [B1]
Cellulose is a polymer of β-glucose, with every second unit turned through 180°, which makes straight, unbranched chains. [B1]
Many hydrogen bonds form between neighbouring chains, forming microfibrils with high tensile strength. [B1]
So the cell wall resists the pressure of water entering the cell by osmosis, and the cell does not burst. [B1]
(Also credit: the cell wall is freely permeable; branched amylopectin has free ends for rapid hydrolysis.)
Q: [5 marks] (#bio.enzymes) ★★★ Lactose is a disaccharide of galactose and glucose. Lactose-free milk is made by adding the enzyme lactase to milk. (a) Name the type of reaction that lactase catalyses and the bond it breaks. (b) Lactase does not hydrolyse sucrose. Explain why. (c) A sample of lactose-free milk gives a positive Benedict's test. Explain why a sample of ordinary milk would also give a positive result.
H: Is lactose a reducing sugar?
A: (a) Hydrolysis; it breaks the glycosidic bond between galactose and glucose. [B1]
(b) Lactase is specific: sucrose has a shape that is not complementary to the active site, so no enzyme–substrate complex forms. [B1]
(c) Lactose is a reducing sugar, so it reduces the blue copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution to a brick-red precipitate. [B1]
Lactose-free milk gives a positive result because of the reducing sugars glucose and galactose, produced by hydrolysis of lactose. [B1]
Ordinary milk is positive because lactose itself is a reducing sugar. [B1]
:::

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A plant cell contains \(1.0 \times 10^{6}\) glucose units stored as starch. Each starch molecule is a chain of about 2000 glucose units. (a) Calculate the number of starch molecules. (b) The water potential of a solution depends on the number of dissolved particles. Use your answer to (a) to explain why storing glucose as starch is better for the cell than storing it as glucose.
H: The number of starch molecules is the number of glucose units divided by the units in each molecule.
H: A particle count is what matters for the osmotic effect, not the mass.
A: (a) \(\dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{6}}{2000}\) [M1] \(= 500\) starch molecules [A1]
(b) As starch there are 500 particles in the cell; as glucose there would be \(1.0 \times 10^{6}\) particles, 2000 times as many. [B1]
Far more dissolved particles would lower the water potential of the cell cytoplasm, so water would enter by osmosis. [B1]
The cell would swell (and may burst, in an animal cell); starch is insoluble and large, so it has almost no osmotic effect. [B1]
Starch can be hydrolysed back to glucose when it is needed. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Two types of rice were fed to volunteers. Rice A is 3% amylose and 97% amylopectin; rice B is 28% amylose and 72% amylopectin. Blood glucose rose faster after eating rice A than after rice B. Use your knowledge of starch to suggest an explanation, and suggest why the glycogen in the volunteers' liver can release glucose much faster than either type of starch can be digested.
H: Which molecule has more free ends, and what do enzymes need?
H: Compare how compact each molecule is.
A: Amylopectin is branched, so it has many free ends; rice A (more amylopectin) has more free ends for digestive enzymes to act on. [B1]
So starch is hydrolysed faster and glucose is released and absorbed faster, which raises blood glucose faster. [B1]
Amylose is a compact unbranched coil with few ends, so it is hydrolysed more slowly; rice B has more amylose, so glucose is released more slowly. [B1]
Glycogen is more highly branched than amylopectin, with a branch about every 8 to 12 units, so it has even more free ends. [B1]
Many enzyme molecules can act at once, so the glycosidic bonds are hydrolysed rapidly. [B1]
Glycogen is already inside the liver cells, so no digestion, absorption or transport from the gut is needed first. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which of these is a reducing sugar?
+ maltose
- sucrose
- starch
- cellulose
> Maltose has a free reducing group, so it gives a brick-red precipitate with Benedict's solution. In sucrose the reducing groups are used up in the glycosidic bond, and starch and cellulose are polysaccharides that give a negative result.
? What is the correct name for the bond between two glucose units in cellulose?
+ β-1,4 glycosidic bond
- α-1,4 glycosidic bond
- hydrogen bond
- α-1,6 glycosidic bond
> Cellulose is a polymer of β-glucose, so its glycosidic bonds are β-1,4. α-1,4 is the bond in amylose, and α-1,6 forms the branches in amylopectin and glycogen. Hydrogen bonds act between the chains, not along them.
? Why does starch have no osmotic effect on a plant cell?
+ It is a large, insoluble molecule, so it does not lower the water potential of the cytoplasm.
- It is a small molecule that diffuses out of the cell.
- It is made of β-glucose, which cannot dissolve.
- It is hydrolysed to glucose as soon as it enters the cell.
> A store of many separate glucose molecules would lower the water potential and draw water in. Starch is large and insoluble, so it is a single particle for each thousand or so units and it stays in the cell.
? Glycogen releases glucose faster than amylose because it
+ is more highly branched, so it has more free ends.
- is made of β-glucose.
- contains no glycosidic bonds.
- is soluble in water.
> Enzymes remove glucose from free ends. Glycogen has a branch every 8 to 12 units, so many enzymes can act at once. It is made of α-glucose and is insoluble.
? Which statement about cellulose is correct?
+ Hydrogen bonds between neighbouring chains hold many chains together in microfibrils.
- Cellulose chains are coiled into helices.
- Cellulose is a polymer of α-glucose.
- Hydrogen bonds join glucose units along the chain.
> The strength of cellulose comes from the very large number of hydrogen bonds between straight chains. Glucose units along a chain are joined by covalent glycosidic bonds, and the units are β-glucose.
? A solution gives no change with Benedict's solution, but gives a brick-red precipitate after boiling with dilute hydrochloric acid and neutralising. What does it contain?
+ a non-reducing sugar such as sucrose
- a reducing sugar such as glucose
- starch only
- no sugar at all
> A negative result before hydrolysis shows that there is no reducing sugar. Acid hydrolysed a glycosidic bond, releasing reducing monosaccharides. A reducing sugar would have given a positive result straight away.
? How many water molecules are removed when a polysaccharide made of 5 glucose units is formed?
+ 4
- 5
- 6
- 1
> Each glycosidic bond removes one water molecule, and 5 units make 4 bonds. A chain of \(n\) units loses \(n - 1\) water molecules.
? Which pair correctly matches a molecule with its monomer?
+ glycogen: α-glucose
- cellulose: α-glucose
- sucrose: two molecules of fructose
- amylose: β-glucose
> Glycogen and amylose are polymers of α-glucose, and cellulose is a polymer of β-glucose. Sucrose is a disaccharide of glucose and fructose.
? Which of these is the only difference between α-glucose and β-glucose?
+ The position of the OH group on carbon 1.
- The number of carbon atoms in the ring.
- The molecular formula.
- The presence of carbon 6.
> Both are \(\ce{C6H12O6}\) rings of five carbons and an oxygen with a \(\ce{CH2OH}\) group on carbon 5. Only the OH on carbon 1 is on opposite sides of the ring.

:::recap
- Carbohydrates contain C, H and O. Monosaccharides are \((\ce{CH2O})_n\): triose 3, pentose 5, hexose 6. Glucose, fructose and galactose are \(\ce{C6H12O6}\).
- Monomer, polymer, macromolecule; monosaccharide (one unit), disaccharide (two units), polysaccharide (many units).
- α-glucose: OH on carbon 1 down; β-glucose: OH on carbon 1 up. CH₂OH (carbon 6) is above carbon 5.
- Condensation joins sugars by a covalent glycosidic bond and removes water: n units make n − 1 bonds. Hydrolysis breaks the bond using water.
- Reducing sugars: glucose, fructose, maltose. Sucrose is non-reducing: boil with acid, neutralise, then Benedict's gives red.
- Amylose: α-1,4, unbranched, helix. Amylopectin: α-1,4 and α-1,6 branches. Glycogen: more branches, more free ends, quicker release. All are compact and insoluble (no osmotic effect).
- Cellulose: β-glucose, every second unit turned over, straight chains, many hydrogen bonds between chains, microfibrils, fibres: high tensile strength, wall resists turgor, freely permeable.
:::
