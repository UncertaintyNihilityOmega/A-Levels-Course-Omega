## What makes something alive

Biology is the study of living things, so the first question is what counts as living. Biologists use seven **characteristics of living things**. A living thing does all seven; a rock does none.

| Characteristic | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Movement** | changes position or moves parts of its body | a plant shoot bends towards light |
| **Respiration** | releases energy from food in cells | every cell, all the time |
| **Sensitivity** | detects changes (stimuli) and responds | a pupil narrows in bright light |
| **Growth** | permanent increase in size or number of cells | a seedling becomes taller |
| **Reproduction** | makes new organisms like itself | a bacterium divides into two |
| **Excretion** | removes the waste products of its chemical reactions | carbon dioxide leaves the lungs; urea leaves in urine |
| **Nutrition** | takes in materials for energy, growth and repair | a plant makes food by photosynthesis; an animal eats |

A memory aid is MRS GREN, using the first letter of each word. Use the full words in an answer.

:::note Is a virus alive?
A virus has none of these characteristics on its own. It can reproduce only inside a host cell. This course treats viruses as non-cellular particles, and you meet them in [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes).
:::

### Levels of organisation

Living things are built in layers. A cell is made of molecules, and a human is made of billions of cells, but a cell is not just a heap of molecules and a human is not just a heap of cells: each level is organised so that it can do something the level below cannot.

:::def Organelle
A structure inside a cell that has a specific function, for example the nucleus or a mitochondrion.
:::

:::def Cell
The basic unit of structure and function of living things. A cell is surrounded by a cell surface membrane and contains cytoplasm and genetic material.
:::

:::def Tissue
A group of similar cells that work together to carry out a particular function, for example muscle tissue.
:::

:::def Organ
A structure made of several different tissues working together to carry out a particular function, for example the heart.
:::

:::def Organ system
A group of organs that work together to carry out a major function, for example the circulatory system.
:::

:::def Organism
A single living individual, made of one cell or of many. A human, an oak tree and a bacterium are organisms.
:::

Beyond one organism, biologists study groups of them.

:::def Population
A group of organisms of the same species living in the same place at the same time.
:::

:::def Community
All the populations of different species living and interacting in the same place.
:::

:::def Ecosystem
A community of living things together with the non-living parts of its surroundings (soil, air, water, climate), all interacting.
:::

Read the figure from the top: molecules are built into organelles, organelles into cells, cells into tissues, tissues into organs, organs into organ systems, and organ systems into an organism. Exam questions often ask you to name the level: "a group of muscle cells that contract together" is a tissue, "the stomach" is an organ, and "all the fish, plants and bacteria in a pond" is a community.

:::eg Name the level of organisation of each: (a) the lungs, (b) all the oak trees in one wood, (c) a mitochondrion, (d) the xylem in a stem.
**(a)** The lungs are made of several tissues working together to carry out gas exchange: an **organ**.

**(b)** Organisms of one species in one place: a **population**. (All the living things in the wood, of every species, would be the community.)

**(c)** A structure inside a cell with one job: an **organelle**.

**(d)** Xylem is a group of similar cells that carry water: a **tissue**.
:::

## Cells in one page

The cell is the unit of life, and most of this course happens inside cells. Here is the version you need before the detailed chapters, which start with [Microscopy and magnification](#bio.microscopy) and [Eukaryotic cells and organelles](#bio.cells).

| Part | What it does | In animal cells | In plant cells |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cell surface membrane** | controls what enters and leaves the cell | yes | yes |
| **Cytoplasm** | jelly-like fluid where many chemical reactions happen | yes | yes |
| **Nucleus** | contains the DNA, which controls the cell's activities | yes | yes |
| **Mitochondria** | release energy from food in respiration | yes | yes |
| **Ribosomes** | make proteins | yes | yes |
| **Cell wall** | strong outer layer (made of cellulose in plants) that supports the cell | no | yes |
| **Chloroplasts** | contain chlorophyll and carry out photosynthesis | no | yes (in green parts) |
| **Permanent vacuole** | a large space filled with cell sap, which helps keep the cell firm | no | yes |

:::cols
:::col Animal cell
- Cell surface membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, mitochondria, ribosomes.
- No cell wall, no chloroplasts, no large permanent vacuole.
- Usually irregular in shape.
:::
:::col Plant cell
- Everything an animal cell has, plus a cell wall, a large permanent vacuole and (in green parts) chloroplasts.
- Fixed shape, held by the wall.
:::
:::

Bacteria are cells too, but a bacterial cell has no nucleus: its DNA lies loose in the cytoplasm. That is why bacteria are called prokaryotes. Animal, plant and fungal cells have a nucleus and are called eukaryotes. Both kinds are covered in the next chapters.

## Chemistry for biologists

Life is chemistry. Every process in this course, from digesting a meal to copying DNA, is a chemical reaction between particles too small to see. You need a short list of chemical ideas and no more.

### Atoms, elements and molecules

:::def Atom
The smallest part of an element that can exist. An atom has a nucleus (containing positive protons and neutral neutrons) surrounded by negative electrons.
:::

:::def Element
A substance made of only one kind of atom, for example carbon, oxygen or hydrogen.
:::

:::def Molecule
Two or more atoms joined together by covalent bonds, for example oxygen \(\ce{O2}\), water \(\ce{H2O}\) and glucose \(\ce{C6H12O6}\).
:::

:::def Compound
A substance made of two or more different elements chemically joined, for example water, carbon dioxide and sodium chloride.
:::

Each element has a symbol of one or two letters. Six elements make up most of every living thing:

| Element | Symbol | Where you find it |
|---|---|---|
| Carbon | C | the backbone of every organic molecule |
| Hydrogen | H | in all organic molecules and in water |
| Oxygen | O | in all organic molecules, in water and in the oxygen we breathe |
| Nitrogen | N | in proteins and in nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) |
| Phosphorus | P | in nucleic acids, ATP and phospholipids (as phosphate) |
| Sulfur | S | in a few amino acids, so in some proteins |

A formula tells you which atoms are in a molecule, and how many. Glucose, \(\ce{C6H12O6}\), has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 6 oxygen atoms: 24 atoms in all. A number in front is the number of molecules: \(\ce{6O2}\) means six molecules of oxygen.

### Bonds hold atoms together

Atoms join by bonds, which involve their outer electrons.

:::def Covalent bond
A bond in which two atoms share a pair of electrons. Covalent bonds are strong, and they hold the atoms inside a molecule together.
:::

In water, an oxygen atom shares one pair of electrons with each of two hydrogen atoms. In carbon dioxide, a carbon atom shares two pairs with each oxygen atom. The sugars, fats, proteins and DNA of living things are all held together by covalent bonds.

A second kind of bond forms between **ions**.

:::def Ion
An atom, or group of atoms, that has gained or lost electrons and so carries an electric charge. Losing electrons gives a positive ion, for example \(\ce{Na+}\); gaining them gives a negative ion, for example \(\ce{Cl-}\).
:::

:::def Ionic bond
The attraction between a positive ion and a negative ion. In sodium chloride, a sodium atom gives one electron to a chlorine atom, forming \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\), and the two oppositely charged ions attract.
:::

Cells use ions all the time: \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{K+}\) in nerve cells, \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) in muscle, \(\ce{H+}\) in respiration and in pH, \(\ce{HCO3-}\) in blood. For more on bonding, see [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding) in the chemistry course.

### Water, polarity and the hydrogen bond

Water is the most important molecule in biology, because every cell is about two-thirds water and every reaction in it takes place in water. Its properties come from the way its electrons are shared.

In a water molecule the shared electrons are pulled closer to the oxygen atom than to the two hydrogen atoms. The oxygen end of the molecule is therefore slightly negative (written \(\delta-\), "delta minus") and each hydrogen is slightly positive (\(\delta+\)). A molecule with charged ends like this is **polar**.

:::def Polar molecule
A molecule in which the electrons are shared unequally, so that one end is slightly negative and the other slightly positive.
:::

:::def Hydrogen bond
A weak attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen atom in one molecule and a slightly negative atom (usually oxygen or nitrogen) in another molecule, or in another part of a large molecule.
:::

A single hydrogen bond is weak, but there are huge numbers of them, so together they are strong. Hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together, help hold a protein in its folded shape, and join the two strands of DNA. You will see them again and again.

Polarity also decides what dissolves in water. Ions and polar molecules, such as salt and glucose, are attracted by water's charged ends and dissolve: they are **hydrophilic** ("water-loving"). Non-polar molecules, such as fats and oils, have no charged ends and do not dissolve: they are **hydrophobic** ("water-hating"). A solution is a **solute** dissolved in a **solvent**; in the cell the solvent is water.

:::warn A hydrogen bond is not a bond inside a hydrogen molecule
Do not confuse a *hydrogen bond* (weak, between molecules or between parts of a molecule) with a *covalent bond* (strong, between the atoms inside a molecule). "Hydrogen bonds hold the atoms of water together" is wrong: covalent bonds hold the hydrogen and oxygen atoms together; hydrogen bonds hold *different* water molecules to each other.
:::

### Acids, alkalis and pH

:::def Acid
A substance that releases hydrogen ions \(\ce{H+}\) when it dissolves in water.
:::

:::def Alkali
A substance that releases hydroxide ions \(\ce{OH-}\) when it dissolves in water. (It neutralises an acid.)
:::

:::def pH
A scale from 0 to 14 that measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is. The lower the pH, the higher the concentration of hydrogen ions.
:::

- **pH 7** is neutral (pure water).
- **Below 7** is acidic: stomach contents are about pH 2.
- **Above 7** is alkaline: blood is slightly alkaline, about pH 7.4.

The scale is **logarithmic**. A solution of pH 3 has ten times more hydrogen ions than a solution of pH 4, and one hundred times more than a solution of pH 5. Each step of 1 on the scale is a tenfold change. You can measure pH with universal indicator (which changes colour with pH) or with a pH meter.

pH matters in biology because hydrogen ions change the charge on parts of protein molecules, so most enzymes work only in a narrow pH range. You meet this in [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes). For more on pH, see [Acids and bases](#chem.acids).

### Catalysts

:::def Catalyst
A substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by the reaction.
:::

Chemical reactions in cells would be far too slow at body temperature, so cells use catalysts made of protein, called **enzymes**. Because a catalyst is not used up, a very small amount can speed up a huge number of reactions. Enzymes are the subject of [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes).

:::eg Spot the error: describe what a catalyst does. [2]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: A catalyst speeds up a reaction.
Line 2: It is used up in the reaction, so it has to be replaced.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: a catalyst is not used up or permanently changed, which is why a small amount works again and again. That mark is lost [B0].**

Correct Line 2: "It is not used up in the reaction, so it can be used again." With that change the answer scores 2 marks.
:::

## Big molecules from small ones

Living things are made largely of four groups of large molecules. All are built the same way: many small molecules are joined end to end.

:::def Monomer
A small molecule that can be joined to many identical or similar molecules to form a polymer.
:::

:::def Polymer
A large molecule made of many monomers joined together by covalent bonds.
:::

:::analogy Beads on a string
A monomer is a bead. A polymer is a necklace: hundreds of beads clipped together in a chain. You can make a necklace by clipping beads on one at a time, and take it apart by unclipping them one at a time. Cells do both, all the time.
:::

The two reactions are opposites.

:::def Condensation reaction
A reaction in which two molecules are joined by a covalent bond and a molecule of water is removed.
:::

:::def Hydrolysis reaction
A reaction in which a molecule of water is used to break a covalent bond, splitting one molecule into two.
:::

:::key Condensation and hydrolysis
- **Condensation**: monomer + monomer \(\rightarrow\) bigger molecule **+ water**. Building up. Water is a product.
- **Hydrolysis**: bigger molecule **+ water** \(\rightarrow\) monomer + monomer. Breaking down. Water is a reactant.
- The word *lysis* means splitting, and *hydro* means water: splitting using water.
:::

Four groups of large molecules make up most of an organism. You study each one in its own chapter.

| Group | Elements | Built from | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | C, H, O | sugars (monosaccharides) | glucose, starch, cellulose |
| **Lipids** | C, H, O | fatty acids and glycerol | fats, oils |
| **Proteins** | C, H, O, N (often S) | amino acids | enzymes, haemoglobin, collagen |
| **Nucleic acids** | C, H, O, N, P | nucleotides | DNA, RNA |

Starch is a polymer of glucose, a protein is a polymer of amino acids, and DNA is a polymer of nucleotides. Lipids are large molecules too, built by condensation from fatty acids and glycerol, but they are not true polymers because they do not form a long chain of repeating units. The details come in [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs), [Lipids and water](#bio.lipids), [Proteins](#bio.proteins) and [Nucleic acids and DNA replication](#bio.dna).

**(a)** Water is **produced** while a larger molecule is built: **condensation**.

**(b)** Water is **added** and a large molecule is split: **hydrolysis**. (This is what amylase does in your mouth.)

**(c)** Three molecules of water are **produced** as a larger molecule is built: **condensation**.
:::

## Energy for life

Living things need a constant supply of energy to move, grow, make new molecules, keep warm and send nerve impulses. Energy cannot be made or destroyed, only transferred from one store to another. Two processes matter most.

:::def Photosynthesis
The process in which plants, algae and some bacteria use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, releasing oxygen. Light energy is stored as chemical energy in glucose.
:::

\[\ce{6CO2 + 6H2O ->[\text{light}] C6H12O6 + 6O2}\]

:::def Respiration
The chemical reactions in all living cells that release energy from nutrient molecules such as glucose. In aerobic respiration, glucose reacts with oxygen to give carbon dioxide and water.
:::

\[\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}\]

Photosynthesis happens in the chloroplasts of plant cells and stores energy. Respiration happens in every living cell (aerobic respiration in the mitochondria) and releases it. Notice that the two equations are almost mirror images, so the oxygen that photosynthesis releases is used in respiration, and the carbon dioxide from respiration is used in photosynthesis.

The energy released in respiration is not used directly. It is used to make a small molecule called **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate), which carries energy to where it is needed in the cell, for example for muscle contraction, active transport and making large molecules. ATP, and how respiration makes it, are the topic of [Energy, ATP and respiratory quotient](#bio.energy). A great deal of the energy is also released as heat, which is why you feel warm.

:::cols
:::col Photosynthesis
- Only in cells with chloroplasts (plants, algae).
- Uses light energy, carbon dioxide and water.
- Makes glucose and oxygen.
- **Stores** energy in chemical bonds.
:::
:::col Respiration
- In all living cells, plant and animal, all the time.
- Uses glucose and oxygen (aerobic).
- Makes carbon dioxide and water, and ATP.
- **Releases** energy for the cell to use.
:::
:::

:::warn Plants respire too
A common mistake is to say "plants photosynthesise and animals respire". Plants do both. In the light they photosynthesise faster than they respire; in the dark they only respire. Every living cell respires.
:::

## Diffusion and why cells need exchange

Every cell needs to take in substances such as oxygen and glucose, and to get rid of waste such as carbon dioxide. The simplest way for particles to move is by diffusion.

:::def Diffusion
The net movement of particles (molecules or ions) from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down a concentration gradient, as a result of their random movement.
:::

Particles in a liquid or gas are always moving at random. In a region where there are many of them, more move out than move in, simply because there are more to leave. The result is a net movement from high to low concentration, until the particles are spread evenly. Diffusion is **passive**: it does not use energy from respiration. It is quicker when:
- the **concentration gradient** is steeper (a bigger difference between the two regions),
- the **distance** is shorter,
- the **surface area** is larger, and
- the **temperature** is higher (particles have more kinetic energy and move faster).

A single-celled organism such as an amoeba takes in oxygen by diffusion across its cell surface membrane: oxygen is used up inside the cell, so its concentration is lower there than outside, and oxygen diffuses in down the gradient. Carbon dioxide diffuses out the same way.

### The surface area to volume ratio

The amount of oxygen a cell needs depends on how much living material it has: its **volume**. The amount of oxygen it can take in depends on its **surface area**. Compare cubes as simple models of cells.

:::def Surface area to volume ratio
The surface area of an object divided by its volume. A small object has a larger ratio than a large object of the same shape.
:::

:::eg Calculate the surface area to volume ratio of cubes with sides of 2 cm, 4 cm and 8 cm, and say what happens as the cube gets larger.
A cube of side \(L\) has six square faces, so its surface area is \(6L^2\) and its volume is \(L^3\).

In the table, \(L\) is the side, SA the surface area and V the volume.

| L / cm | SA / cm² | V / cm³ | SA : V |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 24 | 8 | 3 : 1 |
| 4 | 96 | 64 | 1.5 : 1 |
| 8 | 384 | 512 | 0.75 : 1 |

Check the first row: \(6 \times 2 \times 2 = 24\,\mathrm{cm^2}\), \(2 \times 2 \times 2 = 8\,\mathrm{cm^3}\), and \(24 \div 8 = 3\), written 3 : 1.

Doubling the side makes the volume eight times larger but the surface area only four times larger, so the ratio halves. **As an object gets larger, its surface area to volume ratio falls.**
:::

This is why very large organisms cannot rely on diffusion across their body surface alone. The ratio becomes too small, and the distance from the surface to the centre becomes too long. Large organisms have **exchange surfaces** (lungs, gills, root hairs, villi) that give a huge surface area, and **transport systems** (blood, xylem and phloem) that carry substances over long distances. Those are the topics of [The gas exchange system](#bio.gasexchange), [Plant tissues and water transport](#bio.planttransport) and [Blood vessels, blood and tissue fluid](#bio.mammaltransport).

:::warn Diffusion is not "the diffusion of water"
When water moves across a membrane because of differences in solute concentration, the process has its own name: **osmosis**. You will learn it properly, with water potential, in [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement). Until then, do not write "diffusion of water" in an exam answer, and do not say that particles "want" to move.
:::

## Units and numbers

Biologists measure things from the size of an atom to the size of a forest, so the numbers are very large and very small. This section gives you the tools to handle them.

### Sizes and SI prefixes

The standard unit of length is the metre (m). A **prefix** in front of a unit makes it a larger or smaller unit.

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor |
|---|---|---|
| giga | G | \(10^{9}\) (1 000 000 000) |
| mega | M | \(10^{6}\) (1 000 000) |
| kilo | k | \(10^{3}\) (1 000) |
| milli | m | \(10^{-3}\) (one thousandth) |
| micro | µ | \(10^{-6}\) (one millionth) |
| nano | n | \(10^{-9}\) (one billionth) |

For cells and microscopes you use three lengths, each **1000 times smaller** than the one before:

:::key The length ladder
\[1\,\mathrm{mm} = 1000\,\mathrm{\mu m} \qquad 1\,\mathrm{\mu m} = 1000\,\mathrm{nm} \qquad 1\,\mathrm{mm} = 1\,000\,000\,\mathrm{nm}\]
- Going to a **smaller** unit (mm \(\rightarrow\) µm \(\rightarrow\) nm): the number gets bigger, so **multiply by 1000** for each step.
- Going to a **larger** unit (nm \(\rightarrow\) µm \(\rightarrow\) mm): the number gets smaller, so **divide by 1000** for each step.
:::

:::warn µm and mm differ by 1000, not 100
The µ in µm is a Greek letter "mu", not an "m". A micrometre is 1000 times smaller than a millimetre. The centimetre (1 cm = 10 mm) is not on this ladder, so do not mix up the factors 10, 100 and 1000. A quick check: a cell is a few tens of micrometres wide, which is a few hundredths of a millimetre. If your answer says a cell is 20 mm across, it would be the size of your thumbnail: wrong.
:::

:::eg Convert 0.025 mm to µm, and then to nm.
Going from mm to µm is going to a smaller unit, so multiply by 1000:

\[\begin{aligned} 0.025\,\mathrm{mm} &= 0.025 \times 1000\,\mathrm{\mu m} \\ &= 25\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

Going from µm to nm, multiply by 1000 again:

\[\begin{aligned} 25\,\mathrm{\mu m} &= 25 \times 1000\,\mathrm{nm} \\ &= 25\,000\,\mathrm{nm} \end{aligned}\]

Sense check: 25 µm is the size of a typical cell, and 0.025 mm is a small fraction of a millimetre. Both fit.
:::

### Standard form

Writing 0.000 000 12 m or 4 500 000 000 is slow and easy to get wrong. **Standard form** writes any number as \(A \times 10^{n}\), where \(A\) is at least 1 and less than 10, and \(n\) is a whole number (positive or negative).

:::key Standard form
- Move the decimal point until you have one non-zero digit in front of it. The number of places you moved it is \(n\).
- Moved the point **left** (a big number): \(n\) is **positive**. \(4\,500\,000 = 4.5 \times 10^{6}\).
- Moved the point **right** (a small number): \(n\) is **negative**. \(0.000\,003 = 3 \times 10^{-6}\).
- On a calculator use the key marked EXP or \(\times 10^{x}\); \(4.5 \times 10^{6}\) is entered as 4.5 EXP 6.
:::

Biologists use standard form for rates (for example \(4.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s^{-1}}\)), for very small sizes and for large populations. To convert between units in standard form, use the prefix table: \(1\,\mathrm{nm} = 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{m}\).

:::eg A virus is \(1.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}\) wide. Give its width in nm, and in µm.
\(1\,\mathrm{m} = 10^{9}\,\mathrm{nm}\), so multiply by \(10^{9}\):

\[\begin{aligned} 1.2 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m} &= 1.2 \times 10^{-7} \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{nm} \\ &= 1.2 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{nm} \\ &= 120\,\mathrm{nm} \end{aligned}\]

To change nm to µm, divide by 1000: \(120 \div 1000 = 0.12\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).

The answer 120 nm lies in the virus range on the size ladder (20–300 nm), which confirms it.
:::

### Significant figures

The **significant figures** of a number are its digits, counted from the first non-zero digit. So 0.004 071 has four significant figures (4, 0, 7, 1: the zeros after the first non-zero digit count, the zeros before it do not). To round to \(k\) significant figures, keep the first \(k\) digits and round using the next one.

- 0.004 071 to 2 significant figures is 0.0041.
- 7.396 to 3 significant figures is 7.40. (The final 0 is kept: it shows the precision.)
- 15 960 to 3 significant figures is 16 000, or better \(1.60 \times 10^{4}\).

The syllabus rule: **give a calculated answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data you used, or one more.** If you divide 25.0 cm³ (3 s.f.) by 12 s (2 s.f.), give the answer to 2 or 3 s.f.: 2.1 or 2.08. Never copy all the digits on the calculator display. Round only at the end, and do not use a rounded intermediate value in a later step.

### Percentages and percentage change

A **percentage** is a number out of 100. To find a percentage of a whole, divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100: 18 out of 60 is \(18 \div 60 \times 100 = 30\%\).

:::def Percentage change
The change in a quantity, expressed as a percentage of its original value. It is positive for an increase and negative for a decrease.
:::

\[\text{percentage change} = \frac{\text{new value} - \text{original value}}{\text{original value}} \times 100\]

An increase gives a positive answer and a decrease a negative one. Always divide by the **original** value (the starting value), not the new value.

:::eg Cylinders of potato of mass 2.50 g each were left for 30 minutes in sugar solutions of different concentration. The table shows their final masses. Calculate the percentage change in mass at each concentration, then describe and explain the results.
| sugar / mol dm⁻³ | mass / g |
|---|---|
| 0.0 | 2.95 |
| 0.2 | 2.70 |
| 0.4 | 2.50 |
| 0.6 | 2.30 |
| 0.8 | 2.15 |

**Percentage change**, with the original mass 2.50 g. For 0.0 mol dm⁻³:

\[\begin{aligned} \text{percentage change} &= \frac{2.95 - 2.50}{2.50} \times 100 \\ &= +18\% \end{aligned}\]

The method is change in mass divided by original mass, times 100. [M1] The value at 0.0 mol dm⁻³ is +18%. [A1]

The others are +8% at 0.2, 0% at 0.4, −8% at 0.6 and −14% at 0.8 mol dm⁻³. [A1]

**Describe**, with figures. As the sugar concentration increases from 0.0 to 0.8 mol dm⁻³, the mass of the cylinders changes from an increase of 18% to a decrease of 14%. [B1]

The mass does not change at 0.4 mol dm⁻³. [B1]

**Explain.** At low concentrations the solution outside is more dilute than the contents of the potato cells, so water moves into the cells and the mass increases. [B1]

At 0.4 mol dm⁻³ the concentrations are equal, so there is no net movement of water and the mass stays the same. [B1]

At higher concentrations the solution outside is more concentrated than the cell contents, so water moves out of the cells and the mass decreases. [B1]

(Also credit: the cylinders are surrounded by a partially permeable membrane; the movement of water is osmosis; the more different the concentrations, the bigger the change.)

A weaker answer says "the potato gets heavier then lighter". It quotes no figures, gives no turning point and does not mention water moving. It scores one mark at most. Osmosis is taught properly in [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement).
:::

:::eg Spot the error: a leaf disc had a mass of 4.00 g at the start and 3.40 g at the end. Calculate the percentage change in mass. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Change in mass = 3.40 − 4.00 = −0.60 g.
Line 2: Percentage change = −0.60 ÷ 3.40 × 100.
Line 3: = −17.6%.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: the change is divided by the new mass (3.40 g) but must be divided by the original mass (4.00 g). The method mark is lost [M0], and so the answer mark is lost too [A0].** Line 3 is the correct calculation of the wrong expression.

Correct Line 2: −0.60 ÷ 4.00 × 100 = −15%. With that change the answer scores 3 marks.
:::

### Averages

The **mean** is the sum of the values divided by the number of values. Biologists repeat measurements and use the mean to reduce the effect of chance errors. If one result is far from the others and does not fit the pattern, it is **anomalous**: leave it out of the mean, and say that you have done so. For example, three repeats of 12.1, 12.4 and 11.8 s have the mean \((12.1 + 12.4 + 11.8) \div 3 = 12.1\) s.

### Reading and using graphs

Most data questions give a graph. Read it in this order.

:::key Reading a graph
1. **Axes**: what is on each axis, and in what units? Cambridge writes them as *quantity / unit*, for example *time / s*. The independent variable (the one you change) goes on the x-axis; the dependent variable (the one you measure) goes on the y-axis.
2. **Scale**: how much does one small square or one gridline represent? Work it out before you read a value.
3. **Read values** by ruling a line up or across to the curve and then to the axis. Quote the number with its unit.
4. **Describe the shape**: is it rising, falling, flat, or does it peak? Where are the turning points? Use figures from the graph.
5. **Gradient** gives a rate: gradient \(= \dfrac{\text{change in } y}{\text{change in } x}\), with units "y unit per x unit".
:::

On a straight-line graph the gradient is the same everywhere: choose two points far apart on the line, not two points from a table that lie off it.

:::plot Gas released by a culture of yeast. The line is straight, so the rate is constant: the gradient is 0.6 cm³ s⁻¹.
--f "0.6*x" --label "gas collected" --x 0 60 --y 0 50 --xlabel "time / s" --ylabel "volume of gas / cm³" --pt "50,30,30 cm³ at 50 s@nw" --pt "20,12,12 cm³ at 20 s@se" --legend tr --alt "A straight line through the origin rising to 36 cm³ at 60 s. It passes through 12 cm³ at 20 s and 30 cm³ at 50 s."
:::

:::eg Use the graph to calculate the rate at which yeast released gas.
Choose two points on the line, far apart: \((20\,\mathrm{s},\,12\,\mathrm{cm^3})\) and \((50\,\mathrm{s},\,30\,\mathrm{cm^3})\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{gradient} &= \frac{30 - 12}{50 - 20} \\ &= \frac{18}{30} \\ &= 0.60\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}} \end{aligned}\]

The rate is 0.60 cm³ of gas per second. The unit follows from the axes: cm³ divided by s is cm³ s⁻¹.
:::

If the graph is a **curve**, the rate changes, and the gradient at a point is the gradient of a **tangent**: a straight line that touches the curve at that point and follows its slope. The rate at time zero, the **initial rate**, is the gradient of the tangent at \(t = 0\).

:::plot Product formed in a reaction. The curve flattens as the reactant is used up. The tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate, 0.8 cm³ s⁻¹.
--f "20*(1-exp(0-x/25))" --f "0.8*x+0*sqrt(32-x)" --label "volume of product" --label "tangent at t = 0" --x 0 100 --y 0 30 --xlabel "time / s" --ylabel "volume / cm³" --pt "25,20,tangent reaches 20 cm³ at 25 s@e" --legend tr --alt "The volume of product rises steeply at first and levels off at 20 cm³. A straight tangent drawn at time zero passes through 20 cm³ at 25 s."
:::

From the tangent, the initial rate is \(20 \div 25 = 0.8\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}\). A **mean rate** over a period is simpler: volume formed divided by time taken. You use both in [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes), and the maths of graphs is developed in [Maths skills for biology](#bio.maths).

## How biology is examined: command words

Every exam question begins with a **command word** that tells you what to do. Biology marks are given for creditworthy points, and the command word decides which points count. Four command words matter most.

| Command word | It means | A good answer |
|---|---|---|
| **State** | give a short fact, no explanation | one word or one short sentence |
| **Describe** | say what you see or what happens | the trend or sequence, with figures and units from any graph or table |
| **Explain** | give the biological reasons | cause, then effect, using key terms: "because … so …" |
| **Suggest** | apply your knowledge to a situation you may not have met | a sensible idea based on the biology you know, linked to the data |

Other command words you will meet, all explained in [Papers 2 and 4: structured questions](#bio.exam.p24), include calculate, compare, define, give, identify, name, outline and predict.

Here is one scenario with a question for each of the four words. A drop of dye is placed in a beaker of water. In warm water it spreads faster than in cold water.

- **State** one factor that increases the rate of diffusion. Answer: a higher temperature.
- **Describe** how the dye spreads. Answer: the dye moves from where it is concentrated into the rest of the water until it is evenly spread.
- **Explain** why the dye spreads faster in warm water. Answer: the particles have more kinetic energy, so they move faster and spread out more quickly.
- **Suggest** why the dye spreads more slowly through a thick jelly than through water. Answer: the dye particles keep colliding with the jelly's molecules, so they cannot move so freely. (You have not been told this: you use your knowledge of random movement to propose it.)

:::exam Marks tell you how many points
A question worth 3 marks needs three separate points. Count the marks before you write. For "describe", quote figures with units. For "explain", write a chain: "so", "because", "which means". For "describe and explain", describe first, then explain, in the same order as the data.
:::

:::exam Units, every time
Write the unit with every number you give. A number without its unit loses a mark in calculations, even when the number is right. Check that your unit matches the question: if the table is in mm and the question asks for µm, convert.
:::

A: (a) Surface area \(= 6 \times 3 \times 3 = 54\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) [M1], volume \(= 27\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) [M1], ratio \(= 2 : 1\) [A1]
(b) \(27 \times 6 = 162\,\mathrm{cm^2}\) [B1]
The ratio has increased (from 2 : 1 to 6 : 1, three times larger). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A culture of yeast in sugar solution released carbon dioxide. The table shows the total volume of gas collected.

| time / s | gas / cm³ |
|---|---|
| 0 | 0 |
| 20 | 14 |
| 40 | 24 |
| 60 | 30 |
| 80 | 34 |
| 100 | 36 |

(a) Calculate the mean rate of gas production in the first 20 s. (b) Calculate the mean rate between 60 s and 100 s. (c) Describe how the rate changes during the experiment and suggest an explanation.
H: Rate is volume divided by time, with units.
H: For (c) think about what is happening to the amount of sugar.
A: (a) \(14 \div 20\) [M1] \(= 0.70\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}\) [A1]
(b) \((36 - 30) \div (100 - 60) = 6 \div 40\) [M1] \(= 0.15\,\mathrm{cm^3\,s^{-1}}\) [A1]
(c) The rate decreases, from 0.70 to 0.15 cm³ s⁻¹. [B1]
The sugar is used up, so there is less of it for the yeast to use and the reaction slows. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ Plants grown in a nutrient solution that contained every element they need except nitrogen grew very little. (a) Name two groups of biological molecules that contain nitrogen. (b) Suggest why the plants could not grow without nitrogen. (c) Name the element that is present in the phosphate groups of DNA.
H: Which molecules do cells need to build new cells?
A: (a) Proteins [B1] and nucleic acids (DNA and RNA). [B1]
(b) Without nitrogen the plant cannot make amino acids and proteins, which are needed for enzymes and for the structure of new cells. [B1]
It cannot make nucleotides and DNA either, so cells cannot copy their DNA and divide. [B1]
(c) Phosphorus [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A small animal is shaped like a cube. Oxygen diffuses in over its whole surface at 1.0 unit per cm² per minute. Each cm³ of the animal uses 3.0 units of oxygen per minute. (a) For cubes of side 1 cm, 2 cm and 3 cm, calculate the oxygen supplied and the oxygen needed per minute. (b) State the largest cube that can get all its oxygen by diffusion alone, and give the reason. (c) Suggest two features that allow a larger animal to survive.
H: Supply depends on the surface area, and demand depends on the volume.
H: A cube of side \(L\) has surface area \(6L^2\) and volume \(L^3\).
A: (a) Supply \(= 6L^2 \times 1.0\) and need \(= L^3 \times 3.0\) [M1]
1 cm: supply 6, need 3. 2 cm: supply 24, need 24. 3 cm: supply 54, need 81 (units per minute). [A1]
(b) The 2 cm cube. [B1]
At 2 cm supply equals need, but at 3 cm the supply (54) is less than the need (81). [B1]
(c) A larger animal could have a flattened or branched shape (or an exchange surface such as gills or lungs) to increase the surface area. [B1]
It could have a transport system (blood) to carry oxygen from the exchange surface to all its cells. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ A seedling had a mass of 8.00 g. Its mass rose by 25% in week 1 and then fell by 20% in week 2. A student says: "25% up and 20% down is a net gain of 5%." (a) Calculate the mass at the end of week 2. (b) Explain the student's mistake. (c) State the true net percentage change.
H: The 20% is taken of a different starting mass from the 25%.
A: (a) After week 1: \(8.00 \times 1.25 = 10.00\,\mathrm{g}\) [M1]. After week 2: \(10.00 \times 0.80 = 8.00\,\mathrm{g}\). [A1]
(b) The two percentages are of different masses: 25% of 8.00 g is a gain of 2.00 g, but 20% of 10.00 g is a loss of 2.00 g, so the changes cancel and they cannot be added together. [B1]
(c) The net change is 0%. [B1]
:::

:::quiz
? Which list shows structures in order of size, smallest first?
- bacterium, virus, protein molecule, animal cell
+ protein molecule, virus, bacterium, animal cell
- virus, protein molecule, animal cell, bacterium
- animal cell, bacterium, virus, protein molecule
> A protein molecule is a few nanometres across, a virus tens to hundreds of nanometres, a bacterium 1–5 µm and an animal cell tens of µm. Many students put the virus above the bacterium because both are "germs".
? A cell is 25 µm wide. What is this in millimetres?
+ 0.025 mm
- 0.25 mm
- 2.5 mm
- 25 000 mm
> 1 mm = 1000 µm, so divide by 1000: 25 ÷ 1000 = 0.025 mm. Dividing by 100 (0.25 mm) is the cm and mm mistake; 25 000 would be the value in nm.
? Starch is broken down into maltose by adding water. Which reaction is this?
+ hydrolysis
- condensation
- diffusion
- photosynthesis
> Water is a reactant that splits a large molecule: hydrolysis. In condensation water is a product, as large molecules are built.
? What is a hydrogen bond?
+ a weak attraction between a slightly positive hydrogen atom in one molecule and a slightly negative atom in another
- a strong covalent bond between two hydrogen atoms
- the bond that holds hydrogen and oxygen atoms together inside a water molecule
- the attraction between a hydrogen ion and a hydroxide ion
> The atoms inside a water molecule are held by covalent bonds. Hydrogen bonds act between different water molecules (or between parts of a large molecule) and are weak individually.
? Solution X has pH 3 and solution Y has pH 5. Which statement is correct?
+ X has 100 times more hydrogen ions than Y.
- X has 2 times more hydrogen ions than Y.
- Y has 100 times more hydrogen ions than X.
- X is alkaline and Y is acidic.
> The pH scale is logarithmic: each unit is a tenfold change, and a lower pH means more hydrogen ions. Two units is 10 × 10 = 100. Both solutions are acidic because both are below 7.
? A mass changes from 20.0 g to 15.0 g. What is the percentage change?
+ −25%
- −33%
- +25%
- −5%
> The change is −5.0 g, divided by the original 20.0 g: −25%. Dividing by the new mass (15.0 g) gives −33%, which is the classic error. Dropping the minus sign loses the direction of the change.
? Which statement about a catalyst is correct?
+ It is not used up in the reaction.
- It is used up and must be replaced.
- It supplies energy to the reactants.
- It changes the products of the reaction.
> A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction without being used up or permanently changed.
? Which statement about diffusion is correct?
+ It is the net movement of particles down a concentration gradient and uses no energy from respiration.
- It is the movement of particles up a concentration gradient, using ATP.
- It is the movement of water through a partially permeable membrane only.
- It stops once particles have reached equal concentration, because they stop moving.
> Diffusion is passive and down the gradient. At equal concentration the particles still move randomly, but there is no net movement. The movement of water has its own name, osmosis.
? What is 0.000 35 in standard form?
+ \(3.5 \times 10^{-4}\)
- \(3.5 \times 10^{4}\)
- \(35 \times 10^{-5}\)
- \(3.5 \times 10^{-3}\)
> The decimal point moves 4 places to the right, so the power is −4. \(35 \times 10^{-5}\) is correct in value but not in standard form, because the first number must be between 1 and 10.
? In an exam, "explain" means:
+ give the biological reasons for the observation
- give a short fact with no reasons
- list the figures from a graph
- predict what will happen next
> "State" asks for a short fact, "describe" for what the data show, and "predict" for what will happen. "Explain" needs the cause, usually with "because" and key terms.

:::recap
- Seven characteristics of life: movement, respiration, sensitivity, growth, reproduction, excretion, nutrition.
- Levels: molecule, organelle, cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism, population, community, ecosystem.
- Plant cells have a cell wall, a large permanent vacuole and (in green parts) chloroplasts; animal cells do not. Bacteria have no nucleus.
- Six elements: C, H, O, N, P, S. Covalent bond: shared electron pair (strong). Ionic bond: attraction between oppositely charged ions. Hydrogen bond: weak attraction between δ+ H and a δ− atom of another molecule or part.
- Water is polar. pH 7 is neutral, below 7 acidic, above 7 alkaline; each unit is a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration. A catalyst speeds up a reaction and is not used up.
- Condensation joins monomers and makes water; hydrolysis splits using water. Carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids are the four large-molecule groups.
- Photosynthesis stores light energy in glucose; respiration releases energy to make ATP, in every living cell.
- Diffusion is the net movement of particles down a concentration gradient, and is passive. A larger object has a smaller surface area to volume ratio.
- 1 mm = 1000 µm; 1 µm = 1000 nm. Size order: atom, small molecule, protein, virus, bacterium, animal cell, plant cell.
- Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100. Rate = gradient = change in y ÷ change in x, with units.
- State = short fact; describe = what the data show, with figures; explain = reasons; suggest = apply your knowledge.
:::
