## Why living things need energy

Energy cannot be made or destroyed. Cells do not make energy: they **transfer** the chemical potential energy stored in food molecules, such as glucose, into a form that can drive reactions, and some of it is always lost as heat. That process is **respiration**, and the form of energy it supplies is ATP.

:::def Respiration
The release of energy from organic molecules, such as glucose, by a series of enzyme-catalysed reactions in cells. The energy is used to make ATP.
:::

You need to know three kinds of energy-requiring process.

| Process | Why it needs energy | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Active transport | moves substances **against** a concentration gradient through carrier proteins | sodium ions out of cells; mineral ions into root hairs |
| Movement | muscle proteins slide past each other; cilia beat; vesicles and chromosomes are pulled along protein fibres | contraction of a muscle; beating of cilia in the airway; movement of chromosomes in mitosis |
| Anabolic reactions | building large molecules from small ones joins molecules together and stores energy in new bonds | DNA replication; protein synthesis |

Anabolic reactions deserve a closer look because they are easy to forget. When DNA is copied (see [DNA structure and replication](#bio.dna)), the free nucleotides arrive carrying three phosphate groups, like ATP, and the energy released as two phosphates are removed drives the formation of each phosphodiester bond. In protein synthesis (see [Protein synthesis](#bio.synthesis)), energy from ATP is used to attach each amino acid to its transfer RNA and then to join amino acids together with peptide bonds. Growth, repair and reproduction all depend on anabolic reactions.

:::warn Energy is transferred, not made
Never write that respiration "makes energy" or "produces energy". Write that it **releases** energy from glucose (or other organic molecules) and **transfers** it to ATP. Also, ATP is not the energy source: the food molecule is. ATP is the carrier.
:::

## ATP: the universal energy currency

You met the structure of ATP in [DNA structure and replication](#bio.dna): adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups, a **phosphorylated nucleotide**.

:::def ATP (adenosine triphosphate)
A phosphorylated nucleotide made of adenine, ribose and three phosphate groups. It is the immediate source of energy for most cell processes: when its end phosphate group is removed by hydrolysis, energy is released.
:::

The reaction is a hydrolysis, catalysed by an enzyme, ATP hydrolase:

\[\mathrm{ATP + H_2O \rightarrow ADP + P_i}\]

The standard textbook value for the energy released is about 30.5 kJ per mole of ATP. In the reverse reaction, ADP and a phosphate ion (\(\mathrm{P_i}\), inorganic phosphate) are joined, which needs at least the same amount of energy. That energy comes from respiration. A cell therefore does not store large amounts of ATP: it recycles the same molecules again and again, and an adult human makes and breaks down tens of kilograms of ATP in a day.

Money is a good comparison. You do not pay for a sandwich with a gold bar, because you cannot break off the right amount. You use coins and notes of small value that everyone accepts. ATP is the coin of the cell.

:::analogy ATP is a currency, not a bank
The glucose in a cell is like savings in a bank: a large amount of energy in a form that cannot be spent directly. Respiration changes it into ATP, small coins that every part of the cell accepts. The coins are spent and earned continuously. A cell that tried to pay with glucose would be like a shop that only accepts £500 notes.
:::

### Features of ATP

Each feature in the list below is a mark-scheme point, and each one is linked to a reason, so learn them as pairs.

| Feature of ATP | Why it makes ATP a good energy currency |
|---|---|
| **Releases energy in small, manageable amounts** when the end phosphate bond is hydrolysed (about 30.5 kJ per mole) | the energy matches the needs of one reaction, so little is wasted as heat; a large amount released at once, as from glucose, could not all be used |
| **Hydrolysed in one step**, catalysed by an enzyme, so energy is released **quickly** | the cell can meet a sudden demand, such as the start of muscle contraction |
| **Quickly resynthesised** from ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) | a small supply serves a large demand; energy released in respiration is passed on straight away |
| **Small and soluble in water** | it moves easily through the cytoplasm to wherever energy is needed |
| **The same molecule in all living things and all reactions** (universal) | one molecule links any energy-releasing reaction to any energy-requiring reaction |
| **Cannot pass through the cell surface membrane** (it is charged and large) | it stays inside the cell where it is needed and is not lost |
| **Can transfer a phosphate group** to another molecule | a phosphorylated molecule is more reactive, so ATP can drive reactions that have no obvious link to it |

:::key ATP in one box
- ATP is an energy **carrier** (currency), not a long-term store. Fats and carbohydrates are the stores.
- Hydrolysis of ATP to ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) releases about 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹, in small amounts that suit one reaction.
- ATP is made from ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) using energy from respiration, and used up within a short time.
:::

:::warn "ATP stores lots of energy"
This loses the mark. ATP releases a **small** amount of energy per molecule and the cell holds very little of it at any time. Write "ATP is the immediate source of energy" or "ATP transfers energy". Long-term energy stores are lipids, starch and glycogen.
:::

:::eg Explain why ATP is described as the universal energy currency of cells.
A full-mark answer gives four separate points, each with its reason.

ATP is used in all living organisms and in all kinds of energy-requiring reaction, so one molecule links respiration to every cell process (it is universal). [B1]

Hydrolysis of ATP releases a small, manageable amount of energy (about 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹), so little energy is wasted as heat. [B1]

ATP is hydrolysed in a single step, so energy can be released quickly; and it is quickly resynthesised from ADP and phosphate, so a small supply can meet a large demand. [B1]

ATP is small and soluble, so it moves easily to where it is needed, but it cannot pass out through the cell surface membrane, so it stays in the cell. [B1]

(Also credit: it can transfer a phosphate group to other molecules, making them more reactive.)

A weaker answer says "ATP stores a lot of energy that cells can use". This is wrong (the energy per molecule is small and ATP is not a store) and gives no reason, so it scores nothing.
:::

## Two ways to make ATP

Respiration makes ATP in two different ways. You will see both in detail in [Aerobic respiration](#bio.respiration); here you need to know what each one is.

:::def Substrate-linked phosphorylation
The synthesis of ATP by the direct transfer of a phosphate group from another phosphorylated molecule (a substrate) to ADP, in a reaction catalysed by an enzyme.
:::

In substrate-linked phosphorylation, a phosphorylated intermediate of respiration is converted into a different molecule, and the phosphate that it loses is passed straight to ADP to form ATP. It happens in the cytoplasm (glycolysis) and in the matrix of the mitochondrion (Krebs cycle). It makes only a small share of the ATP from respiration, and it does not need oxygen.

:::def Chemiosmosis
The synthesis of ATP using the energy of a proton gradient across a membrane: protons flow down their gradient through the enzyme ATP synthase, and the energy released is used to join ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\).
:::

Chemiosmosis takes place in the **inner membrane of mitochondria** and in the **membranes of chloroplasts** (the thylakoids, see [Photosynthesis](#bio.photosynthesis)). In both, the energy of electrons passing along a chain of carriers is used to pump protons across a membrane. The protons then flow back through ATP synthase. This makes most of the ATP in aerobic respiration.

| | Substrate-linked phosphorylation | Chemiosmosis |
|---|---|---|
| How ATP is made | phosphate passed from a phosphorylated molecule to ADP | proton gradient drives ATP synthase |
| Where | cytoplasm and mitochondrial matrix | inner mitochondrial membrane; chloroplast membranes |
| Needs a membrane | no | yes |
| Needs oxygen | no | in mitochondria, yes (oxygen is the final electron acceptor) |
| Share of the ATP from aerobic respiration | small | large |

## Energy values of respiratory substrates

Glucose is the substrate that textbooks use, but cells also respire lipids and, when they have to, proteins. These do not release the same energy from each gram.

:::def Energy value (of a respiratory substrate)
The energy released when one gram of the substrate is completely oxidised in respiration, in kJ g⁻¹.
:::

| Substrate | Energy value / kJ g⁻¹ |
|---|---|
| carbohydrate | about 16 |
| lipid | about 39 |
| protein | about 17 |

A gram of lipid releases a little over twice as much energy as a gram of carbohydrate or protein (39 ÷ 16 is about 2.4). The reason is hydrogen. Respiration removes hydrogen atoms from the substrate (dehydrogenation) and passes them to the coenzymes NAD and FAD. These reduced coenzymes deliver the hydrogen to the electron transport chain, where it drives chemiosmosis. **The more hydrogen atoms there are per gram of substrate, the more reduced NAD and reduced FAD form, the more protons are pumped, and the more ATP is made.**

Compare glucose, \(\ce{C6H12O6}\), with tripalmitin, a typical lipid, \(\ce{C51H98O6}\). Using relative atomic masses of C = 12, H = 1 and O = 16, glucose has a relative molecular mass of 180 and tripalmitin 806. Hydrogen makes up \(12 \div 180\), about 6.7%, of the mass of glucose and \(98 \div 806\), about 12%, of the mass of tripalmitin. Lipids also contain very little oxygen, so they have far more oxidising still to do than carbohydrate molecules, which already carry an oxygen atom for every carbon. A gram of tripalmitin needs about 2.7 times as much oxygen to oxidise it as a gram of glucose.

Proteins give about the same energy per gram as carbohydrate. Their amino groups must be removed before the carbon skeleton is respired, and the body uses protein for energy only when there is little carbohydrate or lipid.

:::key Why lipids have the highest energy value
1. A gram of lipid contains **more hydrogen atoms** (more C–H bonds) than a gram of carbohydrate.
2. So more **reduced NAD and reduced FAD** form per gram.
3. So more hydrogen reaches the electron transport chain, more protons are pumped and **more ATP** is made by chemiosmosis.
4. Lipids contain less oxygen, so more oxygen is used per gram.
:::

:::warn Per gram, not per molecule
The energy value is per **gram**. Do not write that lipids release more energy "because they are bigger molecules": a larger molecule of carbohydrate does not release more per gram. The reason is the proportion of hydrogen.
:::

:::eg Compare the energy released when 20 g of carbohydrate and 20 g of lipid are completely respired, and explain the difference.
**Step 1: multiply the mass by the energy value.**

\[\begin{aligned} \text{carbohydrate} &= 20\,\mathrm{g} \times 16\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}} \\ &= 320\,\mathrm{kJ} \\ \text{lipid} &= 20\,\mathrm{g} \times 39\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}} \\ &= 780\,\mathrm{kJ} \end{aligned}\]

**Step 2: compare.** The lipid releases \(780 \div 320 = 2.4\) times as much energy.

**Step 3: explain.** Lipid contains more hydrogen per gram, so more reduced NAD and reduced FAD form, and more ATP is made by oxidative phosphorylation. This is why seeds that must support a seedling for days before it has leaves, and animals that must carry a long-term store, use lipid: the same energy is stored in less than half the mass.
:::

## The respiratory quotient

Respiration takes in oxygen and gives out carbon dioxide. The ratio of the two depends on which substrate is respired, so measuring the gases tells you what an organism is using as fuel.

:::def Respiratory quotient (RQ)
The ratio of the number of molecules of carbon dioxide produced to the number of molecules of oxygen taken in, as a result of respiration.
:::

\[\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{\text{carbon dioxide produced}}{\text{oxygen taken in}}\]

The ratio of molecules is the same as the ratio of volumes of the gases at the same temperature and pressure, so you can use either. RQ has no unit, because both amounts are in the same units and cancel. Carbon dioxide is always on the **top**.

### Calculating RQ from an equation

Write the equation for the complete oxidation of the substrate, balance it, then divide the coefficient of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) by the coefficient of \(\mathrm{O_2}\). For glucose:

\[\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}\]

\[\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{6}{6} = 1.0\]

For any substrate containing only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, \(\ce{C_xH_yO_z}\), the balance is easy to find, because each carbon atom needs one \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to make \(\mathrm{CO_2}\), each four hydrogen atoms need one \(\mathrm{O_2}\) to make water, and the oxygen already in the molecule supplies some of what is needed.

:::formula Balancing complete oxidation and finding RQ
\[\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y\mathrm{O}_z + \left(x + \tfrac{y}{4} - \tfrac{z}{2}\right)\mathrm{O_2} \rightarrow x\,\mathrm{CO_2} + \tfrac{y}{2}\,\mathrm{H_2O}\]

\[\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{x}{x + \frac{y}{4} - \frac{z}{2}}\]

\(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) are the numbers of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen atoms in one molecule of the substrate. You can check any answer by counting oxygen atoms on both sides.
:::

:::eg Calculate the RQ of oleic acid, \(\ce{C18H34O2}\), a fatty acid.
**Step 1: balance the equation.** \(x = 18\), \(y = 34\), \(z = 2\), so the \(\mathrm{O_2}\) needed is \(18 + \tfrac{34}{4} - \tfrac{2}{2} = 18 + 8.5 - 1 = 25.5\). [M1]

\[\ce{C18H34O2 + 25.5O2 -> 18CO2 + 17H2O}\]

Check the oxygen atoms: on the left \(2 + 51 = 53\); on the right \(36 + 17 = 53\). [A1]

**Step 2: divide.**

\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{RQ} &= \frac{18}{25.5} \\ &= 0.71 \end{aligned}\] [A1]

The value is close to 0.7, which is typical of a lipid. A lipid is mostly carbon and hydrogen, so it needs a lot of oxygen for each carbon dioxide produced.
:::

Different substrates give different RQs, and you should learn the typical values:

| Substrate | Typical RQ |
|---|---|
| carbohydrate (glucose) | 1.0 |
| lipid | about 0.7 |
| protein | about 0.9 |
| a mixture, such as a person at rest eating a mixed diet | between 0.7 and 1.0 (about 0.8) |

### Reading an RQ

- **RQ about 1.0**: the substrate is mainly carbohydrate.
- **RQ about 0.7**: the substrate is mainly lipid.
- **RQ about 0.9**: mainly protein, or a mixture of carbohydrate and lipid.
- **RQ above 1.0**: more carbon dioxide is given out than oxygen taken in. Some respiration must be taking place **without oxygen** (anaerobic respiration, see [Anaerobic respiration and investigating respiration](#bio.anaerobic)), or the substrate contains more oxygen than a carbohydrate does, as organic acids do.

:::warn RQ upside down
RQ is carbon dioxide **over** oxygen. If a lipid gives you a value above 1, you have inverted it. Check by asking: lipid needs lots of oxygen, so the oxygen number is the larger one, so RQ is below 1.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: calculate the RQ of lauric acid, \(\ce{C12H24O2}\), a fatty acid. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: \(x = 12\), \(y = 24\), \(z = 2\), so \(\mathrm{O_2}\) needed \(= 12 + 6 - 1 = 17\), and \(\ce{C12H24O2 + 17O2 -> 12CO2 + 12H2O}\).
Line 2: RQ \(=\) oxygen taken in \(\div\) carbon dioxide produced \(= 17 \div 12\).
Line 3: RQ \(= 1.4\).

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: RQ is carbon dioxide produced divided by oxygen taken in, not the other way round. The method mark is lost [M0], and so the answer mark cannot be given [A0].** Line 3 is the correct value of the wrong ratio.

Correct Line 2: RQ \(= 12 \div 17 = 0.71\). That gives 3 marks, and shows the lipid value of about 0.7.
:::

The idea is to measure the volume of oxygen taken in on its own, and then the **net** change in volume with the carbon dioxide left in.

1. **With a carbon dioxide absorber** (potassium hydroxide solution or soda lime): the carbon dioxide produced is absorbed, so only the loss of oxygen changes the volume. The fluid moves towards the tube by a distance \(d_1\). The volume of oxygen taken in equals the volume of the capillary that the fluid has swept through.
2. **Without the absorber** (water in its place): oxygen taken in lowers the volume and carbon dioxide produced raises it. The net movement, \(d_2\), shows (oxygen taken in) minus (carbon dioxide produced). If the fluid moves towards the tube, more oxygen was used than carbon dioxide made.

:::lab Measuring the RQ of germinating seeds or small invertebrates
**Aim**: find the respiratory quotient of germinating seeds (or of small invertebrates such as blowfly larvae) from the volumes of oxygen taken in and carbon dioxide produced.

:::steps
1. Set up two identical respirometers in the same water bath. In the first, put potassium hydroxide solution in the bottom of the tube; in the second, put the same volume of water.
2. Put the same mass of the organisms on the gauze platform in each tube. Do not let the seeds touch the potassium hydroxide. Set up a third tube as a control, with glass beads of the same volume as the organisms (or boiled, dead seeds of the same mass).
3. Fit the bungs and capillary tubes, but leave the open end of each capillary open to the air for 10 minutes so the temperature settles.
4. Introduce a drop of coloured fluid into each capillary, close the system and start the stopwatch.
5. Read the position of the drop on the millimetre scale every minute for 10 minutes. Calculate the distance moved (\(d_1\) with potassium hydroxide, \(d_2\) without).
6. Repeat three times and calculate mean distances.
:::

**Calculations.** The capillary is a cylinder of radius \(r\), so a movement of distance \(d\) is a volume \(\pi r^2 d\). Use the same unit for \(r\) and \(d\), and mm³ comes out for a radius in mm and a distance in mm.

:::formula Respirometer calculations
\[\text{volume of oxygen taken in} = \pi r^2 d_1\]

\[\text{volume of carbon dioxide produced} = \pi r^2 (d_1 - d_2)\]

\[\mathrm{RQ} = \frac{d_1 - d_2}{d_1}\]

\(d_1\) is the movement towards the tube with the absorber, \(d_2\) the net movement towards the tube without it (use a negative \(d_2\) if the fluid moves away from the tube), \(r\) the radius of the capillary. Because \(\pi r^2\) is the same on the top and the bottom, it cancels in the RQ.
:::

**Variables**: the independent variable is the organism (or its stage, or the day of germination); the dependent variables are \(d_1\) and \(d_2\). Standardise the mass (or volume) of the organisms, the temperature (water bath), the volume and concentration of potassium hydroxide, the capillary radius and the time.

**Control**: the tube with glass beads or boiled seeds. Its drop should not move. If it does, the volume is changing because of a change in temperature or atmospheric pressure, and every reading must be corrected by that amount.

**Typical results**: germinating seeds give a drop that moves towards the tube; the movement without absorber is smaller than the movement with it, because carbon dioxide is being produced.

**Sources of error**: the temperature of the tubes drifts; carbon dioxide dissolves in water in the tube; the seeds touch the absorber; there is a leak from a loose bung; gas escapes when the system is closed; the reading is made too soon after closing. Improvements: use a water bath and a long equilibration time, check for leaks by seeing that the drop holds still in the control, use more seeds so the movement is larger, and repeat.

**Safety**: potassium hydroxide is corrosive and soda lime is an irritant. Wear eye protection, wash splashes off at once and keep the absorber away from the seeds. Handle small animals gently, and return them unharmed when you finish.
:::

Try it: set the two distances and the radius of the capillary and watch the volumes and the RQ change. The start values give an RQ of 0.70. Change the radius and see that only the volumes change, not the RQ. Make \(d_2\) negative (the drop moves away from the tube) and see the RQ pass 1.

:::eg A respirometer containing germinating seeds was set up as above. The radius of the capillary was 0.50 mm. With potassium hydroxide the fluid moved 42 mm towards the tube in 10 minutes; without it, 12 mm towards the tube. Calculate the RQ and say which substrate was mainly respired.
**Step 1: the volume of oxygen taken in.** The potassium hydroxide absorbed the carbon dioxide, so \(d_1 = 42\,\mathrm{mm}\) shows oxygen only. [B1]

\[\begin{aligned} V_{\mathrm{O_2}} &= \pi r^2 d_1 \\ &= \pi \times 0.50^2 \times 42 \\ &= 33\,\mathrm{mm^3} \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

**Step 2: the carbon dioxide produced.** Without the absorber the net volume change is \(\pi \times 0.50^2 \times 12 = 9.4\,\mathrm{mm^3}\) (a decrease). The volume of carbon dioxide produced is the oxygen taken in minus the net decrease.

\[\begin{aligned} V_{\mathrm{CO_2}} &= \pi r^2 (d_1 - d_2) \\ &= \pi \times 0.50^2 \times (42 - 12) \\ &= 24\,\mathrm{mm^3} \end{aligned}\] [M1]

**Step 3: RQ.**

\[\begin{aligned} \mathrm{RQ} &= \frac{V_{\mathrm{CO_2}}}{V_{\mathrm{O_2}}} \\ &= \frac{\pi r^2 (d_1 - d_2)}{\pi r^2 d_1} \\ &= \frac{42 - 12}{42} \\ &= 0.71 \end{aligned}\] [A1]

**Step 4: the substrate.** An RQ of about 0.7 shows that the seeds were respiring mainly lipid. [B1]

The radius cancels, so \(\mathrm{RQ} = (d_1 - d_2) \div d_1\) needs no radius at all. A weaker answer divides the net movement by the oxygen movement (\(12 \div 42\)) or treats the net movement as the volume of oxygen, and so scores at most the first mark.
:::

:::eg Real context, germinating seeds: the table shows the oxygen taken in and the carbon dioxide produced by 10 oil-rich seeds on four days after they were soaked. (The data are invented, to show a typical pattern.) Calculate the RQ on each day, describe the trend and explain it.
| day | oxygen / mm³ h⁻¹ | carbon dioxide / mm³ h⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 40 | 28 |
| 4 | 55 | 44 |
| 6 | 70 | 63 |
| 8 | 84 | 84 |

**Step 1: RQ \(=\) carbon dioxide \(\div\) oxygen.**

\[\begin{aligned} \text{day 2: } \mathrm{RQ} &= 28 \div 40 = 0.70 \\ \text{day 4: } \mathrm{RQ} &= 44 \div 55 = 0.80 \\ \text{day 6: } \mathrm{RQ} &= 63 \div 70 = 0.90 \\ \text{day 8: } \mathrm{RQ} &= 84 \div 84 = 1.0 \end{aligned}\]

**Step 2: describe.** The RQ rises steadily from 0.70 on day 2 to 1.0 on day 8, an increase of 0.30.

**Step 3: explain.** On day 2 the RQ of 0.70 shows that the seeds are respiring mainly lipid, their oil store. During germination the lipid is converted into sugars that are transported to the growing shoot and root, so by day 8 the RQ of 1.0 shows that the seeds are respiring mainly carbohydrate. Oxygen uptake also rises, from 40 to 84 mm³ h⁻¹, as the seedling grows and more cells respire.
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State three processes in a cell that need energy from ATP.
A: Any three of: active transport; movement (muscle contraction, cilia beating, moving chromosomes); anabolic reactions such as DNA replication and protein synthesis.
Q: Why must ATP be made again and again in a cell?
A: Each molecule releases only a small amount of energy when it is hydrolysed to ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\), and the cell holds very little ATP, so it is recycled: ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\) are joined again using energy from respiration.
Q: A handful of nuts contains 9.0 g of lipid, 2.0 g of protein and 2.5 g of carbohydrate. Calculate the energy released if they are all completely respired. Use 39, 17 and 16 kJ g⁻¹.
A: \((9.0 \times 39) + (2.0 \times 17) + (2.5 \times 16) = 351 + 34 + 40 = 425\,\mathrm{kJ}\).
Q: Calculate the RQ of palmitic acid, \(\ce{C16H32O2}\).
A: \(\mathrm{O_2}\) needed \(= 16 + 32/4 - 2/2 = 23\), so \(\ce{C16H32O2 + 23O2 -> 16CO2 + 16H2O}\) and RQ \(= 16 \div 23 = 0.70\).
Q: Calculate the RQ of sucrose, \(\ce{C12H22O11}\). What type of substrate does the value suggest?
A: \(\mathrm{O_2}\) needed \(= 12 + 22/4 - 11/2 = 12\), so RQ \(= 12 \div 12 = 1.0\). It is a carbohydrate.
Q: In a respirometer with a capillary of radius 0.40 mm, a drop moved 35 mm with potassium hydroxide and 7.0 mm without it, both towards the tube. Calculate the volume of oxygen taken in and the RQ.
A: \(V_{\mathrm{O_2}} = \pi \times 0.40^2 \times 35 = 18\,\mathrm{mm^3}\). \(\mathrm{RQ} = (35 - 7.0) \div 35 = 0.80\).
Q: In a respirometer without any absorber, the drop did not move at all while mealworms respired. What does this show about the RQ?
A: The net change in volume is zero, so the volume of carbon dioxide produced equals the volume of oxygen taken in: the RQ is 1.0, so the mealworms are respiring mainly carbohydrate.
Q: Why is a tube with glass beads set up beside the tube with the organisms?
A: It is a control. Any movement of its drop is caused by changes in temperature or air pressure, not by respiration, so the readings of the other tubes can be corrected.
:::

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [2 marks] ★ State two features of ATP that make it suitable as the universal energy currency of cells.
A: It releases a small, manageable amount of energy when hydrolysed. [B1]
It is hydrolysed in one step, so energy is released quickly. [B1]
(Also credit: quickly resynthesised from ADP and Pi; small and soluble, so it moves easily within the cell; the same molecule is used in all cells; cannot leave the cell.)
Q: [3 marks] ★ Malic acid, \(\ce{C4H6O5}\), is a substrate that some plant cells respire: \(\ce{C4H6O5 + 3O2 -> 4CO2 + 3H2O}\). (a) Calculate the RQ. (b) Suggest why the value is greater than 1.0 although no anaerobic respiration is taking place.
H: Divide the coefficient of carbon dioxide by the coefficient of oxygen.
H: Compare the proportion of oxygen already in malic acid with the proportion in glucose.
A: (a) \(\mathrm{RQ} = 4 \div 3\) [M1] \(= 1.3\) [A1]
(b) The molecule already contains a lot of oxygen, so less oxygen is needed from the air for each carbon dioxide produced. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain why 1 g of lipid releases more energy than 1 g of carbohydrate when it is respired.
A: A gram of lipid contains more hydrogen atoms (more C–H bonds) than a gram of carbohydrate. [B1]
Respiration removes the hydrogen, so more reduced NAD and reduced FAD are formed per gram. [B1]
These pass more hydrogen to the electron transport chain, so more protons are pumped across the inner mitochondrial membrane. [B1]
So more ATP is made by chemiosmosis (oxidative phosphorylation). [B1]
(Also credit: lipid contains less oxygen, so more oxygen is used per gram.)
Q: [6 marks] ★★ Blowfly larvae were placed in a respirometer with a capillary of radius 0.35 mm. In 10 minutes, with potassium hydroxide, the fluid moved 30 mm towards the tube; without it, 6.0 mm towards the tube. A control tube contained glass beads. (a) Calculate the volume of oxygen the larvae took in during the 10 minutes. (b) Calculate the RQ. (c) Explain why the control tube was used. (d) Suggest what the RQ shows about the substrate the larvae were respiring.
H: Use volume \(= \pi r^2 d\) with the potassium hydroxide reading.
H: RQ \(= (d_1 - d_2) \div d_1\).
A: (a) \(\pi \times 0.35^2 \times 30\) [M1] \(= 12\,\mathrm{mm^3}\) [A1]
(b) \((30 - 6.0) \div 30\) [M1] \(= 0.80\) [A1]
(c) To show whether the drop moves because of changes in temperature or pressure rather than respiration, so that the readings can be corrected. [B1]
(d) A value between 0.7 and 1.0 suggests a mixture of substrates (for example lipid and carbohydrate, or protein). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Two cultures of an aerobic soil bacterium were each given a different single nutrient. The table shows the gases exchanged by each culture in a closed system.

| culture | oxygen taken in / mm³ h⁻¹ | carbon dioxide produced / mm³ h⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| X | 52 | 52 |
| Y | 60 | 43 |

(a) Calculate the RQ for each culture. (b) Deduce the type of nutrient each culture was respiring, giving a reason.
H: RQ is carbon dioxide divided by oxygen.
A: (a) RQ \(= 52 \div 52 = 1.0\) for X [M1] and \(43 \div 60 = 0.72\) for Y [A1] [A1]
(b) X was given a carbohydrate, because an RQ of 1.0 is the value for carbohydrate. [B1]
Y was given a lipid, because an RQ of about 0.7 is the value for lipid. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Compare the way ATP is made by substrate-linked phosphorylation and by chemiosmosis.
A: In substrate-linked phosphorylation, a phosphate group is transferred directly from another phosphorylated molecule to ADP. [B1]
In chemiosmosis, protons flow down their gradient through ATP synthase and the energy released joins ADP and \(\mathrm{P_i}\). [B1]
Substrate-linked phosphorylation happens in the cytoplasm and the mitochondrial matrix and does not need a membrane; chemiosmosis happens in a membrane (the inner mitochondrial membrane or chloroplast membranes). [B1]
Chemiosmosis makes most of the ATP in aerobic respiration, substrate-linked phosphorylation only a small share. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] (#bio.dna) ★★★ A cell that is about to divide has a high demand for ATP. Explain why, in terms of the processes that take place before and during division.
H: Think of DNA copying, making proteins and moving chromosomes.
A: DNA replication is an anabolic process: energy is needed to join nucleotides into new polynucleotide strands. [B1]
Protein synthesis for the new cell material needs energy to attach amino acids to tRNA and join them with peptide bonds. [B1]
Movement of chromosomes along the spindle fibres needs energy. [B1]
ATP supplies the energy, because it releases a small, manageable amount in one hydrolysis step that suits each reaction. [B1]
A cell with a high demand has many mitochondria (and makes ATP quickly), because ATP cannot be stored in large amounts. [B1]
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A mammal's breathing was measured. It took in oxygen and gave out carbon dioxide in a ratio that gave an RQ of 0.88. Assume only carbohydrate (RQ 1.0) and lipid (RQ 0.70) were being respired. (a) Calculate the fraction of the oxygen used that was used to respire carbohydrate. (b) The mammal then fasted for a day, and its RQ fell to 0.75. Suggest what this shows and why a fall in RQ has this meaning.
H: If a fraction \(f\) of the oxygen is used for carbohydrate, the rest, \(1 - f\), is used for lipid, and the RQ is the weighted average \(1.0f + 0.70(1 - f)\).
H: Which store runs out first when there is no food?
A: (a) \(0.88 = 1.0f + 0.70(1 - f)\) [M1], so \(0.88 - 0.70 = 0.30f\) [M1] and \(f = 0.60\), so 60% of the oxygen was used for carbohydrate [A1]
(b) The fall towards 0.7 shows that more lipid and less carbohydrate was being respired. [B1]
The carbohydrate stores (glycogen) were running low, so the body respired more of its lipid store; lipid needs more oxygen per carbon dioxide made, so the ratio of carbon dioxide to oxygen falls. [B1] [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ A student set up a respirometer with woodlice, with water in place of potassium hydroxide, and the drop did not move. The student concluded that the woodlice were not respiring. (a) Explain why this conclusion is not justified. (b) With potassium hydroxide in place, the drop moved 24 mm towards the tube in 10 minutes. State what this shows about the RQ and the woodlice, and what the student should conclude.
H: What are the two gas exchanges in a respirometer without an absorber?
A: (a) The drop shows only the net change in volume, oxygen taken in minus carbon dioxide produced. [B1]
No movement means that the two volumes were equal, so the woodlice could still be respiring with an RQ of 1.0. [B1]
(b) The movement with potassium hydroxide shows that oxygen was taken in, so the woodlice were respiring aerobically. [B1]
The RQ was 1.0 (the absorber removed the carbon dioxide, and the same volume of carbon dioxide was produced as oxygen taken in), so the woodlice were respiring mainly carbohydrate. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which statement about ATP is correct?
+ It releases a small amount of energy when its end phosphate group is hydrolysed.
- It stores large amounts of energy that last for days.
- It is made only in mitochondria.
- It passes easily out of cells to supply neighbouring cells.
> ATP is a carrier that releases about 30.5 kJ per mole in a single step. Cells hold very little of it, it is also made in the cytoplasm and chloroplasts, and it cannot pass through the cell surface membrane.
? In which process is ATP made by chemiosmosis?
+ the flow of protons through ATP synthase in the inner mitochondrial membrane
- the transfer of a phosphate group from a phosphorylated intermediate to ADP in the cytoplasm
- the hydrolysis of ATP in a muscle cell
- the breakdown of glucose by amylase
> Chemiosmosis uses the energy of a proton gradient across a membrane. The second option describes substrate-linked phosphorylation; hydrolysis of ATP releases energy rather than making ATP.
? Why does lipid have a higher energy value per gram than carbohydrate?
+ It contains more hydrogen per gram, so more reduced coenzymes form.
- Its molecules are larger.
- It contains more oxygen.
- It is hydrolysed in fewer steps.
> More hydrogen means more reduced NAD and FAD, more protons pumped and more ATP. Size is irrelevant because the value is per gram, and lipids contain less oxygen than carbohydrates.
? Which equation gives the correct RQ?
+ \(\mathrm{RQ} = \) molecules of carbon dioxide produced \(\div\) molecules of oxygen taken in
- \(\mathrm{RQ} = \) molecules of oxygen taken in \(\div\) molecules of carbon dioxide produced
- \(\mathrm{RQ} = \) mass of glucose used \(\div\) volume of oxygen
- \(\mathrm{RQ} = \) ATP made \(\div\) oxygen taken in
> The RQ has carbon dioxide on top. Inverting it is the common error. The last two options are not ratios of the gases exchanged.
? Glycerol, \(\ce{C3H8O3}\), is respired completely: \(\ce{C3H8O3 + 3.5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}\). What is the RQ?
+ 0.86
- 1.17
- 0.75
- 1.0
> \(\mathrm{RQ} = 3 \div 3.5 = 0.857\), which is 0.86 to 2 d.p. The value 1.17 is the inverted ratio, 0.75 divides by the number of water molecules, and 1.0 assumes every substrate behaves like glucose.
? What is the job of the potassium hydroxide solution in a respirometer?
+ It absorbs carbon dioxide, so the movement of the drop shows only the oxygen taken in.
- It absorbs oxygen, so the movement of the drop shows the carbon dioxide produced.
- It keeps the temperature of the tube constant.
- It supplies water to the organisms.
> Potassium hydroxide removes carbon dioxide from the air in the tube, so the volume falls only because of the oxygen taken in. It does not absorb oxygen, control temperature or hydrate the organisms.
? A culture of cells gives an RQ of 1.3. Which explanation is most likely?
+ Some respiration is taking place without oxygen, or the substrate is rich in oxygen, such as an organic acid.
- The cells are respiring only lipid.
- The cells are respiring only protein.
- The potassium hydroxide absorbed oxygen.
> An RQ above 1 means more carbon dioxide than oxygen. Lipid gives about 0.7 and protein about 0.9, and the absorber takes up carbon dioxide, not oxygen.
? A respirometer has a drop that moves 25 mm towards the tube with potassium hydroxide and 3 mm towards the tube without it. What is the RQ?
+ 0.88
- 1.14
- 0.79
- 0.12
> \(\mathrm{RQ} = (25 - 3) \div 25 = 0.88\). The value 1.14 is the inverted ratio, 0.79 is \(22 \div 28\), which divides by \(d_1 + d_2\) wrongly, and 0.12 is \(3 \div 25\), which treats the net movement as the carbon dioxide produced.

:::recap
- Cells need energy for active transport, movement and anabolic reactions (DNA replication, protein synthesis). Respiration transfers energy from food to ATP; it does not make energy.
- ATP: adenine, ribose, three phosphates. Hydrolysis releases about 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹ in one quick, small, manageable step. It is small, soluble, universal, resynthesised quickly and cannot leave the cell. It is a carrier, not a store.
- ATP is made by substrate-linked phosphorylation (phosphate passed from a substrate to ADP) and by chemiosmosis (proton gradient and ATP synthase, in membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts).
- Energy values: lipid about 39 kJ g⁻¹, protein about 17, carbohydrate about 16. Lipid has more hydrogen per gram, so more reduced NAD and FAD and more ATP.
- RQ = molecules of CO₂ produced ÷ molecules of O₂ taken in. Carbohydrate 1.0, lipid about 0.7, protein about 0.9. Above 1.0: anaerobic respiration or an oxygen-rich substrate.
- Balancing: \(\mathrm{O_2} = x + y/4 - z/2\) for \(\mathrm{C}_x\mathrm{H}_y\mathrm{O}_z\); oleic acid gives 0.71.
- Respirometer: with an absorber, the movement is oxygen taken in only (\(\pi r^2 d_1\)); without it, the movement is the net change. \(\mathrm{RQ} = (d_1 - d_2) \div d_1\). A control tube with glass beads allows for temperature and pressure changes.
:::
