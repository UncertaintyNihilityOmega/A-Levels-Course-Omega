You will learn how hydrogen bonding makes water a solvent that stores and removes heat, how a triglyceride is built from glycerol and three fatty acids joined by ester bonds, why fats store energy and insulate, and how phospholipids form a bilayer.

:::why
Every cell is mostly water, and every cell is wrapped in a skin made of lipids. Water dissolves what the cell needs to move and absorbs heat so that enzymes keep working; lipids store energy, insulate and build membranes. The two meet in one idea: some molecules mix with water (hydrophilic) and others do not (hydrophobic). That idea decides where nutrients go, how a membrane forms and why you sweat, and it appears in structured questions and in multiple-choice questions every year.
:::

The glycosidic bond in [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs) is a close cousin of the ester bond you meet here. The test for lipids is in [Testing for biological molecules](#bio.foodtests).

Q: What does it mean to say that a water molecule is polar? (from [Biology from zero](#bio.f.basics))
A: The shared electrons are pulled closer to the oxygen atom, so oxygen is slightly negative (δ−) and each hydrogen atom is slightly positive (δ+).
Q: What type of reaction joins two sugar molecules by a glycosidic bond, and what else is made? (from [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs))
A: A condensation reaction; a molecule of water is removed (released) for each bond formed.
Q: Give two reasons why glycogen, not glucose, is stored in animal cells. (from [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs))
A: Glycogen is insoluble and a large molecule, so it stays in the cell and has no osmotic effect; it is compact, and its many free ends allow quick hydrolysis to glucose when needed.

## Water and hydrogen bonds

You met the water molecule in [Biology from zero](#bio.f.basics). Here is the part that the syllabus asks you to explain. The oxygen end of a water molecule is slightly negative (\(\delta-\)) and the two hydrogen atoms are slightly positive (\(\delta+\)). Because of this, the \(\delta+\) hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the \(\delta-\) oxygen of a neighbouring molecule. That attraction between molecules is a **hydrogen bond**.

One hydrogen bond is weak, and it breaks and re-forms constantly. But every water molecule can make several, so a mass of water is held together by a huge number of them. Three properties follow from this, and they are the three that you must be able to relate to the roles of water in living things: solvent action, high specific heat capacity and high latent heat of vaporisation.

:::warn Where hydrogen bonds are
Hydrogen bonds form **between** water molecules. They do not hold the hydrogen and oxygen atoms together inside one molecule: those are covalent bonds, which are strong and do not break at body temperature. A mark scheme gives the mark for "δ+ hydrogen attracted to δ− oxygen of **another** molecule".
:::

:::eg Explain how hydrogen bonding occurs between water molecules.
The question has three marking points: the polarity, the attraction and where it happens.

In a water molecule the oxygen atom is slightly negative (δ−) and the hydrogen atoms are slightly positive (δ+), because the electrons are shared unequally. [B1]

The δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of a neighbouring water molecule. [B1]

This attraction between two different molecules is a hydrogen bond; it is weak, but many together are strong. [B1]

A weaker answer says "hydrogen bonds hold the hydrogen and oxygen together". That is a covalent bond, so it earns no marks.
:::

### Water as a solvent

:::def Hydrophilic
"Water-loving": a substance that is attracted to water and dissolves in it, or mixes with it. Polar molecules and ions are hydrophilic.
:::

:::def Hydrophobic
"Water-hating": a substance that is not attracted to water and does not dissolve in it. Non-polar molecules, such as fats and oils, are hydrophobic.
:::

:::def Solvent
A liquid in which other substances (solutes) dissolve to form a solution. Water is the solvent of living things.
:::

Polar molecules and ions dissolve in water because water molecules are attracted to them. Take sodium chloride: the \(\delta-\) oxygen atoms of water are attracted to the positive \(\ce{Na+}\) ions, and the \(\delta+\) hydrogen atoms are attracted to the negative \(\ce{Cl-}\) ions. Water molecules crowd round each ion and separate it from the crystal. Glucose and amino acids dissolve in the same way, because they carry polar groups (such as OH) that form hydrogen bonds with water. Non-polar substances such as lipids have no charges to attract water, so they do not dissolve.

This solvent action matters because **cells and organisms are mostly solutions**:
- **Transport.** Blood plasma carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, ions and urea; xylem sap carries dissolved mineral ions up the plant; phloem carries dissolved sucrose.
- **Reactions.** Chemical reactions in cells happen in solution in the cytoplasm, because dissolved molecules can move and collide. Enzymes and their substrates meet in water.
- **A medium for ions and gases to enter cells** and wastes to leave: substances usually cross the cell surface membrane in solution.

### High specific heat capacity

:::def Specific heat capacity
The energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 g (or 1 kg) of a substance by 1 °C. Water has a high specific heat capacity: about 4.2 J are needed to warm 1 g of water by 1 °C.
:::

When you heat a substance, the energy makes its particles move faster, which is what a rise in temperature is. In water, some of the energy has to break hydrogen bonds first, so more energy must be supplied to make the temperature rise by 1 °C. In the other direction, cooling water releases a lot of energy as hydrogen bonds re-form. The result is that water temperature changes **slowly**.

An experiment shows the difference. A student heated 100 g of water and 100 g of vegetable oil with two identical heaters that supplied the same energy every minute. Both liquids started at 20 °C.

:::eg Describe and explain the results of the heating experiment shown in the graph.
Describe the trend with figures and units, then give the reason.

The temperature of both liquids rises steadily from 20 °C, but oil rises faster: after 5 minutes the oil is at 50 °C and the water at 35 °C. [B1]

The oil warms by 6 °C each minute, twice as fast as water, which warms by 3 °C each minute. [B1]

Water has a higher specific heat capacity: more energy is needed to raise its temperature by 1 °C. [B1]

Some of the energy supplied to water is used to break hydrogen bonds between the molecules, so the temperature rises more slowly. [B1]

A weaker answer says "water heats up slower because it is thicker" or just "water has a high specific heat". It needs the figures in the description and the hydrogen bonds in the explanation.
:::

Roles of this property:
- **Stable temperature inside cells and organisms.** The cytoplasm is mostly water, so its temperature changes slowly, which keeps enzymes working near their optimum (see [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes)). Blood plasma carries heat around the body without much change in its own temperature.
- **Stable habitats.** A pond, lake or ocean warms and cools slowly, so aquatic organisms live in a steadier environment than land organisms do.

### High latent heat of vaporisation

:::def Latent heat of vaporisation
The energy needed to change 1 g (or 1 kg) of a liquid into a gas (vapour) with no change in temperature. Water has a high value: evaporating 1 g of water from the body takes about 2.4 kJ.
:::

To evaporate, a water molecule must escape from the liquid, which means breaking all the hydrogen bonds that hold it to its neighbours. This takes a lot of energy, taken from the liquid that is left. The surface therefore **cools**.

- **Sweating.** Sweat is secreted onto the skin and evaporates. The energy needed comes from the skin and the blood just below it, which cool as a result (see [Homeostasis and the kidney](#bio.homeostasis)).
- **Transpiration.** Water evaporates from the surfaces of cells inside a leaf, which cools the leaf in sunshine (see [Plant tissues and water transport](#bio.planttransport)).

:::key Water in one box
- **Hydrogen bonds** form between the δ+ hydrogen of one water molecule and the δ− oxygen of another.
- **Solvent:** polar molecules and ions dissolve, so water transports substances in blood and xylem and is the medium for reactions in the cell.
- **High specific heat capacity:** much energy (breaking hydrogen bonds) is needed to warm water, so temperatures in cells and in water habitats stay steady.
- **High latent heat of vaporisation:** much energy is needed to evaporate water, so evaporation (sweating, transpiration) cools the surface.
:::

:::eg Relate the properties of water to the way sweating cools the body.
The question wants a property, its cause, and its effect. Four marking points.

Sweat is mainly water, and it evaporates from the surface of the skin. [B1]

Water has a high latent heat of vaporisation: a lot of energy is needed to break the hydrogen bonds between water molecules so that they escape as vapour. [B1]

This energy is taken from the skin and the blood close to it. [B1]

So the surface (and the blood) cools, which lowers body temperature. [B1]

(Also credit: sweat is a solution, mostly water, because water is a good solvent and carries salts out of the body.)

A weaker answer says "water has a high specific heat capacity so it takes in a lot of heat". That is the wrong property: sweating cools because water **evaporates**, so the property is the latent heat of vaporisation.
:::

:::eg Real context: a runner loses 0.50 dm³ of sweat in one hour, all of which evaporates. Calculate the energy removed from the body, if 1 g of water takes 2.4 kJ to evaporate and 1 cm³ of sweat has a mass of 1.0 g.
Convert the volume to mass first, then multiply by the energy per gram.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mass} &= 0.50\,\mathrm{dm^3} \times 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3\,dm^{-3}} \times 1.0\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}} \\ &= 500\,\mathrm{g} \end{aligned}\]

The mass of sweat is 500 g. [M1]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{energy} &= 500\,\mathrm{g} \times 2.4\,\mathrm{kJ\,g^{-1}} \\ &= 1200\,\mathrm{kJ} \end{aligned}\]

About 1200 kJ (1.2 MJ) of heat leaves the body in one hour. [A1]

This is why runners must drink: the water lost has to be replaced. It is also why a humid day makes exercise harder, because sweat evaporates more slowly when the air is already full of water vapour.
:::

## Lipids

Lipids are a mixed group of compounds that are **insoluble in water** but dissolve in organic solvents such as ethanol. They contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, but they have far less oxygen than a carbohydrate has. The lipids you need are **triglycerides** (fats and oils) and **phospholipids**.

A **fat** is a triglyceride that is solid at room temperature, and an **oil** is a triglyceride that is liquid. Both are built from two kinds of smaller molecule.

:::def Glycerol
A small molecule with three carbon atoms, each carrying an OH (hydroxyl) group. Its formula is \(\ce{C3H8O3}\).
:::

:::def Fatty acid
A molecule with a carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end and a long hydrocarbon chain (the "tail"), usually of 14 to 20 carbon atoms. The tail is non-polar.
:::

### Saturated and unsaturated fatty acids

The hydrocarbon tail of a fatty acid is a chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen atoms attached. Whether the carbon atoms are joined by single bonds only decides two kinds of fatty acid.

:::def Saturated fatty acid
A fatty acid whose hydrocarbon tail has only single bonds between the carbon atoms (no C=C double bonds). Every carbon atom holds as many hydrogen atoms as it can, so the tail is "saturated" with hydrogen.
:::

:::def Unsaturated fatty acid
A fatty acid whose hydrocarbon tail has one or more C=C double bonds. A fatty acid with one is monounsaturated, with two or more is polyunsaturated.
:::

The kink matters. Straight tails pack closely side by side, so saturated fats have strong attractions between molecules and are **solid** at room temperature (butter, animal fat). Kinked tails cannot pack so closely, so there is less attraction between molecules and unsaturated fats are **liquid** at room temperature (olive oil, sunflower oil). The more C=C bonds, the lower the melting point.

| Feature | Saturated | Unsaturated |
|---|---|---|
| C=C bonds in the tail | none | one or more |
| Shape of the tail | straight | kinked (bent) |
| Packing of molecules | close | loose |
| At room temperature | usually solid (fat) | usually liquid (oil) |
| Common source | animal fats | plant oils |

:::warn Unsaturated does not mean "no hydrogen"
An unsaturated fatty acid has **fewer** hydrogen atoms than the saturated one with the same number of carbon atoms, because each C=C bond replaces two C–H bonds. It still has many hydrogen atoms. Write "has one or more C=C double bonds" and you cannot go wrong.
:::

### Building a triglyceride

A glycerol molecule has three OH groups, and a fatty acid has a carboxyl group, –COOH. They join in a condensation reaction: the OH from the carboxyl group of the fatty acid and an H from the OH on glycerol leave as a molecule of water, and the oxygen left behind links the two molecules.

:::def Ester bond
A covalent bond formed in a condensation reaction between the carboxyl group of a fatty acid and a hydroxyl (OH) group of glycerol. It has the form –COO–, and it is how fatty acids are attached to glycerol.
:::

Because glycerol has three OH groups, it joins to **three** fatty acids, by three condensation reactions. This makes one **triglyceride**, three **ester bonds** and three molecules of **water**.

:::eg Palmitic acid, a saturated fatty acid, has the formula C₁₆H₃₂O₂. One molecule of glycerol (C₃H₈O₃) reacts with three molecules of palmitic acid to make the triglyceride tripalmitin. Work out the formula of tripalmitin and its relative molecular mass, using C = 12, H = 1, O = 16.
Add the atoms of the reactants, then take away the three molecules of water.

Atoms in the reactants: C \(= 3 + 3 \times 16 = 51\); H \(= 8 + 3 \times 32 = 104\); O \(= 3 + 3 \times 2 = 9\). [M1]

Three molecules of water (\(\ce{H2O}\)) are removed: H \(= 104 - 6 = 98\); O \(= 9 - 3 = 6\). The formula is \(\ce{C51H98O6}\). [A1]

\[\begin{aligned} M_r &= 51 \times 12 + 98 \times 1 + 6 \times 16 \\ &= 612 + 98 + 96 \\ &= 806 \end{aligned}\]

The relative molecular mass is 806. [A1]

Check by the other route: glycerol is 92 and palmitic acid is 256, so \(92 + 3 \times 256 - 3 \times 18 = 806\), the same.
:::

:::key Triglyceride in one box
- **One glycerol + three fatty acids**, joined by **three ester bonds**; **three molecules of water** are released (condensation).
- **Non-polar and hydrophobic:** the tails are hydrocarbon chains, so triglycerides do not dissolve in water.
- **Saturated** (no C=C, straight tails, solid fats) or **unsaturated** (C=C, kinked tails, liquid oils).
:::

:::warn Not a polymer, and not a glycosidic bond
A triglyceride is a large molecule, but it is **not a polymer**: it is not a long chain of repeating monomers, only one glycerol with three fatty acids attached. And the bond is an **ester bond**, not a glycosidic bond. Both form by condensation, but a glycosidic bond joins two sugars and an ester bond joins a fatty acid to glycerol.
:::

:::note Testing for lipids
Because lipids are hydrophobic, the emulsion test dissolves the sample in ethanol, then pours the solution into water: a cloudy white emulsion shows lipid. The method is in [Testing for biological molecules](#bio.foodtests).
:::

### What triglycerides do

The structure of a triglyceride explains each of its functions.

| Function | How the structure explains it |
|---|---|
| **Energy store** | The tails contain many C–H bonds and little oxygen, so lipids release more energy when respired than carbohydrates do: about 39 kJ g⁻¹ compared with 16 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrate, more than twice as much per gram. |
| **No osmotic effect** | Triglycerides are insoluble and hydrophobic, so they do not change the water potential of a cell and can be stored in large amounts. |
| **Thermal insulation** | Fat is a poor conductor of heat. A layer of fat under the skin (adipose tissue), or blubber in a seal, reduces heat loss. |
| **Buoyancy** | Fat is less dense than water, so a layer of fat helps aquatic animals float. |
| **Protection** | A layer of fat around organs such as the kidneys cushions them against knocks. |
| **Source of metabolic water** | Respiration of lipids releases water, as well as energy. |

:::eg During 7 hours of sleep, an adult uses about 2000 kJ of energy. Calculate the mass of fat that provides this energy, and the mass of carbohydrate needed. Use 39 kJ g⁻¹ for lipid and 16 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrate. Suggest why animals store fat rather than carbohydrate for long periods.
Divide the energy by the energy released per gram.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mass of fat} &= \frac{2000}{39} \\ &= 51\,\mathrm{g} \end{aligned}\]

[M1] The sleeper respires about 51 g of fat. [A1]

\[\begin{aligned} \text{mass of carbohydrate} &= \frac{2000}{16} \\ &= 125\,\mathrm{g} \end{aligned}\]

125 g of carbohydrate is needed, about 2.4 times as much mass. [A1]

For long-term storage, fat is a better store because the same energy needs less than half the mass, which matters for an animal that has to carry its store with it; and because fat is insoluble it has no osmotic effect. [B1]
:::

:::note Fat and water in the camel
A camel's hump is a store of **fat**, not of water. Respiration of lipids releases water (metabolic water) as well as energy, and that is one of several ways animals living in dry places gain water. The hump also keeps fat in one place, instead of a layer all over the body, so heat can leave from the rest of the body.
:::

:::eg Real context: a seal spends its life in cold sea water, under a thick layer of blubber (fat). Explain the benefits of the blubber.
Link each property of the fat to a benefit for the seal. Four marking points.

Fat is a poor conductor of heat, so blubber insulates the seal and reduces heat loss to the cold water. [B1]

Fat is less dense than water, so blubber gives the seal buoyancy and helps it float. [B1]

Fat is an energy store, releasing about 39 kJ g⁻¹, so the seal can survive when food is scarce. [B1]

Fat is insoluble, so a large store has no osmotic effect on the cells that hold it. [B1]

(Also credit: respiration of fat releases metabolic water; fat protects organs.)
:::

## Phospholipids

A phospholipid looks like a triglyceride with one fatty acid taken out and a **phosphate group** put in its place. Glycerol therefore carries two fatty acids and one phosphate-containing group.

:::def Phospholipid
A lipid in which glycerol is joined to two fatty acids and a phosphate group. The phosphate end (the "head") is hydrophilic and polar; the two fatty acid tails are hydrophobic and non-polar.
:::

So a phospholipid has two ends with opposite behaviour:
- the **phosphate head** is charged, polar and **hydrophilic**: it is attracted to water;
- the **two fatty acid tails** are non-polar and **hydrophobic**: they are repelled by water and pushed together.

When phospholipids are placed in water, they arrange themselves so that the heads face the water and the tails face one another, away from it. A double layer, a **phospholipid bilayer**, is the most stable arrangement: both sides have heads towards water, with the tails sandwiched between.

:::eg Explain why phospholipids form a bilayer when they are mixed with water.
Use the words hydrophilic and hydrophobic and say which part faces which way. Four marking points.

A phospholipid has a phosphate head, which is polar and hydrophilic, and two fatty acid tails, which are non-polar and hydrophobic. [B1]

The hydrophilic heads are attracted to water, so they face the water. [B1]

The hydrophobic tails are repelled by water, so they point away from it, towards each other. [B1]

This gives two layers with the tails sandwiched in the middle and the heads on the outside on both sides: a bilayer. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the head likes water and the tail hates it" without the words hydrophilic and hydrophobic, or leaves out which part faces the water.

Line 2: They are joined by glycosidic bonds.
Line 3: Each bond is made in a condensation reaction, which releases a molecule of water.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: the bonds in a triglyceride are ester bonds. A glycosidic bond joins two sugar units. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct Line 2: "They are joined by ester bonds." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.

:::eg Spot the error: explain how sweating helps to cool the body. [3]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Sweat is secreted onto the skin and evaporates.
Line 2: Water has a high specific heat capacity, so it takes a lot of heat from the skin as it evaporates.
Line 3: So the skin and blood under it cool down.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: specific heat capacity is the energy to warm water by 1 °C. Evaporation needs the latent heat of vaporisation, because energy is needed to break the hydrogen bonds so that the water becomes a vapour. That mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct Line 2: "Water has a high latent heat of vaporisation, so a lot of energy is taken from the skin to evaporate it." With that change the answer scores 3 marks.

:::exam Name the property, not just the idea
Biology mark schemes give the mark for the **named property** (solvent, high specific heat capacity, high latent heat of vaporisation) joined to the **cause** (hydrogen bonds) and the **effect** on the organism. "Water is cool" or "water is a good substance" earns nothing. For lipids, name the **bond** (ester), the **reaction** (condensation) and the **molecules** (glycerol, fatty acids), and use "hydrophobic" or "hydrophilic", not "water-hating".

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State the meaning of the terms hydrophilic and hydrophobic.
A: Hydrophilic: attracted to water, and dissolves in water (polar substances and ions). Hydrophobic: not attracted to water, and does not dissolve in water (non-polar substances such as lipids).
Q: Name the atoms that form a hydrogen bond between two water molecules and state which carries the partial positive charge.
A: A hydrogen atom of one molecule (δ+) and an oxygen atom of another molecule (δ−).
Q: Explain why sodium chloride dissolves in water.
A: Water is polar. The δ− oxygen atoms are attracted to \(\ce{Na+}\) ions and the δ+ hydrogen atoms to \(\ce{Cl-}\) ions, so water molecules surround each ion and pull it away from the crystal.
Q: A dog cools itself by panting: on a hot afternoon 0.20 dm³ of water evaporates from its tongue and airways. If 1 g of water takes 2.4 kJ to evaporate and the water has a density of 1.0 g cm⁻³, calculate the energy removed.
A: \(0.20\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 200\,\mathrm{cm^3} = 200\,\mathrm{g}\); \(200 \times 2.4 = 480\,\mathrm{kJ}\).
Q: Name the two types of molecule that join to form a triglyceride, and state how many molecules of water are released when one triglyceride is made.
A: Glycerol and fatty acids (one glycerol and three fatty acids). Three molecules of water are released.
Q: State one similarity and one difference between a glycosidic bond and an ester bond.
A: Both are covalent bonds formed by condensation (with water released). A glycosidic bond joins two sugar units; an ester bond joins a fatty acid to glycerol.
Q: Explain why a triglyceride is not described as a polymer.
A: It is not made of many repeating monomer units joined in a long chain; it is a single glycerol with three fatty acids attached.
Q: Which type of fat, saturated or unsaturated, is more likely to be liquid at room temperature? Explain.
A: Unsaturated. The C=C double bonds put kinks in the tails, so the molecules cannot pack closely and there is less attraction between them.
Q: A student says that an unsaturated fatty acid contains no hydrogen. Correct the student.
A: It has fewer hydrogen atoms than the saturated fatty acid with the same number of carbon atoms, because of its C=C bond(s), but it still has many.
Q: A bird stores 25 g of fat before a flight. Calculate the energy released if all of it is respired, at 39 kJ g⁻¹. How much carbohydrate would give the same energy, at 16 kJ g⁻¹?
A: \(25 \times 39 = 975\,\mathrm{kJ}\). Carbohydrate: \(975 \div 16 = 61\,\mathrm{g}\).

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [3 marks] ★ Explain why ions such as sodium and chloride dissolve in water.
A: Water molecules are polar, with δ− oxygen and δ+ hydrogen atoms. [B1]
The δ− oxygen atoms are attracted to the positive ions and the δ+ hydrogen atoms to the negative ions. [B1]
Water molecules surround each ion and separate it from the others, so the ions go into solution. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ The table shows the melting points of three fatty acids, each with 18 carbon atoms.

| fatty acid | C=C bonds | melting point / °C |
|---|---|---|
| stearic | 0 | 69 |
| oleic | 1 | 13 |
| linoleic | 2 | −5 |

(a) Describe the trend shown by the data. (b) Explain the trend.
H: Quote the first and last melting points, with units.
H: Think about the shape of the tail, and how well molecules pack.
A: (a) The melting point decreases as the number of C=C bonds increases. [B1]
From 69 °C with no C=C bonds to −5 °C with two. [B1]
(b) Each C=C bond puts a kink in the hydrocarbon tail. [B1]
Molecules with more kinks cannot pack together as closely, so there are weaker attractions between them. [B1]
So less energy is needed to separate the molecules, and the melting point is lower. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A bear's fat mass was measured through a winter of hibernation. It fell from 60.0 kg at day 0 to 38.4 kg at day 120. Calculate the mean mass of fat used per day and the mean energy released per day, using 39 kJ g⁻¹. Give the energy in kJ.
H: Convert kg to g before you multiply.
A: Fat lost \(= 60.0 - 38.4 = 21.6\,\mathrm{kg}\) over 120 days [M1]
Mean mass per day \(= 21.6 \times 1000 \div 120 = 180\,\mathrm{g}\) [A1]
Energy per day \(= 180 \times 39\) [M1]
\(= 7020\,\mathrm{kJ}\) [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A large lake and the air above it are both warmed by the sun for 12 hours. The temperature of the air rises by 9 °C, and the temperature of the lake by 1 °C. Explain why the temperature of the lake changes less.
A: Water has a high specific heat capacity. [B1]
A lot of energy is needed to raise the temperature of water by 1 °C. [B1]
Some of the energy goes to breaking hydrogen bonds between water molecules rather than raising the temperature. [B1]
So the same energy input gives a much smaller temperature rise than in air. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A leaf in sunlight loses 12 g of water per hour by evaporation from the cells inside. (a) Calculate the energy removed from the leaf in one hour, if 1 g takes 2.4 kJ. (b) Explain why this helps the leaf.
A: (a) \(12 \times 2.4\) [M1] \(= 28.8\,\mathrm{kJ}\), or 29 kJ [A1]
(b) Water has a high latent heat of vaporisation, so evaporation takes a lot of energy from the leaf. [B1]
This cools the leaf, which keeps the enzymes in it from being denatured by overheating. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Describe the structure of a triglyceride and how it is formed from glycerol and fatty acids.
H: Say how many of each molecule, name the bond, and say what is released.
H: Mention what part of a fatty acid is hydrophobic and why.
A: A triglyceride consists of one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids. [B1]
Glycerol has three OH groups, and each fatty acid has a carboxyl group (–COOH). [B1]
The bond between them is an ester bond, formed in a condensation reaction. [B1]
For each ester bond one molecule of water is released, so three molecules are made. [B1]
Each fatty acid has a hydrocarbon tail, which may be saturated (no C=C) or unsaturated (one or more C=C). [B1]
The tails are non-polar, so a triglyceride is hydrophobic and insoluble in water. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.membranes) ★★★ Phospholipids are the main component of the cell surface membrane. (a) Describe the structure of a phospholipid. (b) Explain why phospholipids form a bilayer in an aqueous environment. (c) Suggest why a phospholipid bilayer is a barrier to sodium ions.
H: Compare it with a triglyceride: what replaces one of the fatty acids?
A: (a) A phospholipid has a glycerol joined to two fatty acids and a phosphate group. [B1]
The phosphate head is hydrophilic and polar, and the two fatty acid tails are hydrophobic and non-polar. [B1]
(b) The hydrophilic heads face the water on both sides, and the hydrophobic tails face each other, away from water. [B1]
This arrangement is the most stable, so two layers form with the tails sandwiched between the heads. [B1]
(c) Sodium ions are charged (hydrophilic), so they cannot dissolve in or pass through the hydrophobic middle layer of the tails. [B1]
So the bilayer stops them diffusing freely across the membrane. [B1]

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A small bird needs 780 kJ of energy for a non-stop flight, supplied only by respiring stored food. (a) Calculate the mass of fat needed, and the mass of carbohydrate needed (39 kJ g⁻¹ and 16 kJ g⁻¹). (b) Explain why a bird that migrates uses fat as its fuel for a long flight rather than carbohydrate. (c) Suggest one further advantage of fat for a bird flying over a desert.
H: Compare the masses from part (a): what does the bird have to carry?
H: What is released when fat is respired, apart from energy?
A: (a) Fat: \(780 \div 39\) [M1] \(= 20\,\mathrm{g}\) [A1]; carbohydrate: \(780 \div 16 = 48.75\,\mathrm{g}\), about 49 g [A1]
(b) The mass of fat needed is less than half the mass of carbohydrate for the same energy, so the bird carries less weight and can fly further. [B1]
Fat is insoluble and has no osmotic effect, so a large store can be kept in cells without water entering them. [B1]
(c) Respiration of fat releases water (metabolic water) as well as energy, which helps when the bird has no access to drinking water. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Scientists make tiny hollow spheres, called liposomes, by shaking phospholipids in water. The wall of a liposome is a phospholipid bilayer. Drugs are packed inside them for delivery to cells. A water-soluble drug is carried in the central space, and a lipid-soluble drug in the wall. Explain how the structure of a liposome allows both drugs to be carried.
H: Where is water in a liposome? Where is it not?
H: Which part of the bilayer is hydrophobic?
A: The phospholipids arrange themselves with their hydrophilic heads facing the water, outside and inside the sphere, and their hydrophobic tails facing each other in the middle of the wall. [B1]
The central space is filled with water, so it is an aqueous environment. [B1]
A water-soluble (hydrophilic) drug dissolves in the water in the central space. [B1]
The drug cannot easily cross the hydrophobic middle of the bilayer, so it stays inside until the liposome reaches the cell. [B1]
A lipid-soluble (hydrophobic) drug dissolves in the hydrophobic layer of tails in the wall. [B1]
Because the wall is a bilayer like a cell membrane, it can fuse with the cell surface membrane and release the drug into the cell. [B1]

:::recap
- Water is polar (O δ−, H δ+); hydrogen bonds form between the δ+ H of one molecule and the δ− O of another. They are weak, but there are many.
- Solvent: dissolves ions and polar molecules (transport in blood and xylem; reactions in cells). High specific heat capacity: stable temperatures. High latent heat of vaporisation: evaporation cools (sweat, transpiration).
- Triglyceride = glycerol + 3 fatty acids, 3 ester bonds, 3 water molecules released by condensation. Non-polar and hydrophobic. Not a polymer.
- Saturated: no C=C, straight tails, solid fats. Unsaturated: C=C, kinked tails, liquid oils.
- Functions: energy store (39 kJ g⁻¹ against 16 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrate), no osmotic effect, insulation, buoyancy, protection, metabolic water.
- Phospholipid = glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate: hydrophilic polar head, hydrophobic non-polar tails. In water, a bilayer: heads outwards, tails inwards.
:::
