## The structure of the heart

The mammalian heart is a muscular pump about the size of a clenched fist. It lies in the chest between the lungs, tilted so that its pointed end, the **apex**, points down and to the left. Its wall is made of **cardiac muscle**, a special muscle that contracts and relaxes rhythmically throughout life and does not tire.

From the outside you can see:
- two upper chambers, the **atria** (singular: atrium), and two lower chambers, the **ventricles**. The right atrium and right ventricle are on the right side of the body, which is the left of a front view;
- the four main vessels: the **vena cava** brings blood into the right atrium, the **pulmonary artery** leaves the right ventricle, the **pulmonary veins** enter the left atrium, and the **aorta** leaves the left ventricle;
- the **coronary arteries**, which branch from the base of the aorta and run over the surface of the heart.

:::def Coronary artery
One of the arteries that branch from the aorta just above the semilunar valve and run over the surface of the heart, supplying the cardiac muscle with oxygenated blood.
:::

The blood inside the heart's chambers cannot supply the thick wall, because the oxygen would have to diffuse a long way. So the heart muscle has its own blood supply in the coronary arteries. If a coronary artery becomes blocked, for example by a blood clot or a fatty deposit, part of the cardiac muscle is cut off from oxygen and glucose and begins to die. This is a **heart attack**.

- The heart has **four chambers**. The **septum** is a wall of muscle that separates the right side from the left side, so that deoxygenated and oxygenated blood do not mix.
- Each atrium connects to the ventricle below it through an **atrioventricular (AV) valve**. The right AV valve has three flaps and is the **tricuspid valve**; the left AV valve has two flaps and is the **bicuspid valve** (also called the mitral valve).
- **Tendinous cords** (valve tendons) are strong fibres that join the edges of the AV valve flaps to the muscle of the ventricle wall (to small muscular projections called papillary muscles). They stop the valve turning inside out into the atrium when the ventricle contracts.
- **Semilunar valves** (so called because their cusps are half-moon shaped) are at the start of the aorta and of the pulmonary artery. They are pocket valves: they stop blood flowing back from the arteries into the ventricles.

| Valve | Between | Shape and anchoring |
|---|---|---|
| tricuspid (right AV) valve | right atrium and right ventricle | three flaps, held by tendinous cords |
| bicuspid (left AV) valve | left atrium and left ventricle | two flaps, held by tendinous cords |
| semilunar valve (pulmonary) | right ventricle and pulmonary artery | three pocket-shaped cusps |
| semilunar valve (aortic) | left ventricle and aorta | three pocket-shaped cusps |

:::key The heart in one box
- **Four chambers**: two thin-walled **atria** receive blood from the veins; two thick-walled **ventricles** pump blood out into the arteries.
- **Septum** keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood apart.
- **AV valves** (tricuspid, bicuspid) lie between atrium and ventricle, held by tendinous cords. **Semilunar valves** lie at the base of the aorta and the pulmonary artery.
- **Coronary arteries** supply the cardiac muscle.
:::

## Wall thickness

The walls of the four chambers are not equally thick, and the figure above shows it: the walls of the atria are thin, the wall of the right ventricle is thicker, and the wall of the left ventricle is the thickest. The thickness depends on how much force the chamber needs to produce, because a thicker wall has more cardiac muscle, and more muscle contracts with a greater force and so gives a higher pressure.

:::key Why the walls differ
- **Atria (thin walls)**: an atrium pumps blood only a short distance, into the ventricle just below it. Most of the filling happens by itself, because the blood is flowing in under the low pressure of the veins. A small, low-pressure squeeze is enough.
- **Ventricles (thick walls)**: a ventricle must pump the blood out of the heart and along an artery, so it must generate a much higher pressure.
- **Left ventricle (thickest)**: it pumps blood to the whole body (every organ except the lungs), a long path with a great resistance to flow, so it must produce a high pressure, about 16 kPa at its peak.
- **Right ventricle (thinner)**: it pumps blood only to the lungs, which are close by, at a much lower pressure. Low pressure in the lungs avoids damaging the delicate walls of the lung capillaries and means that less tissue fluid is forced out into the alveoli.
:::

:::warn The two ventricles pump the same volume
The left ventricle does **not** pump "more blood" than the right. Over time, the volume leaving the right ventricle must equal the volume leaving the left, or the blood would pile up in the lungs or in the body. The difference is the **pressure** produced, not the volume.
:::

:::eg Explain the differences in thickness of the walls of the left ventricle and the right ventricle.
A four-mark answer needs both ventricles and a reason for each.

The wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. [B1]

The left ventricle pumps blood to the whole body, which is a long distance with a high resistance to flow, so it needs to produce a higher pressure. [B1]

A thicker wall has more cardiac muscle, which contracts with a greater force. [B1]

The right ventricle pumps blood only to the lungs, nearby, so it needs to produce only a lower pressure; the low pressure also avoids damaging the capillaries in the lungs. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the left ventricle is thicker because it pumps blood further and pumps more blood". It does not link the thickness to the force of contraction and the pressure, and "more blood" is wrong: both ventricles pump the same volume.
:::

## The cardiac cycle

:::def Cardiac cycle
The sequence of events that makes up one heartbeat: the contraction (systole) and relaxation (diastole) of the atria and of the ventricles, and the opening and closing of the valves.
:::

- **Systole** is contraction of the heart muscle. **Diastole** is relaxation.
- The cycle has three phases: **atrial systole** (the atria contract), **ventricular systole** (the ventricles contract) and **diastole** (the whole heart relaxes).
- At a resting heart rate of 75 beats per minute, one cycle takes \(60 \div 75 = 0.8\) s. In this model the three phases take about 0.1 s, 0.3 s and 0.4 s.

:::formula Heart rate from the cardiac cycle
\[\text{heart rate / beats per minute} = \frac{60}{\text{time for one cycle / s}}\]

If you are given the time for several cycles, divide by the number of cycles first.
:::

### Valves open and close by pressure

No muscle opens or closes a valve. A valve is a flap that is pushed by the blood.

:::key The rule for every valve
- **Blood flows from higher pressure to lower pressure.**
- A valve **opens** when the pressure **behind** it (upstream) is **higher** than the pressure in front of it.
- A valve **closes** when the pressure in **front** of it (downstream) is **higher** than the pressure behind it, and so it stops the blood flowing backwards.
- In an answer always say which chambers you compare: "the pressure in the left ventricle is higher than the pressure in the left atrium, so the AV valve closes".
:::

:::steps
1. **Diastole (the whole heart relaxed).** Blood from the veins flows into the atria and on through the **open AV valves** into the ventricles, because the pressure in the atria is a little higher than in the ventricles. The **semilunar valves are closed**, because the pressure in the aorta is higher than in the ventricle. The ventricles fill most of the way.
2. **Atrial systole (about 0.1 s).** The atria contract. The pressure in the atria rises slightly above that in the ventricles and pushes the last of the blood through the open AV valves. The ventricles are now full.
3. **Start of ventricular systole: AV valves close (event 1, about 0.10 s).** The ventricles begin to contract and the pressure in the ventricle rises **above** the pressure in the atrium. Blood pushes the flaps together, and the **AV valve closes**. The tendinous cords stop the flaps from being forced back into the atrium. This is the first heart sound ("lub").
4. **Semilunar valves open (event 2, about 0.17 s).** All the valves are closed for a moment while the ventricular pressure rises steeply. When the pressure in the ventricle rises **above** the pressure in the aorta, the **semilunar valve opens** and blood is forced out into the aorta. The aorta stretches and its pressure rises to its highest value.
5. **End of ventricular systole: semilunar valves close (event 3, about 0.38 s).** The ventricles begin to relax and the pressure in the ventricle falls **below** the pressure in the aorta. Blood in the aorta begins to flow back, fills the pockets of the semilunar valve and **closes** it. This is the second heart sound ("dub"). The aortic pressure stays high, because the elastic wall of the aorta recoils.
6. **Ventricles relax; AV valves open (event 4, about 0.45 s).** The ventricular pressure falls rapidly, and when it drops **below** the pressure in the atrium, the **AV valve opens**. Blood that gathered in the atrium while the AV valve was shut flows into the ventricle, and the cycle begins again.
:::

Read the graph in three parts. During atrial systole the pressure in the left atrium rises a little, to about 1.5 kPa, and the ventricle pressure rises slightly as it fills. During ventricular systole the left ventricle pressure rises to about 16 kPa, far higher than the atrium, and the aortic pressure follows it. During diastole the ventricle pressure falls to about 1 kPa by 0.45 s and stays below 1 kPa, while the aortic pressure falls slowly from about 14 to 10.5 kPa, as the elastic wall recoils. The small dip in the aortic pressure at event 3 is caused by the closing of the semilunar valve.

:::note Heart sounds
The sounds you hear through a stethoscope, "lub-dub", are the closing of the AV valves (lub) followed by the closing of the semilunar valves (dub). You do not need to know them for the exam, but they give you a way to remember the order.
:::

:::eg Using the pressure graph above: state the time at which the AV valve closes and explain why it closes. Then calculate the heart rate and the time for which the semilunar valve is open.
**The AV valve** closes at about 0.10 s (event 1). At that moment the pressure in the left ventricle rises above the pressure in the left atrium. [B1] The blood in the ventricle pushes the flaps of the valve together, so the valve closes and the blood cannot flow back into the atrium. [B1]

**Heart rate**: the whole cycle takes 0.8 s on the graph.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{heart rate} &= \frac{60}{0.8} \\ &= 75\ \text{beats per minute} \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

**Time the semilunar valve is open**: it opens at about 0.17 s (event 2) and closes at about 0.38 s (event 3).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{time open} &= 0.38 - 0.17 \\ &= 0.21\,\mathrm{s} \end{aligned}\]

During those 0.21 s the ventricular pressure is higher than the aortic pressure, and blood is ejected. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the valve closes because the ventricle contracts". The contraction raises the pressure, but a valve closes because of a **pressure difference** between two named chambers: the answer must say that the ventricular pressure is higher than the atrial pressure.
:::

## Controlling the heartbeat

:::def Myogenic
A heartbeat that starts in the cardiac muscle itself. The heart muscle contracts and relaxes rhythmically without a nerve impulse starting each beat.
:::

The heart beats by itself: a heart removed from the body and kept in the right solution goes on beating for a while. A small group of specialised cardiac muscle cells in the wall of the right atrium sets the pace. The nerves and hormones that speed up or slow down the heart are not needed for this syllabus.

:::def Sinoatrial node (SAN)
A small patch of specialised muscle cells in the wall of the right atrium, near where the vena cava enters, that generates a wave of electrical excitation at regular intervals. It is called the pacemaker.
:::

:::def Atrioventricular node (AVN)
A patch of specialised muscle tissue near the septum at the base of the right atrium. It receives the excitation from the atria, delays it, and passes it on to the ventricles.
:::

:::def Purkyne tissue
Specialised muscle fibres that carry the wave of excitation quickly along the septum (the bundle of His) and then through the walls of the ventricles, from the apex upwards.
:::

:::steps
1. The **SAN** fires and sends a **wave of excitation** across the walls of both atria. The atria contract together (atrial systole).
2. A layer of **non-conducting fibrous tissue** between the atria and the ventricles blocks the wave, so it cannot spread directly into the ventricles. The only way across is through the AVN.
3. The **AVN** picks up the excitation and **delays** it for about 0.1 s. This lets the atria finish contracting and the ventricles fill with blood before the ventricles contract.
4. The excitation then passes down the **bundle of His** in the septum and into the **Purkyne tissue**, which spreads it rapidly to the **apex** and up through the ventricle walls.
5. The ventricles contract **from the apex upwards**, which squeezes the blood up towards the arteries at the top of the heart (ventricular systole).
:::

:::key Roles, in the order they act
- **SAN**: sets the rhythm (pacemaker) and starts the wave of excitation across the atria.
- **Fibrous tissue**: stops the wave crossing straight to the ventricles.
- **AVN**: delays the wave (about 0.1 s), so atrial systole finishes before ventricular systole starts.
- **Bundle of His and Purkyne tissue**: carry the wave quickly down the septum to the apex and up the ventricle walls, so the ventricles contract together from the bottom upwards.
:::

:::warn The AVN is not the pacemaker
The **SAN** is the pacemaker. The AVN passes the wave on, with a delay. Also, the ventricles do not contract "from the top": they contract from the **apex upwards**, so blood is pushed towards the outlet at the top.
:::

:::note The ECG
A doctor can record the electrical activity of the heart on the skin as an electrocardiogram (ECG). Its waves match the spread of excitation: atrial excitation, delay at the AVN and ventricular excitation. The ECG is not required for this syllabus.
:::

### The electrical and pressure events together

The wave of excitation and the pressure changes are two views of the same heartbeat. The times below are for the model at 75 beats per minute, with time 0 at the moment the SAN fires.

| time / s | electrical event | pressure and valve event |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | SAN fires; wave spreads across the atria | atria contract (atrial systole); pressure in the left atrium rises to about 1.5 kPa; AV valves open, semilunar valves closed |
| 0 to 0.10 | wave is delayed at the AVN | atria finish contracting and the ventricles fill |
| about 0.10 | wave leaves the AVN, passes down the bundle of His and spreads through the Purkyne tissue | ventricular pressure rises above atrial pressure: AV valves close (event 1) |
| about 0.17 | ventricles contract from the apex upwards | ventricular pressure rises above aortic pressure: semilunar valves open (event 2) |
| about 0.38 | ventricles relax | ventricular pressure falls below aortic pressure: semilunar valves close (event 3) |
| about 0.45 | whole heart relaxed (diastole) | ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure: AV valves open (event 4); the ventricles fill again |

The delay of about 0.1 s at the AVN is exactly what separates the end of atrial systole from the start of ventricular systole. Without it the atria and ventricles would contract together, and the atria could not top up the ventricles first.

:::eg Explain the roles of the sinoatrial node, the atrioventricular node and the Purkyne tissue in the cardiac cycle.
Six marking points, in the order that the wave travels.

The sinoatrial node (SAN) acts as the pacemaker and starts the wave of excitation at regular intervals. [B1]

The wave spreads across the walls of the atria, so that they contract (atrial systole). [B1]

Fibrous tissue stops the wave from passing directly to the ventricles, so it must go through the atrioventricular node (AVN). [B1]

The AVN delays the wave by about 0.1 s, so that the atria finish contracting and the ventricles fill before they contract. [B1]

The wave passes down the septum (bundle of His) into the Purkyne tissue, which carries it quickly to the apex of the ventricles. [B1]

The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, forcing the blood up and out through the arteries. [B1]

A weaker answer says "the SAN makes the heart beat and the AVN and Purkyne tissue send signals to the ventricles". It does not say what the SAN's signal does to the atria, it never mentions the delay and does not explain why it matters, and it misses the apex-up contraction.
:::

:::eg Real context, artificial pacemakers: in a patient whose SAN does not work properly, the heart beats too slowly. A surgeon fits an artificial pacemaker, which sends small electrical pulses to the heart muscle at a steady rate. Explain how this device restores a normal heartbeat.
The artificial pacemaker does the job of the SAN.

The device sends regular electrical impulses to the wall of the right atrium (or ventricle), in place of the SAN. [B1]

The impulse spreads as a wave of excitation, so the atria contract, and the wave goes on through the AVN and the Purkyne tissue to the ventricles, which contract in the normal order. [B1]

So the heart contracts at a regular rate, and enough blood is pumped to the body. [B1]

(Also credit: some devices sense the heart's own beat and fire only when it is missing.)
:::

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: Name the chamber of the heart that pumps blood to the lungs and the chamber that pumps blood to the rest of the body.
A: The right ventricle pumps blood to the lungs; the left ventricle pumps blood to the rest of the body.
Q: State the function of the tendinous cords.
A: They attach the flaps of the atrioventricular valves to the ventricle wall and stop the flaps turning inside out into the atrium when the ventricle contracts.
Q: Explain why the walls of the atria are thinner than the walls of the ventricles.
A: The atria pump blood only a short distance into the ventricles, which needs only a low pressure. The ventricles pump blood out of the heart, so they need more muscle to produce a higher pressure.
Q: A heart takes 0.60 s for one complete cardiac cycle. Calculate the heart rate.
A: \(60 \div 0.60 = 100\) beats per minute.
Q: A person's heart beats 18 times in 15 s. Calculate the time for one cardiac cycle and the heart rate.
A: Time \(= 15 \div 18 = 0.83\) s. Heart rate \(= 18 \times 4 = 72\) beats per minute (or \(60 \div 0.833\)).
Q: Explain why a semilunar valve closes at the end of ventricular systole.
A: The ventricle relaxes, so the pressure in the ventricle falls below the pressure in the aorta. Blood in the aorta tries to flow back and fills the pockets of the valve, which closes it.
Q: State two functions of the septum.
A: It separates the left and right sides of the heart, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix; and the conducting tissue of the bundle of His runs through it. (Also credit: it allows different pressures on the two sides.)
Q: Name the tissue that delays the wave of excitation between the atria and the ventricles and state why the delay is needed.
A: The atrioventricular node (AVN). The delay lets the atria finish contracting and the ventricles fill with blood before the ventricles contract.
Q: Explain why the coronary arteries are needed even though the heart is full of blood.
A: The cardiac muscle of the thick walls is too far from the blood in the chambers for oxygen to diffuse in. The coronary arteries bring oxygenated blood and glucose directly to the muscle cells.
Q: The wall of the left ventricle of a heart is 11 mm thick and that of the right ventricle is 3.5 mm thick. Calculate how many times thicker the left wall is.
A: \(11 \div 3.5 = 3.1\) times thicker.

(a) Explain how the thickness of the right ventricle wall is related to its function. (b) Describe what would happen to the pressure of the blood entering the lung capillaries if the wall were as thick as the left ventricle's, and suggest the effect.
H: What pressure does a thicker wall produce?
H: For (b), what do the walls of lung capillaries look like?
A: (a) The right ventricle pumps blood only to the lungs, a short distance, so a low pressure is enough and a thin wall is enough. [B1] A low pressure avoids damaging the thin walls of the lung capillaries. [B1]
(b) The pressure would be higher. [B1] The thin capillaries could be damaged, and more tissue fluid would be forced out of them into the alveoli. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A patient has a faulty atrioventricular node, so that the wave of excitation is not passed to the ventricles. (a) Describe the effect on the contraction of the atria and ventricles. (b) A surgeon implants an artificial device. Suggest what the device must do for the heart to work normally.
A: (a) The SAN still starts the wave of excitation, so the atria still contract. [B1] The wave does not reach the Purkyne tissue, so the ventricles do not contract (or contract at their own slow rate and not in time with the atria). [B1]
(b) It must send a signal to the ventricles (the bundle of His) in the right sequence after atrial systole. [B1] It should send it about 0.1 s after the atria begin to contract, so the ventricles fill first. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] (#bio.mammaltransport) ★★★ In a healthy heart, the volume of blood leaving the left ventricle in each beat is the same as the volume leaving the right ventricle. A student wrote: "The left ventricle has a thicker wall because it pumps more blood." (a) Evaluate this statement. (b) A faulty valve between the left ventricle and the aorta lets some blood leak back into the ventricle during diastole. Suggest two effects on the heart and the circulation.
H: Think of the volume and the pressure separately.
H: For (b), what happens to the pressure in the aorta, and to the volume that the ventricle must pump?
A: (a) The statement is wrong: both ventricles pump the same volume of blood in each beat. [B1] The left ventricle has a thicker wall because it must produce a higher pressure to push the blood through a longer, higher-resistance circuit to the whole body. [B1] More muscle gives a greater force of contraction. [B1]
(b) Some blood flows back into the ventricle, so the ventricle must pump more blood to deliver the same volume to the body, and works harder. [B1] The pressure in the aorta falls more quickly in diastole, so the pressure and flow to the tissues may be lower. [B1]
(Also credit: the ventricle wall may thicken and enlarge with the extra work.)
Q: [4 marks] (#bio.mammaltransport) ★★★ A baby is born with a hole in the septum between the two ventricles. Blood pressure is higher in the left ventricle than in the right during systole. (a) Suggest the direction in which blood flows through the hole during ventricular systole and explain why. (b) Suggest two consequences for the blood in the pulmonary artery and for the lungs.
H: Blood flows from higher pressure to lower pressure.
A: (a) Blood flows from the left ventricle into the right ventricle. [B1] The pressure in the left ventricle is higher than in the right ventricle. [B1]
(b) The pulmonary artery receives extra, oxygenated blood from the left ventricle, so the volume and pressure of blood in the lungs rise. [B1] Raised pressure may damage the lung capillaries and force tissue fluid into the lungs. [B1]
(Also credit: less oxygenated blood leaves the left ventricle for the body, so the tissues may receive less oxygen.)

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A drug lengthens the delay at the atrioventricular node from 0.1 s to 0.3 s. The sinoatrial node still fires every 0.8 s and ventricular systole still lasts 0.3 s. (a) State the time after the SAN fires at which the ventricles begin to contract, and calculate the time left for diastole in each cycle. (b) Explain why the drug could reduce the filling of the ventricles. (c) Suggest why the atria still contract normally.
H: Diastole is the time left in the 0.8 s cycle after ventricular systole ends.
H: For (c), which structure starts the wave across the atria?
A: (a) The ventricles begin to contract 0.3 s after the SAN fires. [B1] Diastole \(= 0.8 - 0.3 - 0.3\) [M1] \(= 0.2\) s [A1]
(b) The ventricles fill during diastole, which is now only 0.2 s instead of 0.4 s, so there is less time for blood to flow in. [B1]

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ During exercise the heart rate of a person rises from 60 to 150 beats per minute. At rest, the heart has a cycle of 1.0 s, of which atrial and ventricular systole take 0.4 s together. At 150 beats per minute, systole takes 0.3 s in total. (a) Calculate the cycle time at 150 beats per minute, and the time for diastole at rest and at 150 beats per minute. (b) Calculate the percentage fall in the time for diastole and compare it with the percentage fall in the time for systole. (c) Suggest why it matters that diastole shortens by more than systole when the heart rate rises.
H: Diastole \(=\) cycle time \(-\) time for systole.
H: Percentage fall \(=\) (old time − new time) ÷ old time × 100.
A: (a) Cycle time at 150 bpm \(= 60 \div 150 = 0.40\) s [A1] Diastole at rest \(= 1.0 - 0.4 = 0.6\) s, and at 150 bpm \(= 0.40 - 0.30 = 0.10\) s [A1]

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ In a patient, one coronary artery is partly blocked by a fatty deposit. The patient has chest pain on exercise but not at rest. (a) Explain why the heart muscle has enough oxygen at rest but not during exercise. (b) Explain why a complete blockage may cause the death of part of the muscle. (c) State why the heart muscle cells cannot get enough oxygen from the blood in the chambers.
H: What does the heart need more of during exercise, and what limits the supply through a narrowed artery?
A: (a) At rest the narrowed artery still delivers enough oxygenated blood for the slower rate of work. [B1] During exercise the heart beats faster and harder, so the muscle needs more oxygen than the narrowed artery can supply. [B1]
(b) A complete blockage stops oxygen and glucose reaching that part of the muscle, so its cells cannot respire, and they die (heart attack). [B1] [B1]
(c) The wall is thick, so the oxygen would have to diffuse a long way (a long diffusion distance), which is too slow. [B1]

:::recap
- The heart has four chambers, a septum, atrioventricular valves (tricuspid on the right, bicuspid on the left) held by tendinous cords, and semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery. Coronary arteries supply the cardiac muscle.
- Atria: thin walls, low pressure, short distance. Ventricles: thick walls, higher pressure. Left ventricle thickest: it pumps blood to the whole body. Right ventricle thinner: pumps only to the lungs at low pressure, which protects the lung capillaries. Both pump the same volume.
- Cardiac cycle: atrial systole, ventricular systole, diastole (about 0.1, 0.3 and 0.4 s at 75 beats per minute). Heart rate \(= 60 \div\) cycle time (s).
- Valves open and close because of pressure differences. AV valves close when ventricular pressure is higher than atrial pressure; semilunar valves open when ventricular pressure is higher than aortic pressure and close when it falls below it; AV valves open when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure.
- Cardiac muscle is myogenic. The SAN (pacemaker) starts a wave of excitation across the atria; fibrous tissue blocks it from the ventricles; the AVN delays it by about 0.1 s; the bundle of His and the Purkyne tissue carry it to the apex and up the ventricles.
:::
