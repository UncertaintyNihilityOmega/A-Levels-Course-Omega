Vaccination is one of the most successful public health measures ever: it removed smallpox from the world and has made once-feared diseases rare. A: Plasma cells secrete antibodies; memory cells give the fast secondary response.

## The structure of an antibody

:::def Antibody (immunoglobulin)
A protein made by plasma cells that binds to one specific antigen. It is made of four polypeptide chains: two identical heavy chains and two identical light chains, held together by disulfide bonds.
:::

An antibody is a protein with **quaternary structure**, so everything you know about proteins applies. Its four polypeptides fold into a **Y shape**.

Learn the regions of the Y and what each one does.

| Region | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **variable region** | at the tip of each arm: made by parts of one heavy and one light chain, with an amino acid sequence that differs from one antibody to the next | forms an **antigen-binding site** with a shape **complementary** to one antigen only |
| **antigen-binding sites** | two, one on each arm, identical to each other | each can bind one antigen, so one antibody can bind two |
| **constant region** | the stem and the rest of each arm: the same amino acid sequence in all antibodies of the same class | the same shape in every antibody, so phagocytes can bind to it |
| **hinge region** | a flexible part where the arms join the stem | lets the arms swing apart, so the antibody can bind to antigens that are different distances apart |
| **disulfide bonds** | covalent bonds between chains | hold the four polypeptides together |

The variable region is a good example of a link that biology keeps making: **primary structure decides shape and shape decides function**. The sequence of amino acids in the variable region folds into a particular tertiary shape, and only an antigen with a complementary shape binds to it, just as in an enzyme's active site (see [Enzymes](#bio.enzymes)). The body makes millions of different antibodies, each with a different variable region, so there is an antibody for almost any antigen.

:::key Antibody structure in one box
- Four polypeptides: **two heavy, two light**, joined by **disulfide bonds**: quaternary structure.
- **Y-shaped.** The **variable regions** at the tips make **two identical antigen-binding sites**, complementary to one antigen.
- The **constant region** is the same in every antibody of a class (phagocytes recognise it). The **hinge** gives flexibility.
:::

:::eg Explain how the structure of an antibody molecule is related to its functions.
Five marking points: structure on one side, function on the other.

An antibody is made of four polypeptides (two heavy and two light chains) held together by disulfide bonds. [B1]

The variable regions at the tips of the arms have a specific sequence of amino acids, so they have a tertiary shape that is complementary to one antigen only. [B1]

There are two identical antigen-binding sites on each molecule, so an antibody can bind to two antigens and link pathogens together (agglutination). [B1]

The constant region has the same shape in all antibodies of a class, so phagocytes can bind to it and engulf the pathogen that the antibody is attached to. [B1]

The hinge region is flexible, so the two arms can move apart and bind to antigens that are different distances apart. [B1]

(Also credit: the antibody binding to a toxin or virus stops it binding to receptors on body cells.)

A weaker answer says "antibodies are Y-shaped and fit the antigen". It does not name the variable region or the complementary shape, so it scores one mark at most.
:::

## What antibodies do

Antibodies do **not** kill pathogens. They bind to antigens, and that has four effects.

| Function | How it works |
|---|---|
| **neutralisation** | an antibody bound to a toxin (an antitoxin) or to a virus stops it binding to receptors on body cells, so it can do no harm |
| **preventing binding to cells** | an antibody covering the surface proteins of a pathogen stops it attaching to, and entering, body cells |
| **agglutination** | the two binding sites join pathogens into clumps, which phagocytes can engulf together, and which cannot spread easily |
| **labelling for phagocytes (opsonisation)** | the constant regions of bound antibodies stick out of the pathogen. Receptors on a phagocyte bind to them, so the pathogen is engulfed more readily |

:::warn Where the marks are
"Antibodies kill bacteria" scores nothing. Write the function (neutralise, agglutinate, label for phagocytes) and the structure that allows it (two binding sites, or constant region). A question about "how antibody structure is related to function" wants both halves.
:::

## Monoclonal antibodies

When you are infected, many different B-lymphocytes respond. Each has its own receptor and each recognises a different part of the antigen, so your blood contains a mixture of antibodies. Scientists wanted a supply of a **single** antibody, in large amounts.

:::def Monoclonal antibody
An antibody of one type only, made by a clone of identical cells. Every molecule has the same antigen-binding sites, so all of them bind the same antigen.
:::

:::def Hybridoma
A cell made by fusing a B-lymphocyte (plasma cell) that makes a wanted antibody with a myeloma (cancer) cell. It makes that antibody and divides indefinitely.
:::

A B-lymphocyte makes the right antibody, but divides only a few times in culture. A **myeloma** cell (a tumour cell from a B-lymphocyte) divides without limit, but makes no useful antibody. A **hybridoma** has both useful properties.

### The hybridoma method

:::steps
1. **Inject** a mouse with the **antigen**. The mouse makes B-lymphocytes (plasma cells) that make antibodies against it.
2. **Remove** the mouse's **spleen** and take out the **B-lymphocytes** that make the antibody.
3. **Fuse** these cells with **myeloma cells**, using polyethylene glycol or an electric pulse, to make **hybridoma cells**.
4. **Screen** the hybridoma cells. Grow each in a separate well and test its medium for the wanted antibody, because the mouse made B cells for many different antigens.
5. **Clone** the hybridoma cell that makes the wanted antibody. Grow it in large fermenters in culture medium.
6. **Collect** the antibody from the medium and **purify** it.
:::

:::note Humanised antibodies
Antibodies made in mice are non-self to a human. They may be recognised as foreign and removed, so antibodies used in treatment are usually modified to be more like human antibodies.
:::

## Using monoclonal antibodies

Because a monoclonal antibody binds to **one antigen only**, it can find that antigen in a mixture. Two uses follow: it can detect something (diagnosis) or deliver something (treatment).

### Diagnosis

A **labelled** antibody has a molecule attached that can be seen or detected (a coloured bead, a fluorescent dye or a radioactive atom). It binds to its antigen and shows where the antigen is.
- **Pregnancy tests** detect the hormone **hCG**, which is in the urine of a pregnant woman.
- **Locating cancer cells:** an antibody that binds to an antigen found only on one type of cancer cell and carries a radioactive or fluorescent label shows, in a scan, where the cancer cells are.
- **Locating blood clots:** an antibody that binds to a protein found in clots, with a radioactive label, shows where a clot is.
- **Identifying pathogens:** antibodies that bind to an antigen of a pathogen are used in some rapid tests, for example for malaria.

:::eg Real context, a home pregnancy test: explain how a test strip detects the hormone hCG in urine.
A strip has three zones: one holds antibodies that are not fixed, and two lines each hold antibodies that are fixed. All of them are monoclonal.

The first zone contains antibodies specific to hCG, with a coloured label, which are free to move. [B1]

hCG in the urine binds to these labelled antibodies, forming an antibody–hCG complex that moves along the strip. [B1]

At the test line there are fixed antibodies, also specific to hCG. They bind to the complex and hold it, so the coloured label builds up as a visible line. [B1]

At the control line, fixed antibodies bind to the labelled antibodies whether or not hCG is attached, so a second line shows that the test has worked. [B1]

If there is no hCG, the labelled antibodies pass the test line and only the control line appears (negative result). [B1]

The key point is specificity: only hCG, not any other molecule in urine, has the shape that fits the antibodies.
:::

### Treatment

- **Marking cancer cells.** An antibody that binds to an antigen on the surface of a cancer cell makes the cell easier for phagocytes and T-killer cells to find and destroy.
- **Blocking receptors.** An antibody that binds to a receptor on a cancer cell can block the signal that tells the cell to divide.
- **Delivering a drug.** An anticancer drug is attached to a monoclonal antibody. The antibody binds only to cancer cells, so the drug is delivered to them, and healthy cells receive less of it, so there are fewer side effects.

:::tip Why specificity is the whole point
Every use of monoclonal antibodies in a question comes back to one chain: the antibody has a **variable region complementary to one antigen**, so it binds **only** to cells or molecules that carry it. Write that chain first, then say what is attached (a label for diagnosis, a drug for treatment).
:::

## Types of immunity

:::def Active immunity
Immunity produced when the body's own immune system makes antibodies (and memory cells) in response to an antigen. It takes time to start and it lasts a long time, because memory cells are made.
:::

:::def Passive immunity
Immunity produced by receiving antibodies that were made by another individual. It acts at once but it is short-lived, because no memory cells are made and the antibodies are broken down.
:::

:::def Natural immunity
Immunity gained in everyday life, without medical treatment: through infection, or through the mother.
:::

:::def Artificial immunity
Immunity gained as a result of medical treatment: vaccination or an injection of antibodies.
:::

The two pairs are separate questions. **Active or passive** asks: *whose antibodies?* (your own, or someone else's). **Natural or artificial** asks: *how did you get it?* (in everyday life, or by medical treatment). Together they give four types.

| | Active (your own antibodies and memory cells) | Passive (someone else's antibodies) |
|---|---|---|
| **natural** | recovering from an infection | antibodies from the mother across the **placenta**, and in **breast milk** |
| **artificial** | **vaccination** | **injection of antibodies** (for example antivenom, or anti-tetanus antibodies) |

| | Active immunity | Passive immunity |
|---|---|---|
| source of antibodies | the person's own plasma cells | another person or animal |
| memory cells made | yes | no |
| how soon it works | slowly: days (a primary response) | at once |
| how long it lasts | long-term | short: weeks to months |

## Vaccines

:::def Vaccine
A preparation containing antigens of a pathogen (or instructions to make them) that is given to stimulate an immune response and make memory cells, without causing the disease.
:::

A vaccine contains **antigens**, not antibodies. When it is given:
1. the antigens stimulate a **primary immune response** (macrophages, T-helper cells, B cells, plasma cells);
2. the person makes **antibodies** and **memory cells**, but does not get the disease;
3. if the real pathogen arrives later, the memory cells cause a **fast secondary response**, which destroys the pathogen before it makes the person ill.

:::key How a vaccine gives long-term immunity
1. The vaccine contains **antigens** (not antibodies) and cannot cause the disease.
2. It causes a **primary response**: antibodies and **memory cells** are made.
3. Memory cells are **long-lived**, so on later infection there is a **rapid secondary response**.
:::

Vaccines have different forms:

| Type | What it contains | Example idea |
|---|---|---|
| **live attenuated** | the whole pathogen, weakened so that it does not cause disease | many virus vaccines; a strong, long-lasting response, but not safe for people with a weak immune system |
| **inactivated (killed)** | whole pathogens that have been killed | safe, but the response is weaker, so booster doses are needed |
| **toxoid** | a toxin that has been made harmless, but still has the antigen shape | the tetanus vaccine: the body makes antibodies that neutralise the real toxin |
| **purified antigen (subunit)** | only one protein or antigen from the pathogen | no whole pathogen at all, so very safe; boosters are often needed |
| **mRNA** | mRNA that carries instructions for one antigen protein | the body's own cells use it to make the antigen, which then stimulates a response. The mRNA is soon broken down and does not change the cells' DNA |

A **booster dose** is a later dose of the vaccine. It acts as a second exposure: memory cells divide, the secondary response gives a higher concentration of antibody and more memory cells, so protection is stronger and lasts longer.

:::warn Vaccines do not contain antibodies
A vaccine contains **antigens**. Antibodies are not given (that would be passive immunity). In an answer, never write "the vaccine contains antibodies" or "the vaccine kills the pathogen".
:::

## Vaccination programmes and herd immunity

:::def Herd immunity
The protection of people who are not immune when a large proportion of the population is immune. The pathogen finds few susceptible people, so chains of transmission are broken and it cannot spread.
:::

Not everyone can be vaccinated: babies who are too young, and people with a weak immune system. If most people around them are immune, these people are also protected. The proportion that must be immune depends on how easily the pathogen spreads: a very infectious disease such as measles needs about 95% of the population to be immune.

:::eg Real context, a vaccination programme: the table shows the percentage of children vaccinated against a disease and the number of cases per 100 000 people in the following year in six regions (invented data). (a) Describe the relationship. (b) Calculate the percentage decrease in the number of cases between the lowest and the highest vaccination coverage. (c) Explain the relationship.

| 85 | 70 |

**(a)** As the percentage vaccinated rises from 55% to 96%, the number of cases falls from 410 to 2 per 100 000. [B1]

The fall is steep and steady between 55% and 85% (from 410 to 70), and slower above 85% (to 12 at 92% and 2 at 96%). [B1]

**(b)** Percentage decrease is the change divided by the starting value.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{decrease} &= \frac{410 - 2}{410} \times 100 \\ &= 99.5\% \end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]

**(c)** Vaccinated people have memory cells, so they do not become infected, and cannot pass the pathogen on. [B1]

When a high proportion is immune, the pathogen is passed on to few susceptible people, so chains of transmission are broken (herd immunity). [B1]

This also protects the unvaccinated, so cases fall even in people who were not vaccinated. [B1]
:::

### Why some programmes work and others do not

**Smallpox** was eradicated: the World Health Organization declared in 1980 that it no longer occurred anywhere. The disease suited a vaccination programme:
- the virus has **stable antigens**: it did not change, so one vaccine worked for every case;
- it infects **only humans**, so there was no animal reservoir to re-infect people;
- the rash made **cases easy to see**, and people were infectious mainly when they had symptoms, so cases could be found and their contacts traced;
- the vaccine was effective, cheap and easy to store;
- **ring vaccination**: when a case was found, the people around it (contacts, and contacts of contacts) were vaccinated, so the virus found no one to infect.

Other programmes are harder:

| Problem | Why it makes vaccination harder | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **antigenic variation** | the pathogen's surface antigens change (mutation), so memory cells made against the old antigens no longer fit, and a new vaccine is needed | influenza |
| **many strains** | one vaccine may cover only some of the types | many bacteria have dozens of strains |
| **pathogen hides inside cells** | antibodies cannot reach it, and it may have different antigens at different stages of its life cycle | *Plasmodium* (malaria), HIV |
| **storage and cost** | vaccines must be kept cold (the cold chain) and cost money to make and give | hot countries with poor roads or electricity |
| **people do not take part** | people may not be able to reach a clinic, may fear side effects, may refuse, or may miss booster doses | any programme |

:::eg Explain why one course of vaccination against measles gives long-term immunity, but a new influenza vaccine is needed every year.
Five marking points: two for measles and three for influenza.

The measles virus has antigens that do not change, so memory cells made after vaccination remain complementary to the virus. [B1]

The vaccine causes a primary response that makes long-lived memory cells, which give a rapid secondary response if the real virus infects. [B1]

The influenza virus shows antigenic variation: mutations change the shape of its surface antigens. [B1]

Memory cells from an earlier infection or vaccine have receptors that are not complementary to the new antigens. [B1]

So a new vaccine, containing the antigens of the strains that are now circulating, is made every year. [B1]
:::

Questions ask why a programme succeeded or failed. Give the **biology** (stable or changing antigens, only humans, pathogens hidden inside cells) **and** the **practical** side (cost, storage, reaching people, public trust). Use the words "memory cells" and "herd immunity" when you explain what a high coverage achieves.

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State two features of an antibody molecule that are the same in all antibodies of a class and two that differ between antibodies.
A: The same: the constant region; the number of polypeptides (two heavy, two light) and the disulfide bonds. Different: the variable regions and so the shape of the antigen-binding sites.
Q: State the function of the hinge region of an antibody.
A: It is flexible, so the two arms can move apart and bind to antigens that are different distances apart.
Q: Explain why an antibody binds to one antigen only.
A: The variable region has a specific amino acid sequence, which gives the antigen-binding site a tertiary shape complementary to one antigen, so only that antigen can bind.
Q: Explain why myeloma cells are used in the hybridoma method.
A: Myeloma cells are tumour cells that divide indefinitely, so the hybridoma cells formed by fusion can be grown in large numbers for a long time, whereas B-lymphocytes alone divide only a few times in culture.
Q: Antivenom (antibodies against snake venom) is injected into a person bitten by a snake. State which type of immunity this gives, and explain why a second bite a year later would not be dealt with quickly by the person's own immune system.
A: Artificial passive immunity. The antibodies were made by another animal, so the person's body made no memory cells; the person has no faster response a year later, and the antibodies from the first injection have been broken down.
Q: Distinguish between natural and artificial immunity, and give one example of each that is active immunity.
A: Natural immunity is gained in everyday life, for example by recovering from an infection; artificial immunity is gained by medical treatment, for example by vaccination. In both, the person's own lymphocytes make the antibodies and memory cells.
Q: The diphtheria vaccine contains diphtheria toxin that has been treated so that it is harmless. Explain how it gives protection.
A: The harmless toxin still has the same antigens, so it causes a primary response and memory cells. If the real toxin is made in a later infection, memory cells quickly make plasma cells and antibodies that neutralise it.
Q: Explain why a booster dose of a vaccine is often needed.
A: The booster acts as a second exposure to the antigen, so memory cells divide and make more memory cells and plasma cells (a secondary response), giving a higher concentration of antibody and longer protection.
Q: State what is meant by herd immunity.
A: When a large proportion of the population is immune, the pathogen cannot spread easily, so people who are not immune are also protected.
Q: Suggest why an antibody made by a mouse hybridoma may not be suitable to treat a person without modification.
A: The mouse antibody is a foreign protein to a human, so the person's immune system may treat it as non-self and attack it.

Q: [3 marks] ★ State three ways in which antibodies help to protect the body against pathogens.
A: Any three of: neutralise toxins (or viruses) by binding to them [B1]
agglutinate (clump) pathogens so phagocytes can engulf them [B1]
label pathogens (opsonisation) so phagocytes engulf them more readily [B1]
(Also credit: stop pathogens attaching to or entering body cells.)
Q: [3 marks] ★★ Monoclonal antibodies can be used to deliver an anticancer drug to cancer cells. (a) Explain how this works. (b) State one advantage of treating cancer in this way.
H: What is the antibody specific to, and what is attached to it?
A: (a) The antibody has a variable region that is complementary to an antigen on the surface of the cancer cells. [B1]
The drug is attached to the antibody, which binds only to the cancer cells, so the drug is delivered to them. [B1]
(b) Healthy cells receive much less of the drug, so there are fewer side effects. [B1]
(Also credit: a lower dose is needed.)
Q: [6 marks] ★★ In an outbreak of smallpox in a village, ring vaccination started at the end of week 3. The table shows the number of new cases each week.

| week | new cases |
|---|---|
| 1 | 8 |
| 2 | 15 |
| 3 | 26 |
| 4 | 21 |
| 5 | 6 |
| 6 | 1 |
| 7 | 0 |

(a) Describe the change in the number of new cases. (b) Calculate the percentage decrease in new cases from week 3 to week 5. (c) Suggest why the number of new cases was still high in week 4, after vaccination had started, and explain how ring vaccination stops the spread.
H: For (c), how long does a primary immune response take, and how long between infection and the first symptoms?
A: (a) New cases rise from 8 in week 1 to a peak of 26 in week 3. [B1]
They then fall, to 6 in week 5 and 0 in week 7. [B1]
(b) \((26 - 6) \div 26 \times 100\) [M1] \(= 77\%\) [A1]
(c) People already infected were incubating the virus, and vaccinated people take days to make a primary response, so some cases still appeared. [B1]
Ring vaccination makes the people around each case immune, so the virus has no susceptible people to infect and the chain of transmission is broken. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain why it has been difficult to develop an effective vaccine against *Plasmodium*, the pathogen that causes malaria.
H: Where in the body does *Plasmodium* spend most of its life, and are the antigens always the same?
A: *Plasmodium* spends part of its life cycle inside liver cells and red blood cells, where antibodies cannot reach it. [B1]
It has different forms in the host, with different antigens at each stage, so a vaccine against one stage does not protect against the others. [B1]
There are several species and strains with different antigens. [B1]
Memory cells made against one antigen are not effective against the others. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (a) Explain why the B-lymphocytes taken from the spleen of an immunised mouse cannot be used alone to make large amounts of one antibody. (b) Explain why the hybridoma cells must be screened.
A: (a) The B-lymphocytes divide only a limited number of times in culture, so they cannot be grown to make large amounts. [B1]
The mouse's spleen has B cells that make many different antibodies, so the antibody would not be of one type. [B1]
(b) Only some of the hybridoma cells make the antibody that is wanted, because the fused B cells were of many types. [B1]
Screening finds the hybridoma that makes it, and this one is cloned so that all of its cells make the same antibody. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ A monoclonal antibody that binds to a protein found in blood clots is labelled with a radioactive atom and injected into a patient. Explain how it shows where a clot is.
A: The antibody has a variable region complementary to the protein found in clots, so it binds to clots and not to other tissues. [B1]
The radioactive label stays with the antibody, so it builds up where the clots are. [B1]
A scanner detects the radiation, which shows the position of the clot. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] (#bio.proteins) ★★★ A mutation changes one amino acid in the variable region of an antibody from a plasma cell. (a) Explain why this could stop the antibody binding to its antigen. (b) Explain why a mutation in the amino acid sequence of the constant region may not stop the antibody binding to the antigen.
H: Which structure of a protein is changed first, and what follows from it?
A: (a) A change in the primary structure changes the way the polypeptide folds, so the tertiary shape of the variable region changes. [B1]
The antigen-binding site is then no longer complementary to the antigen. [B1]
So the antigen cannot bind (or binds less well). [B1]
(b) The antigen binds to the variable region, not the constant region. [B1]
A change in the constant region does not alter the shape of the antigen-binding sites. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A woman travelling abroad is bitten by a dog that may have rabies. She is given an injection of antibodies against the virus and, at the same time, the first dose of a rabies vaccine. Explain why she is given both.
H: One acts at once, the other protects for longer.
A: The injected antibodies are passive immunity, so they bind to the virus straight away. [B1]
This protects her while her own response is slow to start. [B1]
But the injected antibodies make no memory cells and are soon broken down, so alone they would give short-term protection only. [B1]
The vaccine contains antigens that stimulate her own primary response, making antibodies and memory cells (active immunity). [B1]
The memory cells give long-term protection by a rapid secondary response if the virus returns. [B1]

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A disease spreads in a population until herd immunity is reached when 75% of the population is immune. A vaccine gives immunity to 85% of the people who receive it. (a) In a programme 80% of the population is vaccinated. Calculate the percentage of the population that is immune because of the vaccine. (b) State whether herd immunity is reached. (c) Calculate the percentage of the population that must be vaccinated to reach herd immunity. (d) Suggest one reason why a country may fail to reach this percentage.
H: The fraction immune is the fraction vaccinated multiplied by the fraction the vaccine works for.
H: For (c), rearrange: coverage \(=\) immune fraction needed \(\div\) 0.85.
A: (a) \(0.80 \times 0.85\) [M1] \(= 0.68\), so 68% [A1]
(b) No: 68% is less than 75%. [B1]
(c) \(75 \div 85\) [M1] \(= 88.2\%\) [A1]
(d) Some people cannot reach clinics, refuse the vaccine, or the vaccine is too costly to store and deliver (cold chain). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A child cannot make antibodies because of an inherited condition. The child receives antibodies from blood donors by injection every month, and is not given vaccines. Explain why the injections are needed every month, and why vaccines would not work.
H: Whose antibodies are they? Are any memory cells made?
H: What does a vaccine need the child's own body to do?
A: The antibodies come from donors, so this is passive immunity. [B1]
No memory cells are made, so the child does not make a response of its own. [B1]
The antibodies are broken down within weeks, so protection fades. [B1]
So new antibodies must be injected regularly to keep the concentration high enough. [B1]
A vaccine works only if the child's own lymphocytes respond and make plasma cells and memory cells, and this child cannot do so. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which description of an antibody molecule is correct?
+ four polypeptides held by disulfide bonds, with two identical antigen-binding sites
- two polypeptides held by peptide bonds, with one antigen-binding site
- four identical polypeptides, each with its own antigen-binding site
- one polypeptide folded into a Y shape, with a variable region at the stem
> An antibody has two heavy and two light chains joined by disulfide bonds; the variable regions at the tips of the two arms form two identical antigen-binding sites. The stem is the constant region.
? Which part of an antibody is the same shape in all antibodies of a class?
+ the constant region
- the variable region
- the antigen-binding site
- the hinge region
> The constant region has the same amino acid sequence, so it can be recognised by phagocytes. The variable region and antigen-binding sites differ between antibodies, and the hinge gives flexibility.
? How do antibodies help to destroy a bacterium?
+ they label it so that phagocytes engulf it more easily
- they digest it with enzymes
- they make holes in its cell wall
- they release cytokines that stimulate memory cells
> Antibodies bind to antigens: they neutralise, clump and label pathogens. Enzymes from lysosomes digest pathogens in a phagocyte, and cytokines are released by T-helper cells.
? Why is a myeloma cell used to make a hybridoma?
+ it divides indefinitely
- it makes the antibody that is needed
- it is a type of T-lymphocyte
- it removes the need to inject a mouse
> The myeloma cell gives the hybridoma its unlimited division. The antibody comes from the B-lymphocyte.
? What does "monoclonal" mean in "monoclonal antibodies"?
+ the antibodies come from one clone of identical cells and bind one antigen
- the antibodies come from one patient
- the antibodies come from one species
- the antibodies bind to several different antigens
> All the antibodies are made by a single clone of hybridoma cells, so they are identical. The source (patient or mouse) is not what makes them monoclonal.
? Which gives artificial passive immunity?
+ an injection of antibodies made by a horse
- an injection of a vaccine that contains a harmless toxin
- antibodies that pass from a mother to her baby across the placenta
- recovery from an infection
> Antibodies made by another animal and given as medical treatment are artificial and passive. A vaccine gives artificial active immunity, antibodies from the mother are natural passive and recovery from infection is natural active.
? A hiker bitten by a snake is given antivenom, which contains antibodies against the venom. What is true of this immunity?
+ It acts at once but no memory cells are made.
- It is slow to act but gives memory cells.
- It lasts for years because the antibodies are copied.
- It is active, because the hiker's blood now contains antibodies.
> The antibodies were made in another animal, so the hiker's own lymphocytes were not involved: passive. The effect is immediate and short-term, and there are no memory cells.
? What does a vaccine contain?
+ antigens (or instructions to make them) from a pathogen
- antibodies against a pathogen
- memory cells
- plasma cells
> A vaccine gives antigens, so that the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells. Giving antibodies would be passive immunity.
? Which feature helped to make the eradication of smallpox possible?
+ the virus infected only humans, so there was no animal reservoir
- the virus changed its antigens each year, so memory cells kept up
- the vaccine contained antibodies that worked at once
- the virus lived inside cells where antibodies could not reach it
> Smallpox infected only humans, had stable antigens and produced a visible rash, so ring vaccination could find and remove every chain of transmission. Changing antigens and hidden pathogens make a programme harder, not easier, and a vaccine contains antigens, not antibodies.
? What is herd immunity?
+ non-immune people are protected because most of the population is immune
- every person in a population has been vaccinated
- a vaccine protects every person who receives it
- people gain immunity by being close to infected people
> A high proportion of immune people breaks the chains of transmission, so those who cannot be vaccinated also benefit. It does not require everyone to be immune.

:::recap
- Antibody: four polypeptides (two heavy, two light) joined by disulfide bonds, Y-shaped. Variable regions form two identical antigen-binding sites complementary to one antigen; the constant region is recognised by phagocytes; the hinge is flexible.
- Antibodies neutralise toxins and viruses, prevent binding to cells, agglutinate pathogens and label them for phagocytes. They do not kill pathogens.
- Monoclonal antibodies: mouse injected with antigen, spleen B cells fused with myeloma cells to make hybridomas, screened, cloned, grown in a fermenter and purified.
- Diagnosis uses labelled antibodies (pregnancy test for hCG; locating cancers and clots). Treatment: mark or block cancer cells, or deliver a drug to them.
- Active: own antibodies and memory cells, slow, long-term. Passive: another's antibodies, immediate, short-term, no memory. Natural: infection, placenta, breast milk. Artificial: vaccination, injected antibodies.
- Vaccines contain antigens (live attenuated, inactivated, toxoid, purified antigen, mRNA), cause a primary response and memory cells, so a later infection meets a fast secondary response. Boosters strengthen it.
- Herd immunity: with most people immune, transmission chains break and the unvaccinated are protected. Smallpox was eradicated (stable antigens, humans only, visible cases, ring vaccination). Antigenic variation, many strains, pathogens inside cells, cost and storage make other programmes harder.
:::
