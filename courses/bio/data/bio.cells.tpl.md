## Eukaryotic cells and their organelles

All living things are made of cells, and cells are of two kinds. In a **eukaryotic cell** the DNA is kept in a nucleus, and the cytoplasm contains many structures wrapped in membranes. Animals, plants, fungi and protoctists are made of eukaryotic cells. The other kind, the prokaryotic cell of a bacterium, is the subject of [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes).

:::def Eukaryotic cell
A cell that has a nucleus, in which the DNA is enclosed by a nuclear envelope, and membrane-bound organelles in the cytoplasm.
:::

:::def Organelle
A structure within a cell that has a particular function. Most organelles of eukaryotic cells are surrounded by one or two membranes.
:::

Think of a cell as a factory. The nucleus is the office that holds the instructions (DNA). Ribosomes are the machines that build products (proteins). The endoplasmic reticulum is the assembly line, the Golgi body is the packaging and dispatch department, and the cell surface membrane is the factory wall with a security gate. Mitochondria are the power stations that release energy in a form (ATP) that the rest of the factory can use. **Membranes divide the factory into compartments**, so that different reactions can happen in different places without interfering, and so that each organelle can hold the conditions it needs.

The drawings in this chapter are based on **electron micrographs**, because most organelles are too small to see with a light microscope. In a transmission electron micrograph (TEM) you see thin slices of organelles, so a long thin mitochondrion may look round in section. Learn the **shape cues** for each organelle (given below) rather than the exact outline.

## The cell surface membrane

Every cell is surrounded by a **cell surface membrane**. It is a thin layer made mainly of phospholipids and proteins (its structure is the subject of [Membrane structure and cell signalling](#bio.membranes)). It controls which substances enter and leave the cell, so it is **partially permeable**: water and some small molecules pass through, but large molecules and many ions need help from proteins. It also holds the cell's contents together, and it has receptors that let the cell respond to signals. In plant cells the membrane lies just inside the cell wall.

## The nucleus

:::def Nucleus
The largest organelle in most cells. It contains the cell's DNA and controls the activities of the cell by controlling which proteins are made.
:::

The nucleus has these parts:
- **Nuclear envelope**: two membranes, with **nuclear pores** through both. The pores let molecules such as the copies of genes that carry instructions for making protein (see [Protein synthesis and gene mutation](#bio.synthesis)) leave the nucleus, and let proteins that are needed in the nucleus enter.
- **Chromatin**: DNA wound around proteins. When a cell divides, it condenses into visible chromosomes ([Chromosomes and the mitotic cell cycle](#bio.mitosis)).
- **Nucleolus**: a dense, dark region inside the nucleus where **ribosomes are made**. It makes the RNA of the ribosomes and assembles it with proteins into ribosome subunits, which leave through the pores. There can be one or more nucleoli.

**Recognise it**: the biggest round or oval structure in the cell, with a double boundary (the envelope, often with small gaps for the pores) and a darkly stained nucleolus inside.

## Ribosomes

:::def Ribosome
A very small organelle, made of RNA and protein, that is the site of protein synthesis. It is not surrounded by a membrane.
:::

A ribosome has two parts (a large and a small subunit) and is only about 20–30 nm across, so you see ribosomes in a micrograph only as tiny dark dots. They are found free in the cytoplasm, where they make proteins that stay in the cell, and attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum, where they make proteins that are secreted or that become part of membranes.

Ribosomes are described by an **S value**, such as **80S** or **70S**. S stands for a measure of how fast a particle sediments when spun in a centrifuge, so it indicates the particle's size. A larger S value means a larger ribosome:
- **80S ribosomes** are found in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells, free or on the RER.
- **70S ribosomes** are smaller. They are found in **mitochondria and chloroplasts** (and in bacteria; see [Prokaryotes and viruses](#bio.prokaryotes)).

## Endoplasmic reticulum

:::def Rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER)
A network of flattened, membrane-bound sacs (cisternae) with ribosomes on the outer surface. Proteins made on these ribosomes pass into the sacs, where they are folded and processed, and are then carried away in vesicles.
:::

:::def Smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER)
A network of membrane-bound tubes and sacs with no ribosomes on its surface. It makes lipids, including phospholipids and steroids, and processes them.
:::

Cells that make a lot of protein for export (digestive gland cells) have a lot of RER. Cells that make steroid hormones or large amounts of lipid (cells in the testes or adrenal glands) have a lot of SER.

**Recognise it**: the RER is a stack of flat, parallel membranes with small black dots (ribosomes) along them. The SER is a set of smooth tubes with no dots.

## The Golgi body

:::def Golgi body
A stack of flattened, membrane-bound sacs, with vesicles at the edges, that modifies proteins and packages them into vesicles. Also called the Golgi apparatus or Golgi complex.
:::

The Golgi body receives vesicles from the RER on one side. Inside the sacs the proteins are **modified**: for example, carbohydrate groups are added to make glycoproteins. The finished products are **packaged** into vesicles that bud off from the other side. The Golgi body also makes lysosomes and, in plant cells, helps to make cell wall materials.

:::warn The Golgi body does not make proteins
Proteins are made on ribosomes. The Golgi body modifies, sorts and packages them. If an answer says that the Golgi body "makes" proteins it loses the mark.
:::

**Recognise it**: a stack of 4–8 curved, flat sacs that looks like a pile of pitta breads, with small round vesicles near the edges.

## Lysosomes

:::def Lysosome
A small, spherical organelle surrounded by a single membrane, which contains hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes. Lysosomes are made by the Golgi body.
:::

Lysosomes digest worn-out organelles and break down substances that the cell has taken in. White blood cells use them to digest bacteria that they have engulfed. The membrane keeps the enzymes away from the rest of the cell, which they would otherwise digest.

**Recognise it**: a small, round, single-membrane sac that stains evenly dark.

## The secretory pathway

Some cells make proteins to send out of the cell, such as digestive enzymes and hormones. The route that these proteins take through the cell links four of the organelles above.

:::html
%%FIG5%%
:::

:::key The secretory pathway (the marking points)
1. The protein is made on **ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum**.
2. It passes into the RER, where it is **folded** (and processed).
3. A **vesicle** buds off the RER and carries it to the **Golgi body**.
4. The Golgi body **modifies and packages** the protein into **secretory vesicles**.
5. The secretory vesicles move to the cell surface membrane and **fuse with it**, releasing the protein outside the cell. This is **exocytosis**.
:::

:::eg A cell in the pancreas makes a digestive enzyme and secretes it into the small intestine. Describe how the enzyme is made and leaves the cell.
This is a 5-mark question. Follow the secretory pathway, using the organelle names in order.

The enzyme (a protein) is made by ribosomes on the rough endoplasmic reticulum. [B1]

It passes into the RER, where it is folded into its three-dimensional shape. [B1]

Vesicles carry the enzyme from the RER to the Golgi body. [B1]

The Golgi body modifies the enzyme and packages it into secretory vesicles. [B1]

The vesicles move to the cell surface membrane and fuse with it, releasing the enzyme by exocytosis. [B1]

(Also credit: ATP from mitochondria provides the energy for the movement of vesicles and for protein synthesis.)

A weaker answer says "the enzyme is made in the nucleus and goes out through the membrane". The nucleus holds the instructions but does not make proteins, and the enzyme leaves in vesicles, not through the membrane.
:::

## Mitochondria

:::def Mitochondrion
An organelle surrounded by two membranes, the inner one folded into cristae. It is the site of aerobic respiration, which releases energy from nutrients in the form of ATP.
:::

:::html
%%FIG3%%
:::

- **Two membranes**: the smooth outer membrane and the inner membrane, which is folded into **cristae**. The folds give the inner membrane a large surface area for the reactions that make ATP.
- The **matrix** is the fluid inside, containing enzymes for respiration.
- The matrix also contains a small loop of **circular DNA** and **70S ribosomes**. Mitochondria use them to make some of their own proteins, and they can divide inside the cell.

:::note Why does a mitochondrion have its own DNA?
Mitochondria and chloroplasts have 70S ribosomes and circular DNA, like bacteria, and they have a double membrane. This is evidence for the endosymbiont theory: that they evolved from prokaryotes that were taken into larger cells. You do not need the detail of this theory, but it explains why these two organelles resemble bacteria.
:::

Cells that use a lot of energy, such as muscle cells and the cells in the walls of kidney tubules, have many mitochondria. You will meet the reactions of respiration in [Energy, ATP and respiratory quotient](#bio.energy) and [Aerobic respiration](#bio.respiration).

:::warn Mitochondria do not make energy
Energy cannot be made. Mitochondria **release** the energy stored in glucose and other nutrients, in the process of **respiration**, and use it to make **ATP**. Write "release energy" or "make ATP", never "make energy".
:::

**Recognise it**: a rod-shaped or oval structure with a double boundary and a folded inner membrane, which show as lines (cristae) running across the inside.

## Chloroplasts

:::def Chloroplast
An organelle surrounded by two membranes (the envelope), found in plant cells that photosynthesise. It contains stacks of flattened thylakoids (grana) in a fluid stroma, and is the site of photosynthesis.
:::

:::html
%%FIG4%%
:::

- The **envelope** is two membranes.
- Inside are **thylakoids**, flattened membrane sacs that contain the green pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs light. Thylakoids are stacked into **grana** (singular: granum) and joined by single thylakoids.
- The **stroma** is the fluid around the grana, which contains enzymes that build sugars. It may also contain **starch grains** (a store of carbohydrate), circular DNA and 70S ribosomes.

Chloroplasts are found in the leaf cells of plants and in the green parts of stems. They are not found in root cells (no light). You will learn how photosynthesis works in [Photosynthesis](#bio.photosynthesis).

**Recognise it**: a larger, oval structure with a double boundary and neat stacks of flat discs (grana). The mitochondrion has folds across its inside; the chloroplast has stacks of discs.

## Centrioles, microtubules, cilia and microvilli

### Microtubules and centrioles

:::def Microtubule
A hollow tube made of protein (tubulin). Microtubules form part of the cytoskeleton, which supports the cell and gives it shape, and they make the spindle in cell division and move vesicles and organelles around the cell.
:::

:::def Centriole
A small hollow cylinder made of microtubules (nine triplets in a ring). Animal cells have a pair of centrioles at right angles near the nucleus, which organise the microtubules of the spindle when the cell divides.
:::

**Typical plant cells do not have centrioles.** Their spindles form without them. In a micrograph, a centriole cut lengthways looks like a short rod, and the other centriole, at right angles to it, appears as a small ring with nine groups of tubes if cut across.

### Cilia

:::def Cilium
A hair-like projection from the cell surface, containing microtubules (in the usual arrangement, a ring of nine pairs and two in the centre). Cilia beat to move fluid, mucus or particles over the cell surface, or to move the cell itself.
:::

:::html
%%FIG6%%
:::

Cells lining the airways have cilia that beat in waves and sweep mucus, with trapped dust and bacteria, up and out of the lungs. Cells lining the oviduct have cilia that move the egg along. Beating takes energy (ATP), so these cells contain many mitochondria close to the base of the cilia.

### Microvilli

:::def Microvillus
A very small, finger-like projection of the cell surface membrane that increases the surface area of the cell for absorption or secretion.
:::

The cells lining the small intestine have thousands of microvilli on their surface facing the gut. Together they form a "brush border" that gives a very large area for absorbing digested food. Microvilli do not move. A cilium is longer and **moves**; a microvillus is shorter and **does not** (it just increases area).

## Plant cells

Plant cells have the organelles above except centrioles, cilia and microvilli (and lysosomes are not usually drawn in plant cells). They also have four features that animal cells lack.

:::html
%%FIG2%%
:::

:::def Cell wall
A rigid layer outside the cell surface membrane of plant cells, made mainly of the polysaccharide cellulose. It supports the cell, gives it its shape and prevents it from bursting if water enters it. It is fully permeable: it does not control which substances pass through.
:::

:::def Plasmodesma
A narrow channel through the cell walls of adjacent plant cells, lined by the cell surface membrane and containing a thin strand of cytoplasm, through which substances can pass from one cell to the next. Plural: plasmodesmata.
:::

:::def Large permanent vacuole and tonoplast
The large permanent vacuole of a plant cell is a fluid-filled sac that contains cell sap: a solution of water, sugars, amino acids, mineral ions and pigments. It is surrounded by a single membrane called the tonoplast, which is partially permeable.
:::

- **Cell wall**: Its cellulose fibres are strong, which resists the pressure of the cell contents. A plant cell can take in water without bursting (see [Movement into and out of cells](#bio.movement)). See also [Carbohydrates](#bio.carbs).
- **Plasmodesmata** link the cytoplasm of neighbouring cells, so water, assimilates and signals can pass between cells without having to cross a membrane and a wall.
- **Large permanent vacuole**: stores sugars, ions and pigments, and the pressure of its contents against the cell wall holds the cell firm. This keeps soft plant tissue upright. An animal cell has only small, temporary vacuoles.
- **Chloroplasts**: in cells that photosynthesise.

:::warn Cell wall and cell membrane are not the same
The cell wall is a thick, rigid layer of cellulose outside the cell, fully permeable. The cell surface membrane is a thin, partially permeable layer of phospholipid and protein just inside it. Animal cells have a membrane but no wall.
:::

| Feature | Animal cell | Plant cell |
|---|---|---|
| cell surface membrane, nucleus, ribosomes, RER, SER, Golgi body, mitochondria | present | present |
| cell wall | absent | present (cellulose) |
| chloroplasts | absent | present in photosynthetic cells |
| large permanent vacuole and tonoplast | absent (small temporary vacuoles only) | present |
| plasmodesmata | absent | present |
| centrioles | present | absent in typical cells |
| lysosomes | present | not usually seen |
| cilia and microvilli | some cells | not in typical cells |
| carbohydrate store | glycogen | starch |
| shape | irregular or rounded | fixed, often regular |

:::eg Compare the structure of a typical animal cell and a typical plant cell.
This is a 4-mark question. The mark scheme wants differences stated as comparisons, with both cells mentioned.

A plant cell has a cellulose cell wall, but an animal cell does not. [B1]

A plant cell has chloroplasts, but an animal cell does not. [B1]

A plant cell has a large permanent vacuole surrounded by a tonoplast, but an animal cell has only small temporary vacuoles. [B1]

An animal cell has centrioles, but a typical plant cell does not. [B1]

(Also credit: plant cells have plasmodesmata; plant cells store starch but animal cells store glycogen; plant cells have a fixed shape. Both have a nucleus, mitochondria, RER and Golgi body.)

A weaker answer lists the plant features alone ("plants have a wall, chloroplasts and a vacuole") without saying that animal cells lack them. A comparison must state both sides.
:::

:::exam "Compare" and "state the differences"
Use a table or a sentence for each point that mentions **both** cells ("A has …, but B does not" or "A has …, whereas B has …"). Do not write "similarities" if the question asks for differences. Give one point per mark and use the exact structure names (tonoplast, plasmodesmata, centrioles).
:::

## ATP: the energy currency of cells

Many of the processes above need energy: protein synthesis, moving vesicles, beating cilia, active transport across the cell surface membrane, cell division and muscle contraction.

:::key ATP and the cell
Cells use **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate), made in **respiration**, as the immediate source of energy for energy-requiring processes. ATP is made from the energy released when nutrients such as glucose are broken down, mostly in the mitochondria; it is broken down when a cell needs energy, and the energy released is used for processes such as active transport, protein synthesis, movement and cell division.
:::

You do not need the structure of ATP or the stages of respiration here: they are in [Energy, ATP and respiratory quotient](#bio.energy). All you need is the link: **cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria.**

:::eg Data and context: heart muscle cells and skin cells. Thin sections of two cell types were photographed in an electron microscope at ×5000. Each micrograph measures 90 mm by 70 mm. The heart muscle micrograph shows 36 mitochondria, and the skin cell micrograph shows 6. Calculate the number of mitochondria per 100 µm² of section in each, and explain the difference.
**Step 1: actual size of the micrograph.** Use \(A = I/M\).

\[\begin{aligned} \text{width} &= \frac{90\,\mathrm{mm}}{5000} = 0.018\,\mathrm{mm} = 18\,\mathrm{\mu m} \\ \text{height} &= \frac{70\,\mathrm{mm}}{5000} = 0.014\,\mathrm{mm} = 14\,\mathrm{\mu m} \end{aligned}\]

**Step 2: area.** \(18 \times 14 = 252\,\mathrm{\mu m^2}\).

**Step 3: mitochondria per 100 µm².**

\[\begin{aligned} \text{heart muscle} &= \frac{36}{252} \times 100 = 14.3 \\ \text{skin cell} &= \frac{6}{252} \times 100 = 2.38 \end{aligned}\]

So there are 14.3 per 100 µm² in heart muscle and 2.4 per 100 µm² in skin, about six times as many in the heart.

**Explanation**: heart muscle contracts continuously, so it needs a large and steady supply of ATP. Mitochondria release the energy from respiration as ATP, so heart muscle cells contain many mitochondria. Skin cells need much less ATP.

**Caution**: a thin section cuts mitochondria at random, so counts from one section are an estimate. Counting several micrographs would make the comparison more reliable.
:::

## Reading electron micrographs

Questions give you an electron micrograph or a drawing, usually with letters on it, and ask you to name structures, describe them or link them to a function. Work through these clues.

| If you see … | It is probably … |
|---|---|
| a large round or oval body with a double boundary and a dark patch inside | nucleus (with nucleolus) |
| flat parallel sheets with black dots along them | rough ER |
| smooth tubes or sacs with no dots | smooth ER |
| a stack of flat curved sacs with small round vesicles nearby | Golgi body |
| a small, round, single-membrane sac, evenly dark | lysosome |
| an oval or rod with a double boundary and internal folds | mitochondrion |
| a larger oval with a double boundary and stacks of flat discs | chloroplast |
| small dark dots, no membrane | ribosomes |
| two short tubes at right angles, ring of nine groups in cross-section | centrioles |
| very large, central, pale sac inside a thick wall | plant vacuole and cell wall |
| fine finger-like projections from the surface | microvilli (short and stubby) or cilia (long, in groups) |

:::eg Real context, cilia and disease. In primary ciliary dyskinesia, the cilia of cells lining the airways cannot beat. People with it have frequent chest infections. Explain why.
This is a 3-mark question.

Normally, cilia on the cells lining the airways beat to sweep mucus upwards and out of the lungs. [B1]

The mucus traps bacteria and dust particles, so sweeping it away removes them from the lungs. [B1]

If the cilia cannot beat, mucus with trapped bacteria stays in the airways, so bacteria can multiply and cause infections. [B1]

(Also credit: the cilia contain microtubules, and a fault in their structure stops them moving.)

## Practice

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State the function of the nucleolus.
A: It makes ribosomes (it makes the RNA of ribosomes and assembles ribosome subunits).
Q: State two differences between rough and smooth endoplasmic reticulum.
A: The RER has ribosomes on its surface and the SER does not; the RER makes and processes proteins, and the SER makes lipids (phospholipids and steroids).
Q: A mitochondrion appears 54 mm long in an electron micrograph at ×18 000. Calculate its actual length in µm.
A: \(54 \div 18\,000 = 0.0030\,\mathrm{mm} = 3.0\,\mathrm{\mu m}\).
Q: Name three structures found in plant cells but not in animal cells.
A: Any three of: cell wall, chloroplasts, large permanent vacuole (with tonoplast), plasmodesmata.
Q: Cells lining the oviduct have cilia. Explain how this helps the oviduct to work.
A: The cilia beat and move the egg (and fluid) along the oviduct. They need ATP, so the cells have many mitochondria.
Q: What do the numbers 80S and 70S tell you about ribosomes, and where is each type found?
A: The S value is a measure of how fast a particle sediments, so it indicates size; 80S ribosomes are larger and are found in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells; the smaller 70S ribosomes are found in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
Q: Apart from the nucleus, name two organelles that contain DNA.
A: Mitochondria and chloroplasts (each has a small loop of circular DNA).
Q: Lysosomes contain digestive enzymes. Explain why these enzymes are enclosed in a membrane.
A: The membrane keeps the enzymes separate from the rest of the cytoplasm. Otherwise they would digest the cell's own proteins and organelles.
Q: Cells in the adrenal glands make large amounts of steroid hormones. Suggest which organelle is likely to be abundant in these cells, and why.
A: Smooth endoplasmic reticulum, because it makes lipids including steroids.
Q: A student writes that "mitochondria make energy for the cell". Correct this statement.
A: Mitochondria release energy from nutrients such as glucose, by respiration, and use it to make ATP. Energy is not made.
Q: A cell makes large amounts of protein for export. List the organelles that you would expect to be abundant, and say why each is needed.
A: Rough ER (ribosomes make the protein and it is folded there); Golgi body (modifies and packages it); vesicles (carry it to the surface); mitochondria (ATP for synthesis and vesicle movement).
Q: State one structural difference and one functional difference between a centriole and a cilium.
A: A centriole is a short cylinder of nine triplets of microtubules and a cilium is a long projection with nine pairs and two central microtubules; centrioles organise the spindle microtubules in cell division, whereas cilia beat to move substances over the cell surface.
:::

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ An electron micrograph of a cell lining the small intestine, at ×4000, shows microvilli. One microvillus measures 3.2 mm in the micrograph. (a) Calculate the actual length of the microvillus in µm. (b) Explain how microvilli help the cell to absorb digested food. (c) The cells contain many mitochondria. Suggest why.
H: (c) Absorption of some nutrients needs energy.
A: (a) \(3.2 \div 4000\) [M1] \(= 0.0008\,\mathrm{mm} = 0.80\,\mathrm{\mu m}\) [A1]
(b) Microvilli increase the surface area of the cell surface membrane. [B1]
So more digested food can be absorbed in a given time (more carrier proteins in the membrane). [B1]
(c) Mitochondria release energy in the form of ATP by respiration. [B1]
Absorption by active transport needs ATP. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] (#bio.microscopy) ★★★ Compare the appearance in an electron micrograph of a mitochondrion and a chloroplast, and describe two features that they share, to explain why they might both be thought to resemble bacteria.
H: Think about the membranes, the inside, and what DNA and ribosomes they contain.
A: Both have two membranes (an envelope), and a mitochondrion's inner membrane is folded into cristae. [B1]
A chloroplast has no cristae but has stacks of discs (grana) and a stroma. [B1]
A chloroplast may contain starch grains; a mitochondrion does not. [B1]
Both contain small loops of circular DNA. [B1]
Both contain 70S ribosomes, which are the same size as those of bacteria. [B1]
Both are of a size similar to a bacterium (a few µm long), and they divide inside the cell. [B1]

Q: [5 marks] ★★★ Mitochondria and chloroplasts have a double membrane, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes and can divide inside a cell. A scientist suggests that they evolved from free-living prokaryotes that were taken inside a larger cell. (a) Explain how each of these four features supports the suggestion. (b) Suggest what evidence a scientist could look for in the DNA of a mitochondrion to test the idea.
H: Compare each feature with a typical bacterium.
A: (a) Bacteria have circular DNA, so the DNA of mitochondria and chloroplasts is like theirs, not like the linear DNA of the nucleus. [B1]
Bacteria have 70S ribosomes, so these organelles have ribosomes of bacterial size. [B1]
The outer of the two membranes could have come from the cell that engulfed the prokaryote, and the inner one from the prokaryote's own membrane, giving two membranes. [B1]
Dividing independently resembles bacterial reproduction (binary fission). [B1]
(b) Compare the base sequence of mitochondrial DNA with that of bacteria and with the nuclear DNA, looking for greater similarity to bacteria. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A drug stops the vesicles that bud from the rough endoplasmic reticulum from fusing with the Golgi body. Cells treated with the drug make a protein that they normally secrete. Predict (a) where the protein will build up, (b) what will be seen in an electron micrograph of the cell, and (c) the effect on the amount of protein secreted.

A: (a) In the rough ER and in the vesicles budding from it. [B1]
(b) Swollen RER and many vesicles (accumulated near the Golgi body), and few secretory vesicles near the cell surface membrane. [B1]
The Golgi body would be smaller, because it is not receiving vesicles. [B1]
(c) Much less protein is secreted, because it never reaches the Golgi body to be modified and packaged. [B1]

:::recap
- A eukaryotic cell has a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles. Membranes make compartments.
- Nucleus: DNA; double envelope with pores; nucleolus makes ribosomes. Ribosomes (no membrane): 80S in the cytoplasm and on the RER, 70S in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
- RER: ribosomes, makes and folds protein. SER: no ribosomes, makes lipids. Golgi body: modifies and packages protein into vesicles. Lysosome: one membrane, hydrolytic enzymes.
- Secretory pathway: ribosome on RER, folded in RER, vesicle, Golgi body, secretory vesicle, exocytosis at the cell surface membrane.
- Mitochondrion: two membranes, cristae, matrix, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes; releases energy as ATP by aerobic respiration. Chloroplast: two membranes, grana, stroma, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes; photosynthesis.
- Centrioles (animal cells): nine triplets of microtubules, organise the spindle. Cilia move fluid; microvilli increase surface area.
- Plant cells also have a cellulose cell wall, chloroplasts, a large permanent vacuole with tonoplast, and plasmodesmata. Animal cells have centrioles; typical plant cells do not.
- Cells use ATP from respiration for energy-requiring processes. Cells with high energy needs have many mitochondria.
:::
