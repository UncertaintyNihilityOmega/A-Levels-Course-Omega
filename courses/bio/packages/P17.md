## bio.evolution: Evolution and speciation (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 17.3. Candidates should be able to:
1. outline the theory of evolution as a process leading to the formation of new species from pre-existing species over
   time, as a result of changes to gene pools from generation to generation
2. discuss how DNA sequence data can show evolutionary relationships between species
3. explain how speciation may occur as a result of genetic isolation by: geographical separation (allopatric
   speciation); ecological and behavioural separation (sympatric speciation)
Cover: evolution defined via gene pools; species (biological species concept as working definition here: link
bio.classification for the three concepts); evidence from DNA sequences: the more differences, the longer since a
common ancestor; comparing base sequences of a gene (e.g. cytochrome c or mitochondrial DNA) and protein sequences;
mutation rate as a molecular clock (assumption that differences accumulate at a roughly constant rate: "discuss" =
strengths and limitations); building a simple tree from a table of differences; allopatric speciation (geographical
barrier → no gene flow → different selection pressures, mutations and drift → gene pools diverge → reproductive
isolation even if barriers are removed); sympatric (ecological: different habitats or food in the same area;
behavioural: courtship, breeding times; polyploidy as a named mechanism in plants in a `:::note`); reproductive
isolation mechanisms (prezygotic and postzygotic, briefly, e.g. hybrid infertility).
Pitfalls: "individuals evolve"; "a species is a group that looks alike" without the breeding test; "the barrier makes
them mutate"; "speciation happens when two populations look different"; DNA differences mean "less evolved".
Examples: build a tree from a table of sequence differences (four species); explain allopatric speciation of
island birds or fish in separate lakes (exam 6 marks); discuss DNA evidence (6 marks); real context: cichlid fish or
apple maggot flies as sympatric examples (qualitative, state as examples studied by biologists without numbers).
Figures: allopatric v sympatric flow (fig, two rows); a simple tree drawn from given data (fig small, data/fig_tree.py).
Links: PMT selection-and-evolution; SME 17-selection-and-evolution; OpenStax ch 18; PhET natural-selection;
Learn.Genetics evolution.

## bio.classification: Classification (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 18.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. discuss the meaning of the term species, limited to the biological species concept, morphological species concept
   and ecological species concept
2. describe the classification of organisms into three domains: Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya
3. state that Archaea and Bacteria are prokaryotes and that there are differences between them, limited to differences
   in membrane lipids, ribosomal RNA and composition of cell walls
4. describe the classification of organisms in the Eukarya domain into the taxonomic hierarchy of kingdom, phylum,
   class, order, family, genus and species
5. outline the characteristic features of the kingdoms Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia
6. outline how viruses are classified, limited to the type of nucleic acid (RNA or DNA) and whether this is single
   stranded or double stranded
Cover: species concepts with strengths and problems (biological: interbreed to produce fertile offspring; fails for
asexual organisms, fossils, geographically separated populations; morphological: shared observable features;
problems with variation within species and similar-looking different species; ecological: same niche); three domains
(Woese, rRNA evidence); Archaea v Bacteria: membrane lipids (archaea: branched chains with ether bonds; bacteria:
unbranched, ester bonds), rRNA sequences differ, cell walls (bacteria peptidoglycan; archaea no peptidoglycan);
hierarchy with a worked human or lion example; binomial naming (Genus species, italics, capital genus); kingdom
features in a table: Protoctista (eukaryotic, mostly unicellular, diverse, some with chloroplasts), Fungi (eukaryotic,
chitin cell walls, heterotrophic, absorb food by extracellular digestion, hyphae, spores, no chloroplasts), Plantae
(multicellular, cellulose walls, chloroplasts, photosynthesis, large vacuoles), Animalia (multicellular, no walls,
heterotrophic, nervous coordination, usually move); virus classification by nucleic acid: dsDNA, ssDNA, dsRNA, ssRNA
(examples: HIV ssRNA (retrovirus); others named only if certain).
Pitfalls: "bacteria are in the kingdom Prokaryotae" (old system; use domains); "fungi are plants"; mixing the order of
the hierarchy (mnemonic welcome); writing species names without italics; "viruses are classified into kingdoms".
Examples: discuss species concepts with a ring-species or mule example (6 marks); classify an organism from a
description; Archaea v Bacteria table (3 marks).
Figures: three-domain diagram (fig small); hierarchy nesting (fig small).
Links: PMT biodiversity-classification-conservation; SME 18-classification-biodiversity-and-conservation; OpenStax ch 20.

## bio.biodiversity: Biodiversity and sampling (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 4 and 5`, `@time 5–6 hours`)
Syllabus 18.2. Candidates should be able to:
1. define the terms ecosystem and niche
2. explain that biodiversity can be assessed at different levels, including: the number and range of different
   ecosystems and habitats; the number of species and their relative abundance; the genetic variation within each
   species
3. explain the importance of random sampling in determining the biodiversity of an area
4. describe and use suitable methods to assess the distribution and abundance of organisms in an area, limited to frame
   quadrats, line transects, belt transects and mark-release-recapture using the Lincoln index (the formula for the
   Lincoln index will be provided, as shown in the Mathematical requirements)
5. use Spearman's rank correlation and Pearson's linear correlation to analyse the relationships between two variables,
   including how biotic and abiotic factors affect the distribution and abundance of species (the formulae for these
   correlations will be provided, as shown in the Mathematical requirements)
6. use Simpson's index of diversity (D) to calculate the biodiversity of an area, and state the significance of
   different values of D (the formula for Simpson's index of diversity will be provided, as shown in the Mathematical
   requirements)
Cover: ecosystem, niche, habitat, population, community as `:::def`s; three levels of biodiversity; species richness v
evenness; random sampling (random number coordinates on a grid; avoids bias) v systematic (transects along a
gradient); frame quadrats (percentage cover, frequency, density; how many quadrats: running mean levels off); line and
belt transects (zonation, e.g. across a shore or from woodland to grassland); mark-release-recapture (marking that
does not harm or make animals more visible, mixing time; assumptions: no births, deaths, migration, marks not lost);
Lincoln index N = n₁ × n₂ / m₂ (given); Simpson's index D = 1 − Σ(n/N)² (given; D from 0 to 1, higher = more diverse,
a value near 1 suggests a stable, diverse community); correlation: scatter graph first; Pearson (normal, linear,
continuous), Spearman (ranks; ties get mean ranks); rₛ = 1 − 6ΣD²/(n³ − n) (given); critical values given in the
question (give them in every question, as the exam does); interpreting r and rₛ: sign, strength, significance;
correlation not causation; abiotic and biotic factors.
Pitfalls: Lincoln index with m₂ on top; Simpson's index without squaring each n/N; ranking ties; "a high rₛ proves
cause"; random quadrats placed "where there are plants"; D compared between sites of very different sampling effort.
Examples: Lincoln index (e.g. 48 marked, 60 caught, 12 marked → 240); Simpson's index for two sites (5 species each,
compute D for both); Spearman's rank on 8 pairs along a transect (with ties) and its conclusion using a given critical
value; Pearson's r from given partial sums (finishing working); plan a belt transect (Paper 5 style). Practice uses
new numbers. Check scripts compute ranks with ties.
Figures: quadrat grid with random coordinates (fig small); belt transect along a gradient (fig small); scatter
(`:::plot` points not supported as data series: draw a small SVG scatter in data/fig_scatter.py).
Widget **bio-diversity** after Simpson's index and again after the Lincoln index (data-mode).
`Next: [Conservation](#bio.conservation)`.

## Widget spec: bio-diversity
Name `bio-diversity`, file `widgets/bio-diversity.js`. Attributes: `data-mode="simpson|lincoln|spearman"`.
- `simpson`: an editable table of species and counts (add/remove rows, up to 10); computes N, each n/N, (n/N)², Σ, and
  D (3 d.p.); a second column set for a second site to compare; a sentence on which site is more diverse and why
  (richness and evenness).
- `lincoln`: n₁, n₂, m₂ fields; N = n₁n₂/m₂ (rounded to a whole number); friendly error if m₂ = 0 or m₂ > n₂ or
  m₂ > n₁; list of assumptions.
- `spearman`: paired data entry (up to 15 pairs); ranks with ties averaged, D, D², ΣD², rₛ (3 d.p.); a field for the
  critical value the reader is given, and a conclusion; small scatter plot.
- Start each mode with worked-style data different from the chapter's examples. Survive zero size.
