## bio.energy: Energy, ATP and respiratory quotient (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 12.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. outline the need for energy in living organisms, as illustrated by active transport, movement and anabolic
   reactions, such as those occurring in DNA replication and protein synthesis
2. describe the features of ATP that make it suitable as the universal energy currency
3. state that ATP is synthesised by: transfer of phosphate in substrate-linked reactions; chemiosmosis in membranes of
   mitochondria and chloroplasts
4. explain the relative energy values of carbohydrates, lipids and proteins as respiratory substrates
5. state that the respiratory quotient (RQ) is the ratio of the number of molecules of carbon dioxide produced to the
   number of molecules of oxygen taken in, as a result of respiration
6. calculate RQ values of different respiratory substrates from equations for respiration
7. describe and carry out investigations, using simple respirometers, to determine the RQ of germinating seeds or
   small invertebrates (e.g. blowfly larvae)
Cover: energy needs; ATP structure recap; features (small, water-soluble, releases energy in small manageable amounts
by hydrolysis of one phosphate bond, quickly hydrolysed and resynthesised, universal, cannot pass out of the cell);
hydrolysis ATP → ADP + Pi releases about 30.5 kJ mol⁻¹ (standard textbook value; state "about"); substrate-linked
phosphorylation v chemiosmosis (one paragraph each; detail in bio.respiration); energy values: about 16 kJ g⁻¹
carbohydrate, 39 kJ g⁻¹ lipid, 17 kJ g⁻¹ protein (COURSE.md values), explained by the number of hydrogen atoms (more
H → more reduced NAD/FAD → more ATP via oxidative phosphorylation; lipids have more H per gram); RQ: definition;
glucose \(\ce{C6H12O6 + 6O2 -> 6CO2 + 6H2O}\) RQ = 1.0; a fatty acid such as oleic acid
\(\ce{C18H34O2 + 25.5O2 -> 18CO2 + 17H2O}\) RQ ≈ 0.71 (compute and check in the script; typical lipid RQ 0.7);
proteins about 0.9 (typical value, state "about"); RQ > 1 with anaerobic respiration contributing; respirometer
`:::lab`: two tubes, soda lime/KOH absorbs CO₂, coloured fluid in a capillary manometer, the distance moved with KOH
gives O₂ uptake; without KOH gives O₂ uptake − CO₂ output; RQ calculation; volume from capillary: \(\pi r^2 h\);
controls (glass beads, same volume), equilibrium time, water bath for temperature.
Pitfalls: "ATP stores lots of energy" (it transfers energy in small amounts; it is not a long-term store); "energy is
made"; RQ upside down (CO₂/O₂); signs in respirometer readings; "lipids have more energy because they are bigger".
Examples: RQ of glucose and of a given fatty acid (choose a different one from the practice questions); respirometer
calculation (exam 6 marks: two readings → volumes → RQ → identify likely substrate); real context: RQ changing as
seeds germinate (lipid store first in some seeds; invented data, stated).
Figures: ATP structure and hydrolysis (fig small); respirometer (fig, viewBox ~560).
Widget **bio-rq** in the respirometer section.

## bio.respiration: Aerobic respiration (`@time 5–6 hours`)
Syllabus 12.2.1–12.2.9 and 12.2.11. Candidates should be able to:
1. state where each of the four stages in aerobic respiration occurs in eukaryotic cells: glycolysis in the cytoplasm;
   link reaction in the mitochondrial matrix; Krebs cycle in the mitochondrial matrix; oxidative phosphorylation on the
   inner membrane of mitochondria
2. outline glycolysis as phosphorylation of glucose and the subsequent splitting of fructose 1,6-bisphosphate (6C)
   into two triose phosphate molecules (3C), which are then further oxidised to pyruvate (3C), with the production of
   ATP and reduced NAD
3. explain that, when oxygen is available, pyruvate enters mitochondria to take part in the link reaction
4. describe the link reaction, including the role of coenzyme A in the transfer of acetyl (2C) groups
5. outline the Krebs cycle, explaining that oxaloacetate (4C) acts as an acceptor of the 2C fragment from acetyl
   coenzyme A to form citrate (6C), which is converted back to oxaloacetate in a series of small steps
6. explain that reactions in the Krebs cycle involve decarboxylation and dehydrogenation and the reduction of the
   coenzymes NAD and FAD
7. describe the role of NAD and FAD in transferring hydrogen to carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane
8. explain that during oxidative phosphorylation: hydrogen atoms split into protons and energetic electrons; energetic
   electrons release energy as they pass through the electron transport chain (details of carriers are not expected);
   the released energy is used to transfer protons across the inner mitochondrial membrane; protons return to the
   mitochondrial matrix by facilitated diffusion through ATP synthase, providing energy for ATP synthesis (details of
   ATP synthase are not expected); oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor to form water
9. describe the relationship between the structure and function of mitochondria using diagrams and electron
   micrographs
11. explain why the energy yield from respiration in aerobic conditions is much greater than the energy yield from
   respiration in anaerobic conditions (a detailed account of the total yield of ATP from the aerobic respiration of
   glucose is not expected)
Cover: overview map of four stages with locations; glycolysis (2 ATP used to phosphorylate glucose → fructose
1,6-bisphosphate → 2 triose phosphate → oxidised (dehydrogenated) to 2 pyruvate; 4 ATP made by substrate-linked
phosphorylation, net 2; 2 reduced NAD); link reaction (pyruvate → decarboxylated and dehydrogenated → acetyl joins
coenzyme A; per pyruvate 1 CO₂, 1 reduced NAD); Krebs cycle (acetyl CoA 2C + oxaloacetate 4C → citrate 6C → two
decarboxylations → 4C; per turn 2 CO₂, 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP by substrate-linked phosphorylation; two
turns per glucose); oxidative phosphorylation exactly as outcome 8 (chemiosmosis); mitochondrial structure: outer
membrane, inner membrane folded into cristae (large surface area for ETC and ATP synthase), intermembrane space
(proton gradient), matrix (enzymes of link reaction and Krebs, circular DNA, 70S ribosomes); a "per glucose" tally
table of CO₂, reduced NAD, reduced FAD and ATP made directly (no total ATP yield required; if mentioned, "about 30"
in a `:::note` only, labelled as not required); why aerobic yields much more than anaerobic (oxidative
phosphorylation needs oxygen as final acceptor; without it only the 2 net ATP of glycolysis). Link bio.photosynthesis
for the parallel chemiosmosis in chloroplasts.
Pitfalls: "glycolysis happens in mitochondria"; "Krebs cycle produces most ATP"; "oxygen combines with carbon to make
CO₂" (CO₂ comes from decarboxylation); "protons pumped into the matrix"; "ATP synthase pumps protons"; numbers per
turn v per glucose.
Examples: tally per glucose (exam 4 marks); explain the role of oxygen (exam 4 marks); interpret data on a
mitochondrial poison that makes the inner membrane leaky to protons (stated in the stem: ATP falls, O₂ use continues);
label a mitochondrion EM drawing.
Figures: overview (fig, viewBox ~600); Krebs cycle simplified (fig small, carbon numbers only); inner membrane
chemiosmosis (fig, viewBox ~560, data/fig_resp.py); mitochondrion (fig small).
Links: PMT energy-and-respiration; SME 12-energy-and-respiration; OpenStax ch 7.

## bio.anaerobic: Anaerobic respiration and investigating respiration (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 4 and 5`, `@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 12.2.10, 12.2.12–12.2.14. Candidates should be able to:
10. outline respiration in anaerobic conditions in mammals (lactate fermentation) and in yeast cells (ethanol
   fermentation)
12. explain how rice is adapted to grow with its roots submerged in water, limited to the development of aerenchyma in
   roots, ethanol fermentation in roots and faster growth of stems
13. describe and carry out investigations using redox indicators, including DCPIP and methylene blue, to determine the
   effects of temperature and substrate concentration on the rate of respiration of yeast
14. describe and carry out investigations using simple respirometers to determine the effect of temperature on the
   rate of respiration
Cover: without O₂ the ETC stops, reduced NAD cannot be reoxidised, so glycolysis would stop; lactate fermentation
(pyruvate reduced to lactate by lactate dehydrogenase, regenerating NAD; lactate later oxidised back to pyruvate or
converted to glucose in the liver: oxygen debt); ethanol fermentation (pyruvate decarboxylated to ethanal, ethanal
reduced to ethanol by alcohol dehydrogenase, regenerating NAD; CO₂ released); compare the two (table); rice: aerenchyma
(air spaces let O₂ diffuse down to roots), roots tolerate ethanol (ethanol fermentation, high alcohol dehydrogenase
activity), stems grow fast to keep leaves above water; redox indicators `:::lab`: methylene blue or DCPIP accept
hydrogen (electrons) from respiration and become colourless; time to decolourise ∝ 1/rate; yeast in glucose at
different temperatures or glucose concentrations; controls (boiled yeast), equal volumes, water bath, standardise
shaking (shaking reoxygenates the dye); respirometer for temperature effect (link bio.energy's respirometer); rate =
volume ÷ time; Q10 is not in the syllabus (do not use).
Pitfalls: "anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid in yeast"; "lactate fermentation releases CO₂"; "no ATP is made
anaerobically" (2 net from glycolysis); rate = time (it is 1/time); methylene blue "turns blue when respiring".
Examples: rates from decolourisation times at five temperatures (1/t, plot with `:::plot`); explain why ethanol
fermentation lets rice survive flooding (exam 5 marks); compare the two pathways (4 marks); Spot the error: rate taken
as the time itself.
Figures: both pathways side by side (fig, viewBox ~560); rice adaptations annotated (fig small).

## Widget spec: bio-rq
Name `bio-rq`, file `widgets/bio-rq.js`. Attributes: `data-mode="formula|respirometer"` (default formula).
- `formula`: the reader enters a respiratory substrate formula CxHyOz (fields for x, y, z; presets: glucose
  C6H12O6, tripalmitin C51H98O6, oleic acid C18H34O2, alanine is not allowed because N: keep to C, H, O only).
  The widget balances complete oxidation CxHyOz + (x + y/4 − z/2) O₂ → x CO₂ + y/2 H₂O and shows the balanced equation
  and RQ = x / (x + y/4 − z/2) to 2 d.p., with a verdict line ("RQ ≈ 1: carbohydrate", "≈ 0.7: lipid", "> 1: some
  anaerobic respiration", "between: a mixture"). Friendly errors for impossible inputs (O₂ ≤ 0).
- `respirometer`: sliders for distance moved with KOH (d₁, mm) and without (d₂, mm, signed: towards or away from the
  tube), capillary radius (mm). Shows O₂ uptake volume = πr²d₁, CO₂ output = O₂ uptake − (net change), and RQ, with a
  small drawing of the U-tube or capillary with the fluid position. Start values give RQ 0.70 (germinating
  oil-rich seeds), so the reader sees a lipid value.
- Survive zero size.
