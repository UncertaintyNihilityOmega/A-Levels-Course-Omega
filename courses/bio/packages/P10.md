## bio.immunity: The immune response (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 11.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe the mode of action of phagocytes (macrophages and neutrophils)
2. explain what is meant by an antigen (see 4.1.3) and state the difference between self antigens and non-self
   antigens
3. describe the sequence of events that occurs during a primary immune response with reference to the roles of:
   macrophages; B-lymphocytes, including plasma cells; T-lymphocytes, limited to T-helper cells and T-killer cells
4. explain the role of memory cells in the secondary immune response and in long-term immunity
Cover: non-specific v specific responses; phagocytosis step by step (chemotaxis towards chemicals released by
pathogens or damaged cells, recognition of antigens/opsonins as "antibodies attached to a pathogen make it easier
to engulf", engulfing by endocytosis into a phagosome (phagocytic vacuole), lysosomes fuse and release hydrolytic
enzymes, digestion); neutrophils (short-lived, first to arrive, numerous) v macrophages (larger, longer-lived,
antigen-presenting cells); antigen: a molecule (often protein or glycoprotein) that stimulates an immune response;
self v non-self; origin of lymphocytes (bone marrow; B mature in bone marrow, T in thymus); clonal selection: each
lymphocyte has receptors for one antigen; antigen presentation by macrophages; T-helper cells with a complementary
receptor are activated, divide by mitosis (clonal expansion), release cytokines that stimulate B cells and
T-killer cells; B cells with complementary antibody receptors divide by mitosis, differentiate into plasma cells
(secrete antibodies; much RER and Golgi: link bio.cells) and memory cells; T-killer cells kill infected body cells
(by releasing chemicals that make holes or trigger cell death: keep it at "destroy infected cells"); primary response
slow (days) because few lymphocytes have the right receptor; secondary response faster, larger, longer because many
memory cells exist; long-term immunity; graph of antibody concentration against time for first and second exposure
(`:::plot` model curves, log scale not needed). Autoimmune disease (the immune system attacking self antigens) at
most as a one-line `:::note`: it is not in this syllabus.
Pitfalls: "antibodies kill pathogens" (they neutralise, agglutinate, label for phagocytosis); "plasma cells
become memory cells"; "T-helper cells make antibodies"; "memory cells produce antibodies directly"; "the secondary
response involves no plasma cells".
Examples: sequence the primary response (exam 6 marks); interpret an antibody concentration graph; explain why a
second infection does not cause symptoms; real context: why HIV weakens the immune response (T-helper cells
destroyed, link bio.disease).
Figures: phagocytosis steps (fig, viewBox ~600, four panels); clonal selection and expansion (fig, viewBox ~560).
Links: PMT immunity; SME 11-immunity; OpenStax ch 42; Amoeba Sisters.

## bio.antibodies: Antibodies and vaccination (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 11.2. Candidates should be able to:
1. relate the molecular structure of antibodies to their functions
2. outline the hybridoma method for the production of monoclonal antibodies
3. outline the principles of using monoclonal antibodies in the diagnosis of disease and in the treatment of disease
4. describe the differences between active immunity and passive immunity and between natural immunity and artificial
   immunity
5. explain that vaccines contain antigens that stimulate immune responses to provide long-term immunity
6. explain how vaccination programmes can help to control the spread of infectious diseases
Cover: antibody (immunoglobulin) structure: four polypeptides (two heavy, two light) held by disulfide bonds,
Y shape, constant region, variable regions forming two identical antigen-binding sites complementary to one antigen,
hinge region for flexibility; functions: agglutination, neutralisation of toxins, labelling for phagocytes
(opsonisation), preventing pathogens binding to cells; monoclonal antibodies: hybridoma method (mouse injected with
antigen, B cells (plasma cells) from spleen fused with myeloma (cancer) cells, hybridoma cells divide indefinitely and
make one antibody; screen and clone); diagnosis (pregnancy tests detecting hCG: describe as a principle, labelled
antibodies; locating cancers, blood clots), treatment (antibody binds to an antigen on cancer cells and triggers
destruction or blocks a receptor; antibodies may carry a drug); active (own lymphocytes, memory cells, long-term) v
passive (antibodies given or received, short-term, no memory); natural v artificial (four combinations with examples:
infection; vaccination; antibodies across the placenta and in breast milk; injection of antibodies e.g. antivenom or
anti-tetanus); vaccines (dead or weakened pathogens, inactivated toxins (toxoids), isolated antigens, mRNA vaccines
as "instructions for cells to make the antigen": keep it brief and accurate); herd immunity (most of a population
immune, chains of transmission broken, protects those who cannot be vaccinated); why some vaccination programmes
work better than others (antigenic variation, as in influenza; pathogens inside cells, as Plasmodium; many strains;
cold chain) and smallpox eradication as the success story (stable antigen, visible symptoms, ring vaccination).
Pitfalls: "vaccines contain antibodies"; "passive immunity produces memory cells"; "monoclonal = from one patient";
"antibodies are made by T cells"; mixing active/passive with natural/artificial.
Examples: 2 × 2 immunity table with examples; explain why measles vaccination gives long-term immunity but influenza
needs a new vaccine (exam 5 marks); outline the hybridoma method; data: vaccination coverage v cases (invented,
stated).
Figures: antibody structure (fig small, labelled); hybridoma flow (fig, viewBox ~560).
Links: PMT immunity; OpenStax ch 42.
