## bio.homeostasis: Homeostasis and the kidney (`@time 5–6 hours`)
Syllabus 14.1.1–14.1.8. Candidates should be able to:
1. explain what is meant by homeostasis and the importance of homeostasis in mammals
2. explain the principles of homeostasis in terms of internal and external stimuli, receptors, coordination systems
   (nervous system and endocrine system), effectors (muscles and glands) and negative feedback
3. state that urea is produced in the liver from the deamination of excess amino acids
4. describe the structure of the human kidney, limited to: fibrous capsule; cortex; medulla; renal pelvis; ureter;
   branches of the renal artery and renal vein
5. identify, in diagrams, photomicrographs and electron micrographs, the parts of a nephron and its associated blood
   vessels and structures, limited to: glomerulus; Bowman's capsule; proximal convoluted tubule; loop of Henle; distal
   convoluted tubule; collecting duct
6. describe and explain the formation of urine in the nephron, limited to: the formation of glomerular filtrate by
   ultrafiltration in the Bowman's capsule; selective reabsorption in the proximal convoluted tubule
7. relate the detailed structure of the Bowman's capsule and proximal convoluted tubule to their functions in the
   formation of urine
8. describe the roles of the hypothalamus, posterior pituitary gland, antidiuretic hormone (ADH), aquaporins and
   collecting ducts in osmoregulation
Cover: homeostasis definition and why (enzymes, water potential of cells, temperature, glucose supply); the control
loop (stimulus → receptor → coordinator → effector → response) and negative feedback (with a general graph of a
fluctuating variable around a set point); temperature control as a worked illustration of the principle (receptors
in the hypothalamus and skin; effectors: sweat glands, arterioles, muscles) kept brief; deamination: amino group
removed as ammonia, converted to urea in the liver (urea cycle names not required); kidney gross structure; nephron
parts and blood vessels (afferent arteriole wider than efferent → high hydrostatic pressure in glomerulus);
ultrafiltration: capillary endothelium with pores, basement membrane (the filter), podocytes with slits; what passes
(water, glucose, urea, ions, amino acids) and what stays (cells, plasma proteins); selective reabsorption in the PCT:
microvilli, many mitochondria, Na⁺/K⁺ pumps on the basal membrane lower Na⁺ inside, Na⁺ enters with glucose or
amino acids by cotransport, glucose leaves by facilitated diffusion into blood, water follows by osmosis; all glucose
reabsorbed normally; loop of Henle and DCT: only what the syllabus requires (no counter-current detail; the loop
creates a low water potential in the medulla: one sentence); osmoregulation: osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect
a fall in blood water potential → ADH released from the posterior pituitary → binds receptors on collecting duct cells
→ vesicles with aquaporins fuse with the membrane → more water reabsorbed by osmosis into the medulla → small volume
of concentrated urine; and the reverse.
Pitfalls: "ADH is made in the pituitary" (made in the hypothalamus, released from the posterior pituitary); "glucose
is filtered out of blood permanently"; "proteins are filtered"; "ultrafiltration is active"; "negative feedback means
something bad".
Examples: compare concentrations in plasma, filtrate and urine from a table (glucose, urea, protein) and explain each;
explain the effect of a long run in hot weather on urine (exam 6 marks); identify nephron parts in a drawing; real
context: glucose in urine in uncontrolled diabetes (the reabsorption carriers are saturated: qualitative).
Figures: kidney LS (fig small); nephron with blood vessels (fig, viewBox ~560, data/fig_kidney.py); Bowman's capsule
filter layers (fig small); PCT cell with transport proteins (fig small); ADH flow (fig small).
Links: PMT homeostasis; SME 14-homeostasis; OpenStax ch 41.

## bio.glucose: Blood glucose, cell signalling and biosensors (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 14.1.9–14.1.11 and 15.1.1–15.1.2. Candidates should be able to:
14.1.9 describe the principles of cell signalling using the example of the control of blood glucose concentration by
  glucagon, limited to: binding of hormone to cell surface receptor causing conformational change; activation of
  G-protein leading to stimulation of adenylyl cyclase; formation of the second messenger, cyclic AMP (cAMP);
  activation of protein kinase A by cAMP leading to initiation of an enzyme cascade; amplification of the signal
  through the enzyme cascade as a result of activation of more and more enzymes by phosphorylation; cellular response
  in which the final enzyme in the pathway is activated, catalysing the breakdown of glycogen
14.1.10 explain how negative feedback control mechanisms regulate blood glucose concentration, with reference to the
  effects of insulin on muscle cells and liver cells and the effect of glucagon on liver cells
14.1.11 explain the principles of operation of test strips and biosensors for measuring the concentration of glucose in
  blood and urine, with reference to glucose oxidase and peroxidase enzymes
15.1.1 describe the features of the endocrine system with reference to the hormones ADH, glucagon and insulin (see
  14.1.8, 14.1.9 and 14.1.10)
15.1.2 compare the features of the nervous system and the endocrine system
Cover: endocrine glands, hormones carried in blood, target cells with receptors; islets of Langerhans (α cells
glucagon, β cells insulin); a rise in blood glucose → β cells secrete insulin → muscle and liver cells take up glucose
(more GLUT4 transporters in muscle cell membranes; glucose → glycogen, glycogenesis; respiration increases) → blood
glucose falls → less insulin (negative feedback); a fall → α cells secrete glucagon → liver cells only (muscle has no
glucagon receptors) → glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis → glucose released → rises; the cascade exactly as 14.1.9
with a figure; amplification (one hormone molecule → many cAMP → many enzymes → very many glucose); test strips
(glucose oxidase: glucose + oxygen → gluconic acid (gluconolactone) + hydrogen peroxide; peroxidase: hydrogen peroxide
+ colourless chromogen → coloured product; compare colour with a chart: semi-quantitative); biosensors (glucose oxidase
immobilised on an electrode; the reaction produces a current proportional to glucose concentration; digital
reading; quantitative); nervous v endocrine comparison table (electrical impulses v chemical in blood; neurones v
blood; fast v slower; short-lived v long-lasting; localised v widespread).
Pitfalls: "insulin converts glucose to glycogen" (it stimulates cells that do); "glucagon acts on muscle"; "cAMP is the
hormone"; "adenylyl cyclase is the receptor"; confusing glycogen, glucagon and glycogenesis.
Examples: explain a blood glucose graph after a meal (exam 6 marks); describe the glucagon cascade (6 marks);
explain why a test strip is only semi-quantitative (2 marks); real context: type 1 diabetes and insulin injections
(insulin made by genetically modified bacteria: link bio.genetech2).
Figures: negative feedback loop for glucose (fig, viewBox ~560); glucagon second-messenger cascade (fig,
viewBox ~560, data/fig_glucose.py); test strip reactions (fig small).

## bio.hormones: Control and coordination in plants (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 14.2 and 15.2. Candidates should be able to:
14.2.1 explain that stomata respond to changes in environmental conditions by opening and closing and that regulation of
  stomatal aperture balances the need for carbon dioxide uptake by diffusion with the need to minimise water loss by
  transpiration
14.2.2 explain that stomata have daily rhythms of opening and closing
14.2.3 describe the structure and function of guard cells and explain the mechanism by which they open and close stomata
14.2.4 describe the role of abscisic acid in the closure of stomata during times of water stress, including the role of
  calcium ions as a second messenger
15.2.1 describe the rapid response of the Venus fly trap to stimulation of hairs on the lobes of modified leaves and
  explain how the closure of the trap is achieved
15.2.2 explain the role of auxin in elongation growth by stimulating proton pumping to acidify cell walls
15.2.3 describe the role of gibberellin in the germination of barley (see 16.3.4)
Cover: stomata trade-off; daily rhythm (open in the day, closed at night; continues for a while in constant light:
an internal rhythm); guard cells (thick inner wall, cellulose microfibrils arranged radially, chloroplasts); opening:
proton pumps (ATP) move H⁺ out → K⁺ channels open, K⁺ enters → water potential falls → water enters by osmosis → guard
cells become turgid and bend apart; closing: reverse; ABA: water stress → ABA binds receptors on guard cells →
Ca²⁺ enters as a second messenger → ion channels open, K⁺ (and anions) leave → water leaves → guard cells flaccid →
stoma closes; Venus fly trap: two touches of trigger hairs within a short time → action potentials across the lobe →
rapid movement of ions (described in textbooks as acid growth of outer cells and water movement; keep to: cells in
the outer layer expand quickly, the lobes flip from convex to concave and snap shut); auxin: made in shoot tips,
diffuses back, binds receptors, stimulates proton pumps in cell surface membranes → H⁺ into the cell wall → low pH
activates expansins, which loosen bonds between cellulose microfibrils → wall stretches as the cell takes in water
→ elongation; gibberellin in barley germination: seed absorbs water, embryo releases gibberellin, gibberellin acts
on the aleurone layer → amylase synthesis (via breakdown of DELLA proteins: link bio.genecontrol) → starch in
endosperm hydrolysed to maltose → glucose for the embryo's respiration and growth.
Pitfalls: "stomata close because guard cells lose turgor in the light"; "ABA opens stomata"; "auxin makes cells divide"
in this context (elongation); "Venus fly trap closes after one touch"; "gibberellin digests starch".
Examples: explain stomatal opening (exam 5 marks); interpret data on aperture over 48 h including constant light;
explain the acid-growth hypothesis (5 marks); data: amylase activity in barley seeds with and without gibberellin
(invented, stated).
Figures: guard cell pair open and closed (fig small); ABA pathway (fig small); auxin acid growth (fig small); barley
seed with aleurone (fig small).
Links: PMT control-and-coordination; SME 15-control-and-coordination; OpenStax ch 30.
