## bio.stats: Statistics for biologists (`@time 5–6 hours`)
Mathematical requirements, A Level part (syllabus pages 64–67). Candidates should be able to: relate genetic ratios to
probabilities; understand the principles of sampling as applied to biological situations and data; understand the
importance of chance and probability when interpreting data; understand the difference between a normal distribution
and a distribution that is non-normal; understand the use of descriptive statistics to simplify data, including the
mean, median, mode, range, standard deviation, standard error and 95% confidence intervals; calculate sample standard
deviation, standard error and 95% confidence intervals (formulae provided); use standard deviations, standard errors or
95% confidence intervals to plot error bars on graphs; understand the difference between correlation and causation;
calculate the results of chi-squared tests and t-tests (formulae provided); calculate the number of degrees of freedom
(formulae not provided); use the results with tables of critical values (provided); use Pearson's linear correlation and
Spearman's rank correlation (formulae provided); understand when it is appropriate to use each test and the conditions
in which each is valid.
Also the syllabus notes: candidates will not be expected to carry out all the steps of s, t, r and rₛ in the exam but may
be given partly completed calculations to finish; χ² only on one row or one column of data.
Cover: this is the hub chapter that brings the tests together (χ² is first taught in bio.dihybrid, t in bio.variation,
correlation, Lincoln and Simpson in bio.biodiversity: link them, and give each test its own full worked example here
with new data); types of data (nominal, ordinal, continuous: FACTS.md table) → choosing a test (a decision `:::key`
table: question asked, data type, test, conditions); null hypotheses written correctly for each; normal v skewed
distributions; s with n − 1; SE = s/√n; 95% CI = x̄ ± 2SE (the syllabus approximation); error bars: if 95% CI bars
overlap, the difference is probably not significant; if they do not overlap, it probably is (state as a rule of
thumb, and that a t-test decides); p = 0.05 meaning (a 5% probability that a difference this large arises by chance
alone); significance language for each test (FACTS.md "Significance"); correlation v causation; finishing partly
completed calculations.
Pitfalls: SD with n; SE confused with SD; df errors; "significant = important"; choosing Pearson for ranked data;
"the null hypothesis is proved".
Examples: s, SE and 95% CI from 6 values and drawing error bars; choosing tests for five described studies (exam 5
marks); finish a partly completed t-test; a Pearson r from given sums. Use new data throughout.
Links: math.s1.normal and math.s2.hypothesis (maths course), tools.f.bio, PMT practical-skills.

## bio.planning: Planning investigations (Paper 5) (`@time 5–6 hours`)
Paper 5 planning skills (syllabus pages 59–61). Candidates should be able to, using the context provided:
- state a relevant prediction, either in words or in the form of a sketch graph showing the expected result, and link
  this to an underlying hypothesis; identify the independent and dependent variables; identify which key variables
  must be standardised in order to test a hypothesis
- describe how to vary the independent variable; describe how to measure the values of the independent and dependent
  variables accurately and to an appropriate precision; describe how to standardise each of the other key variables;
  describe, where appropriate, suitable volumes and concentrations of reagents (concentrations may be specified in %
  (w/v) or mol dm⁻³); describe how different concentrations would be prepared by serial dilution or proportional
  dilution; describe appropriate control experiments; describe, in a logical sequence, the steps involved in the
  procedure, including how to use the apparatus to collect results; describe how the quality of results can be
  assessed by considering the occurrence of anomalous results and the spread of results including the use of standard
  deviation, standard error and/or 95% confidence intervals; describe how to assess the validity of the results by
  considering both the accuracy of the measurements and the repeatability of the results; prepare a simple risk
  assessment taking into account the severity of any hazards and the probability that a problem could occur; describe
  the precautions that would need to be taken to minimise risks
Cover: Paper 5 format (FACTS.md: 1 h 15 min, 30 marks, two or more questions, no laboratory; planning 14–16 marks);
a planning framework as a `:::steps` box (hypothesis and prediction → variables → how to vary IV (at least five values,
range justified) → how to measure DV (apparatus and units) → standardised variables with how and values → controls →
replicates → method in order → data processing (means, SD/SE, graph, test) → risk assessment); % (w/v) and mol dm⁻³
calculations (mass needed for a volume: % (w/v) = g per 100 cm³; moles = concentration × volume; molar mass given in
the question); serial and proportional dilution tables; accuracy v precision v repeatability v validity defined
carefully as `:::def`s; three full model plans in unfamiliar contexts built point by point (e.g. effect of a heavy
metal ion concentration on seedling root growth; effect of temperature on the rate of water uptake by a shoot; effect
of light wavelength on the rate of photosynthesis with algal balls); mark-scheme style (each creditworthy detail one
mark).
Pitfalls: vague variables ("the amount of enzyme"); no values for standardised variables; "repeat to make it
accurate" (repeats show repeatability and allow a mean); controls that change two things; risk assessment that just
says "wear goggles".
Examples: write a hypothesis and a sketch-graph prediction; make a dilution table; full 12-mark plan with B1 points;
Spot the error: a plan with a confounded variable.
Links: PMT practical-skills; PMT past papers cie-paper-5 `[unofficial]`.

## bio.evaluation: Analysis, conclusions and evaluation (Paper 5) (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Paper 5 analysis skills (syllabus pages 61–62). Candidates should be able to: use tables and graphs to show the key
points in quantitative data; sketch or draw suitable graphs with the independent variable on the x-axis and the
dependent variable on the y-axis, including, where required, confidence limit error bars; decide which calculations are
necessary in order to draw conclusions; carry out appropriate calculations to simplify or explain data, including
means, percentages and rates of change; carry out calculations in order to compare data, including percentage gain or
loss; use values of standard deviation or standard error, or graphs with standard error bars, to determine whether
differences in mean values are likely to be statistically significant; choose and carry out statistical tests
appropriate to the type of data collected and justify use of these tests; state a null hypothesis; recognise the
different types of variable and data (nominal, ordinal, continuous); summarise the main conclusions; identify key
points of the raw and processed data, including graphs and statistical test results; discuss the extent to which a
given hypothesis is supported by experimental data and the strengths and weaknesses of the evidence; give detailed
scientific explanations of the conclusions; make further predictions and hypotheses; identify anomalous values and
suggest how to deal with them; suggest possible explanations for anomalous readings; assess whether the results have
been replicated sufficiently; assess whether the range and intervals of the independent variable were appropriate;
assess whether the method of measuring is appropriate for the dependent variable; assess the extent to which selected
variables have been effectively controlled; make informed judgements about the validity of the investigation, the
extent to which the data can be used to test the hypothesis, and how much confidence can be put in the conclusions;
suggest how an investigation could be improved to increase confidence in the results.
Cover: analysis toolkit (means excluding anomalies, % change, rates from graphs, SE and error bars); deciding whether a
difference is significant from error bars and from test results; writing conclusions that quote data with units and
stay within the data; evaluation phrases with reasons (each weakness linked to its effect on confidence); two full
data-analysis questions with supplied tables and graphs in unfamiliar contexts (e.g. a field study of lichen cover v
distance from a road with Spearman's rank; a drug trial with means and SE).
Pitfalls: "the results prove"; including anomalies in the mean; overlapping SE bars read as "significant"; conclusions
beyond the range tested; improvements without saying how they help.
Examples: identify an anomaly and recalculate a mean; judge significance from 95% CI bars; evaluate a method (6 marks);
justify a test choice (3 marks).
