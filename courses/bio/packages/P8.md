## bio.mammaltransport: Blood vessels, blood and tissue fluid (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 8.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. state that the mammalian circulatory system is a closed double circulation consisting of a heart, blood and blood
   vessels including arteries, arterioles, capillaries, venules and veins
2. describe the functions of the main blood vessels of the pulmonary and systemic circulations, limited to pulmonary
   artery, pulmonary vein, aorta and vena cava
3. recognise arteries, veins and capillaries from microscope slides, photomicrographs and electron micrographs and make
   plan diagrams showing the structure of arteries and veins in transverse section (TS) and longitudinal section (LS)
4. explain how the structure of muscular arteries, elastic arteries, veins and capillaries are each related to their
   functions
5. recognise and draw red blood cells, monocytes, neutrophils and lymphocytes from microscope slides,
   photomicrographs and electron micrographs
6. state that water is the main component of blood and tissue fluid and relate the properties of water to its role in
   transport in mammals, limited to solvent action and high specific heat capacity
7. state the functions of tissue fluid and describe the formation of tissue fluid in a capillary network
Cover: closed (blood stays in vessels), double (through the heart twice per circuit: pulmonary and systemic), why
(high pressure to body after low pressure in lungs); the four named vessels (pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated
blood to the lungs, etc.); vessel walls: tunica intima (endothelium), media (smooth muscle, elastic fibres), externa
(collagen); elastic arteries (aorta: much elastic tissue, stretch and recoil, smooth flow), muscular arteries
(more smooth muscle, control distribution), arterioles, capillaries (one-cell-thick endothelium, narrow lumen about
the width of a red cell, gaps between cells), venules and veins (thin walls, wide lumen, valves, low pressure,
skeletal muscle squeezes); plan diagrams TS and LS (link bio.practical2); blood cells: red blood cells (biconcave,
no nucleus, about 7 µm), neutrophils (multi-lobed nucleus), monocytes (large, kidney-shaped nucleus), lymphocytes
(large round nucleus filling most of the cell); water's properties in blood (solvent: glucose, ions, urea, hormones;
heat capacity: distributes heat, resists change); tissue fluid: hydrostatic pressure at the arterial end pushes
fluid out through gaps (proteins and cells too large stay in), lower water potential of plasma (proteins) draws
water back at the venous end, excess drains into lymph (lymph vessels only as a one-liner); functions: supplies O₂,
glucose, amino acids; removes CO₂ and wastes; bathes cells.
Pitfalls: "arteries carry oxygenated blood" (pulmonary artery does not); "capillaries have thin walls of muscle";
"veins have thick walls"; "tissue fluid contains red blood cells"; "osmosis pushes fluid out at the arterial end".
Examples: compare artery and vein (exam 5 marks, table); identify blood cells from drawings and count a differential
from a described field; explain tissue fluid formation (6 marks); real context: oedema when blood protein falls (one
paragraph, qualitative).
Figures: double circulation diagram (fig small); TS artery, vein, capillary plan diagrams (fig, three panels); four
blood cells (fig small); capillary bed with pressure arrows (fig, viewBox ~560).
Links: PMT transport-in-mammals; SME 8-transport-in-mammals; OpenStax ch 40.

## bio.oxygen: Transport of oxygen and carbon dioxide (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 8.2. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe the role of red blood cells in transporting oxygen and carbon dioxide with reference to the roles of:
   haemoglobin; carbonic anhydrase; the formation of haemoglobinic acid; the formation of carbaminohaemoglobin
2. describe the chloride shift and explain the importance of the chloride shift
3. describe the role of plasma in the transport of carbon dioxide
4. describe and explain the oxygen dissociation curve of adult haemoglobin
5. explain the importance of the oxygen dissociation curve at partial pressures of oxygen in the lungs and in
   respiring tissues
6. describe the Bohr shift and explain the importance of the Bohr shift
Cover: haemoglobin recap (link bio.proteins); partial pressure in kPa; loading in lungs, unloading in tissues;
S-shaped curve and cooperative binding (first O₂ binds with difficulty, changes shape, second and third bind more
easily, fourth less easily as few sites remain); read % saturation at about 13 kPa (lungs, about 97–98%) and at
low pO₂ in active muscle (steep part: small fall in pO₂ releases lots of O₂); CO₂ transport: about 85% as hydrogen
carbonate ions, about 5% dissolved in plasma, about 10% as carbaminohaemoglobin (state as approximate textbook
figures); carbonic anhydrase: \(\ce{CO2 + H2O <=> H2CO3}\), \(\ce{H2CO3 <=> H+ + HCO3-}\); H⁺ binds to haemoglobin →
haemoglobinic acid (buffers pH, promotes O₂ release); HCO₃⁻ diffuses out into plasma, Cl⁻ moves in (chloride shift:
keeps the charge balanced); plasma carries HCO₃⁻ and dissolved CO₂; Bohr shift: higher pCO₂ shifts the curve to the
right, lower affinity, more O₂ released where respiration is fast; reverse in lungs. Fetal haemoglobin and myoglobin
are not in this syllabus: allow one `:::note` at most, labelled as beyond the syllabus.
Pitfalls: "haemoglobin carries CO₂ as carbon dioxide on the haem" (carbamino forms on amine groups of the globin);
"the Bohr shift increases affinity in tissues"; reading the curve with the wrong axis; "the chloride shift moves Cl⁻
out of the red cell".
Examples: read % saturation from a curve at two partial pressures and calculate the % unloaded; volume of O₂
released given the O₂ capacity of blood (e.g. 20 cm³ per 100 cm³ fully saturated; state as given data in the
question); exam 6 marks explain the Bohr shift; real context: carbon monoxide binds haem (one paragraph,
qualitative, as a stretch context described in the stem).
Figures: the dissociation curve with Bohr shift (`:::plot` with a Hill-type function, e.g.
`--f "100*x**2.7/(3.4**2.7+x**2.7)"` and a shifted one; say "model curve" in the caption); the red-cell diagram of CO₂
transport and chloride shift (fig, viewBox ~600, data/fig_oxygen.py).
Widget **bio-odc** after the Bohr shift.

## bio.heart: The heart and the cardiac cycle (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 8.3. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe the external and internal structure of the mammalian heart
2. explain the differences in the thickness of the walls of the: atria and ventricles; left ventricle and right
   ventricle
3. describe the cardiac cycle, with reference to the relationship between blood pressure changes during systole and
   diastole and the opening and closing of valves
4. explain the roles of the sinoatrial node, the atrioventricular node and the Purkyne tissue in the cardiac cycle
   (knowledge of nervous and hormonal control is not expected)
Cover: external (coronary arteries, atria, ventricles, major vessels) and internal (four chambers, septum,
atrioventricular valves: tricuspid right and bicuspid/mitral left, semilunar valves, tendinous cords) structure;
wall thickness: atria pump only to ventricles; left ventricle pumps to the whole body against greater resistance
(right only to the lungs, low pressure avoids damaging capillaries and allows tissue fluid balance); cardiac cycle:
atrial systole, ventricular systole, diastole; valves open and close because of pressure differences (always
explain with "pressure in X is higher than in Y"); the pressure graph (aorta, left ventricle, left atrium) with valve
events; heart rate from the graph (60 ÷ cycle time in s); cardiac muscle is myogenic; SAN (pacemaker) → wave of
excitation across atria → AVN delays (about 0.1 s, so atria finish contracting and ventricles fill) → bundle of His
→ Purkyne tissue → ventricles contract from the apex upwards; ECG not required (one `:::note` allowed).
Pitfalls: "valves open because muscles open them"; "left ventricle pumps more blood" (same volume, higher
pressure); "the AVN is the pacemaker"; reading the pressure graph without saying which chamber; ventricles contract
from the top.
Examples: from a pressure graph, state when the AV valve closes and explain why; calculate heart rate from cycle
length; explain wall thickness (exam 4 marks); real context: artificial pacemakers (qualitative).
Figures: internal heart structure LS (fig, viewBox ~560, labelled, drawn in data/fig_heart.py); conducting system
(fig small); pressure graph (via bio-cardiac and a static `:::plot`-free SVG version so the page works without it).
Widget **bio-cardiac** in the cardiac cycle section.

## Widget spec: bio-odc
Name `bio-odc`, file `widgets/bio-odc.js`. Attributes: `data-pco2="5.3"` (kPa).
- Model: % saturation = 100·p^n/(P50^n + p^n), n = 2.7, P50 = 3.4 kPa at pCO₂ 5.3 kPa; P50 rises with pCO₂
  (P50 = 3.4 + 0.25·(pCO₂ − 5.3), clamp pCO₂ 4–10 kPa). Say "model curve" in the widget note: values illustrate the
  shape and shift; they are not data for questions.
- Canvas: x pO₂ 0–14 kPa, y % saturation 0–100; reference curve at 5.3 kPa drawn faint, current curve in `sc`.
  Two draggable markers: "lungs" (default 13 kPa) and "tissue" (default 4 kPa, range 1–8). Readout: saturation at
  each and % unloaded (difference). Slider for pCO₂. A line of text: "Higher pCO₂ → curve shifts right → more O₂
  released at the same pO₂ (Bohr shift)".
- Survive zero size; no colour words.

## Widget spec: bio-cardiac
Name `bio-cardiac`, file `widgets/bio-cardiac.js`. Attributes: `data-bpm="75"`.
- One cycle of 0.8 s (at 75 beats per minute) of model pressure traces (kPa) for left atrium, left ventricle and
  aorta, drawn as smooth piecewise curves (generator in data/fig_cardiac.py: ventricle 0→ about 16 kPa peak; aorta
  about 10.5–16 kPa; atrium 0–about 1.5 kPa). Label each trace at its right end (no legend box).
- A time slider (and Play button that loops, reduced motion respected) moves a vertical cursor. Readout: phase
  (atrial systole / ventricular systole / diastole), state of the AV (mitral) valve and the semilunar (aortic) valve
  (open or closed), and the reason ("ventricular pressure > atrial pressure, so the AV valve is closed").
  Valve events marked with small labelled ticks where traces cross.
- bpm slider 50–150 rescales time (diastole shortens most); heart rate readout = 60/cycle time.
- Must match the chapter's statements exactly; the static figure in the chapter uses the same generator.
