## bio.foodtests: Testing for biological molecules (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 1, 2 and 3`, `@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 2.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe and carry out the Benedict's test for reducing sugars, the iodine test for starch, the emulsion test for
   lipids and the biuret test for proteins
2. describe and carry out a semi-quantitative Benedict's test on a reducing sugar solution by standardising the test
   and using the results (time to first colour change or comparison to colour standards) to estimate the concentration
3. describe and carry out a test to identify the presence of non-reducing sugars, using acid hydrolysis and Benedict's
   solution
Cover each test as a `:::lab` with method in steps, positive and negative results, and what the result means:
- Benedict's: equal volumes of sample and Benedict's solution, heat in a water bath (about 80–95 °C, a few minutes);
  blue → green → yellow → orange → brick-red precipitate as the concentration of reducing sugar rises (Cu²⁺ reduced to
  copper(I) oxide: `\(\ce{Cu^2+}\)` in words is enough).
- Semi-quantitative: standardise volumes, concentration of Benedict's, temperature, time; make colour standards from
  known glucose concentrations (serial dilution: link bio.practical) or time to first colour change; estimate the
  unknown; limits (subjective colour judgement; use a colorimeter, or filter and weigh the precipitate as extensions).
- Non-reducing sugar: negative Benedict's first; boil a fresh sample with dilute hydrochloric acid (hydrolysis of
  glycosidic bonds), cool, neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate (test with pH paper), then Benedict's; a positive
  result now shows a non-reducing sugar such as sucrose was present.
- Iodine (in potassium iodide solution): orange-brown → blue-black with starch (amylose helix traps iodine).
- Emulsion: dissolve in ethanol (shake), pour into water: cloudy white emulsion = lipid; ethanol is flammable (no
  flames).
- Biuret: biuret reagent or potassium hydroxide then copper sulfate solution; blue → lilac/purple with peptide bonds;
  no heating.
- Identifying unknown solutions with a sequence of tests (Paper 3 skill: "identify the contents of unknown solutions
  using biological molecule tests"); results tables with clear descriptions; hazard and risk (low, medium, high:
  syllabus wording, FACTS.md "Paper 3").
Pitfalls: "Benedict's turns red with sucrose"; not heating Benedict's; heating biuret; forgetting to neutralise
before Benedict's; "iodine test for glucose"; positive results described vaguely ("it changed colour").
Examples: a results table of five unknowns to identify; design a semi-quantitative test; Spot the error: a student
concludes sucrose absent because the first Benedict's was negative; exam-style 5 marks: plan the non-reducing test.
Figures: colour scale for Benedict's as labelled boxes with text names (never rely on colour alone: label each box);
flow chart for identifying an unknown (fig small).
Links: PMT biological-molecules; SME 2-biological-molecules; Cognito.

## bio.enzymes: Enzymes (gold chapter: written by the LEAD session before any Writer starts)
`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 1, 2 and 3`, `@time 5–6 hours`. Syllabus 3.1 and 3.2.1. Candidates should be able to:
3.1.1 state that enzymes are globular proteins that catalyse reactions inside cells (intracellular enzymes) or are
  secreted to catalyse reactions outside cells (extracellular enzymes)
3.1.2 explain the mode of action of enzymes in terms of an active site, enzyme–substrate complex, lowering of activation
  energy and enzyme specificity, including the lock-and-key hypothesis and the induced-fit hypothesis
3.1.3 investigate the progress of enzyme-catalysed reactions by measuring rates of formation of products using catalase
  and rates of disappearance of substrate using amylase
3.1.4 outline the use of a colorimeter for measuring the progress of enzyme-catalysed reactions that involve colour
  changes
3.2.1 investigate and explain the effects of the following factors on the rate of enzyme-catalysed reactions:
  temperature; pH (using buffer solutions); enzyme concentration; substrate concentration; inhibitor concentration

## bio.enzymes2: Enzyme kinetics, inhibitors and immobilised enzymes (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 1, 2 and 3`, `@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 3.2.2–3.2.4. Candidates should be able to:
2. explain that the maximum rate of reaction (Vmax) is used to derive the Michaelis–Menten constant (Km), which is
   used to compare the affinity of different enzymes for their substrates
3. explain the effects of reversible inhibitors, both competitive and non-competitive, on enzyme activity
4. investigate the difference in activity between an enzyme immobilised in alginate and the same enzyme free in
   solution, and state the advantages of using immobilised enzymes
Cover: recap of the rate–substrate concentration curve from bio.enzymes (link, one paragraph); Vmax (all active
sites occupied); Km = substrate concentration at ½Vmax, read from a graph (worked twice); low Km = high affinity
(reaches half its maximum rate at a lower substrate concentration); comparing two enzymes or isoenzymes; competitive
inhibitors (similar shape to substrate, bind to the active site, effect reduced by more substrate, Vmax unchanged, Km
increases) and non-competitive (bind elsewhere, an allosteric site, change the active site shape, Vmax decreases, Km
unchanged); reading inhibitor graphs; reversible v irreversible (one line); real context: drugs as inhibitors
(penicillin inhibits enzymes that build bacterial cell walls: one sentence and a link to bio.antibiotics),
end-product inhibition as a `:::note`; immobilised enzymes `:::lab`: sodium alginate + enzyme
dripped into calcium chloride to make beads; column of beads; compare product formation with free enzyme (e.g.
lactase with milk, test glucose); advantages: enzyme reused, product not contaminated with enzyme, more stable to
temperature and pH changes, can be used in continuous processes; disadvantages (set-up cost, slower diffusion of
substrate into beads may reduce rate).
Do not use Lineweaver–Burk or any equation for Km beyond reading ½Vmax from a graph (not in the syllabus).
Pitfalls: "Km is half of Vmax" (it is a substrate concentration); "competitive inhibitors change the active site";
"non-competitive inhibition can be overcome by more substrate"; "high Km = high affinity".
Examples: read Vmax and Km from a plotted curve (`:::plot` with `--f "Vmax*x/(Km+x)"` style numbers and `--hline`
at ½Vmax); compare two enzymes; exam-style 6 marks interpreting curves with and without an inhibitor; Spot the error:
student says Km halves when a competitive inhibitor is added. Exam practice needs a data table to plot mentally and
a ★★★ question linking inhibitors to a real drug or pesticide described in the stem.
Figures: rate v [S] with Vmax and Km marked (`:::plot`); competitive and non-competitive curves (`:::plot`, two
series max per plot: do two plots); immobilised bead column (fig small).
Use bio-enzyme in its `data-mode="kinetics"`.

## Widget spec: bio-enzyme
Name `bio-enzyme`, file `widgets/bio-enzyme.js`. Attribute `data-mode` = `temperature | ph | kinetics` (default
`kinetics`); tabs let the reader switch modes inside the widget.
- `temperature`: rate (arbitrary units) v temperature 0–80 °C, a model curve rising (rate roughly doubles every 10 °C
  at low temperatures) then falling steeply as denaturation sets in; optimum about 40 °C for a human-like enzyme. A
  slider moves a marker; the readout says "Rate rising: more kinetic energy, more successful collisions",
  "Near the optimum", or "Rate falling: bonds holding the tertiary structure break, active site changes shape".
  Note: model curve only, values arbitrary. Do not label a precise optimum value as a fact.
- `ph`: rate v pH 1–13 with a bell curve; a select for two example enzymes (a stomach protease, optimum about pH 2,
  and an enzyme with optimum about pH 7; label them "stomach protease (pepsin)" and "salivary amylase"). Readout text
  explains ionic and hydrogen bonds between R groups being disrupted by H⁺ or OH⁻ away from the optimum.
- `kinetics`: Michaelis–Menten curve rate = Vmax·[S]/(Km + [S]). Sliders: Vmax 20–100, Km 0.5–10 (mmol dm⁻³),
  [S] range 0–50. Checkboxes: competitive inhibitor (Km × 3), non-competitive inhibitor (Vmax × 0.5). Draw the
  uninhibited curve faint when an inhibitor is on. Mark Vmax (dashed line) and ½Vmax with the Km read-off (dashed
  drop line). Readout: Vmax, Km, rate at the selected [S]. Axes follow the values. Start: Vmax 80, Km 4,
  competitive on, so the reader sees the shifted curve at once.
- Survive zero size; no colours in text; the note says what to try ("Switch on the competitive inhibitor: Vmax
  stays, Km rises").
