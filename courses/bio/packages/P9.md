## bio.gasexchange: The gas exchange system (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 9.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe the structure of the human gas exchange system, limited to: lungs; trachea; bronchi; bronchioles; alveoli;
   capillary network
2. describe the distribution in the gas exchange system of cartilage, ciliated epithelium, goblet cells, squamous
   epithelium of alveoli, smooth muscle and capillaries
3. recognise cartilage, ciliated epithelium, goblet cells, squamous epithelium of alveoli, smooth muscle and
   capillaries in microscope slides, photomicrographs and electron micrographs
4. recognise trachea, bronchi, bronchioles and alveoli in microscope slides, photomicrographs and electron micrographs
   and make plan diagrams of transverse sections of the walls of the trachea and bronchus
5. describe the functions of ciliated epithelial cells, goblet cells and mucous glands in maintaining the health of
   the gas exchange system
6. describe the functions in the gas exchange system of cartilage, smooth muscle, elastic fibres and squamous
   epithelium
7. describe gas exchange between air in the alveoli and blood in the capillaries
Cover: the airway tree; a distribution table (tissue × trachea, bronchus, bronchiole, alveolus: cartilage C-shaped
rings in trachea, irregular plates in bronchi, none in bronchioles; ciliated epithelium and goblet cells in trachea,
bronchi, larger bronchioles; smooth muscle in trachea to bronchioles, most in bronchioles; elastic fibres
throughout, around alveoli; squamous epithelium in alveoli); functions (cartilage keeps airways open; smooth muscle
adjusts diameter; elastic fibres stretch on inhalation and recoil to help expel air; goblet cells and mucous glands
secrete mucus that traps particles and pathogens; cilia beat to move mucus up to the throat); alveoli: large total
surface area, thin squamous epithelium and capillary endothelium (short diffusion distance), good blood supply and
ventilation maintain steep concentration gradients; describe the route O₂ takes (alveolar air → squamous epithelium
→ capillary endothelium → plasma → red blood cell) and CO₂ the reverse; plan diagrams of trachea and bronchus walls
(link bio.practical2); recognising each in micrographs by cues (cartilage present or not, wall thickness).
Smoking is not in this syllabus: no smoking section (one `:::note` on mucus and cilia damage is allowed as context
only).
Pitfalls: "alveoli have cilia"; "cartilage in bronchioles"; "elastic fibres contract" (they recoil; only muscle
contracts); "gas exchange happens in the bronchi"; diffusion "of oxygen into the alveoli" for exchange (into blood).
Examples: complete the distribution table; identify trachea v bronchiole from a described section; exam 5 marks:
explain how the alveoli are adapted (each feature → reason); real context: asthma narrows bronchioles (smooth
muscle contraction, qualitative).
Figures: airway tree with labels (fig small); trachea wall plan (fig small); alveolus and capillary with diffusion
arrows (fig small).
Links: PMT gas-exchange-smoking; SME 9-gas-exchange; OpenStax ch 39.

## bio.disease: Infectious diseases (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 10.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. state that infectious diseases are caused by pathogens and are transmissible
2. state the name and type of pathogen that causes each of the following diseases: cholera – caused by the bacterium
   Vibrio cholerae; malaria – caused by the protoctists Plasmodium falciparum, Plasmodium malariae, Plasmodium ovale
   and Plasmodium vivax; tuberculosis (TB) – caused by the bacteria Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Mycobacterium bovis;
   HIV/AIDS – caused by the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)
3. explain how cholera, malaria, TB and HIV are transmitted
4. discuss the biological, social and economic factors that need to be considered in the prevention and control of
   cholera, malaria, TB and HIV (details of the life cycle of the malarial parasite are not expected)
Cover: pathogen, infectious, transmissible, vector, carrier as `:::def`s; for each disease one section: pathogen
(italic species names: write them in italics with `*Vibrio cholerae*`), transmission (cholera: faecal–oral, water and
food contaminated with faeces; malaria: female Anopheles mosquito vector, also blood transfusion and mother to fetus;
TB: droplets in air from coughs and sneezes of infected people, M. bovis in unpasteurised milk or meat from infected
cattle; HIV: body fluids, unprotected sex, shared needles, blood products, mother to child across the placenta, at
birth or in breast milk), and prevention and control under three headings biological, social, economic (cholera:
clean water, sewage treatment, ORS, vaccines of limited effect; malaria: insecticide-treated nets, draining standing
water, insecticides, prophylactic drugs, resistance of mosquitoes and parasites, no fully effective vaccine
widely used: state cautiously; TB: BCG vaccination, contact tracing, long multi-drug treatment (several months),
DOTS-style supervised treatment named only as "supervised treatment", MDR-TB, link with HIV; HIV: condoms, needle
exchange, screening blood, antiretroviral drugs that control but do not cure, no vaccine, education, testing). The
"discuss" outcome: a balanced 8–10 mark style model answer built point by point. Mention HIV infects T-helper
cells (link bio.immunity). No statistics or dates you cannot verify (no "x million deaths per year").
Pitfalls: "malaria is caused by mosquitoes" (by Plasmodium; the mosquito is the vector); "HIV is AIDS"; "TB is spread
in water"; "antibiotics cure HIV"; italics and capitals of species names.
Examples: a table of the four diseases; discuss the control of malaria (8 marks, model answer with B1 points);
interpret a graph of cases before and after a control programme (invented data, clearly stated as such); real
context: a cholera outbreak after a flood.
Figures: transmission routes diagram (fig small) or a comparison table instead.
Links: PMT infectious-disease; SME 10-infectious-diseases.

## bio.antibiotics: Antibiotics and resistance (`@time 2–3 hours`)
Syllabus 10.2. Candidates should be able to:
1. outline how penicillin acts on bacteria and why antibiotics do not affect viruses
2. discuss the consequences of antibiotic resistance and the steps that can be taken to reduce its impact
Cover: antibiotic definition (a substance, originally from microorganisms, that kills or inhibits bacteria with
little harm to human cells); penicillin: inhibits enzymes that form cross-links in peptidoglycan cell walls of
growing bacteria; water enters by osmosis; weakened wall cannot resist; cell bursts (osmotic lysis); acts only on
growing, dividing bacteria; human cells have no cell walls so are unaffected; viruses have no cell wall,
no peptidoglycan, no ribosomes or metabolism of their own (they use the host cell), so there is nothing to target;
resistance: random mutation produces resistance allele (e.g. an enzyme that breaks down penicillin), selection
pressure from antibiotic use, resistant bacteria survive and reproduce, allele frequency rises; spread by vertical
(reproduction) and horizontal (plasmids) transfer; A Level natural selection detail is in bio.selection: here keep
to the outline; consequences: infections harder to treat, longer illness, more deaths, more expensive drugs,
surgery and chemotherapy riskier, MRSA and MDR-TB as named examples; steps: prescribe only when needed (not for
viral infections), finish the course as prescribed, narrow-spectrum where possible, rotate antibiotics, hygiene in
hospitals, reduce use in farming, develop new antibiotics, surveillance. Antibiotic disc diffusion as a data
question (zone of inhibition diameter → area \(\pi r^2\)).
Pitfalls: "bacteria become resistant because they get used to the antibiotic" (resistance comes from random
mutation, then selection); "antibiotics kill viruses"; "the person becomes resistant"; "penicillin breaks down the
cell wall directly" (it stops cross-links forming in new wall).
Examples: zone of inhibition areas; explain why penicillin does not harm human cells; discuss steps to reduce
resistance (6 marks); data: graph of resistant isolates over years (invented data, stated).
Figures: penicillin action steps (fig small, flow); disc diffusion plate (fig small).
