## bio.membranes: Membrane structure and cell signalling (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 1 and 2`, `@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 4.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe the fluid mosaic model of membrane structure with reference to the hydrophobic and hydrophilic
   interactions that account for the formation of the phospholipid bilayer and the arrangement of proteins
2. describe the arrangement of cholesterol, glycolipids and glycoproteins in cell surface membranes
3. describe the roles of phospholipids, cholesterol, glycolipids, proteins and glycoproteins in cell surface membranes,
   with reference to stability, fluidity, permeability, transport (carrier proteins and channel proteins), cell
   signalling (cell surface receptors) and cell recognition (cell surface antigens – see 11.1.2)
4. outline the main stages in the process of cell signalling leading to specific responses: secretion of specific
   chemicals (ligands) from cells; transport of ligands to target cells; binding of ligands to cell surface receptors
   on target cells
Cover: why "fluid" (phospholipids and many proteins move laterally) and "mosaic" (proteins scattered like tiles);
bilayer self-assembly from hydrophobic tails away from water; intrinsic (transmembrane) and extrinsic proteins;
cholesterol between phospholipids (regulates fluidity: reduces it at higher temperatures, prevents tight packing at
low temperatures; reduces permeability to water and ions; adds stability); glycolipids and glycoproteins on the
outer surface (glycocalyx) for recognition, receptors and cell–cell adhesion; channel v carrier proteins; membrane
thickness about 7 nm; membranes inside cells too (organelles: link bio.cells). Cell signalling: ligand → transport
(in blood or by diffusion) → binds a complementary receptor → response (may involve a second messenger: name only,
detail in A Level bio.glucose; say "A Level"). Practical-flavoured data: beetroot membrane permeability with
temperature or ethanol (pigment leaks; colorimeter readings) as a data-handling question.
Pitfalls: "cholesterol makes membranes more fluid" without temperature; proteins "float on top"; "glycoproteins are
inside the cell"; confusing receptor (binds ligand) with carrier (moves substance across).
Examples: label a fluid mosaic diagram; explain beetroot results (exam 5 marks); explain why the membrane is
partially permeable; real context: insulin receptors (ligand–receptor; link forward to bio.glucose).
Figures: fluid mosaic model (fig, viewBox ~600, the one wide figure); cell signalling flow (fig small).
Links: PhET membrane-transport (explore), PMT cell-membranes-transport, OpenStax ch 5.

## bio.movement: Movement into and out of cells (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 1, 2 and 3`, `@time 5–6 hours`)
Syllabus 4.2. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe and explain the processes of simple diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, active transport,
   endocytosis and exocytosis
2. investigate simple diffusion and osmosis using plant tissue and non-living materials, including dialysis (Visking)
   tubing and agar
3. illustrate the principle that surface area to volume ratios decrease with increasing size by calculating surface
   areas and volumes of simple 3-D shapes (as shown in the Mathematical requirements)
4. investigate the effect of changing surface area to volume ratio on diffusion using agar blocks of different sizes
5. investigate the effects of immersing plant tissues in solutions of different water potentials, using the results
   to estimate the water potential of the tissues
6. explain the movement of water between cells and solutions in terms of water potential and explain the different
   effects of the movement of water on plant cells and animal cells (knowledge of solute potential and pressure
   potential is not expected)
Cover: diffusion (net movement down a concentration gradient; factors: gradient, distance, surface area,
temperature, size and lipid solubility of the molecule); facilitated diffusion (channel and carrier proteins,
polar and charged substances, passive); osmosis defined with water potential (net movement of water molecules from a
region of higher water potential to a region of lower water potential through a partially permeable membrane);
water potential: pure water 0 kPa, solutions negative, more solute → more negative; plant cells (turgid, flaccid,
plasmolysed, incipient plasmolysis; wall prevents bursting) v animal cells (crenate, burst/haemolysis); active
transport (carrier proteins, ATP, against the gradient; Na⁺/K⁺ pump as example: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in); bulk transport:
endocytosis (phagocytosis, pinocytosis) and exocytosis with vesicles and ATP. Practicals as `:::lab`: Visking tubing
with starch and glucose (or sucrose and water levels); agar blocks with indicator (e.g. phenolphthalein-alkali agar
in acid, time to decolourise; or the Paper 3 materials list thymolphthalein); potato or other tissue in sucrose
solutions: % change in mass v concentration, the x-intercept (no change) gives the concentration with the same water
potential as the tissue; serial dilution of sucrose (link bio.practical). SA:V: cube side \(L\): SA \(6L^2\),
V \(L^3\), ratio \(6/L\); cylinders \(2\pi r^2 + 2\pi rh\) and \(\pi r^2 h\); why large organisms need exchange
surfaces and transport systems (links to bio.gasexchange, bio.mammaltransport).
Pitfalls: "water moves from low to high concentration" stated without saying of what; "osmosis is diffusion of
solutes"; "plant cells burst"; "active transport uses energy from diffusion"; percentage change computed from final
mass; reading the x-intercept wrongly; SA:V units (it is per unit length, e.g. cm⁻¹ or a ratio like 6:1).
Examples: SA:V of cubes of side 1, 2, 3 cm; % change in mass and estimating tissue water potential from a graph (use a
`:::plot` with points and a line); explain plasmolysis with water potential terms (exam 4 marks); real context: why
oral rehydration solution contains glucose and salt (link cholera in bio.disease; keep to "cells of the gut lining absorb
sodium ions and glucose together, lowering their water potential, so water follows by osmosis", as a `:::note`), or why fresh water fish/cells in hypotonic
solution.
Figures: plant cell turgid/flaccid/plasmolysed (fig small, three panels); active transport carrier (fig small);
Visking tubing set-up (fig small).
Widget **bio-sav** in the SA:V section.

## Widget spec: bio-sav
Name `bio-sav`, file `widgets/bio-sav.js`. Attributes: `data-shape="cube|cuboid|cylinder"` (default cube).
- Inputs: side length (cube) 0.5–5 cm, or three sides (cuboid), or radius and length (cylinder), via `W.sliderNum`.
- Readout: surface area (cm²), volume (cm³), SA:V (as "n : 1" and in cm⁻¹), 3 s.f.
- Canvas: draws the shape in simple oblique projection (asymmetric angle, back edges dashed as a depth cue) to scale
  in a fixed box; under it a small bar chart of SA:V for sides 1–5 cm with the current one highlighted (drawn with
  `sc`, others `ink3`).
- Diffusion mode (tab "Agar block"): assume an indicator front moves inwards at a constant 0.1 cm per minute
  (state "model" in the note); show the time for the colour to reach the centre (half the smallest side ÷ rate) and
  the % of the volume still uncoloured after t minutes (slider 0–25 min), with a cross-section square that shrinks.
  This lets the reader see why big blocks take longer: values are model values, never quoted as data in the chapter.
- Survive zero size. Note line: "Double the side: the volume rises 8 times but the surface only 4 times."
