# P19: AS skills: maths and Paper 3

Chapters: `bio.maths`, `bio.practical`, `bio.practical2` (all `@level as`; `@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 1, 2 and 3` for
maths, `Paper 3` for the two practical chapters). No widgets (you may use `units` from physics). Read COURSE.md,
kit/STYLE.md and the gold chapter first. These are skills chapters but use the full topic-chapter format (worked
examples with marks, try sets, exam practice, quiz, checklist, recap, links). Paper 3 is a hands-on practical
exam: the student cannot do it here, so teach the decisions, recording, drawing and analysis with realistic supplied
results and drawings, and say clearly what happens in the real exam room (FACTS.md "Paper 3").

## bio.maths: Maths skills for biology (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Mathematical requirements, AS and A Level part (syllabus pages 63–64). Candidates should be able to:
understand and use the prefixes giga (G), mega (M), kilo (k), milli (m), micro (µ) and nano (n); select and use the
most appropriate units for recording data and the results of calculations; recognise and use numbers in decimal and
standard form; understand and use the symbols <, >, ⩽, ⩾, / (solidus followed by unit in table headings and labels
for graph axes), ∝ and Σ; make estimations of the results of calculations; use a calculator for addition,
subtraction, multiplication and division, and to calculate squares, square roots, reciprocals, logs (lg) and means;
take account of significant figures in calculations so that significant figures are neither lost unnecessarily nor
carried beyond what is justified (the correct number of significant figures for calculated quantities is the same as,
or one more than, the smallest number of significant figures in the data used in the calculation); record data from
experiments to an appropriate and consistent precision; calculate magnifications and actual sizes; calculate areas of
triangles, rectangles and circles; calculate perimeters of rectangles and circumferences of circles; calculate surface
areas and volumes of cuboids and cylinders; calculate the mean, median, mode and range of a set of values; recognise
and use ratios; calculate percentages and percentage changes; express errors in experimental work as percentage
errors; translate information between graphical, numerical and algebraic forms; construct and interpret
diagrammatic representations of data, including line graphs, pie charts, bar charts and histograms; understand when
data should be presented in the form of a bar chart, histogram or line graph; plot data on graph paper with the
variables correctly orientated on the axes and with each axis scaled appropriately; recognise when it is appropriate to
join the points on a graph with straight ruled lines and when it is appropriate to use a line (straight or curved) of
best fit; calculate the rate of change from the gradient of a straight line on a graph; calculate the rate of change
from the gradient of a tangent to a curved line on a graph.
Cover each as a short section with a biology example: unit conversions (including cm³ ↔ dm³, s ↔ min), standard form,
estimation, sig figs (the Cambridge rule; work unrounded, round at the end), percentage change (final − initial) ÷
initial × 100 and percentage error (uncertainty ÷ reading × 100, uncertainty = half the smallest division for a
single reading), ratios (simplest form, 1 : n), means, median, mode, range, rates from gradients and tangents (with a
`:::plot` curve and a drawn tangent: use `--f` for the tangent line too), graph type choice (line, bar, histogram,
pie), table headings "quantity / unit", lg as a calculator skill (e.g. pH, or counts on a log scale). Link
bio.microscopy (magnification) and bio.movement (SA:V) rather than repeating them.
Pitfalls: % change from the final value; rounding mid-calculation; tangents drawn as chords; units in the body of a
table; "rate = time".
Examples: % change in mass of tissue samples; rate from a tangent at t = 2 min on a product–time curve (choose a curve
different from the gold chapter's catalase data); percentage error of a 10 cm³ measuring cylinder reading; choose the
graph type for four data sets.
Links: PMT practical-skills; Cognito; SME topic questions.

## bio.practical: Practical skills: investigations (Paper 3) (`@time 5–6 hours`)
Paper 3 skills, investigation parts (syllabus pages 51–55; FACTS.md "Practical assessment"). Copy the expectations
precisely. Candidates should be able to, within an investigation:
- identify the independent variable and dependent variable; decide a suitable range of values for the independent
  variable; decide the number of different values of the independent variable (a minimum of five) and the intervals
  between them; decide how to change the value of the independent variable; decide how the dependent variable should
  be measured; decide the number of replicates at each value; decide on appropriate controls; decide which variables
  need to be standardised and how to standardise them (variables expected to have a minimal effect, such as variation
  between test-tubes of the same type, do not need to be standardised)
- follow instructions to collect results; consider the hazards of the procedure, including the use of any solutions and
  reagents, and assess the risk as low, medium or high; take readings to obtain accurate data (quantitative results)
  or observations (qualitative results)
- record raw results and calculated results in an appropriate table with descriptive headings including any required
  units (no units in the body of the table), with the independent variable to the left of (or above) the dependent
  variable; record quantitative data to the number of decimal places appropriate for the measuring instrument; record
  qualitative observations using clear descriptions
- display calculations clearly, showing all the steps and reasoning; use the correct number of significant figures
- display data as a graph (continuous data), bar chart (discontinuous or categoric) or histogram (frequency data), with
  the independent variable on the x-axis; axes labelled to match table headings with units; a scale using most of the
  grid that can be read to within half a square; points plotted as small crosses or dots in circles; a clear line of
  best fit, smooth curve or ruled straight lines joining points; no extrapolation unless it can be assumed
- calculate answers with correct significant figures; use a graph to find unknown values; estimate the concentrations
  of unknown solutions from qualitative results; identify the contents of unknown solutions using biological molecule
  tests; identify anomalous results and suggest how to deal with anomalies; describe patterns and trends; evaluate the
  confidence with which conclusions might be made
- summarise the main conclusions; state and explain whether a hypothesis is supported; make predictions; suggest
  explanations
- identify systematic or random errors (systematic errors may not affect the trend, random errors may); identify the
  main sources of error; suggest improvements (more effective standardising, more accurate measuring of the dependent
  variable, smaller intervals, replicates and means); suggest how to extend the investigation; describe improvements
  clearly in words or diagrams
Cover: one running investigation through the chapter (e.g. effect of sucrose concentration on mass of potato or
beetroot tissue, or effect of temperature on rate of a reaction with a colour change; not the gold chapter's
catalase data); serial and proportional dilution with a worked table (e.g. making 0.1–1.0 mol dm⁻³ from 1.0 mol dm⁻³
stock: proportional; tenfold serial dilution); judging an end point by colour against a standard; timing; using a
water bath and thermostatically controlled heating; the Paper 3 apparatus list (FACTS.md) so students know the
equipment; hazard table (substance, hazard, risk level, precaution) using the syllabus hazard codes; model answers
to typical Paper 3 command lines ("Identify the independent variable", "Suggest one significant source of error",
"Describe how you would modify …").
Pitfalls: fewer than five IV values; units in table cells; inconsistent decimal places; joining points dot-to-dot when a
trend line is expected (and the reverse); "use more accurate equipment" without saying which or why; "human error"
as a source of error; extrapolating.
Examples: complete a results table and graph from supplied raw data; estimate an unknown concentration from a
calibration graph; identify sources of error and improvements (exam 4 marks); risk assessment (3 marks).
Figures: a model results table (Markdown); model graph (`:::plot` with points and best-fit line); water bath set-up
(fig small).
Links: PMT practical-skills; PMT past papers cie-paper-3 `[unofficial]`.

## bio.practical2: Practical skills: microscopy and drawing (Paper 3) (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Paper 3 skills, microscope parts (syllabus pages 53–55). Candidates should be able to:
- set up a light microscope to view and observe specimens; follow instructions to find and draw particular tissues in
  plant and animal specimens and label the drawings appropriately; follow instructions to find and draw particular
  cells and structures within the cells; make a temporary slide of stained cells or tissues; calculate actual sizes of
  tissues or cells from measurements of photomicrographs, using magnifications, scale bars or representations of
  eyepiece graticules and stage micrometers; estimate the number of cells or cell organelles in a given area using a
  sampling method, such as grids or fields of view
- draw plan diagrams to show the distribution of tissues in a specimen, with no cells drawn and the correct proportions
  of layers of tissues; draw the observable features of cells in a specimen showing the correct shapes, the thicknesses
  of cell walls where applicable (drawn with two lines or drawn with three lines where two cells touch), the relative
  sizes and proportions, observable cell contents only
- measure tissue layers or cells from photomicrographs using a ruler or given scale, including representations of
  eyepiece graticules; make accurate observations from specimens including counting numbers of cells or cell
  organelles; record similarities and differences between two specimens using only their observable features
- record the fine details of the specimen, including drawing the detailed shapes of layers or outlines of specimens in
  plan diagrams and drawing the shape and position of observable cell organelles in cells
- make drawings using a sharp pencil to give finely drawn lines that are clear and unbroken; use most of the available
  space; show all the features observed with no shading; organise comparative observations, showing differences and
  similarities between specimens
- compare observable features of specimens, including similarities and differences between specimens on a slide and in
  photomicrographs
Cover: the drawing rules as a `:::key` checklist; plan diagram v cell drawing (side-by-side figure of a good and a weak
version); the "three lines where two cells touch" convention drawn; label lines (ruled, touching the structure, no
arrowheads, not crossing); comparison tables (feature | specimen 1 | specimen 2), observable features only; measuring
and calculating with eyepiece graticules (link bio.microscopy) and with a ruler on photomicrographs (convert mm to µm);
counting with a grid or across fields of view and scaling up; typical Paper 3 specimens (leaf, stem and root sections,
blood smear, onion epidermis, potato starch grains, cheek cells, anther or other plant organs) described in words.
Pitfalls: shading; sketchy or broken lines; drawing cells in a plan diagram; drawing what "should" be there, not what
is seen; labels on the drawing rather than with ruled lines; comparison rows with features of only one specimen;
proportions wrong (measure first).
Examples: critique two plan diagrams of a stem TS (a "Spot the error"); calculate the actual diameter of a vascular
bundle from a photomicrograph drawing with a magnification; estimate stomatal density from counts in five fields of
view with a known field diameter (area πr²); a comparison table of two leaf sections.
Figures: good v poor plan diagram (fig, two panels, data/fig_drawing.py); cell drawing with two- and three-line walls
(fig small); field-of-view counting grid (fig small).
