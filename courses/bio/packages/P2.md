## bio.microscopy: Microscopy and magnification (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 1.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. make temporary preparations of cellular material suitable for viewing with a light microscope
2. draw cells from microscope slides and photomicrographs
3. calculate magnifications of images and actual sizes of specimens from drawings, photomicrographs and electron
   micrographs (scanning and transmission)
4. use an eyepiece graticule and stage micrometer scale to make measurements and use the appropriate units,
   millimetre (mm), micrometre (µm) and nanometre (nm)
5. define resolution and magnification and explain the differences between these terms, with reference to light
   microscopy and electron microscopy
Cover: parts of a light microscope (eyepiece ×10, objectives ×10 and ×40 as in the Paper 3 kit; total magnification =
eyepiece × objective); making a temporary slide (`:::lab`: thin layer, drop of water or stain such as iodine for
onion epidermis or methylene blue for cheek cells, coverslip lowered at an angle to avoid air bubbles, safety);
units and conversions; \(M = I/A\) with the triangle and three worked forms; scale bars (measure the bar, use its
label to find M, then actual sizes); eyepiece graticule calibration with a stage micrometer (1 stage division = 10 µm
on the usual 1 mm/100 slide; find µm per eyepiece unit; it changes with each objective); magnification v resolution
(`:::def` cards with syllabus-style wording: magnification = how many times larger the image is than the actual
object; resolution = the ability to distinguish two points close together as separate); why electrons resolve more
(shorter wavelength); light about 200 nm, TEM about 0.5 nm (COURSE.md values only); TEM v SEM (thin section, 2D
internal detail v surface 3D image; both black and white, vacuum so specimens dead; false colour may be added);
when to use which. Drawing cells from slides is introduced here briefly (clear unbroken lines, no shading, label
lines ruled, proportions) and taught fully in bio.practical2: link it.
Pitfalls: mixing units in \(M = I/A\); measuring the image in mm and forgetting × 1000; calibrating once and using it at
another objective; saying "magnification increases resolution"; quoting a magnification with units.
Examples: (1) actual size of a mitochondrion from an electron micrograph measurement; (2) magnification from a scale
bar; (3) graticule calibration (e.g. 25 eyepiece units = 6 stage divisions of 10 µm at ×40 → 2.4 µm per unit) then a
cell length of 18 units; (4) exam-style: compare TEM and light microscope images, 4 marks; real context: diagnosing
malaria parasites in blood smears by light microscopy.
Figures: light microscope labelled (fig small); graticule over stage micrometer (fig small, lines checked with
Python); a scale-bar micrograph drawing.
Widget **bio-magnify** goes after the \(M = I/A\) section and again in graticule practice (data-mode attribute).

## bio.cells: Eukaryotic cells and organelles (`@time 5–6 hours`)
Syllabus 1.2.1–1.2.4. Candidates should be able to:
1. recognise organelles and other cell structures found in eukaryotic cells and outline their structures and
   functions, limited to: cell surface membrane; nucleus, nuclear envelope and nucleolus; rough endoplasmic reticulum;
   smooth endoplasmic reticulum; Golgi body (Golgi apparatus or Golgi complex); mitochondria (including the presence
   of small circular DNA); ribosomes (80S in the cytoplasm and 70S in chloroplasts and mitochondria); lysosomes;
   centrioles and microtubules; cilia; microvilli; chloroplasts (including the presence of small circular DNA); cell
   wall; plasmodesmata; large permanent vacuole and tonoplast of plant cells
2. describe and interpret photomicrographs, electron micrographs and drawings of typical plant and animal cells
3. compare the structure of typical plant and animal cells
4. state that cells use ATP from respiration for energy-requiring processes
Cover: the eukaryotic cell as a factory; each structure with structure (membranes: none, single, double) and function
in a table plus short paragraphs; the secretory pathway (RER → vesicle → Golgi → vesicle → exocytosis) as a worked
sequence; 80S v 70S ribosomes (S = sedimentation rate, a size measure; do not explain beyond that); circular DNA in
mitochondria and chloroplasts (no endosymbiosis detail needed, one sentence allowed as a `:::note`); cilia and
microvilli contrasted (moving v increasing surface area); recognising organelles in electron micrograph drawings
(shape cues: cristae, grana, stacked cisternae, ribosome dots on RER); plant v animal table; ATP: cells use ATP from
respiration for energy-requiring processes (active transport, movement, synthesis) without detail.
Pitfalls: "the cell membrane is a cell wall"; "mitochondria make energy" (they release energy in ATP via respiration);
"ribosomes are organelles with membranes"; Golgi body "makes proteins"; centrioles in plant cells (not in typical
plant cells); the nucleolus makes ribosomes (it makes rRNA and assembles ribosome subunits; keep to "makes
ribosomes" level).
Examples: label an electron micrograph drawing; trace the path of a secreted enzyme (pancreas cell); exam-style
compare plant and animal cell (4 marks); estimate the number of mitochondria (link magnification).
Figures: animal cell as seen with TEM (fig, viewBox ~560 wide, labelled); plant cell (fig); mitochondrion and
chloroplast details (fig small each).

## bio.prokaryotes: Prokaryotes and viruses (`@time 2–3 hours`)
Syllabus 1.2.5–1.2.7. Candidates should be able to:
5. outline key structural features of a prokaryotic cell as found in a typical bacterium, including: unicellular;
   generally 1–5 µm diameter; peptidoglycan cell walls; circular DNA; 70S ribosomes; absence of organelles surrounded
   by double membranes
6. compare the structure of a prokaryotic cell as found in a typical bacterium with the structures of typical
   eukaryotic cells in plants and animals
7. state that all viruses are non-cellular structures with a nucleic acid core (either DNA or RNA) and a capsid made
   of protein, and that some viruses have an outer envelope made of phospholipids
Cover: bacterium structure (also plasmids, capsule, flagellum, pili as "some bacteria have": mark as extra);
three-way comparison table; viruses: non-cellular, nucleic acid core, capsid, some enveloped (HIV is enveloped: link
bio.disease); are viruses alive? (debate in a `:::note`); sizes with magnification practice; link forward to
antibiotics (peptidoglycan target: bio.antibiotics) and classification (bio.classification). AS only: do not mention
Archaea (A Level).
Pitfalls: "bacteria have no DNA" or "no ribosomes"; "bacteria have a nucleus"; virus "cells"; 70S in eukaryotes only in
mitochondria and chloroplasts.
Examples: compare bacterium and plant cell (exam-style 5 marks); calculate actual length of a bacterium from a
micrograph; identify a virus from a description.
Figures: bacterium (fig small); virus (enveloped and non-enveloped, fig small).

## Widget spec: bio-magnify
Name `bio-magnify`, file `widgets/bio-magnify.js`. Attributes: `data-mode="mia|graticule"` (default `mia`).
- Mode `mia` (magnification, image, actual): three linked fields I, A, M; the reader picks which to calculate (tabs
  "Find M", "Find A", "Find I"). Unit selects for I and A (mm, µm, nm). Shows the working in a readout:
  convert → divide → answer to 3 s.f., and M written as "× 2500" (no units). Validation with friendly `.w-err`
  messages (zero, negative, empty). Start state: image 35 mm, actual 7 µm (a red blood cell) → M = × 5000.
- Mode `graticule`: a canvas showing an eyepiece graticule (0–100 units) laid over a stage micrometer (divisions of
  10 µm, 0.01 mm). Slider: objective ×10 / ×40 (tabs). The stage scale's apparent spacing changes with the objective;
  the reader drags the stage scale to line up zeros and reads how many stage divisions match how many eyepiece
  units; readout: µm per eyepiece unit. Second part: a cell spans N eyepiece units (slider) → actual length. Use
  values: at ×10, 10 eyepiece units line up with 10 stage divisions (100 µm), so 10 µm per eyepiece unit; at ×40,
  40 eyepiece units line up with 10 stage divisions, so 2.5 µm per eyepiece unit. Canvas must survive zero size.
  (The chapter's worked examples use other numbers so the widget does not give the answers away.)
- Note line: "Change the objective and watch the calibration change: you must recalibrate for every objective."
