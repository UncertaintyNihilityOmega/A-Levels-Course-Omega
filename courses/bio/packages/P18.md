## bio.conservation: Conservation (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 18.3. Candidates should be able to:
1. explain why populations and species can become extinct as a result of: climate change; competition; hunting by
   humans; degradation and loss of habitats
2. outline reasons for the need to maintain biodiversity
3. outline the roles of zoos, botanic gardens, conserved areas (including national parks and marine parks), 'frozen
   zoos' and seed banks, in the conservation of endangered species
4. describe methods of assisted reproduction used in the conservation of endangered mammals, limited to IVF, embryo
   transfer and surrogacy
5. explain reasons for controlling invasive alien species
6. outline the role in conservation of the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) and the Convention on
   International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES)
Cover: extinction causes, each explained as a chain (e.g. climate change shifts habitats faster than species can
migrate or adapt; competition from introduced species; hunting reduces population below a viable size, loss of
genetic diversity; habitat loss and fragmentation); reasons to conserve (moral/ethical, ecological (food webs,
keystone species), economic (food, medicines, tourism, ecosystem services like pollination), aesthetic, genetic
resources for crops); in situ (national parks, marine parks: protection, managed habitats) v ex situ (zoos: captive
breeding, education, research, reintroduction; problems: small gene pools, behaviour; botanic gardens; seed banks:
dried seeds stored cold, tested for viability, long-term storage; frozen zoos: gametes, embryos, tissue in liquid
nitrogen); assisted reproduction: IVF (eggs collected, fertilised in the lab), embryo transfer into a female of the same
species, surrogacy (a female of a related, more common species carries the embryo); invasive alien species (no natural
predators, outcompete natives, spread disease, hybridise; control by removal, biological control with care); IUCN
(assesses conservation status, publishes the Red List of threatened species, advises) and CITES (international
agreement regulating trade in endangered species by listing them in appendices; banning or licensing trade). No
invented statistics.
Pitfalls: "zoos solve extinction" (reintroduction is hard); "seed banks store plants"; "CITES protects habitats" (it
regulates trade); "invasive species are always animals"; "surrogacy uses the same species" (in this context often a
related species).
Examples: explain how habitat fragmentation leads to extinction (exam 5 marks); outline seed banks (4 marks); discuss
zoos (6 marks); real context: a named endangered mammal conservation programme described qualitatively.
Figures: in situ v ex situ summary (fig small or table).
Links: PMT biodiversity-classification-conservation; OpenStax ch 47.

## bio.genetech: Tools of genetic technology (`@time 5–6 hours`)
Syllabus 19.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. define the term recombinant DNA
2. explain that genetic engineering is the deliberate manipulation of genetic material to modify specific
   characteristics of an organism and that this may involve transferring a gene into an organism so that the gene is
   expressed
3. explain that genes to be transferred into an organism may be: extracted from the DNA of a donor organism; synthesised
   from the mRNA of a donor organism; synthesised chemically from nucleotides
4. explain the roles of restriction endonucleases, DNA ligase, plasmids, DNA polymerase and reverse transcriptase in the
   transfer of a gene into an organism
5. explain why a promoter may have to be transferred into an organism as well as the desired gene
6. explain how gene expression may be confirmed by the use of marker genes coding for fluorescent products
7. explain that gene editing is a form of genetic engineering involving the insertion, deletion or replacement of DNA at
   specific sites in the genome
8. describe and explain the steps involved in the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to clone and amplify DNA, including the
   role of Taq polymerase
9. describe and explain how gel electrophoresis is used to separate DNA fragments of different lengths
10. outline how microarrays are used in the analysis of genomes and in detecting mRNA in studies of gene expression
11. outline the benefits of using databases that provide information about nucleotide sequences of genes and genomes,
   and amino acid sequences of proteins and protein structures
Cover: recombinant DNA (DNA made by joining pieces from two or more different sources); three gene sources with pros
and cons (mRNA route via reverse transcriptase gives cDNA without introns, useful for bacteria); restriction enzymes
(cut at specific recognition sequences, often palindromic; sticky ends; same enzyme for plasmid and gene), DNA ligase
(phosphodiester bonds), plasmids as vectors (origin of replication, marker genes), DNA polymerase (copying, making the
second strand of cDNA), reverse transcriptase (mRNA → cDNA); promoter needed so RNA polymerase can bind and
transcribe in the host (eukaryotic promoters are not recognised by bacteria); fluorescent marker genes (e.g. GFP)
show which cells took up and express the plasmid (replacing antibiotic-resistance markers); gene editing defined
(CRISPR–Cas9 may be named as an example tool: guide RNA directs Cas9 to a specific sequence, cuts, the cell repairs:
keep to an outline in a `:::note`); PCR steps (denaturation about 95 °C breaks hydrogen bonds; annealing about 55–65 °C
primers bind; extension about 72 °C Taq polymerase adds nucleotides; repeated cycles double the DNA: \(2^n\) copies;
Taq is thermostable, from a thermophilic bacterium, so it is not denatured at 95 °C); gel electrophoresis (DNA is
negatively charged (phosphate groups), moves towards the anode; agarose gel acts as a sieve; shorter fragments move
further; DNA ladder of known sizes; stain or fluorescent label); microarrays (many DNA probes fixed on a chip; labelled
sample DNA or cDNA hybridises to complementary probes; fluorescence shows which sequences are present or which genes
are expressed; comparing two samples with two labels); databases (sharing sequences, comparing genes between species,
predicting protein structures, identifying mutations, designing primers and drugs).
Pitfalls: "restriction enzymes cut randomly"; "ligase forms hydrogen bonds between sticky ends" (it forms
phosphodiester bonds; sticky ends pair by hydrogen bonds); "PCR primers are enzymes"; "DNA moves to the negative
electrode"; "bigger fragments move further"; "after 10 cycles there are 20 copies".
Examples: number of copies after n cycles from one molecule (and from a given starting number); fragment sizes from a
restriction map, then predict a gel (link bio-gel); explain the role of each enzyme in making insulin-producing
bacteria (exam 6 marks); read fragment size from a ladder (log-linear relationship: interpolate between bands);
interpret a microarray grid (described).
Figures: making recombinant plasmid (fig, viewBox ~600, data/fig_genetech.py); PCR cycle with temperatures (fig small);
gel with ladder and samples (fig small).
Widget **bio-gel** in the electrophoresis section.
Links: Learn.Genetics labs gel and pcr; PMT genetic-technology; SME 19-genetic-technology; OpenStax ch 17.

## bio.genetech2: Genetic technology in medicine and agriculture (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 19.2 and 19.3. Candidates should be able to:
19.2.1 explain the advantages of using recombinant human proteins to treat disease, using the examples insulin, factor
  VIII and adenosine deaminase
19.2.2 outline the advantages of genetic screening, using the examples of breast cancer (BRCA1 and BRCA2), Huntington's
  disease and cystic fibrosis
19.2.3 outline how genetic diseases can be treated with gene therapy, using the examples severe combined
  immunodeficiency (SCID) and inherited eye diseases
19.2.4 discuss the social and ethical considerations of using genetic screening and gene therapy in medicine
19.3.1 explain that genetic engineering may help to solve the global demand for food by improving the quality and
  productivity of farmed animals and crop plants, using the examples of GM salmon, herbicide resistance in soybean and
  insect resistance in cotton
19.3.2 discuss the ethical and social implications of using genetically modified organisms (GMOs) in food production
Cover: recombinant human proteins (identical to human protein, so fewer immune or allergic reactions than animal
insulin; large quantities; no risk of infection from donated blood products (factor VIII); ethical and religious
acceptability; cost); adenosine deaminase (ADA) as enzyme replacement for ADA-deficient SCID; genetic screening
(testing for alleles: BRCA1/2 increase breast and ovarian cancer risk → choices such as more frequent screening or
preventive surgery; Huntington's (dominant, late onset, no cure: knowing helps decisions about having children);
cystic fibrosis (recessive: carrier testing, embryo testing in IVF (preimplantation genetic diagnosis)), newborn
screening); gene therapy (vectors: viruses, liposomes; somatic v germ line; SCID: ADA gene into the patient's
T-lymphocytes or bone marrow stem cells, then returned; inherited eye diseases: e.g. a gene delivered by a viral vector
into retinal cells (the eye is small, enclosed and accessible); problems: immune responses to vectors, short-lived
effect, insertion into the wrong place (risk of cancer)); ethics: consent, discrimination by insurers or employers,
anxiety, abortion choices, cost and access, germ line changes passed to future generations; GM salmon (growth hormone
gene with a promoter active all year → grows faster; farmed in contained sites; sterile females to reduce escape
risk); herbicide-resistant soybean (spray herbicide kills weeds not crop; higher yield; risk of resistant weeds);
insect-resistant (Bt) cotton (gene for a toxin from Bacillus thuringiensis; fewer insecticide sprays; resistance in
insects; effect on non-target insects); GMO food ethics (safety testing, labelling, gene flow to wild relatives,
monopoly and seed costs for farmers, benefits to food security).
Pitfalls: "gene therapy changes all the patient's cells"; "screening cures disease"; "GM crops are unsafe to eat" as a
fact (say "concerns include …"); one-sided "discuss" answers; Bt toxin "kills all insects".
Examples: explain the advantages of recombinant insulin (4 marks); discuss genetic screening for Huntington's (6 marks,
balanced); outline gene therapy for SCID (5 marks); discuss GM soybean (6 marks).
Figures: gene therapy ex vivo flow (fig, viewBox ~560).
Links: PMT genetic-technology; SME 19-genetic-technology; Learn.Genetics basics.

## Widget spec: bio-gel
Name `bio-gel`, file `widgets/bio-gel.js`. Attributes: `data-dna="5000"` (length, bp), `data-cuts="1200,3100"`
(cut positions for lane 1; linear DNA).
- Lanes: a DNA ladder (100, 200, 500, 1000, 2000, 3000, 5000 bp, labelled), and up to three sample lanes. For each
  sample the reader sets a linear DNA length and cut positions (or chooses a preset "enzyme A", "enzyme B", "A + B");
  the widget computes the fragment lengths and lists them.
- Run button animates bands moving down the gel (reduced motion: draw the end state); distance travelled ∝
  (log₁₀(max) − log₁₀(length)) so short fragments travel further; two fragments of the same length make one thicker
  band. Wells at the top labelled "−" (cathode) and the bottom "+" (anode).
- Readout: fragment lengths sorted; a note "DNA is negatively charged so it moves towards the anode; shorter fragments
  move further through the gel".
- Animation must be clicked and checked in the browser pane. Survive zero size.
