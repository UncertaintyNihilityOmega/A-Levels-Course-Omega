## bio.genecontrol: Genes, phenotype and gene control (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 16.2.6, 16.2.7 and 16.3. Candidates should be able to:
16.2.6 explain the relationship between genes, proteins and phenotype with respect to the: TYR gene, tyrosinase and
  albinism; HBB gene, haemoglobin and sickle cell anaemia; F8 gene, factor VIII and haemophilia; HTT gene, huntingtin
  and Huntington's disease
16.2.7 explain the role of gibberellin in stem elongation including the role of the dominant allele, Le, that codes for a
  functional enzyme in the gibberellin synthesis pathway, and the recessive allele, le, that codes for a non-functional
  enzyme
16.3.1 describe the differences between structural genes and regulatory genes and the differences between repressible
  enzymes and inducible enzymes
16.3.2 explain genetic control of protein production in a prokaryote using the lac operon (knowledge of the role of cAMP
  is not expected)
16.3.3 state that transcription factors are proteins that bind to DNA and are involved in the control of gene expression
  in eukaryotes by decreasing or increasing the rate of transcription
16.3.4 explain how gibberellin activates genes by causing the breakdown of DELLA protein repressors, which normally
  inhibit factors that promote transcription
Cover: gene → protein → phenotype chain for each of the four examples: TYR (tyrosinase converts tyrosine to the
pigment melanin via DOPA; recessive non-functional alleles → no melanin → albinism); HBB (β-globin; a base
substitution changes the codon for glutamic acid to one for valine (sixth amino acid); HbS polymerises at low O₂,
sickled red cells block capillaries and carry less O₂; codominance \(Hb^A\)/\(Hb^S\) and sickle cell trait); F8 (factor
VIII, a blood-clotting protein; X-linked recessive mutant alleles → haemophilia; link bio.inheritance); HTT
(huntingtin; dominant allele with an expanded CAG repeat (more repeats than normal) → abnormal protein → nerve cell
death in the brain, onset usually in adulthood); Le/le: Le codes a functional enzyme that converts an inactive
gibberellin precursor to active gibberellin (GA₁): tall plants; lele dwarf; structural v regulatory genes; inducible
(made only when the substrate is present, e.g. β-galactosidase) v repressible (made unless the end product is present);
lac operon: regulatory gene lacI codes for repressor protein; operator, promoter, structural genes lacZ (β-galactosidase),
lacY (lactose permease), lacA (transacetylase); no lactose: repressor binds operator, RNA polymerase cannot bind/
transcribe; lactose present: lactose binds repressor, changes its shape, it leaves the operator, transcription; no
cAMP; transcription factors in eukaryotes; gibberellin → DELLA proteins broken down → transcription factors (e.g.
PIF) freed → genes for growth or amylase transcribed (link bio.hormones barley).
Pitfalls: "lactose binds the operator"; "the repressor is made only when lactose is absent" (lacI is always
expressed); "albinism is caused by a lack of tyrosine"; HTT "recessive"; sickle cell "deletion".
Examples: explain how a single base change causes sickle cell anaemia (exam 6 marks; use the codon table given in
the stem); predict lac operon outcomes for mutants (stretch: no repressor, operator that cannot bind); explain dwarf
peas; data: β-galactosidase activity before and after lactose added (invented, stated).
Figures: lac operon off and on (fig, viewBox ~600, data/fig_lac.py); gibberellin and DELLA (fig small).
Links: PMT inherited-change; OpenStax ch 16; Learn.Genetics basics.

## bio.variation: Variation and the t-test (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 4 and 5`, `@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 17.1. Candidates should be able to:
1. explain, with examples, that phenotypic variation is due to genetic factors or environmental factors or a
   combination of genetic and environmental factors
2. explain what is meant by discontinuous variation and continuous variation
3. explain the genetic basis of discontinuous variation and continuous variation
4. use the t-test to compare the means of two different samples (the formula for the t-test will be provided, as shown
   in the Mathematical requirements)
Cover: genetic causes (alleles, mutation, meiosis, fertilisation) and environmental causes (diet, light, soil
minerals, temperature), with examples of each and both (height, skin colour); discontinuous (distinct categories,
one or few genes, little environment, e.g. ABO groups) v continuous (range, many genes (polygenes) with additive
effects, plus environment, e.g. height, mass); bar chart v histogram; normal distribution and its spread; mean,
standard deviation (formula given; link bio.stats for full detail); t-test: when it applies (continuous data, normal
distribution, similar SDs), null hypothesis ("there is no significant difference between the means"), the formula
(given), df = n₁ + n₂ − 2 (not given), critical value at p = 0.05 (table given; values in FACTS.md), conclusion in
words; Papers 4 and 5 may give partly completed working (show how to finish it).
Pitfalls: df = n − 1 for two samples; "t > critical → accept null"; claiming a cause from a t-test alone; SD computed
with n instead of n − 1.
Examples: classify variation examples; a full t-test on two small samples (e.g. leaf lengths in sun and shade: 8 and 10
values, compute means, s, t, df, conclusion); finishing a partly completed calculation; real context: comparing drug
and placebo groups (invented data). Check scripts compute s with n − 1 and import data/crit.py.
Figures: bar chart (discontinuous) and histogram (continuous) side by side (fig, two panels).
Widget **bio-ttest** after the worked t-test.

## bio.selection: Natural and artificial selection (`@paper CAIE 9700 Papers 4 and 5`, `@time 5–6 hours`)
Syllabus 17.2. Candidates should be able to:
1. explain that natural selection occurs because populations have the capacity to produce many offspring that compete
   for resources; in the 'struggle for existence', individuals that are best adapted are most likely to survive to
   reproduce and pass on their alleles to the next generation
2. explain how environmental factors can act as stabilising, disruptive and directional forces of natural selection
3. explain how selection, the founder effect and genetic drift, including the bottleneck effect, may affect allele
   frequencies in populations
4. outline how bacteria become resistant to antibiotics as an example of natural selection
5. use the Hardy–Weinberg principle to calculate allele and genotype frequencies in populations and state the conditions
   when this principle can be applied (the two equations for the Hardy–Weinberg principle will be provided, as shown in
   the Mathematical requirements)
6. describe the principles of selective breeding (artificial selection)
7. outline the following examples of selective breeding: the introduction of disease resistance to varieties of wheat
   and rice; inbreeding and hybridisation to produce vigorous, uniform varieties of maize; improving the milk yield of
   dairy cattle
Cover: natural selection as a chain (variation from mutation, overproduction, competition, selection pressure, best
adapted survive and reproduce, pass on alleles, allele frequency changes over generations); three types with
distribution graphs (stabilising: extremes selected against, e.g. human birth mass; directional: one extreme favoured,
e.g. antibiotic resistance; disruptive: both extremes favoured, two peaks); gene pool and allele frequency; genetic
drift (chance changes, strongest in small populations), founder effect, bottleneck effect; antibiotic resistance
outlined as selection (link bio.antibiotics); Hardy–Weinberg: p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (given), start from the
homozygous recessive frequency q², carriers 2pq; conditions (large population, random mating, no mutation, no
migration, no selection); using a change in frequencies as evidence that selection is occurring; selective breeding
principles (choose parents with desired traits, cross, select best offspring, repeat over generations; humans are the
selective agent); wheat and rice disease resistance (cross a high-yield variety with a resistant variety, select and
backcross); maize inbreeding (homozygous, uniform but weak: inbreeding depression) and hybridisation of two inbred
lines (F1 hybrids are vigorous (hybrid vigour) and uniform, seed bought each year); dairy cattle (milk yield is a
sex-limited trait: bulls are judged by daughters' yield (progeny testing); artificial insemination spreads semen of
chosen bulls).
Pitfalls: "organisms adapt because they need to"; "mutation caused by the antibiotic"; "survival of the fittest" without
reproduction and alleles; Hardy–Weinberg with p from the dominant phenotype frequency directly; carriers = q²;
"drift happens only in large populations".
Examples: Hardy–Weinberg from the frequency of a recessive condition (e.g. 1 in 2500 → q = 0.02, carriers 2pq ≈ 0.039:
check numbers in the script; practice uses different numbers); identify the type of selection from graphs; explain
the founder effect on an island population; selective breeding outline for maize (exam 5 marks).
Figures: three selection graphs (fig, three small panels, before/after curves, data/fig_selection.py).
Widget **bio-hardy** in the Hardy–Weinberg section.

## Widget spec: bio-ttest
Name `bio-ttest`, file `widgets/bio-ttest.js`. Attributes: `data-a="..."`, `data-b="..."` (comma-separated values).
- Two text areas for samples A and B (numbers separated by commas or spaces; friendly errors; at least 2 values each).
- Readout per sample: n, mean, s (n − 1), SE; then t = |x̄₁ − x̄₂|/√(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂), df = n₁ + n₂ − 2, critical t at
  p = 0.05 (two-tailed) from a table for df 1–30 (values in FACTS.md; for df > 30 say "use the table given"), and a
  conclusion sentence in exam wording.
- Canvas: means as bars or points with ±2 SE error bars (95% CI approximation from the syllabus), labelled A and B.
  Axis follows the data.
- Start with two plausible samples of 8 values (e.g. leaf lengths, in mm) giving a significant difference.

## Widget spec: bio-hardy
Name `bio-hardy`, file `widgets/bio-hardy.js`. Attributes: `data-q2="0.04"`.
- Tab "Calculate": the reader enters any one of q², p², 2pq (as a frequency, a percentage, or "1 in N"), and the widget
  shows q, p, p², 2pq, q² (3 s.f.), the working (q = √q², p = 1 − q, …) and a bar of the three genotype frequencies.
  Friendly errors for values outside 0–1.
- Tab "Drift": a seeded (W.rng) simulation of allele frequency over 50 generations for population sizes N = 10, 100,
  1000 (select) with 5 replicate lines; "Run again" reseeds. Shows that small populations drift and fix. Note: model
  with random mating and no selection (Wright–Fisher sampling of 2N alleles each generation).
- Survive zero size.
