## bio.carbs: Carbohydrates (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 2.2.1–2.2.8. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe and draw the ring forms of α-glucose and β-glucose
2. define the terms monomer, polymer, macromolecule, monosaccharide, disaccharide and polysaccharide
3. state the role of covalent bonds in joining smaller molecules together to form polymers
4. state that glucose, fructose and maltose are reducing sugars and that sucrose is a non-reducing sugar
5. describe the formation of a glycosidic bond by condensation, with reference to disaccharides, including sucrose,
   and polysaccharides
6. describe the breakage of a glycosidic bond in polysaccharides and disaccharides by hydrolysis, with reference to
   the non-reducing sugar test
7. describe the molecular structure of the polysaccharides starch (amylose and amylopectin) and glycogen and relate
   their structures to their functions in living organisms
8. describe the molecular structure of the polysaccharide cellulose and outline how the arrangement of cellulose
   molecules contributes to the function of plant cell walls
Cover: the six definitions as `:::def` cards; monosaccharides (triose, pentose, hexose; general formula
\((\ce{CH2O})_n\)); α- and β-glucose ring drawn with carbon numbers 1–6 and the difference at C1 (OH below the ring in
α, above in β, in the standard drawing); condensation forming a 1,4 glycosidic bond (maltose from two α-glucose;
sucrose from glucose + fructose; lactose optional as `:::note`); hydrolysis (link to the non-reducing sugar test:
acid hydrolysis frees reducing monosaccharides); amylose (1,4 α, coils/helix, compact), amylopectin (1,4 and 1,6
branches), glycogen (more 1,6 branches, more ends for rapid hydrolysis by enzymes, compact, insoluble so no osmotic
effect); cellulose (β-glucose, alternate molecules rotated 180°, straight chains, hydrogen bonds between chains →
microfibrils → fibres, high tensile strength, wall resists turgor pressure, freely permeable).
Pitfalls: drawing α/β the wrong way round; "glycosidic bond is a hydrogen bond"; "starch is a reducing sugar"; "glucose
is a polymer"; mixing up branched (amylopectin, glycogen) and unbranched (amylose, cellulose); hydrogen bonds within v
between cellulose chains.
Examples: draw the condensation of two α-glucose (labelled glycosidic bond and water); compare glycogen and cellulose
(exam 5 marks); explain why glycogen suits an animal's energy store; real context: why humans cannot digest
cellulose (no enzyme to hydrolyse β 1,4 bonds), ruminants rely on microorganisms.
Figures: α- and β-glucose rings (fig small each, or one fig with two panels, coordinates in data/fig_carbs.py);
maltose condensation (fig); amylose helix, amylopectin and glycogen branching (fig small, schematic); cellulose
chains and microfibril (fig small).

## bio.lipids: Lipids and water (`@time 3–4 hours`)
Syllabus 2.2.9–2.2.11 and 2.4.1. Candidates should be able to:
9. state that triglycerides are non-polar hydrophobic molecules and describe the molecular structure of triglycerides
   with reference to fatty acids (saturated and unsaturated), glycerol and the formation of ester bonds
10. relate the molecular structure of triglycerides to their functions in living organisms
11. describe the molecular structure of phospholipids with reference to their hydrophilic (polar) phosphate heads and
   hydrophobic (non-polar) fatty acid tails
2.4.1 explain how hydrogen bonding occurs between water molecules and relate the properties of water to its roles in
   living organisms, limited to solvent action, high specific heat capacity and latent heat of vaporisation
Cover: water first (it explains hydrophobic and hydrophilic): polarity (δ+ H, δ− O), hydrogen bonds (link
chem.bonding); solvent action (polar and ionic substances dissolve; transport in blood and xylem; reactions in
solution); high specific heat capacity (stable temperatures in cells and water habitats); high latent heat of
vaporisation (cooling by sweating and transpiration). Then lipids: glycerol + 3 fatty acids, 3 condensation
reactions, ester bonds, 3 water molecules; saturated v unsaturated (C=C, kinks, liquid oils); functions: energy
store (more C–H bonds, so more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate: about 39 kJ g⁻¹ v 16 kJ g⁻¹), insoluble so no osmotic effect, insulation,
buoyancy, protection, metabolic water; phospholipids: one fatty acid replaced by a phosphate group, polar head and
non-polar tails, bilayer forms in water (link bio.membranes). Emulsion test: one line, link bio.foodtests.
Pitfalls: "fats are polymers" (triglycerides are not polymers: no repeating monomer); "ester bond = glycosidic bond";
"hydrogen bonds inside a water molecule"; "unsaturated means no hydrogens"; "lipids are hydrophilic".
Examples: draw formation of a triglyceride (one ester bond in detail); explain why camels' fat in the hump is a good
water source (metabolic water, as a `:::note` with care: say "respiration of lipids releases water"); exam 4 marks:
relate water's properties to sweating.
Figures: water hydrogen bonding (fig small); triglyceride formation (fig, viewBox ~560); phospholipid and bilayer
(fig small).

## bio.proteins: Proteins (`@time 4–5 hours`)
Syllabus 2.3. Candidates should be able to:
1. describe and draw the general structure of an amino acid and the formation and breakage of a peptide bond
2. explain the meaning of the terms primary structure, secondary structure, tertiary structure and quaternary
   structure of proteins
3. describe the types of interaction that hold protein molecules in shape: hydrophobic interactions; hydrogen bonding;
   ionic bonding; covalent bonding, including disulfide bonds
4. state that globular proteins are generally soluble and have physiological roles and fibrous proteins are
   generally insoluble and have structural roles
5. describe the structure of a molecule of haemoglobin as an example of a globular protein, including the formation of
   its quaternary structure from two alpha (α) chains (α–globin), two beta (β) chains (β–globin) and a haem group
6. relate the structure of haemoglobin to its function, including the importance of iron in the haem group
7. describe the structure of a molecule of collagen as an example of a fibrous protein, and the arrangement of
   collagen molecules to form collagen fibres
8. relate the structures of collagen molecules and collagen fibres to their function
Cover: amino acid (amine group, carboxyl group, R group, central carbon; 20 types differ only in R; link
chem.org.nitrogen); peptide bond by condensation and its hydrolysis; dipeptide, polypeptide; four levels of
structure (primary = sequence; secondary = α-helix and β-pleated sheet held by hydrogen bonds between backbone
groups; tertiary = 3D folding held by R-group interactions; quaternary = more than one polypeptide); the four
interactions with where each forms and how strong (disulfide strongest, between cysteine R groups); globular v
fibrous; haemoglobin: 4 polypeptides (2 α-globin, 2 β-globin), each with a haem group containing Fe²⁺ that binds one
O₂, so 4 O₂ per molecule; hydrophilic R groups outside → soluble; collagen: three polypeptide chains wound into a
triple helix, glycine every third amino acid allows tight winding, hydrogen bonds between chains, covalent
cross-links between molecules, staggered ends → fibrils → fibres; high tensile strength, inelastic; tendons,
skin, bone, artery walls. Biuret test: one line, link bio.foodtests. Denaturation: link to bio.enzymes.
Pitfalls: "peptide bonds hold tertiary structure"; secondary structure by R groups; "haem is a protein"; iron "is
oxygen-carrying" without the haem/Fe²⁺ detail; collagen "is elastic"; disulfide bridges between any amino acids.
Examples: draw dipeptide formation; explain how a change in one amino acid can change a protein's function (link
sickle cell: A Level detail is in bio.genecontrol; at AS say "a different R group can change folding"); exam-style 6
marks comparing haemoglobin and collagen; real context: vitamin C deficiency (scurvy) weakens collagen cross-links as
a `:::note` (keep it qualitative).
Figures: amino acid general structure (fig small); dipeptide condensation (fig); levels of structure (fig, four small
panels); collagen triple helix to fibre (fig small, schematic).
