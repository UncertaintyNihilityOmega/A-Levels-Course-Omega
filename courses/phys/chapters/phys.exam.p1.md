## The paper in numbers

| | Paper 1: Multiple Choice |
|---|---|
| Questions and marks | 40 questions, 40 marks (one mark each) |
| Options | four per question, one correct |
| Time | 1 hour 15 minutes |
| Content | AS topics 1–11, which is every chapter up to [Particle physics](#phys.particles) |
| Weighting | 31% of an AS qualification, 15.5% of an A Level |
| Assessment objectives | AO1 (knowledge and understanding) and AO2 (handling, applying and evaluating information), 50% each |
| Data and formulae | page 2 of the paper |

Seventy-five minutes for 40 questions is 1.875 minutes, or about 1 min 53 s, each. That is under two minutes per question, and some will take 20 seconds while a few will take three minutes. Do not spend the same time on each: spend your time where the marks are hardest to find.

:::tip Plan the time in two passes
Aim for **1.5 minutes per question on the first pass**: \(40 \times 1.5 = 60\) minutes. That leaves **15 minutes** for the questions you flagged and a last check. Checkpoints: about 15 minutes gone after question 10, 30 after question 20, 45 after question 30. If you are behind a checkpoint, speed up rather than panic: the questions you can answer in 30 seconds are worth exactly as much as the hard ones.
:::

:::tip Never leave a blank
Each question is worth one mark, so a blank can never score and a guess can. There is no reason to leave a blank. Make the guess an informed one: remove the options you can show are wrong (this guide shows you how), then choose from what is left.
:::

Both assessment objectives matter. AO1 questions test recall: a definition, a law, a unit, a standard graph shape. AO2 questions give you a new situation and ask you to apply what you know to it, such as an unfamiliar formula whose units you must work out, or a graph from an experiment you have not seen. The demonstrations below include both kinds.

## A working method for every question

:::steps
1. **Read the whole stem first, with the options covered.** Read it twice if it has numbers. Many wrong answers come from answering a nearby question (the stem asks for the *total* distance and you find the final speed).
2. **Mark what is given and what is asked.** Write each number with its unit on the question paper's margin or your rough paper, and note the quantity wanted and the unit it must be in.
3. **Predict before you look.** Work out the answer, or at least its rough size and its units, then look at the options. A prediction protects you from options built to look attractive.
4. **Compare, and if nothing matches, check your slip.** If your answer is not an option, you have almost certainly made a slip (a unit, a square, an inverted ratio): do not pick the nearest one.
5. **Eliminate with tools, not feelings.** Units, limiting cases (what if the length were zero or the angle 90°?), signs and sense checks (a person does not run at 300 m s⁻¹) remove options quickly.
6. **Choose, then move on.** If you are still stuck after about two minutes, mark the question lightly, put your best guess down and return to it on the second pass.
7. **Use the last 15 minutes on the marked questions first,** then re-check any calculation you did in a hurry.
:::

:::warn A match is not a proof
Wrong options are often exactly what you get from a common slip: forgetting to square, using the diameter as the radius, dividing instead of multiplying. If your answer matches an option very quickly, spend five seconds asking whether it is the slip answer. The demonstrations below point out which option each slip produces.
:::

:::exam Use the data and formulae sheet, and know what is not on it
The constants (\(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(c = 3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), \(h = 6.63 \times 10^{-34}\,\mathrm{J\,s}\) and the others) are on page 2, so you never need to remember them. The formulae on page 2 are few: \(s = ut + \tfrac12 at^2\), \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\), \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\), the upthrust \(F = \rho g V\), the Doppler formula, \(I = Anvq\), and the resistor combination rules. Everything else, such as \(F = ma\), \(E_K = \tfrac12 mv^2\), \(V = IR\), \(P = VI\) and \(v = f\lambda\), you must recall. Open the sheet at the start of the paper and read it once, so that you know what it offers.
:::

## Ten techniques, ten demonstrations

Each demonstration is a multiple-choice question in the style of Paper 1, with the reasoning that finds the answer quickly. Cover the options and try the question first.

### Technique 1: units and homogeneity

When the options differ only in their indices, work with units alone. Follow kilograms, metres and seconds one at a time. Any number or constant with no unit (such as \(6\pi\)) can be ignored.

:::eg Technique 1, units: the base units of the viscosity
The drag force on a small sphere of radius \(r\) moving at speed \(v\) through a liquid is \(F = 6\pi\eta r v\), where \(\eta\) is the viscosity of the liquid. What are the base units of \(\eta\)?

- **A** \(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\)
- **B** \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\)
- **C** \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\)
- **D** \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-1}}\)

Make \(\eta\) the subject: \(\eta = \dfrac{F}{6\pi r v}\). The \(6\pi\) has no unit. A force is \(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\) (from \(F = ma\)), and \(rv\) has units \(\mathrm{m \times m\,s^{-1} = m^2\,s^{-1}}\). So
\[\eta:\quad \frac{\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}}{\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-1}}} = \mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-1}}\]
**Answer: B.** Option C is the base unit of pressure (\(F/A\)), which you get if you divide by the area of a face instead of by \(rv\). Revise [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units).
:::

### Technique 2: estimation

Some questions give no numbers and ask for the order of magnitude. Use round, defensible values and find the power of ten. You do not need a calculator: you need to be within a factor of about three.

:::eg Technique 2, estimation: the kinetic energy of a jogging student
Which is the best estimate of the kinetic energy of a student jogging to school?

- **A** 7.5 J
- **B** 75 J
- **C** 750 J
- **D** 7500 J

A student has a mass of about 60 kg. Jogging is about 5 m s⁻¹ (a person who runs 1 km in just over 3 minutes). Then
\[E_K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 60 \times 5^2 = 750\,\mathrm{J}\]
**Answer: C.** Test the neighbours: option B needs a speed of only 1.6 m s⁻¹ (a slow walk), and option D needs 16 m s⁻¹, faster than any student can run. Only C fits a jog. Revise [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
:::

:::tip Standard sizes worth knowing
Mass of a person 60–80 kg · height of a person 1.7 m · walking speed 1.5 m s⁻¹ · car speed on a motorway 30 m s⁻¹ · speed of sound in air 340 m s⁻¹ · mass of a car 1000–1500 kg · mass of a sheet of paper 5 g · power of a kettle 2 kW · power of a filament lamp 60 W · domestic mains p.d. 230 V (or 120 V) · diameter of an atom \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\) · diameter of a nucleus \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\).
:::

### Technique 3: ratio and proportion

If a question changes a quantity by a factor, do not calculate anything with the numbers. Write the formula as a proportion, replace each changed quantity by its factor, and multiply. For the inverse-square law \(F \propto 1/r^2\), doubling \(r\) gives a factor \((1/2)^2 = 1/4\), and tripling \(r\) gives \(1/9\).

:::eg Technique 3, ratio: a wire with twice the length and half the diameter
A wire has resistance \(R\). A second wire made of the same material has twice the length and half the diameter. What is the resistance of the second wire?

- **A** 16\(R\)
- **B** 8\(R\)
- **C** 4\(R\)
- **D** 2\(R\)

Start from \(R = \rho L/A\) with \(A \propto d^2\) (the area of a circle). So \(R \propto L/d^2\).

Length doubled: factor 2. Diameter halved: \(d^2\) becomes \((1/2)^2 = 1/4\), so dividing by it gives factor 4.
\[\text{new resistance} = R \times 2 \times 4 = 8R\]
**Answer: B.** Option C comes from halving the *area* instead of the diameter, D from ignoring the diameter change, and A from squaring both factors. Revise [Electricity](#phys.electricity).
:::

### Technique 4: reading graphs (gradient or area)

Before you read a graph, ask what each axis is. The **gradient** is the change in the \(y\)-quantity per unit of the \(x\)-quantity. The **area** is the \(y\)-quantity multiplied by the \(x\)-quantity, so it is only meaningful if that product is a real quantity.

| Graph | Gradient | Area under the graph |
|---|---|---|
| displacement–time | velocity | no meaning |
| velocity–time | acceleration | displacement |
| acceleration–time | no meaning | change in velocity |
| force–displacement | no meaning | work done |
| force–extension | spring constant (straight part) | energy stored (elastic) |
| pressure–volume | no meaning | work done by or on the gas |
| current–time | no meaning | charge |
| power–time | no meaning | energy |

:::eg Technique 4, graphs: the distance from a velocity–time graph
A cyclist accelerates uniformly for 4.0 s, travels at a constant 8.0 m s⁻¹ for 6.0 s, then brakes uniformly to rest in 4.0 s, as shown. What is the total distance travelled?

The distance is the area under the graph. The trapezium has parallel sides of 6.0 s (the top) and 14 s (the base) and a height of 8.0 m s⁻¹:

**Answer: C.** Option A is only the constant-speed part. B is a triangle of base 14 s, ignoring that the cyclist stays at top speed. D is \(14 \times 8.0\), as if the cyclist had gone at top speed throughout. Revise [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics).
:::

### Technique 5: vector options

For two perpendicular vectors, sketch the right-angled triangle and use Pythagoras for the size. For the direction, sketch first: the larger component is closer to the resultant, which lets you reject options without any calculation.

:::eg Technique 5, vectors: a resultant force
Two forces act on a point: 6.0 N due east and 8.0 N due north. Which row gives the magnitude and direction of the resultant force?

- **A** 10 N at 37° north of east
- **B** 10 N at 53° north of east
- **C** 14 N at 37° north of east
- **D** 14 N at 53° north of east

The forces are at right angles, so \(F = \sqrt{6.0^2 + 8.0^2} = 10\,\mathrm{N}\). That removes C and D at once (14 N is the sum of the sizes, which is only right if the forces point the same way).

For the angle, the northward force is larger, so the resultant leans more towards north: its angle from east must be more than 45°. That removes A. Check: \(\tan\theta = 8.0/6.0\), so \(\theta = 53^\circ\).

**Answer: B.** The slip that gives A is swapping the opposite and adjacent sides. Revise [Dynamics](#phys.dynamics) and [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces).
:::

### Technique 6: choosing between near-identical definitions

Definition options are usually the right idea with one phrase altered. Look for the **key phrases** that the exam wording needs, and test each option for them. For e.m.f. the key phrases are "per unit charge", "converted into electrical energy" and "source".

:::eg Technique 6, definitions: electromotive force
Which statement correctly defines the electromotive force (e.m.f.) of a cell?

- **A** the energy transferred per unit charge as charge passes through a resistor
- **B** the potential difference across the terminals of the cell when it supplies a current
- **C** the energy converted from chemical to electrical form per unit charge when the cell drives charge round a complete circuit
- **D** the total energy stored in the cell

Option A is the definition of **p.d.**: energy transferred *from* electrical *to* other forms, in a component. Option B describes the terminal p.d., which is less than the e.m.f. when a current flows because of the internal resistance. Option D has no "per unit charge", so it cannot be a ratio and its unit would be the joule, not the volt. Option C has all three key phrases.

**Answer: C.** Revise [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc). The master table in [Papers 2 and 4: structured questions](#phys.exam.p24) lists the exam wording of every definition in this course.
:::

### Technique 7: combining uncertainties

For a quantity found by **multiplying or dividing** measurements, add the **percentage** (or fractional) uncertainties. For **adding or subtracting**, add the **absolute** uncertainties. Powers multiply the percentage by the power.

:::eg Technique 7, uncertainties: a resistance from V and I
A p.d. of \(8.0 \pm 0.2\,\mathrm{V}\) and a current of \(0.50 \pm 0.01\,\mathrm{A}\) are measured. The resistance is found from \(R = V/I\). Which is the resistance with its absolute uncertainty?

- **A** \(16 \pm 0.08\,\Omega\)
- **B** \(16 \pm 0.2\,\Omega\)
- **C** \(16 \pm 0.7\,\Omega\)
- **D** \(16 \pm 4.5\,\Omega\)

\(R = 8.0/0.50 = 16\,\Omega\). Percentage uncertainty in \(V\): \(0.2/8.0 = 2.5\%\). In \(I\): \(0.01/0.50 = 2.0\%\). For a quotient the percentages **add**: \(2.5\% + 2.0\% = 4.5\%\).
\[\Delta R = 0.045 \times 16 = 0.72\,\Omega \approx 0.7\,\Omega\]
**Answer: C.** Option A subtracts the percentages (a quotient does not cancel uncertainty). B adds the absolute uncertainties (0.2 + 0.01, which mixes volts and amperes). D forgets to convert 4.5% to a fraction. Revise [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units).
:::

### Technique 8: circuit questions and potentials

For a bridge or any circuit with two parallel branches, choose **one** reference point (usually the negative terminal) and find the potential of every point you need relative to it. A voltmeter between two points then reads the difference of those potentials. Where a branch is two equal resistors, symmetry says that the middle point is exactly half-way.

### Technique 9: reading wave graphs

A graph of displacement against **distance** is a snapshot of the whole wave: it gives the **wavelength** (crest to crest). A graph of displacement against **time** follows one point: it gives the **period** (crest to crest again). The two together give \(v = \lambda/T\). Read the axis label before you read anything else.

:::eg Technique 9, wave graphs: a speed from two graphs
A graph of displacement against distance for a wave on a string shows that adjacent crests are 0.80 m apart. A graph of displacement against time for one point on the string shows that a crest and the **next trough** are 0.010 s apart. What is the speed of the wave?

- **A** \(0.016\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
- **B** \(0.025\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
- **C** \(40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
- **D** \(80\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)

The wavelength is \(\lambda = 0.80\,\mathrm{m}\). A crest to the next trough is only **half** a cycle, so the period is \(T = 2 \times 0.010 = 0.020\,\mathrm{s}\). Then
\[v = \frac{\lambda}{T} = \frac{0.80}{0.020} = 40\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\]
**Answer: C.** Option D uses 0.010 s as the period. A multiplies \(\lambda\) by \(T\), and B divides the other way round (a speed of 0.025 m s⁻¹ would be a snail's pace for a wave on a string). Revise [Waves](#phys.waves).
:::

### Technique 10: counting charge in particle physics

Write the charge of every quark beside its symbol: up \(+\tfrac23\), down \(-\tfrac13\), and the antiquark has the **opposite** sign. Add. Then use the same method for baryon number (quarks \(+\tfrac13\) each, antiquarks \(-\tfrac13\)) and lepton number.

:::eg Technique 10, quarks: the charge of a meson
A meson is made of an up quark and an anti-down quark. What is its charge, in units of the elementary charge \(e\)?

- **A** −1
- **B** 0
- **C** \(+\tfrac13\)
- **D** +1

Up quark: \(+\tfrac23\). Down quark: \(-\tfrac13\), so the **anti**-down quark is \(+\tfrac13\). Total: \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = +1\).

**Answer: D.** Option C comes from forgetting to flip the sign of the antiquark (\(\tfrac23 - \tfrac13\)). A meson is a quark and an antiquark, so its baryon number is \(\tfrac13 - \tfrac13 = 0\). Check with a familiar case: a proton (uud) has \(\tfrac23 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = +1\). Revise [Particle physics](#phys.particles).
:::

## Common traps

| Trap | Why it happens | How to avoid it |
|---|---|---|
| Calculator in radians when the angle is in degrees (or the reverse) | the mode was left from a previous question | before any trig, test \(\sin 30\): it must give 0.5 in degree mode |
| \(\mathrm{mm^2}\) to \(\mathrm{m^2}\) | \(1\,\mathrm{mm^2} = 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\), not \(10^{-3}\) | convert the length first, then square: \((10^{-3})^2\) |
| Confusing wavelength and period | both are "crest to crest" | read the \(x\)-axis label: distance gives \(\lambda\), time gives \(T\) |
| Adding percentages for a sum | the rule differs for sums and products | sums and differences: add **absolute** uncertainties; products and quotients: add **percentage** |
| Area where the gradient is wanted | both are used on graphs | ask what the \(y\)-quantity times the \(x\)-quantity would mean |
| Weight and mass | both are "how heavy" | weight is a force in newtons, \(W = mg\); mass is in kg |
| Ignoring internal resistance | the cell is drawn as a simple symbol | when current flows, terminal p.d. = e.m.f. \(- Ir\) |
| A wrong Newton's third-law pair | the pair must be the same type of force on **different** bodies | the pair of "Earth pulls book down" is "book pulls Earth up", not "table pushes book up" |
| Components with sin and cos swapped | no sketch was drawn | the component adjacent to the angle uses cos; check with an angle of 0° |

## Calculator and time management

- **Mode.** Degrees for angles in degrees. Make this a habit at the start of the paper.
- **Brackets.** Put a numerator and a denominator each in brackets, or press the equals key after the numerator and divide the answer. \(\dfrac{6.63 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.00 \times 10^{8}}{5.0 \times 10^{-7}}\) is a common source of one-line slips.
- **Standard form.** Use the exponent key (EXP or \(\times 10^x\)), never the multiplication key followed by 10 and a power.
- **Unrounded values.** Keep a number in the memory (ANS) when a second step needs it; round only at the end.
- **Sense check.** Compare with an estimate: a car's kinetic energy is not \(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{J}\). If the power of ten is wrong, the key press was wrong.
- **Time.** First pass 1.5 minutes per question, flag anything that has not yielded in about two minutes, 15 minutes at the end for flagged questions.
- **Answer every question.** Never leave a blank.

## Check yourself: ten Paper 1 style questions

:::quiz Ten multiple-choice questions on AS topics
? A stone is dropped from rest from a bridge. Ignoring air resistance, how far has it fallen after 3.0 s?
- 15 m
- 29 m
+ 44 m
- 88 m
> \(s = \tfrac12 gt^2 = \tfrac12 \times 9.81 \times 3.0^2 = 44\,\mathrm{m}\). 29 m is \(gt\), the speed, not the distance. 88 m forgets the \(\tfrac12\). 15 m is \(\tfrac12 gt\). Revise [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics).

? A 2.0 kg trolley moving at 3.0 m s⁻¹ collides with a stationary 1.0 kg trolley and the two stick together. How much kinetic energy is lost?
- 0 J
+ 3.0 J
- 6.0 J
- 9.0 J
> Momentum is conserved: \(6.0 = 3.0v\), so \(v = 2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The kinetic energy falls from \(\tfrac12 \times 2.0 \times 3.0^2 = 9.0\,\mathrm{J}\) to \(\tfrac12 \times 3.0 \times 2.0^2 = 6.0\,\mathrm{J}\), a loss of 3.0 J. 0 J assumes the collision is elastic. 6.0 J is the final energy and 9.0 J the initial energy. Revise [Dynamics](#phys.dynamics).

? A uniform metre rule of weight 1.2 N is pivoted at the 0.30 m mark. A weight \(W\) hung at the 0 m end makes the rule balance horizontally. What is \(W\)?
- 0.36 N
+ 0.80 N
- 1.2 N
- 1.8 N
> The weight of the rule acts at its centre, the 0.50 m mark, which is 0.20 m from the pivot: \(W \times 0.30 = 1.2 \times 0.20\), so \(W = 0.80\,\mathrm{N}\). 1.8 N swaps the two distances. 0.36 N is \(1.2 \times 0.30\) with no division. 1.2 N assumes equal weights balance. Revise [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces).

? A crane lifts a 400 kg load at a steady speed of 0.50 m s⁻¹. What is the useful power output of the crane?
- \(2.0 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W}\)
+ \(2.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W}\)
- \(7.8 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W}\)
> At steady speed the lifting force equals the weight, so \(P = Fv = mgv = 400 \times 9.81 \times 0.50 = 1.96 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W}\), which is \(2.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W}\) to 2 s.f. \(2.0 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W}\) is \(mv\), with \(g\) forgotten. \(7.8 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W}\) divides by the speed. Revise [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).

? A wire extends by \(x\) under a load \(F\). A second wire of the same material, with twice the length and twice the diameter, carries the same load. What is its extension?
- \(x/4\)
+ \(x/2\)
- \(x\)
- \(2x\)
> The extension is \(x = FL/(AE)\), so \(x \propto L/d^2\). The length doubles (factor 2) and \(d^2\) becomes four times larger (divide by 4): \(2/4 = \tfrac12\). \(x/4\) leaves out the length. \(x\) assumes that doubling both cancels. Revise [Deformation of solids](#phys.solids).

? The amplitude of a progressive wave of fixed frequency is multiplied by 3. What happens to the intensity?
- it is multiplied by 3
- it is multiplied by 6
+ it is multiplied by 9
- it is multiplied by 27
> Intensity is proportional to the **square** of the amplitude, so it is multiplied by \(3^2 = 9\). Multiplying by 3 treats it as linear; 27 is the cube. Revise [Waves](#phys.waves).

? A string fixed at both ends vibrates in its third harmonic at a frequency of 150 Hz. What is the frequency of the fundamental (first harmonic)?
- 37.5 Hz
+ 50 Hz
- 75 Hz
- 450 Hz
> For a string fixed at both ends the \(n\)th harmonic has frequency \(nf_1\), so \(f_1 = 150/3 = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}\). 37.5 Hz treats the third harmonic as the third overtone (the fourth harmonic). 450 Hz multiplies instead of dividing. Revise [Superposition](#phys.superposition).

? A copper wire carries a steady current. Another copper wire carries the same current but has twice the diameter. How does the mean drift speed of the electrons in the second wire compare with that in the first?
+ one quarter
- one half
- twice
- four times
> \(I = Anvq\) with \(n\) and \(q\) unchanged, so \(v \propto 1/A\). The area is proportional to \(d^2\), so the area becomes four times larger and \(v\) falls to one quarter. "One half" forgets that area depends on the square of the diameter. Revise [Electricity](#phys.electricity).

? A cell of e.m.f. 6.0 V and internal resistance 1.0 Ω is connected to a resistor of resistance 5.0 Ω. What is the p.d. across the resistor?
- 1.0 V
+ 5.0 V
- 6.0 V
> The current is \(I = E/(R + r) = 6.0/6.0 = 1.0\,\mathrm{A}\), so the p.d. across the resistor is \(IR = 5.0\,\mathrm{V}\). 6.0 V ignores the internal resistance. 1.0 V is the lost volts \(Ir\). Revise [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc).

? Which row describes what happens to the nucleon number \(A\) and the proton number \(Z\) of a nucleus that undergoes β⁻ decay?
- \(A\) decreases by 4, \(Z\) decreases by 2
- \(A\) unchanged, \(Z\) decreases by 1
+ \(A\) unchanged, \(Z\) increases by 1
- \(A\) unchanged, \(Z\) unchanged
> In β⁻ decay a neutron changes into a proton (a down quark into an up quark), emitting an electron and an antineutrino. The number of nucleons stays the same and the number of protons rises by one. The first row describes α decay, the second β⁺ decay and the last γ emission. Revise [Particle physics](#phys.particles).

:::recap
- Paper 1: 40 four-option questions, 40 marks, 1 hour 15 minutes, AS content; about 1.9 minutes each. Two passes: 1.5 minutes each first, 15 minutes at the end.
- Never leave a blank. Eliminate options with units, limiting cases and sense checks.
- Method: read with options covered, mark given and asked, predict, compare, eliminate, choose and move on.
- Gradient and area depend on the axes: velocity–time gradient is acceleration and area is displacement.
- Percentage uncertainties add for products and quotients; absolute uncertainties add for sums and differences.
- Wave graph against distance gives the wavelength, against time gives the period.
- Quark charges: up \(+\tfrac23\), down \(-\tfrac13\), antiquarks opposite.
- Keep an error log and redo your missed questions.
:::
