:::why
Structured papers are won by method as much as by knowledge. The same physics is worth full marks in one answer and one mark in another, depending on whether you showed the formula, kept the unit, gave a reason for each step and answered the command word that was set. This guide shows you what examiners are looking for in every kind of answer, with weak and full versions side by side, and gives you one place to learn every definition in the course.

**Before you start**, you should know the topic chapters your paper covers: AS chapters (to [Particle physics](#phys.particles)) for Paper 2, and every chapter for Paper 4. For multiple choice read [Paper 1 survival guide](#phys.exam.p1). Marks in this guide use the notation of the course: **C1** a correct formula or substitution (compensation mark, implied by a correct answer), **A1** the correct final answer with its unit, **B1** an independent mark for a statement, **M1** a method mark in "show that" questions and derivations.

| Data and formulae | page 2 | pages 2 and 3 |
| Minutes per mark | \(75/60 = 1.25\) | \(120/100 = 1.2\) |

Both papers allow a little over **one minute per mark**. A 4-mark part deserves about 5 minutes and a 1-mark part about a minute. Use the marks as a guide to how much to write: a 2-mark "state" needs two short points, and a 4-mark "explain" needs four linked statements. A one-line answer to a 4-mark question cannot score full marks, and a page of text for a 1-mark question wastes time you will need later.

:::tip Time per part
At 1.25 minutes per mark (Paper 2), a 1-mark part is about 1 minute, a 2-mark part 2.5 minutes, a 4-mark part 5 minutes and a 6-mark part 7.5 minutes. Paper 4 is 1.2 minutes per mark, so slightly less. If you have spent twice the time that a part allows, leave a gap, mark it, and move on: later parts often earn marks from your own working or from information given in the question.
:::

**What "AS knowledge required" means.** Paper 4 tests the A Level topics, but its questions assume that you can use everything from AS: kinematics, forces, energy, circuits and waves. Many questions are **synoptic**: they combine an A Level idea with AS physics, such as circular motion with energy, or an electric field with kinematics. The two demonstrations near the end of this guide are of that kind.

## Command words: what each one asks for

Every question starts with a **command word**. It tells you what kind of answer earns the marks. The syllabus uses 15. Learn the meaning of each, and what a full answer looks like.

- **Calculate.** *Work out from given facts, figures or information.* Show the formula or substitution (C1), then the answer with its unit and a sensible number of significant figures (A1). *Example:* "Calculate the kinetic energy of a 1200 kg car at 25 m s⁻¹." \(\tfrac12 \times 1200 \times 25^2 = 3.75 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}\), which is \(3.8 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}\) to 2 s.f. [C1] [A1]
- **Comment.** *Give an informed opinion.* A judgement supported by a number or a reason from the question. *Example:* "A student finds \(g = 9.4 \pm 0.5\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Comment on this result." The range 8.9 to 9.9 includes 9.81, so the result agrees with the accepted value within the uncertainty.
- **Compare.** *Identify or comment on similarities and/or differences.* Say both things, about the same property each time. *Example:* "Compare the penetration of α and β⁻ radiation." α is stopped by a sheet of paper; β⁻ passes through paper but is stopped by a few millimetres of aluminium.
- **Define.** *Give a precise meaning.* The exam wording, usually one mark per essential phrase, with any symbols explained. *Example:* "Define the volt." The potential difference between two points when one joule of energy is transferred per coulomb of charge.
- **Describe.** *State the points of a topic or give its characteristics and main features.* A set of facts or steps in order, with no need for reasons. *Example:* "Describe how to measure the diameter of a wire accurately." Use a micrometer; check for a zero error; measure at several places along the wire and in two perpendicular directions; take the mean.
- **Determine.** *Establish an answer using the information available.* Use the data, graph or values given, and show how. *Example:* "Determine the spring constant from the graph." Take the gradient of the force–extension graph using a large triangle, and give its unit.
- **Explain.** *Set out purposes or reasons, make the relationships between things evident, provide why and/or how and support with relevant evidence.* A chain of statements joined by "because", so that each link earns a mark. *Example:* "Explain why the car reaches a terminal velocity." See the demonstration below.
- **Give.** *Produce an answer from a given source or recall from memory.* One word, value or short phrase, with no working. *Example:* "Give the unit of the Young modulus." The pascal (or \(\mathrm{N\,m^{-2}}\)).
- **Identify.** *Name, select or recognise.* Name the item. *Example:* "Identify the particle emitted in β⁻ decay." An electron (an antineutrino is emitted too).
- **Justify.** *Support a case with evidence or argument.* Give the claim, then the evidence, usually a number. *Example:* "Justify the statement that the collision is inelastic." The kinetic energy before is 9.0 J and after is 6.0 J, so kinetic energy has been lost.
- **Predict.** *Suggest what may happen based on available information.* Give the outcome, with the reason from the data given. *Example:* "Predict the effect on the period of a mass on a spring when the mass is increased by a factor of four." \(T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}\), so \(T\) doubles.
- **Show (that).** *Provide structured evidence that leads to a given result.* Every step must appear, and the final value is given to more significant figures than the value in the question. The demonstration below shows the form.
- **Sketch.** *Make a simple freehand drawing showing the key features.* Labelled axes, the correct shape, the key points marked, no exact scale. See the section on graphs below.
- **State.** *Express in clear terms.* One short, accurate sentence, with no explanation unless asked. *Example:* "State Newton's first law." A body remains at rest or continues to move with constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant force.
- **Suggest.** *Apply knowledge and understanding to situations where there are a range of valid responses in order to make proposals.* A sensible idea that fits the context, with the physics behind it. *Example:* "Suggest why the efficiency of an electric motor is less than 100%." Energy is transferred as thermal energy in the resistance of the windings and by friction in the bearings, so not all the input energy is useful output.

:::warn Read the command word, not just the topic
"State Newton's first law", "Explain why the car reaches a terminal velocity" and "Show that the speed is about 3 m s⁻¹" can all be about the same car. The first wants one sentence. The second wants a chain of four linked points. The third wants a calculation that finishes on a number the examiner has already given you. Writing an explanation when the command word was *State* wastes time, and writing a statement when it was *Explain* loses marks.
:::

Learn them word for word, and write the symbol meanings if you give an equation. The tables follow the order of the course and link the chapter where each term is taught. For e.m.f., p.d. and internal resistance the wording is the one in [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc).

## "Show that" questions

A **"show that"** question gives you the answer and asks for the route. The examiner awards marks for the steps, so every step must appear. The common ways to lose marks are to omit the substitution, to work backwards from the given number, or to round the answer to the same figures as the value in the question, which hides whether you really calculated it.

:::eg Show that a ball dropped from 1.8 m hits the floor at about 6 m s⁻¹
A ball is dropped from rest from a height of 1.8 m. Air resistance is negligible. Show that its speed as it reaches the floor is about \(6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

The acceleration is constant, so use \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) with \(u = 0\). [M1]
\[v^2 = 0 + 2 \times 9.81 \times 1.8 = 35.3\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\] [M1]
\[v = 5.94\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\ \text{(about } 6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\text{)}\] [A1]
Notice what the answer does. It writes the equation, then the substitution with the data-sheet \(g\), then the result to **three** significant figures (5.94), which is more than the "about 6" in the question. Only then does it say that this is about 6. Starting with "\(6^2 = 36 = 2 \times 9.81 \times 1.8\)" would be working backwards from the answer, and would not earn the final mark.
:::

## Calculations: the C1 and A1 pattern

Almost every numerical answer is marked in two steps: **C1** for the correct formula, with the numbers substituted in, and **A1** for the correct answer with its unit. If your answer is wrong but your substitution is right and visible, you keep the C1. If you show nothing and the answer is wrong, you score nothing. Show the substitution, always.

:::steps
1. Write the formula you will use.
2. Convert every quantity into base SI units, **before** you substitute.
3. Substitute, keeping the unit with each value if you can.
4. Keep every digit your calculator holds until the end. Do not round a value that you will use again.
5. Round the final answer to the number of significant figures of the least precise data (at least 2), and write the unit.
:::

:::eg Calculate the average force on a tennis ball
A tennis ball of mass 55 g arrives at a racket at \(4.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and leaves in the opposite direction at \(28\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The contact lasts 6.0 ms. Calculate the average force on the ball.

Change of momentum, taking the leaving direction as positive and converting the mass to kilograms:
\[\Delta p = m(v - u) = 0.055 \times (28 - (-4.0)) = 1.76\,\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-1}}\] [C1]
Force is the rate of change of momentum:
\[F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{1.76}{6.0 \times 10^{-3}} = 293\,\mathrm{N}\] [C1]
To 2 significant figures, as the data are, the force is \(2.9 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1]

**Why not round in the middle?** If you round \(\Delta p\) to 1.8 and continue, \(F = 1.8/0.0060 = 300\,\mathrm{N}\), which is \(3.0 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{N}\): the last digit of the answer has changed. Keep 1.76 (or use the calculator's memory) and round only the final answer.
:::

:::warn Common unit slips
Convert first, then calculate. The slips that cost marks most often are in this table.

| Given | In SI | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| 55 g | 0.055 kg | divide grams by 1000 |
| 6.0 ms | \(6.0 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}\) | milli is \(10^{-3}\) |
| 4.5 cm² | \(4.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^2}\) | square the conversion: \((10^{-2})^2\) |
| 0.35 litre | \(3.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m^3}\) | 1 litre is \(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\) |
| 90 km h⁻¹ | \(25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) | divide by 3.6 |
| 1500 rev min⁻¹ | \(157\,\mathrm{rad\,s^{-1}}\) | multiply by \(2\pi/60\) |
| 20 °C | 293 K | add 273.15 (273 if the data are to the nearest degree) |
| 3.2 eV | \(5.1 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\) | multiply by \(1.60 \times 10^{-19}\) |
:::

**Significant figures.** Give the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data, and never fewer than 2. A "show that" gives one more than the value in the question. A calculation involving \(g = 9.81\) is not limited by \(g\) to 3 figures if another value has only 2. Never give a long calculator display such as 0.2879999: the examiner then wonders whether you understood the data.

## Explanations: one mark for each link in the chain

An **explain** question is marked by counting links. A good answer is a chain: cause, then effect, then consequence, each linked by "so", "because" or "therefore". An answer that states the result without the chain, or lists true facts in no order, scores far fewer marks. In both demonstrations below, the weak answer is true as far as it goes.

:::eg Explain why a skydiver reaches a terminal velocity
A skydiver jumps from a stationary balloon. Explain why she reaches a terminal velocity. [4 marks]

**A weak answer.** "She speeds up until the air resistance gets big enough, and then she goes at a steady speed." This scores **1 mark of 4**: it names the steady speed, but it does not say why the air resistance grows, it does not mention the resultant force, and it does not say why the acceleration stops.

**A full answer.**

At the start the only force is her weight, so the resultant force is her weight and she accelerates downwards at \(g\). [B1]

As her speed increases, the air resistance (drag) on her increases. [B1]

The resultant force (weight minus drag) therefore decreases, so her acceleration decreases. [B1]

When the drag equals her weight, the resultant force is zero, so (by Newton's first law) the acceleration is zero and she moves at constant velocity: the terminal velocity. [B1]
:::

:::eg Explain why the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons is independent of the intensity
Light of frequency above the threshold frequency falls on a metal surface. Explain why the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectrons does not depend on the intensity of the light. [3 marks]

**A weak answer.** "The energy depends on the frequency, not on the intensity." This scores **0 marks of 3**: it repeats the question's claim and gives no reason.

**A full answer.**

Each photon gives all of its energy \(hf\) to a single electron, and the electron needs at least the work function \(\Phi\) to escape, so the maximum kinetic energy is \(hf - \Phi\). [B1]

This depends only on the frequency of the light, because \(h\) and \(\Phi\) are constants. [B1]

Increasing the intensity increases the number of photons arriving per second, not the energy of each photon, so it changes the number of photoelectrons, not their maximum energy. [B1]
:::

:::tip Build the chain before you write
For any explain question, first write the two ends of the chain (the starting condition and the thing to be explained), then fill in the links between them. Count the marks: a 4-mark explanation needs about four links. If you have fewer than four, you have missed one. If you have many more, some are repetition.
:::

## Graphs and sketches

A **sketch** is a freehand graph that shows the key features. It does not need a scale, but it does need everything the examiner will look for. Always: label each axis with the quantity (and unit if you can), draw the correct overall shape, mark the intercepts and other key values the question gives, and do not draw curves where the physics says they cannot go (a decay does not cross the axis).

| Sketch | What must be shown |
|---|---|
| I–V of a diode | near-zero current for reverse bias, and for forward bias until about 0.6 V (silicon); then the current rises steeply; axes labelled I and V with the origin marked |
| Binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number | rises steeply for light nuclei, a peak near iron-56 (about 8.8 MeV per nucleon), then a gentle fall to about 7.6 MeV for uranium-235 |
| Lightly damped oscillation (displacement against time) | a sine-like curve whose amplitude decreases exponentially, with the period unchanged; with heavy damping there is no oscillation |
| Capacitor discharge (p.d., charge or current against time) | an exponential decay that starts at the initial value on the vertical axis and approaches zero without ever reaching it |

:::plot A sketch of the p.d. across a capacitor discharging through a resistor: it starts at its initial value V₀ and falls to 37% of that value after one time constant RC.
--f "6*exp(-x/2.2)+0*sqrt(11-x)" --x 0 14 --y 0 7.5 --xlabel "t / s" --ylabel "V / V" --pt "0,6,V₀@ne" --pt "2.2,2.207,t = RC: V = 0.37V₀@ne" --xstep 2
:::

The same fall by a fixed fraction happens in every equal interval: after each further \(RC\) the p.d. is multiplied by 0.37 again. This is why an exponential is never zero, and a good sketch shows the curve flattening towards the axis, not hitting it. For a **graph in an analysis question**, do more than a sketch: label the axes with the quantity and unit (for example "\(V\) / V"), use a sensible scale that fills at least half of the grid, plot the points, and draw a best-fit line with similar numbers of points above and below it.

| \(s = ut + \tfrac12 at^2\), \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) | for constant acceleration, \(a = (v - u)/t\) and \(s = \) average velocity \(\times\, t\); eliminate \(t\) for the second | [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics) |
| \(\Delta E_P = mg\Delta h\) | work done \(= Fs\) with \(F = mg\) to lift the mass a height \(\Delta h\) at constant speed | [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy) |
| \(E_K = \tfrac12 mv^2\) | work done by the resultant force \(F = ma\) over a distance \(s\), using \(v^2 = 2as\) from rest | [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy) |
| \(P = Fv\) | \(P = W/t = Fs/t = F(s/t) = Fv\) | [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy) |
| \(\Delta p = \rho g\Delta h\) | the weight of a column of fluid, \(\rho A\Delta h \times g\), divided by its area \(A\) | [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces) |
| \(v = f\lambda\) | the wave travels one wavelength in one period, so \(v = \lambda/T = f\lambda\) | [Waves](#phys.waves) |
| resistors in series and in parallel | series: the same current, p.d.s add; parallel: the same p.d., currents add (Kirchhoff's laws and \(V = IR\)) | [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc) |
| \(g = GM/r^2\) | Newton's law of gravitation for the force on a mass \(m\), then \(g = F/m\) | [Gravitational fields](#phys.gravity) |
| \(pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle c^2\rangle\) | the momentum change \(2mc_x\) of one molecule at a wall, the rate of collisions, the sum over \(N\) molecules and \(\langle c_x^2\rangle = \tfrac13\langle c^2\rangle\) | [Ideal gases](#phys.gases) |
| \(\tfrac12 m\langle c^2\rangle = \tfrac32 kT\) | compare \(pV = \tfrac13 Nm\langle c^2\rangle\) with \(pV = NkT\) | [Ideal gases](#phys.gases) |
| capacitors in series and in parallel | parallel: the same p.d., charges add; series: the same charge, p.d.s add (from \(C = Q/V\)) | [Capacitance](#phys.capacitance) |
| \(V_H = BI/(ntq)\) | magnetic force \(Bqv\) balanced by the Hall field force, with \(I = nAvq\); the width cancels | [Magnetic fields](#phys.magnetic) |

:::exam How derivations are marked
A derivation scores marks for the starting principle, for each algebra step that leads to the result, and for the final line. Write a reason beside each line, and finish on the result you were asked for, not on something close to it. Do not start from the result you are supposed to prove.
:::

## Paper 4 synoptic questions

Paper 4 questions often chain several topics. The method is the same: write the principle for each stage, and use the answer to one stage in the next. Common combinations are in this table.

| Link | Typical question |
|---|---|
| circular motion, gravitational fields and energy | an orbit, its speed, its period, and the energy needed to change it |
| electric field and kinematics | a charged particle crossing a uniform field, as a projectile crosses a gravitational field |
| magnetic field and circular motion | a charged particle in a magnetic field, \(r = mv/(Bq)\), its period |
| capacitor discharge and logarithms | the time constant from the gradient of a graph of \(\ln V\) against \(t\) |
| oscillations and energy | the speed at a given displacement, the exchange of kinetic and potential energy |
| gases, thermodynamics and kinetic theory | the first law applied to a cycle on a \(p\)–\(V\) diagram |

:::eg Paper 4 demonstration 1: a satellite (circular motion, gravitational fields and energy)
A satellite of mass 1200 kg is in a circular orbit of radius \(7.0 \times 10^{6}\,\mathrm{m}\) around the Earth. The mass of the Earth is \(5.97 \times 10^{24}\,\mathrm{kg}\) and \(G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\,\mathrm{N\,m^2\,kg^{-2}}\).

**(a)** Show that the orbital speed is about \(7.5 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [3 marks]

The gravitational force provides the centripetal force: \(\dfrac{GMm}{r^2} = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\). [B1]
\[v^2 = \frac{GM}{r} = \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times 5.97 \times 10^{24}}{7.0 \times 10^{6}} = 5.69 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m^2\,s^{-2}}\] [C1]
\[v = 7.54 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\ \text{(about } 7.5 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\text{)}\] [A1]

**(b)** Calculate the period of the orbit. [2 marks]

\[T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = \frac{2\pi \times 7.0 \times 10^{6}}{7.54 \times 10^{3}}\] [C1]
\[T = 5.8 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{s}\ \text{(97 minutes)}\] [A1]

**(c)** Calculate the total energy of the satellite in this orbit. [3 marks]

\[E_K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 1200 \times 5.69 \times 10^{7} = 3.41 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\] [C1]
\[E_P = -\frac{GMm}{r} = -6.83 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\] [C1]
\[E_K + E_P = -3.4 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\] [A1]

**(d)** The satellite is moved to a circular orbit of radius \(1.0 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m}\). Calculate the minimum energy that must be supplied. [2 marks]

The total energy in a circular orbit is \(-\dfrac{GMm}{2r}\), so
\[\Delta E = \frac{GMm}{2}\left(\frac{1}{7.0 \times 10^{6}} - \frac{1}{1.0 \times 10^{7}}\right)\] [C1]
\[\Delta E = 1.0 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\] [A1]

**(e)** Explain why the speed of the satellite is smaller in the higher orbit, even though its total energy has increased. [2 marks]

\(v^2 = GM/r\), so \(v\) decreases as \(r\) increases. [B1]

The potential energy increases by \(2.05 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\), which is twice as much as the kinetic energy decreases (\(1.02 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\)), so the total rises by \(1.02 \times 10^{10}\,\mathrm{J}\). [B1]
:::

:::eg Paper 4 demonstration 2: an electron in a uniform electric field (electric fields and kinematics)
An electron enters, at right angles to the field, the uniform electric field between two horizontal parallel plates that are 0.020 m apart, with a p.d. of 250 V across them. The electron enters with a horizontal speed of \(3.0 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) and the plates are 0.050 m long. Calculate the vertical deflection of the electron as it leaves the plates. The weight of the electron is negligible. [6 marks]

The field strength between the plates is
\[E = \frac{V}{d} = \frac{250}{0.020} = 1.25 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{V\,m^{-1}}\] [C1]
The force is \(F = eE\), so the acceleration (perpendicular to the plates) is
\[a = \frac{eE}{m} = \frac{1.60 \times 10^{-19} \times 1.25 \times 10^{4}}{9.11 \times 10^{-31}} = 2.195 \times 10^{15}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\] [C1] [A1]
There is no horizontal force, so the horizontal speed is constant and the time between the plates is \(t = L/v\): [B1]
\[t = \frac{0.050}{3.0 \times 10^{7}} = 1.67 \times 10^{-9}\,\mathrm{s}\]
Starting with no vertical velocity, the deflection is
\[y = \tfrac12 at^2 = \tfrac12 \times 2.195 \times 10^{15} \times (1.667 \times 10^{-9})^2 = 3.05 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}\] [C1] [A1]
This is 3.0 mm, less than half the plate separation (10 mm), so the electron does leave the plates. This is projectile motion again, with the electric force in place of the weight.
:::

## Check yourself: exam technique

:::quiz Which answer would score full marks?
? The question reads "State Newton's first law." Which answer would score the mark?
- An object keeps moving unless something stops it.
+ A body remains at rest or continues to move with constant velocity unless acted on by a resultant force.
- The force on a body equals its mass multiplied by its acceleration.
- An object at rest stays at rest.
> The first option is vague (what is "something"? what about constant velocity?). The third is the second law, and the last is half of the first law. A "state" answer must be exact and complete. Learn the wording in the definitions table, and revise [Dynamics](#phys.dynamics).

? A question says "A 0.45 kg ball moves at 12 m s⁻¹. Show that its kinetic energy is about 32 J." Which answer would score full marks?
- \(E_K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = 32\,\mathrm{J}\)
+ \(E_K = \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 0.45 \times 12^2 = 32.4\,\mathrm{J}\), which is about 32 J
- \(\tfrac12 \times 0.45 \times 12 = 2.7\,\mathrm{J}\), so about 32 J
- \(32 = \tfrac12 \times 0.45 \times v^2\), so \(v = 11.9\), which is about 12 m s⁻¹
> A "show that" needs the equation, the substitution and the result to more figures than the given value (32.4 J). The first option gives no working. The third forgets to square the speed and so does not give 32 J. The last works backwards from the answer, which proves nothing. Revise [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).

? "Explain why the current in a filament lamp is not proportional to the p.d. across it" (3 marks). Which answer would score full marks?
- Because a filament lamp does not obey Ohm's law.
+ As the p.d. increases the current increases and the filament gets hotter; the ions vibrate more, so there are more collisions with electrons and the resistance increases; so V/I rises and the current rises less than proportionally.
- Because the resistance changes.
- Because of the internal resistance of the cell.
> The first option restates the question and gives no reason. The third names the effect but gives no cause (the temperature) and no link to the current. The last is about the source, not the lamp. The correct answer is a chain of three linked points. Revise [Electricity](#phys.electricity).

? The data in a calculation are 2.4 m s⁻¹ and 0.12 kg, and the calculator gives the momentum as 0.288 kg m s⁻¹. Which is the best final answer?
- 0.3 kg m s⁻¹
+ 0.29 kg m s⁻¹
- 0.288 kg m s⁻¹
- 0.2880 kg m s⁻¹
> Both data are given to 2 significant figures, so the answer is given to 2 significant figures: 0.29. One figure (0.3) is fewer than 2, which is not allowed. Extra figures claim a precision that the data do not have. Revise [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units).

? "Sketch a graph of the p.d. across a capacitor against time as it discharges through a resistor." Which description would score full marks?
- A straight line sloping down from \(V_0\) to zero.
+ Axes labelled V and t; a curve starting at \(V_0\) on the V axis, falling with a decreasing gradient and approaching, but never reaching, zero.
- A curve that reaches the time axis at \(t = RC\).
- A curve that starts at the origin and rises to \(V_0\).
> The discharge is exponential. A straight line has a constant gradient, which is the wrong physics. At \(t = RC\) the p.d. has fallen to only 37% of \(V_0\), not to zero. The last option describes charging. Revise [Capacitance](#phys.capacitance).

? Which statement is a correct definition of Hooke's law?
- The force is proportional to the extension.
+ The extension of an object is proportional to the load applied, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded.
- Stress is proportional to strain for all loads.
- The spring returns to its original length when the load is removed.
> The first option misses the limit of proportionality, which is the mark that separates a full answer from a part answer. The third has "for all loads", which is false beyond the limit. The last describes elastic behaviour, which is a different idea. Revise [Deformation of solids](#phys.solids).

? "Suggest why the efficiency of an electric motor that lifts a load is less than 100%" (2 marks). Which answer would score full marks?
- Because the motor is not perfect.
+ Some of the input energy is transferred as thermal energy in the windings and by friction in the bearings, so the useful output energy is smaller than the input energy.
- Because energy is lost.
- Because energy is destroyed as heat.
> A "suggest" answer needs a specific, physical idea that fits the context. The first and third are too vague to earn a mark. The last is wrong: energy is never destroyed, it is transferred to the surroundings. Revise [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).

? A 4-mark question begins with "Explain". About how many separate, creditable points do you need to score full marks?
- 1
- 2
+ 4
- 8
> Marks are awarded for separate points, usually one for each link in the chain. A 4-mark question needs about four. One or two points cannot score 4, and eight points do not score more than 4: spend the time you would use on extra points on the next question.
:::

:::recap
- Paper 2: 60 marks, 75 minutes (1.25 minutes per mark). Paper 4: 100 marks, 120 minutes (1.2 minutes per mark), A Level content with AS knowledge required.
- The 15 command words: calculate, comment, compare, define, describe, determine, explain, give, identify, justify, predict, show (that), sketch, state, suggest. The command word decides the form of the answer.
- Definitions have exact wordings, one mark per essential phrase. Learn them from the master table.
- "Show that": equation, substitution, result to more figures than the given value. Never work backwards.
- Calculations: formula, SI conversions, substitution (C1), answer with unit and sensible significant figures (A1); do not round in the middle.
- Explanations: a chain of linked points, about one mark per link.
- Sketches: labelled axes, correct shape, key values; an exponential decay approaches zero but never reaches it.
- Derivations: principle, steps with reasons, the result asked for.
- Synoptic questions: apply the principle for each stage and carry the unrounded result forward.
:::
