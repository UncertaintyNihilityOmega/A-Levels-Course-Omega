@summary Fifteen multiple-choice questions and eighteen structured questions that mix every AS topic, from units to particle physics, each with a full mark scheme and a link back to the chapter to revise.

In an exam the topics do not come in chapter order. One question on a car braking needs kinematics, energy and forces together, and one on a wire needs materials and waves. This page gives you that mix: first fifteen Paper 1-style multiple-choice questions, then four sets of structured questions in the style of Paper 2. They are practice, not a mock paper: there is no fixed total and no clock until you choose to use one.

:::tip How to use this page
Do each set in one sitting, without notes, at about 1.25 minutes per mark (a 7-mark question gets about 9 minutes). Then mark yourself honestly against the scheme: a mark needs the idea or the working shown, not just the right final number. For every mark you lose, open the chapter named under the answer and read that section again. Questions that cross two or more chapters end their mark scheme with an "Also revise" line. Use the data sheet values in the course: \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), \(e = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\), and give answers to the number of significant figures of the data.
:::

## Paper 1-style multiple choice

:::quiz Paper 1-style multiple choice
? Pressure is force per unit area. Which expression gives the unit of pressure in SI base units?
+ \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\)
- \(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\)
- \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{2}\,s^{-2}}\)
- \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-2}\,s^{-2}}\)
> The newton is \(\mathrm{kg\,m\,s^{-2}}\), and dividing by an area in \(\mathrm{m^{2}}\) gives \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-1}\,s^{-2}}\). The second option is the unit of force and the third is the unit of energy (the joule). Revise [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units).
? A quantity is calculated from \(Q = x^2 y\). The percentage uncertainty in \(x\) is 2.0% and in \(y\) is 3.0%. What is the percentage uncertainty in \(Q\)?
- 5.0%
- 6.0%
+ 7.0%
- 10%
> A power multiplies the percentage uncertainty by the power, so \(x^2\) contributes \(2 \times 2.0 = 4.0\%\). Percentage uncertainties then add: \(4.0 + 3.0 = 7.0\%\). Forgetting the power gives 5.0%, multiplying the percentages gives 6.0%, and doubling both gives 10%. Revise [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units).
? A ball is thrown vertically upwards at \(14\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Air resistance is negligible. How long does it take to return to the thrower's hand?
- 1.4 s
+ 2.9 s
- 4.3 s
- 5.7 s
> The time to reach the top is \(v/g = 14/9.81 = 1.43\,\mathrm{s}\), and the return takes the same time, so the total is \(2v/g = 2.9\,\mathrm{s}\). 1.4 s is only the upward journey, and 5.7 s doubles the answer again. Revise [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics).
? A car slows down uniformly from \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) to rest in 5.0 s. What distance does it travel while slowing down?
- 4.0 m
- 25 m
+ 50 m
- 100 m
> The distance is the area under the \(v\)–\(t\) graph, a triangle: \(\tfrac12 \times 20 \times 5.0 = 50\,\mathrm{m}\). 100 m is \(vt\), which assumes the speed stays at \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\), and 4.0 is the size of the deceleration in \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\), the gradient. Revise [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics).
? A book rests on a table. The weight of the book is the gravitational pull of the Earth on the book. Which force forms the Newton's third law pair with this weight?
- The upward contact force of the table on the book.
- The downward contact force of the book on the table.
+ The gravitational pull of the book on the Earth.
- The weight of the table.
> A third-law pair acts on two different bodies and is the same type of force. The pull of the Earth on the book pairs with the pull of the book on the Earth. The contact force of the table balances the weight, but that is a coincidence of equilibrium, not a third-law pair. Revise [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics).
? A trolley of mass 2.0 kg moving at \(3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) hits a stationary trolley of mass 1.0 kg and the two stick together. How much kinetic energy is lost?
- 0
+ 3.0 J
- 6.0 J
- 9.0 J
> Momentum: \(2.0 \times 3.0 = 3.0v\), so \(v = 2.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The kinetic energy falls from \(\tfrac12 \times 2.0 \times 3.0^2 = 9.0\,\mathrm{J}\) to \(\tfrac12 \times 3.0 \times 2.0^2 = 6.0\,\mathrm{J}\), a loss of 3.0 J. Zero would be an elastic collision, and 6.0 J is the energy that is left. Revise [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics).
? A block of density \(600\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) floats in water of density \(1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). What fraction of its volume is below the water surface?
- 0.40
+ 0.60
- 0.50
- 1.0
> Floating means upthrust equals weight: \(\rho_\text{water} g V_\text{below} = \rho_\text{block} g V\), so \(V_\text{below}/V = 600/1000 = 0.60\). 0.40 is the fraction above the surface. Revise [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces).
? A car travels at a constant speed of \(25\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) when the driving force is 1.2 kN. What is the useful power delivered to the road?
- 48 W
- 1.2 kW
- 15 kW
+ 30 kW
> At constant speed \(P = Fv = 1200 \times 25 = 30\,000\,\mathrm{W} = 30\,\mathrm{kW}\). Dividing force by speed gives 48 (and the wrong unit), 1.2 kW forgets the speed, and 15 kW wrongly halves it as in kinetic energy. Revise [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
? Wire Y is made of the same material as wire X, is twice as long and has twice the diameter. The same load hangs from each. The extension of X is \(x\). What is the extension of Y?
- \(x/4\)
+ \(x/2\)
- \(x\)
- \(2x\)
> The extension is \(FL/(AE)\). Doubling the length doubles it, and doubling the diameter makes the area four times larger, so the extension is divided by four. The net effect is \(2/4 = \tfrac12\). Answering \(2x\) ignores the diameter, and \(x\) treats the area as only doubling. Revise [Deformation of solids](#phys.solids).
? The amplitude of a progressive wave is tripled. By what factor does its intensity change?
- 3
- 6
+ 9
- 27
> Intensity is proportional to the square of the amplitude, so tripling the amplitude multiplies the intensity by \(3^2 = 9\). Revise [Waves](#phys.waves).
? A stretched string is fixed at both ends and vibrates in a stationary wave with three loops. The string is 1.2 m long. What is the wavelength?
- 0.40 m
+ 0.80 m
- 1.2 m
- 3.6 m
> Each loop is half a wavelength, so \(3 \times \tfrac{\lambda}{2} = 1.2\), giving \(\lambda = 0.80\,\mathrm{m}\). Dividing the length by three gives 0.40 m, which is a loop, not a wavelength. Revise [Superposition](#phys.superposition).
? In a double-slit experiment the slit separation is halved and the distance from the slits to the screen is doubled. The wavelength is unchanged. How does the fringe separation change?
- It is divided by 4.
- It stays the same.
- It is doubled.
+ It is multiplied by 4.
> The fringe separation is \(x = \lambda D/a\). Halving \(a\) doubles \(x\) and doubling \(D\) doubles it again, so the net factor is 4. Revise [Superposition](#phys.superposition).
? A wire of resistance \(R\) is stretched uniformly to twice its original length. Its volume and resistivity do not change. What is its new resistance?
- \(R\)
- \(2R\)
+ \(4R\)
- \(8R\)
> The length doubles and, with the volume fixed, the area halves, so \(R = \rho L/A\) increases by \(2 \times 2 = 4\). \(2R\) forgets that the wire gets thinner. Revise [Electricity](#phys.electricity).
? A \(6.0\,\mathrm{V}\) battery of negligible internal resistance is connected across a \(4.0\,\Omega\) resistor and a \(12\,\Omega\) resistor in parallel. What is the total power supplied by the battery?
- 2.3 W
- 3.0 W
- 9.0 W
+ 12 W
> Each resistor has the full 6.0 V across it: \(6.0^2/4.0 = 9.0\,\mathrm{W}\) and \(6.0^2/12 = 3.0\,\mathrm{W}\), which add to 12 W. 2.3 W is what you get with the resistors in series (\(6.0^2/16\)). Revise [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc).
? Which equation correctly describes the β⁻ decay of a down quark inside a neutron?
- \(\mathrm{u} \rightarrow \mathrm{d} + \mathrm{e}^{-} + \bar{\nu}_\mathrm{e}\)
- \(\mathrm{d} \rightarrow \mathrm{u} + \mathrm{e}^{-} + \nu_\mathrm{e}\)
+ \(\mathrm{d} \rightarrow \mathrm{u} + \mathrm{e}^{-} + \bar{\nu}_\mathrm{e}\)
- \(\mathrm{d} \rightarrow \mathrm{u} + \mathrm{e}^{+} + \nu_\mathrm{e}\)
> A down quark (charge \(-\tfrac13 e\)) becomes an up quark (\(+\tfrac23 e\)), so the charge must be carried away by an electron: \(-\tfrac13 = +\tfrac23 - 1\). Lepton number must also be conserved: the electron (+1) needs an antineutrino (−1). The first option does not conserve charge, the second does not conserve lepton number, and the fourth has the wrong charge. Revise [Particle physics](#phys.particles).
:::

## Structured questions

Four sets follow. Set A mixes mechanics and materials, set B waves, set C electricity and particles, and set D is fully synoptic. Marks are in square brackets, and a ★ rating shows the difficulty. The hints open one at a time on the hardest questions.

:::try Set A: mechanics and materials
Q: [8 marks] (#phys.kinematics) ★★ A car of mass 1200 kg is travelling at \(24\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) when the driver brakes. The car decelerates uniformly and stops after 62 m. (a) Calculate the deceleration. (b) Calculate the time taken to stop. (c) Calculate the energy dissipated in the brakes and the average braking force. (d) The car now travels at twice the speed and the braking force is the same. State the new stopping distance and explain your answer.
A: (a) \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\) gives \(a = \dfrac{24^2}{2 \times 62}\) [C1] \(= 4.6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). [A1]
(b) \(t = \dfrac{2s}{u} = \dfrac{2 \times 62}{24} = 5.2\,\mathrm{s}\). [A1]
(c) Energy dissipated \(= \tfrac12 mv^2 = \tfrac12 \times 1200 \times 24^2\) [C1] \(= 3.5 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{J}\). [A1] Average force \(F = E/s = 3.456 \times 10^{5}/62 = 5.6 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1]
(d) The stopping distance becomes four times as long: 250 m. [B1] The kinetic energy is proportional to \(v^2\), and the work done by a constant force is \(Fs\), so \(s \propto v^2\). [B1]
Also revise: [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy) and [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics).
Q: [8 marks] (#phys.solids) ★★ A spring of spring constant \(450\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\) is compressed by 6.0 cm on a smooth horizontal table. It then pushes a ball of mass 25 g, which leaves the spring and slides off the edge of the table. The table top is 1.2 m above the floor. Assume that all the energy stored in the spring becomes kinetic energy of the ball and that air resistance is negligible. (a) Calculate the energy stored in the spring. (b) Calculate the speed of the ball as it leaves the table. (c) Calculate the time the ball takes to fall to the floor. (d) Calculate the horizontal distance from the edge of the table to the point where the ball lands.
A: (a) \(E = \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 \times 450 \times 0.060^2\) [C1] \(= 0.81\,\mathrm{J}\). [A1]
(b) \(\tfrac12 mv^2 = 0.81\) so \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 0.81}{0.025}}\) [C1] \(= 8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(c) The vertical motion starts from zero vertical velocity, so \(h = \tfrac12 gt^2\) and \(t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 1.2}{9.81}}\) [C1] \(= 0.49\,\mathrm{s}\). [A1]
(d) The horizontal velocity stays constant, so \(x = vt = 8.05 \times 0.495\) [C1] \(= 4.0\,\mathrm{m}\). [A1]
Also revise: [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics) and [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
Q: [8 marks] (#phys.dynamics) ★★ A trolley A of mass 0.40 kg moves at \(3.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) along a straight track and collides head-on with a stationary trolley B of mass 0.60 kg. After the collision A moves backwards at \(0.60\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). (a) Calculate the velocity of B after the collision. (b) Show that the collision is elastic. (c) The collision lasts 0.050 s. Calculate the average force on B. (d) State the average force on A during the collision.
A: (a) Taking the initial direction of A as positive, momentum before = momentum after: \(0.40 \times 3.0 = 0.40 \times (-0.60) + 0.60 v\) [C1] so \(v = 2.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) in the original direction of A. [A1]
(b) Kinetic energy before \(= \tfrac12 \times 0.40 \times 3.0^2 = 1.8\,\mathrm{J}\). [C1] Kinetic energy after \(= \tfrac12 \times 0.40 \times 0.60^2 + \tfrac12 \times 0.60 \times 2.4^2 = 0.072 + 1.728 = 1.8\,\mathrm{J}\). [A1] The kinetic energy is unchanged, so the collision is elastic. [B1]
(c) \(F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \dfrac{0.60 \times 2.4}{0.050}\) [C1] \(= 29\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1]
(d) The force on A is also 29 N, in the opposite direction (Newton's third law). [B1]
Also revise: [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
Q: [9 marks] (#phys.forces) ★★ A uniform block of wood measures 0.20 m by 0.20 m by 0.10 m and has mass 3.2 kg. It floats in water of density \(1000\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) with a \(0.20\,\mathrm{m} \times 0.20\,\mathrm{m}\) face horizontal. (a) Explain why the upthrust on the block is equal to its weight, and calculate it. (b) Calculate the depth of the block below the water surface. (c) Calculate the pressure due to the water on the lower face of the block. (d) Use your answers to explain how the pressure of the water produces the upthrust.
A: (a) The block is in equilibrium, so there is no resultant force: the upthrust balances the weight. [B1] \(W = mg = 3.2 \times 9.81\) [C1] \(= 31\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1]
(b) Upthrust \(= \rho g V_\text{below}\), so \(V_\text{below} = \dfrac{3.2}{1000} = 3.2 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^3}\). [C1] The depth is \(\dfrac{3.2 \times 10^{-3}}{0.20 \times 0.20} = 0.080\,\mathrm{m}\). [A1]
(c) \(p = \rho g h = 1000 \times 9.81 \times 0.080\) [C1] \(= 7.8 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). [A1]
(d) The lower face is deeper than the upper face (which is above the water), so the pressure on it is larger. [B1] The resultant upward force is \(pA = 785 \times 0.040 = 31\,\mathrm{N}\), the same as the weight, which is the upthrust. [B1]
Also revise: [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics).
Q: [9 marks] (#phys.superposition) ★★★ A steel wire of length 1.200 m and cross-sectional area \(0.30\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) is held between two fixed points by a tension of 90 N. The Young modulus of steel is \(2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). (a) Calculate the stress in the wire and its extension. (b) A vibrator drives the wire at 160 Hz and the wire forms a stationary wave with four loops between the fixed ends. Take the length of the wire as 1.200 m. Calculate the wavelength and the speed of the waves on the wire. (c) Explain how the stationary wave is formed.
H: Stress is force divided by area, and the area must be in \(\mathrm{m^2}\): \(0.30\,\mathrm{mm^2} = 0.30 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m^2}\).
H: Strain is extension divided by original length, and the Young modulus is stress divided by strain.
H: Each loop of a stationary wave is half a wavelength.
A: (a) Stress \(= F/A = \dfrac{90}{0.30 \times 10^{-6}}\) [C1] \(= 3.0 \times 10^{8}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). [A1] Extension \(x = \dfrac{\sigma L}{E} = \dfrac{3.0 \times 10^{8} \times 1.200}{2.0 \times 10^{11}}\) [C1] \(= 1.8 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m}\) (1.8 mm). [A1]
(b) Four loops fill the wire, so \(4 \times \tfrac{\lambda}{2} = 1.200\) [C1] and \(\lambda = 0.60\,\mathrm{m}\). [A1] \(v = f\lambda = 160 \times 0.60 = 96\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(c) The wave from the vibrator travels along the wire and reflects at the fixed end. The incident and reflected waves, with the same frequency and speed but travelling in opposite directions, superpose. [B1] The ends cannot move, so there is a node at each end, and nodes and antinodes form at fixed positions along the wire. [B1]
Also revise: [Deformation of solids](#phys.solids).

Q: [7 marks] (#phys.waves) ★★ A microphone is connected to an oscilloscope (c.r.o.) with the time-base set to 0.50 ms per division and the y-gain set to 0.20 V per division. The trace of a pure note shows adjacent peaks 3.2 divisions apart, and a peak-to-peak height of 4.0 divisions. (a) Calculate the period of the sound. (b) Calculate its frequency. (c) Calculate the amplitude of the signal in volts. (d) The source of the note now moves directly towards a stationary listener at \(20\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). The speed of sound is \(340\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). Calculate the frequency the listener hears.
A: (a) \(T = 3.2 \times 0.50 = 1.6\,\mathrm{ms}\). [C1] \(= 1.6 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{s}\). [A1]
(b) \(f = 1/T = 1/(1.6 \times 10^{-3}) = 6.3 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{Hz}\). [A1]
(c) The peak-to-peak voltage is \(4.0 \times 0.20 = 0.80\,\mathrm{V}\). [C1] The amplitude is half of this: \(0.40\,\mathrm{V}\). [A1]
(d) \(f_o = \dfrac{f_s v}{v - v_s} = \dfrac{625 \times 340}{340 - 20}\) [C1] \(= 6.6 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{Hz}\). [A1] The frequency is higher because the source approaches, so the wavefronts reach the listener closer together.
Q: [6 marks] (#phys.waves) ★★ A small loudspeaker emits sound of total power 4.0 W equally in all directions. Assume that no energy is absorbed by the air. (a) Calculate the intensity of the sound at a distance of 5.0 m. (b) Calculate the intensity at 20 m. (c) Calculate the ratio of the amplitude of the sound at 20 m to its amplitude at 5.0 m.
A: (a) The energy spreads over a sphere, so \(I = \dfrac{P}{4\pi r^2} = \dfrac{4.0}{4\pi \times 5.0^2}\) [C1] \(= 1.3 \times 10^{-2}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). [A1]
(b) \(I = \dfrac{4.0}{4\pi \times 20^2}\) [C1] \(= 8.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{W\,m^{-2}}\). [A1]
(c) The intensity is proportional to the amplitude squared, so the amplitude ratio is the square root of the intensity ratio: \(\sqrt{(8.0 \times 10^{-4})/(1.3 \times 10^{-2})}\) [C1] \(= 0.25\). [A1]
Q: [8 marks] (#phys.superposition) ★★ Light of wavelength 589 nm passes at normal incidence through a diffraction grating with 300 lines per millimetre. (a) Calculate the grating spacing \(d\). (b) Calculate the angle of the first-order maximum to the straight-through direction. (c) Calculate the highest order of maximum that can be seen, and the total number of maxima. (d) The wavelength is increased. State what happens to the angle of the first-order maximum.
A: (a) \(d = \dfrac{1}{300 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{m^{-1}}}\) [C1] \(= 3.33 \times 10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}\). [A1]
(b) \(d\sin\theta = n\lambda\) with \(n = 1\): \(\sin\theta = \dfrac{589 \times 10^{-9}}{3.33 \times 10^{-6}}\) [C1] so \(\theta = 10.2^\circ\). [A1]
(c) \(\sin\theta\) cannot exceed 1, so \(n \le d/\lambda = 5.66\) [C1] and the highest order is 5. [A1] There are five maxima each side of the central maximum, so 11 in total. [A1]
(d) The angle increases, because \(\sin\theta = \lambda/d\) rises with \(\lambda\). [B1]
Q: [8 marks] (#phys.superposition) ★★★ A tuning fork of frequency 320 Hz is held over the top of a tall tube. The tube is open at the top and closed at the bottom by water, and the water level is lowered. The air column resonates strongly when its length is 0.240 m and again when it is 0.770 m. (a) Explain how the resonance occurs and state where there is a node and where there is an antinode in the air column. (b) Calculate the wavelength and the speed of sound in air. (c) Predict the length of the next air column that resonates. (d) Explain why the wavelength is found from the difference between the two lengths and not from the first length alone.
H: Between one resonance and the next, the air column becomes longer by half a wavelength.
H: For (d), the antinode at the open end is not exactly at the rim of the tube.
A: (a) A sound wave travels down the tube and reflects at the water surface. The incident and reflected waves superpose to form a stationary wave. [B1] There is a node at the closed end (the water surface) and an antinode at (or just above) the open end. [B1]
(b) Successive resonances differ in length by \(\lambda/2\): \(\lambda = 2(0.770 - 0.240)\) [C1] \(= 1.06\,\mathrm{m}\). [A1] \(v = f\lambda = 320 \times 1.06 = 339\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(c) The next resonance is half a wavelength further: \(0.770 + \tfrac12 \times 1.06\) [C1] \(= 1.30\,\mathrm{m}\). [A1]
(d) The antinode forms slightly above the open end, so the first length is not exactly \(\lambda/4\). [B1] The difference between two resonances is exactly \(\lambda/2\), whatever the position of the antinode, so this gives the correct wavelength.
Also revise: [Waves](#phys.waves).

Q: [8 marks] (#phys.dc) ★★ A heater element is made from nichrome wire of diameter 0.40 mm and resistivity \(1.1 \times 10^{-6}\,\Omega\,\mathrm{m}\). The element must have a resistance of \(4.0\,\Omega\). It is connected to a 12 V battery of internal resistance \(0.50\,\Omega\). (a) Calculate the cross-sectional area of the wire. (b) Calculate the length of wire needed. (c) Calculate the current in the heater. (d) Calculate the power dissipated in the heater.
A: (a) \(A = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4} = \dfrac{\pi \times (0.40 \times 10^{-3})^2}{4}\) [C1] \(= 1.3 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2}\). [A1]
(b) \(R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A}\) so \(L = \dfrac{RA}{\rho} = \dfrac{4.0 \times 1.257 \times 10^{-7}}{1.1 \times 10^{-6}}\) [C1] \(= 0.46\,\mathrm{m}\). [A1]
(c) \(I = \dfrac{E}{R + r} = \dfrac{12}{4.0 + 0.50}\) [C1] \(= 2.7\,\mathrm{A}\). [A1]
(d) \(P = I^2 R = 2.667^2 \times 4.0\) [C1] \(= 28\,\mathrm{W}\). [A1] The battery itself dissipates \(I^2 r = 3.6\,\mathrm{W}\), so some of the energy is wasted inside it.
Also revise: [Electricity](#phys.electricity).
Q: [5 marks] (#phys.electricity) ★★ A copper wire of cross-sectional area \(2.5\,\mathrm{mm^2}\) carries a current of 5.0 A. The number density of free electrons in copper is \(8.5 \times 10^{28}\,\mathrm{m^{-3}}\). (a) Calculate the mean drift velocity of the electrons. (b) The wire is joined to a length of the same copper with half the cross-sectional area. State the drift velocity in the thinner wire. (c) A lamp at the far end of the circuit lights almost as soon as the switch is closed, although the drift velocity is very small. Explain why.
A: (a) \(I = Anvq\) so \(v = \dfrac{I}{Anq} = \dfrac{5.0}{2.5 \times 10^{-6} \times 8.5 \times 10^{28} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19}}\) [C1] \(= 1.5 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(b) The current is the same, and \(v \propto 1/A\), so the drift velocity doubles to \(2.9 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(c) Free electrons are already present throughout the whole circuit, including the lamp filament. [B1] The electric field is set up along the wire at close to the speed of light, so all the electrons start to drift almost together. [B1]
Q: [7 marks] (#phys.dc) ★★ A fan is switched on by a circuit in which a thermistor is in series with a \(2.2\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) fixed resistor across a 5.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance. The fan switches on when the p.d. across the fixed resistor reaches 2.0 V. (a) Calculate the resistance of the thermistor when the fan switches on. (b) State and explain how the p.d. across the fixed resistor changes as the temperature rises. (c) A voltmeter of resistance \(10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\) is connected across the fixed resistor while the thermistor has the resistance found in (a). Calculate the reading on the voltmeter and explain why it is not 2.0 V.
A: (a) The p.d. across the thermistor is \(5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0\,\mathrm{V}\). The p.d.s are in the ratio of the resistances: \(\dfrac{R_\text{th}}{2.2} = \dfrac{3.0}{2.0}\) [C1] so \(R_\text{th} = 3.3\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). [A1]
(b) The resistance of the thermistor decreases as the temperature rises, so it takes a smaller share of the 5.0 V. [B1] The p.d. across the fixed resistor therefore increases. [B1]
(c) The voltmeter is in parallel with the fixed resistor: \(\dfrac{2.2 \times 10}{2.2 + 10} = 1.8\,\mathrm{k\Omega}\). [C1] The reading is \(\dfrac{1.8}{1.8 + 3.3} \times 5.0 = 1.8\,\mathrm{V}\). [A1] The voltmeter draws some current, so the resistance of the lower part of the divider is smaller and takes a smaller share of the supply p.d. [B1]
Q: [7 marks] (#phys.particles) ★★★ A nucleus of americium-241 (proton number 95) decays by emitting an α-particle. The α-particle has kinetic energy 5.5 MeV. The nucleus was at rest before the decay. Take the mass of the α-particle as 4.00 u and the mass of the daughter nucleus, neptunium, as 237 u. (a) Write down the nuclear equation for the decay. (b) Calculate the speed of the α-particle. (c) Calculate the speed at which the neptunium nucleus recoils. (d) Explain why the neptunium nucleus has far less kinetic energy than the α-particle.
H: Convert the energy to joules using \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\).
H: The momentum before the decay is zero, so the two momenta are equal in size and opposite in direction.
A: (a) \(^{241}_{\;95}\mathrm{Am} \rightarrow {}^{237}_{\;93}\mathrm{Np} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\). Nucleon number \(241 = 237 + 4\) [B1] and proton number \(95 = 93 + 2\). [B1]
(b) \(E = 5.5 \times 10^{6} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-19} = 8.8 \times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}\) and \(E = \tfrac12 mv^2\), so \(v = \sqrt{\dfrac{2 \times 8.8 \times 10^{-13}}{4.00 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27}}}\) [C1] \(= 1.6 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(c) Momentum is conserved, so \(m_\alpha v_\alpha = M v_\mathrm{Np}\): \(v_\mathrm{Np} = \dfrac{4.00 \times 1.628 \times 10^{7}}{237}\) [C1] \(= 2.7 \times 10^{5}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(d) The two particles have the same size of momentum \(p\), and \(E_K = p^2/2m\), so the much heavier nucleus has much less kinetic energy. [B1]
Also revise: [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics) and [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).

Q: [7 marks] (#phys.units) ★★★ A student measures a metal cylinder. Mass: \(85.2 \pm 0.1\,\mathrm{g}\). Diameter: \(12.00 \pm 0.02\,\mathrm{mm}\). Length: \(95.0 \pm 0.5\,\mathrm{mm}\). (a) Calculate the density of the metal in \(\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). (b) Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the density. (c) State the density with its absolute uncertainty. Identify the measurement that contributes most to the uncertainty and suggest an improvement.
H: Density is mass divided by volume, and the volume of a cylinder is \(\tfrac14 \pi d^2 L\). Change every quantity to SI units first.
H: A quantity that is squared contributes twice its percentage uncertainty.
A: (a) \(\rho = \dfrac{4m}{\pi d^2 L} = \dfrac{4 \times 85.2 \times 10^{-3}}{\pi \times (12.00 \times 10^{-3})^2 \times 95.0 \times 10^{-3}}\) [C1] \(= 7.93 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). [A1]
(b) Percentage uncertainties: mass \(\dfrac{0.1}{85.2} = 0.12\%\), diameter \(2 \times \dfrac{0.02}{12.00} = 0.33\%\) (the diameter is squared), length \(\dfrac{0.5}{95.0} = 0.53\%\). [C1] The total is \(0.98\%\). [A1]
(c) The absolute uncertainty is \(0.98\% \times 7930 = 77\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\), so \(\rho = (7.93 \pm 0.08) \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). [A1] The length contributes most (0.53%). [B1] Measure it with vernier calipers instead of a rule, which reduces the absolute uncertainty. [B1]
Q: [8 marks] (#phys.forces) ★★★ A steel ball bearing of radius 2.0 mm is released at the surface of a deep tank of oil. The density of steel is \(7800\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\) and the density of the oil is \(900\,\mathrm{kg\,m^{-3}}\). (a) Calculate the weight of the ball and the upthrust on it. (b) After a time the ball falls at a constant (terminal) velocity. Calculate the drag force on the ball at this velocity. (c) Calculate the acceleration of the ball at the moment it is released.
H: The volume of a sphere is \(\tfrac43 \pi r^3\). Use it for both the mass of the ball and the volume of oil displaced.
H: At the moment of release the ball has no speed, so the drag is zero.
A: (a) \(V = \tfrac43 \pi (2.0 \times 10^{-3})^3 = 3.35 \times 10^{-8}\,\mathrm{m^3}\). Weight \(= \rho_\text{steel} V g = 7800 \times 3.35 \times 10^{-8} \times 9.81\) [C1] \(= 2.6 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1] Upthrust \(= \rho_\text{oil} V g = 900 \times 3.35 \times 10^{-8} \times 9.81\) [C1] \(= 3.0 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1]
(b) At terminal velocity the forces are balanced, so the drag equals the weight minus the upthrust. [B1] Drag \(= 2.56 \times 10^{-3} - 2.96 \times 10^{-4} = 2.3 \times 10^{-3}\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1]
(c) At release the drag is zero, so \(a = \dfrac{W - U}{m} = \dfrac{2.268 \times 10^{-3}}{2.614 \times 10^{-4}}\) [C1] \(= 8.7\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). [A1] This is the initial gradient of the \(v\)–\(t\) graph: the gradient then falls to zero as the ball reaches terminal velocity.
Also revise: [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics) and [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics).
Q: [7 marks] (#phys.energy) ★★ A cyclist and her bicycle have a combined mass of 85 kg. She rides at a constant speed of \(8.0\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) up a slope that is inclined at \(3.0^\circ\) to the horizontal. The total resistive force from the air and the bearings is 20 N. (a) Calculate the component of the weight along the slope. (b) Calculate the driving force and the power she must deliver to the road. (c) Her muscles convert chemical energy into useful power with an efficiency of 25%. Calculate the rate at which chemical energy is used.
A: (a) \(mg\sin\theta = 85 \times 9.81 \times \sin 3.0^\circ\) [C1] \(= 44\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1]
(b) At constant speed the driving force balances the weight component and the resistive force: \(F = 43.6 + 20 = 64\,\mathrm{N}\). [A1] \(P = Fv = 63.6 \times 8.0\) [C1] \(= 5.1 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{W}\). [A1]
(c) Efficiency \(= \dfrac{\text{useful power}}{\text{total power input}}\), so the input is \(\dfrac{509}{0.25}\) [C1] \(= 2.0 \times 10^{3}\,\mathrm{W}\). [A1]
Also revise: [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces).
Q: [7 marks] (#phys.solids) ★★ A student stretches a steel wire of length 2.000 m and diameter 0.40 mm. The graph of force \(F\) against extension \(x\) is a straight line through the origin. It passes through the point \(F = 32\,\mathrm{N}\), \(x = 2.5\,\mathrm{mm}\). (a) Calculate the gradient of the graph. (b) Show that the Young modulus is \(E = \text{gradient} \times L/A\), where \(L\) is the length and \(A\) the cross-sectional area, and calculate \(E\). (c) Calculate the elastic energy stored in the wire when the force is 32 N.
A: (a) gradient \(= \dfrac{32}{2.5 \times 10^{-3}}\) [C1] \(= 1.3 \times 10^{4}\,\mathrm{N\,m^{-1}}\). [A1]
(b) \(E = \dfrac{\text{stress}}{\text{strain}} = \dfrac{F/A}{x/L} = \dfrac{F}{x} \times \dfrac{L}{A}\), and \(F/x\) is the gradient. [M1] \(A = \pi \times (0.20 \times 10^{-3})^2 = 1.257 \times 10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m^2}\), so \(E = \dfrac{12\,800 \times 2.000}{1.257 \times 10^{-7}}\) [C1] \(= 2.0 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{Pa}\). [A1]
(c) \(E_P = \tfrac12 Fx = \tfrac12 \times 32 \times 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\) [C1] \(= 0.040\,\mathrm{J}\). [A1]
Also revise: [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
Q: [8 marks] (#phys.dynamics) ★★★ A bullet of mass 10 g is fired horizontally into a block of mass 2.0 kg that hangs from long light strings. The bullet embeds in the block, and the block and bullet swing up together to a maximum height of 0.10 m above the starting level. (a) Calculate the speed of the block and bullet immediately after the impact. (b) Calculate the speed of the bullet before the impact. (c) Calculate the kinetic energy of the bullet before the impact and the percentage of it that is transferred to other forms during the impact. (d) State two forms that the lost energy is transferred to.
H: After the impact, kinetic energy changes into gravitational potential energy as the block rises 0.10 m. Use the total mass 2.01 kg.
H: During the impact (which is very short) momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not.
A: (a) \(\tfrac12 (M + m)v^2 = (M + m)gh\) [C1] so \(v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 0.10} = 1.4\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(b) Momentum is conserved in the impact: \(0.010\,u = 2.01 \times 1.40\) [C1] so \(u = 2.8 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(c) \(E_K = \tfrac12 \times 0.010 \times 281.5^2 = 4.0 \times 10^{2}\,\mathrm{J}\). [C1] After the impact the kinetic energy is \(\tfrac12 \times 2.01 \times 1.40^2 = 1.97\,\mathrm{J}\), so \(\dfrac{396 - 1.97}{396} = 99.5\%\) is transferred to other forms. [A1]
(d) Thermal energy (heating of the bullet and block). [B1] Sound, or work done deforming the block. [B1]
Also revise: [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
