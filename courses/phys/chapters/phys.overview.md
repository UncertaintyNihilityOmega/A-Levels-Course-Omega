:::why
Physics asks why things happen and answers with measurements and equations that predict what will happen next. That habit of thought is the basis of engineering, medicine, computing, finance and every quantitative degree, and it is useful even if you never use a formula again. It suits people who like asking "why?", and who are willing to practise the maths until it is automatic. You do not need to be a genius; you need to be curious and to practise a lot of problems.
:::

This chapter is the map of the course. Read it once now, and come back to it when you plan your year or when you wonder where a topic belongs.

## The 9702 exam

This course follows **Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics (9702)**, for examination in 2025, 2026 and 2027. There are five papers.

| Paper | Title | Time | Marks | What it tests | AS weight | A Level weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Multiple Choice | 1 h 15 min | 40 | 40 four-option questions on AS content | 31% | 15.5% |
| 2 | AS Level Structured Questions | 1 h 15 min | 60 | structured questions on AS content | 46% | 23% |
| 3 | Advanced Practical Skills | 2 h | 40 | laboratory practical: two questions of 20 marks, 1 hour each | 23% | 11.5% |
| 4 | A Level Structured Questions | 2 h | 100 | A Level content, with AS knowledge required | – | 38.5% |
| 5 | Planning, Analysis and Evaluation | 1 h 15 min | 30 | written: two compulsory questions of 15 marks | – | 11.5% |

The AS qualification is Papers 1, 2 and 3, a total of 140 marks. The full A Level adds Papers 4 and 5, so it is 270 marks in all. AS content (topics 1 to 11) is assumed in Papers 4 and 5, so the A Level never lets you forget the first year.

### Routes, grades and series

- **AS only:** Papers 1, 2 and 3, all taken in one series. Grades **a to e**.
- **Staged A Level:** AS in the first year, then Papers 4 and 5 in a later series. Grades **A\* to E**.
- **A Level in one series:** all five papers together.
- Series are **June and November** (March in India only).

Paper 1 and Paper 2 give you a data and formulae sheet on page 2, and Paper 4 on pages 2 and 3. It lists constants such as \(g\), \(e\) and \(h\) and a few formulae. Learn where things are on it, not only what is on it (more in "How to reach A\*" below). Answers use SI units.

### Assessment objectives

Every mark in every paper tests one of three skills.

| Objective | What it means | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Knowledge and understanding: definitions, laws, how things work | 40% |
| AO2 | Handling, applying and evaluating information: calculations, unfamiliar contexts, graphs | 40% |
| AO3 | Experimental skills and investigations: measuring, plotting, analysing, planning, evaluating | 20% |

Papers 1, 2 and 4 are half AO1 and half AO2. Papers 3 and 5 are entirely AO3. So a fifth of your marks are for **doing and understanding experiments**, and two fifths are for using physics in contexts you have not seen before.

:::tip What AO2 means for you
AO2 marks are for unfamiliar situations. You cannot memorise your way to them. Learn each idea so well that you can apply it to a satellite, a heart monitor or a roller coaster that you have never seen. The practice questions in every chapter here are built to train exactly that.
:::

This course has one chapter per topic, in syllabus order, with two exceptions: topic 20 is split into two chapters, and there are extra chapters for practical skills and exam preparation. ## How Cambridge compares with other boards

Most of the physics is shared between boards. One clear difference is in how practical skills are assessed.

:::board
**AQA (7408)** has no timed practical exam: laboratory work is assessed by the teacher as a practical endorsement, reported separately from the A Level grade. In Cambridge 9702, practical skills are examined in Papers 3 and 5, and those marks count towards the grade.
:::

If you are studying for a different board, this course covers the physics, but follow your own board's paper structure and use its specification for the exact content list.

## Study order and pace

Follow the order in the tables above. Each chapter assumes the ones before it, and the A Level chapters assume all of AS. The syllabus puts guided learning at about **180 hours for AS and about 360 hours for the full A Level**. "Guided" hours are time a teacher would normally supervise, and the real time you need depends on where you start. At about 9 hours a week, 180 hours is 20 weeks. This table is a sample plan: shorten or stretch it to fit your year.

| Weeks | Work |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | [Physics from zero](#phys.f.basics) (if needed), [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units); start [Practical skills](#phys.practical) |
| 3–4 | [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics) |
| 5–6 | [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics) |
| 7–9 | [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces), [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy), [Deformation of solids](#phys.solids) |
| 10–13 | [Waves](#phys.waves), [Superposition](#phys.superposition) |
| 14–16 | [Electricity](#phys.electricity), [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc) |
| 17 | [Particle physics](#phys.particles) |
| 18–20 | AS revision, [Mixed practice: AS Level](#phys.mixed.as), the Paper 1 and Paper 2 guides, timed past papers |
| 21–29 | [Motion in a circle](#phys.circular) to [Electric fields](#phys.efields): circular motion, gravity, thermal physics, oscillations, electric fields |
| 30–39 | [Capacitance](#phys.capacitance) to [Astronomy and cosmology](#phys.astro): capacitors, magnetic fields, induction, quantum, nuclear, medical, astronomy; [Planning, analysis and evaluation](#phys.planning) alongside |
| 40–44 | A Level revision, [Mixed practice: A Level](#phys.mixed.a2), the Paper 4 guide, timed past papers |

Within a chapter, work through it in order: read a section, try its examples yourself before reading the working, then do the practice questions without looking at the answers. Reread anything that you got wrong a day later, then a week later.

**Start [Practical skills](#phys.practical) early**, alongside your first AS topics, because the skills (tables, graphs, uncertainties) are used in every later chapter.

## How to reach A\*

An A\* is not about being the cleverest person. It comes from doing a few things well, every time.

1. **Definitions word-perfect.** Many marks are for exact statements (for example, the definitions of e.m.f., half-life or Young modulus). Each essential phrase earns a mark, so learn the wording in the definition cards in each chapter.
2. **Derivations rehearsed.** Several syllabus outcomes say "derive". Practise each until you can write it out in a few lines with a reason on every line.
3. **Units and significant figures, every time.** A correct number without a unit, or with the wrong number of significant figures, loses a mark again and again. Give answers to the same s.f. as the data, and never fewer than 2.
4. **Past papers, under timed conditions, with the mark scheme.** After the topics, do whole papers in the time allowed. Mark yourself strictly against the scheme, then rewrite every answer that lost a mark.
5. **Know the data sheet.** Know where each constant and formula is on it, and which formulae are *not* on it (you must remember those).
6. **Practical work, hands-on.** A fifth of the marks are experimental skills. The syllabus expects you to have done practical work, and doing it is the only way to feel what an uncertainty or a poor method really is. If you study alone, ask your exam centre what you need for Paper 3, and look for a school, college or science club that lets private candidates use a laboratory. Where you cannot, repeat each chapter's lab box with kitchen and hardware-shop apparatus (a ruler, a stopwatch, a spring, a torch, a multimeter), and practise the paper skills in [Practical skills](#phys.practical) and [Planning, analysis and evaluation](#phys.planning).
7. **Read every command word.** "State", "describe", "explain" and "determine" ask for different things. See [Exam technique](#road.technique) and [Command words decoded](#road.commands) in the study guide. The command words in the 9702 syllabus are: calculate, comment, compare, define, describe, determine, explain, give, identify, justify, predict, show (that), sketch, state and suggest.

:::exam Marks are for the working
In a calculation, write the formula, substitute with units, and show the answer with its unit. In Cambridge mark schemes a correct formula or substitution earns a mark even if you slip later, and a wrong final answer with no working earns little or nothing.
:::

:::warn The most common ways to lose marks
Forgetting units, rounding too early, mixing prefixes (mA and A in one equation), answering "explain" questions with one sentence, and leaving an answer blank. A guess with working can earn a mark. A blank earns none.
:::
