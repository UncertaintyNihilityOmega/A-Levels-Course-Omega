## The nuclear atom

Around 1909, most scientists pictured an atom as a ball of positive charge with electrons scattered through it like fruit in a pudding. Ernest Rutherford's team tested this by firing **α-particles** (fast, positively charged particles from a radioactive source) at a very thin gold foil. Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden counted how many α-particles came out at each angle, and the result forced a completely new picture of the atom.

The apparatus has a source of α-particles and a narrow beam, a very thin gold foil, and a detector that can be moved round to any angle. Everything is in a **vacuum**, because α-particles are stopped by a few centimetres of air.

## Protons, neutrons and electrons

The simple model of the **nuclear atom** has a nucleus of **protons** and **neutrons** (together called **nucleons**) and electrons in orbit around it. The numbers matter for the exam:

| Particle | Charge | Mass |
|---|---|---|
| Proton | \(+e = +1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\) | \(1.67 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\) (about 1 u) |
| Neutron | 0 | slightly more than a proton (about 1 u) |
| Electron | \(-e\) | \(9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\) (about \(5.5 \times 10^{-4}\) u, roughly 1/1800 of a proton) |

The nucleus contains almost all of the mass, and the electrons are not in it. A neutral atom has the same number of electrons as protons. Two numbers identify a nucleus.

:::def Proton number
The number of protons in a nucleus. It is given the symbol \(Z\), and it decides which element the atom is.
:::

:::def Nucleon number
The number of nucleons (protons and neutrons) in a nucleus. It is given the symbol \(A\), so the number of neutrons is \(N = A - Z\).
:::

:::def Nuclide
A particular type of nucleus with a specific number of protons and neutrons.
:::

A nuclide is written with the nucleon number at the top left and the proton number at the bottom left of the element symbol: \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\). For example, \(^{12}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) has 6 protons and \(12 - 6 = 6\) neutrons.

:::def Isotopes
Nuclei of the same element (the same number of protons) with different numbers of neutrons.
:::

Carbon has three well-known isotopes, \(^{12}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\), \(^{13}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\), with 6, 7 and 8 neutrons. Because the electron arrangement depends only on the number of protons, isotopes of an element have the same chemical properties. Only the masses (and the stability of the nucleus) differ.

:::eg State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in (a) a neutral atom of \(^{235}_{\;92}\mathrm{U}\) and (b) the ion \(^{56}_{26}\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}\).
(a) Protons: \(Z = 92\). Neutrons: \(A - Z = 235 - 92 = 143\). A neutral atom has 92 electrons. [B1]
(b) Protons: 26. Neutrons: \(56 - 26 = 30\). The ion has lost three electrons, so it has \(26 - 3 = 23\) electrons. [B1]
The charge of the ion changes the number of electrons only. It never changes the nucleus.
:::

:::warn Where the electrons are
Electrons are not inside the nucleus. When a nucleus emits a β⁻ particle, the electron is **created** in the decay, as you will see below. And do not confuse the nucleon number (an integer count) with the atomic mass, which is a measured mass in u and is not an integer.
:::

## Alpha, beta and gamma radiation

Some nuclei are unstable and **decay** by emitting radiation. Three types were discovered first, and a fourth appears in the syllabus.

| Radiation | What it is | Charge | Approximate mass | Typical stopping |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| α | helium nucleus, \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\) (2 protons + 2 neutrons) | \(+2e\) | 4 u | paper or a few cm of air; strongly ionising |
| β⁻ | fast electron from the nucleus | \(-e\) | \(5.5 \times 10^{-4}\) u | a few mm of aluminium |
| β⁺ | fast **positron** | \(+e\) | \(5.5 \times 10^{-4}\) u | a few mm of aluminium, then it annihilates |
| γ | photon of high-energy electromagnetic radiation | 0 | 0 | intensity reduced, never fully stopped, by thick lead; weakly ionising |

The charges show up when the radiations pass through an electric field (between two charged plates) or a magnetic field. α and β⁻ are deflected in **opposite** directions, because their charges have opposite signs; β⁺ is deflected the same way as α; γ is not deflected at all. β-particles are deflected far more than α-particles, even though they usually move faster, because their charge-to-mass ratio is about 3600 times larger (\(1.76 \times 10^{11}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\) against \(4.8 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\), found in the example on the unified atomic mass unit below). A favourite Paper 1 question shows three tracks between two plates and asks you to name each one.

### Antiparticles and neutrinos

Every particle has an **antiparticle**, and the **positron** is the antiparticle of the electron.

:::def Antiparticle
A particle with the same mass as its corresponding particle but opposite charge.
:::

So the positron has mass \(9.11 \times 10^{-31}\,\mathrm{kg}\) and charge \(+e\). In β⁻ decay an **electron antineutrino** is emitted as well as the electron, and in β⁺ decay an **electron neutrino** is emitted as well as the positron. A neutrino has almost no mass, no charge and interacts extremely weakly: tens of billions of solar neutrinos pass through every square centimetre of your body each second without any effect.

### Energy spectra: why β-particles are different

When a nucleus decays by emitting an α-particle, only two bodies are produced, the α-particle and the daughter nucleus. Conservation of momentum fixes how they share the energy released, so the α-particles from one type of decay all have the same energy, or one of a few discrete values.

:::eg A stationary radium-226 nucleus decays by α emission into radon-222. Show that the α-particle takes about 98% of the kinetic energy released. Use nucleon numbers in place of masses.
The nucleus is at rest, so the total momentum afterwards is zero. The α-particle (mass 4) and the radon nucleus (mass 222) have equal and opposite momenta of magnitude \(p\). [B1]
Kinetic energy is \(E_K = p^{2}/2m\), so for the same \(p\), \(E_K \propto 1/m\). The α-particle has the smaller mass, so it takes the larger share. The ratio of energies is \(E_\alpha/E_\text{Rn} = 222/4 = 55.5\). [C1]
\[\text{fraction taken by } \alpha = \frac{222}{222 + 4} = 0.98\] [A1]
Because the fraction is fixed, every α-particle from this decay has the same energy.
:::

In β decay, **three** bodies share the energy: the β-particle, the (anti)neutrino and the daughter nucleus. The energy released in each decay is the same, but it can be shared between the β-particle and the (anti)neutrino in any proportion. So β-particles have a **continuous range** of kinetic energies, from almost zero up to a maximum. That puzzled physicists because energy seemed to go missing, and it is the reason the neutrino was first proposed.

## Decay equations

A **decay equation** shows the nuclide before, the nuclides and particles after, and balances two things.

:::key Two conservation rules for every nuclear process
**Nucleon number** is conserved: the top numbers on each side add up to the same total.
**Charge** is conserved: the bottom numbers (proton number, or charge in units of \(e\)) add up to the same total.
:::

The particles use the same notation: α is \(^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}\), an electron β⁻ is \(^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e}\), a positron β⁺ is \(^{0}_{+1}\mathrm{e}\), a neutrino is \(\nu\) and an antineutrino is \(\bar{\nu}\). The neutrinos have \(A = 0\) and charge 0, so they never affect the balance, but the equation is incomplete without them.

- **α decay:** the nucleus loses 4 from \(A\) and 2 from \(Z\). Example: \(^{238}_{\;92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{\;90}\mathrm{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha\).
- **β⁻ decay:** a neutron changes into a proton. \(A\) stays the same and \(Z\) increases by 1.
- **β⁺ decay:** a proton changes into a neutron. \(A\) stays the same and \(Z\) decreases by 1.
- **γ emission:** an excited nucleus (written \(X^{*}\)) drops to a lower energy level and emits a photon. Neither \(A\) nor \(Z\) changes.

:::eg Technetium-99m is an excited nucleus (the "m" means it stays excited for a while). Write the equation for its γ emission and say what changes.
\(^{99m}_{\;43}\mathrm{Tc} \rightarrow {}^{99}_{\;43}\mathrm{Tc} + \gamma\). [B1]
The nucleon number (99) and the proton number (43) are the same on both sides: the nucleus is the same nuclide, and only energy leaves, carried away by the photon. Tc-99m is the commonest medical tracer, because its γ-rays pass out of the body to a detector; see [Medical physics](#phys.medical).
:::

:::eg Complete the decay equations for (a) α decay of radium-226, (b) β⁻ decay of carbon-14 and (c) β⁺ decay of fluorine-18.
(a) \(^{226}_{\;88}\mathrm{Ra} \rightarrow {}^{222}_{\;86}\mathrm{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha\). Nucleon number: \(226 = 222 + 4\). Charge: \(88 = 86 + 2\). [B1]
(b) \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{\;7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar{\nu}\). Nucleon number: \(14 = 14 + 0 + 0\). Charge: \(6 = 7 + (-1) + 0\). [B1] [B1]
(c) \(^{18}_{\;9}\mathrm{F} \rightarrow {}^{18}_{\;8}\mathrm{O} + {}^{\;0}_{+1}\mathrm{e} + \nu\). Nucleon number: \(18 = 18 + 0 + 0\). Charge: \(9 = 8 + 1 + 0\). [B1] [B1]
The neutrino in (c) is a neutrino, not an antineutrino, because it goes with β⁺. Fluorine-18 is used in PET scanners, covered in [Medical physics](#phys.medical).
:::

:::eg Uranium-238 decays by α emission and the daughter then decays by β⁻ emission. Write down both equations.
Step 1: \(^{238}_{\;92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{\;90}\mathrm{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha\). The new nucleus has \(A = 238 - 4 = 234\) and \(Z = 92 - 2 = 90\), which is thorium. [B1]
Step 2: \(^{234}_{\;90}\mathrm{Th} \rightarrow {}^{234}_{\;91}\mathrm{Pa} + {}^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar{\nu}\). \(A\) is unchanged and \(Z = 90 + 1 = 91\), which is protactinium. [B1]
Find the new element from the proton number in the periodic table, or from the data you are given.
:::

:::eg A nucleus of \(^{232}_{\;90}\mathrm{Th}\) emits one α-particle and then two β⁻ particles. Find the nucleon number and proton number of the final nucleus, and say how it is related to the original.
After the α-particle: \(A = 232 - 4 = 228\) and \(Z = 90 - 2 = 88\). [C1]
Each β⁻ particle increases \(Z\) by 1 and leaves \(A\) unchanged, so after two: \(A = 228\) and \(Z = 88 + 2 = 90\). [A1]
The final nucleus is \(^{228}_{\;90}\mathrm{Th}\). It has the same proton number as the original, so it is an isotope of thorium with four fewer neutrons. [B1]
:::

:::exam Decay equation checklist
Write the parent and the daughter with both numbers. Add the top numbers on each side, then the bottom numbers. ## The unified atomic mass unit

Masses of atoms and particles in kilograms are awkward numbers, so a smaller unit is used.

:::def The unified atomic mass unit (u)
One twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12. \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\).
:::

A proton or neutron has a mass of almost exactly 1 u, so the mass in u of a nucleus is close to its nucleon number. An electron's mass is only \(5.5 \times 10^{-4}\) u.

:::eg Find the mass of an α-particle, taken as 4.00 u, in kg. Compare its charge-to-mass ratio with that of a proton.
Mass: \(4.00 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 6.64 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\). [C1] [A1]
The α-particle has charge \(+2e = 3.20 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{C}\), so
\[\frac{Q}{m} = \frac{3.20 \times 10^{-19}}{6.64 \times 10^{-27}} = 4.8 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\]
For a proton: \(\dfrac{1.60 \times 10^{-19}}{1.67 \times 10^{-27}} = 9.6 \times 10^{7}\,\mathrm{C\,kg^{-1}}\), about twice as large. [B1] A proton has about twice the charge-to-mass ratio, so the same field accelerates it about twice as much.
:::

## Quarks, hadrons and leptons

Protons and neutrons are not fundamental. Each is made of smaller particles called **quarks**.

:::def Quark
A quark is a fundamental particle, one of six types (**flavours**): up (u), down (d), strange (s), charm (c), top (t) and bottom (b).
:::

A fundamental particle has no internal structure that we know of. Quarks have charges that are fractions of \(e\), and each has an **antiquark** with the opposite charge.

| Flavour | Symbol | Charge / \(e\) | Antiquark | Charge / \(e\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| up | u | \(+\tfrac23\) | \(\bar{\mathrm{u}}\) | \(-\tfrac23\) |
| charm | c | \(+\tfrac23\) | \(\bar{\mathrm{c}}\) | \(-\tfrac23\) |
| top | t | \(+\tfrac23\) | \(\bar{\mathrm{t}}\) | \(-\tfrac23\) |
| down | d | \(-\tfrac13\) | \(\bar{\mathrm{d}}\) | \(+\tfrac13\) |
| strange | s | \(-\tfrac13\) | \(\bar{\mathrm{s}}\) | \(+\tfrac13\) |
| bottom | b | \(-\tfrac13\) | \(\bar{\mathrm{b}}\) | \(+\tfrac13\) |

Quarks are never found alone. They combine into particles called **hadrons**.

:::def Hadron
A particle made of quarks (or antiquarks). Hadrons are either baryons or mesons.
:::

:::def Baryon
A hadron made of three quarks. Protons and neutrons are baryons. Three antiquarks form an antibaryon.
:::

:::def Meson
A hadron made of one quark and one antiquark.
:::

:::def Lepton
A fundamental particle of the family that includes the electron and the neutrinos. Leptons have no quarks inside them and do not form hadrons. The electron, the neutrinos and their antiparticles (the positron and the antineutrinos) are leptons.
:::

The proton is **uud** and the neutron is **udd**.

## Beta decay inside the nucleon

β decay changes one type of nucleon into another, and the quark model shows how. A neutron (udd) becomes a proton (uud) by changing one **down** quark into an **up** quark.

:::key Beta decay at the quark level
\(\beta^{-}\) decay: \(\mathrm{d} \rightarrow \mathrm{u} + {}^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar{\nu}\), so a neutron (udd) becomes a proton (uud).
\(\beta^{+}\) decay: \(\mathrm{u} \rightarrow \mathrm{d} + {}^{\;0}_{+1}\mathrm{e} + \nu\), so a proton (uud) becomes a neutron (udd).
:::

Charge is conserved. In β⁻ decay, the d quark has charge \(-\tfrac13\), and on the right-hand side we have \(+\tfrac23 + (-1) + 0 = -\tfrac13\). In β⁺ decay, the u quark has \(+\tfrac23\), and afterwards \(-\tfrac13 + 1 + 0 = +\tfrac23\). The nucleon number of the whole nucleus is unchanged because a nucleon turns into another nucleon. In nuclei β⁺ decay can happen, but a free proton does not decay this way, because a neutron has more mass than a proton.

:::eg The decay of a free neutron, \(\mathrm{n} \rightarrow \mathrm{p} + \mathrm{e}^{-} + \bar{\nu}\), is a β⁻ decay. Describe the change in quark composition and show that charge is conserved.
The neutron is udd and the proton is uud, so one down quark changes into an up quark. [B1]
Charge before: \(0\). Charge after: \(+1\) (proton) \(+ (-1)\) (electron) \(+ 0\) (antineutrino) \(= 0\). Charge is conserved. [B1]
:::

If your specification asks for conservation of lepton or baryon number, use that language there; here, conservation of charge and nucleon number is enough.

Line 1: \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{\;7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \nu\)
Line 2: nucleon number: \(14 = 14 + 0 + 0\); charge: \(6 = 7 - 1 + 0\)

The nuclide, the electron and the balancing are all correct. [C1] **The particle on the right is an antineutrino, \(\bar{\nu}\), not a neutrino: β⁻ decay emits an antineutrino and β⁺ decay emits a neutrino. The answer mark is lost.** [A0]

Q: How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in a neutral atom of \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\)?
A: Protons 6, neutrons \(14 - 6 = 8\), electrons 6.
Q: Name the two numbers needed to describe a nuclide, and give their symbols.
A: Proton number \(Z\) and nucleon number \(A\). The neutron number is \(A - Z\).
Q: \(^{12}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) are isotopes. Why do they have the same chemical properties?
A: They have the same number of protons, so the same number of electrons in the neutral atom, and chemistry depends on the electrons.
Q: Write down the nucleon number and proton number of the nucleus formed when \(^{210}_{\;84}\mathrm{Po}\) emits an α-particle.
A: \(A = 210 - 4 = 206\) and \(Z = 84 - 2 = 82\), so \(^{206}_{\;82}\mathrm{Pb}\).
Q: Which has the larger mass, a proton or an electron? Give the approximate ratio.
A: A proton. The ratio is \(1.67 \times 10^{-27}/9.11 \times 10^{-31} \approx 1800\).
Q: Calculate the charge on a particle made of an up quark and an anti-down quark.
A: \(+\tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = +1\).
Q: State the quark composition of an antineutron and of a \(\pi^{-}\) meson made of a down quark and an anti-up quark. Check the charge of the meson.
A: An antineutron is the antiparticle of udd, so \(\bar{\mathrm{u}}\bar{\mathrm{d}}\bar{\mathrm{d}}\). The meson is \(\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathrm{u}}\), with charge \(-\tfrac13 - \tfrac23 = -1\).
Q: Complete: \(^{19}_{\;8}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow {}^{19}_{\;9}\mathrm{F} + \dots + \dots\)
A: A neutron has changed into a proton, so this is β⁻ decay: \(^{19}_{\;8}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow {}^{19}_{\;9}\mathrm{F} + {}^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar{\nu}\).
Q: Why must α-scattering experiments be done in a vacuum?
A: α-particles are stopped by a few centimetres of air, so in air they would never reach the foil and the detector.
Q: A carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly 12 u. Find this mass in kg.
A: \(12 \times 1.66 \times 10^{-27} = 1.99 \times 10^{-26}\,\mathrm{kg}\).

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [2 marks] ★ Define isotopes.
A: Nuclei of the same element, with the same number of protons, [B1] but different numbers of neutrons. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Describe two observations from the α-particle scattering experiment and state what each shows about the structure of the atom.
A: Most α-particles pass straight through with little or no deflection, [B1] so most of the atom is empty space. [B1] A few are deflected through angles greater than 90° (some almost straight back), [B1] so the positive charge and most of the mass are concentrated in a very small, dense nucleus. [B1]
Q: [2 marks] ★ A nucleus of carbon-14, \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\), decays by β⁻ emission. Write the decay equation.
A: \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C} \rightarrow {}^{14}_{\;7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar{\nu}\). The nitrogen-14 nucleus with correct numbers [B1] and the electron with the antineutrino. [B1]
Q: [2 marks] ★★ Explain why the β-particles emitted from a sample of one isotope have a continuous range of energies.
A: The energy released in each decay is shared between the β-particle and the antineutrino, [B1] in different proportions in different decays, so the kinetic energy of the β-particle can have any value from zero to a maximum. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ α, β⁻ and γ radiation pass together between two parallel charged plates, as in the sketch. The top plate is positive. (a) Identify tracks A, B and C. (b) Explain why α-particles and β⁻-particles bend in opposite directions. (c) Explain why the β⁻ track bends much more than the α track, although β⁻-particles usually move faster.
A: (a) A is β⁻, B is γ and C is α. [B1] (b) The α-particle is positive and is pulled towards the negative plate, but the β⁻ particle is negative and is pulled towards the positive plate: opposite charges give forces in opposite directions. [B1] (c) The force on each is of the same order (charge \(2e\) or \(e\)), but the β⁻ mass is thousands of times smaller, so its acceleration is far larger: its charge-to-mass ratio is about 3600 times larger. [B1]
Q: [2 marks] ★★ Describe the change in quark composition that takes place in β⁺ decay.
A: An up quark changes into a down quark, [B1] so a proton (uud) becomes a neutron (udd), with a positron and a neutrino emitted. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A nucleus of uranium-238, \(^{238}_{\;92}\mathrm{U}\), decays through a series of α and β⁻ decays to the stable nucleus lead-206, \(^{206}_{\;82}\mathrm{Pb}\). Determine the number of α-decays and the number of β⁻ decays in the series.
H: Only α decay changes the nucleon number.
H: After you know the number of α decays, find how much the proton number would have fallen, then compare with the actual fall.
A: The nucleon number falls by \(238 - 206 = 32\). [C1] Each α decay reduces it by 4, so there are \(32/4 = 8\) α-decays. [A1] Eight α-decays reduce the proton number by 16, from 92 to 76. [C1] The actual proton number is 82, so it must have increased by 6, and each β⁻ decay increases \(Z\) by 1. [B1] So there are 6 β⁻ decays. [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ A meson is made of an up quark and an anti-strange quark. (a) Calculate its charge. (b) State why it is a meson. (c) State the quark composition of its antiparticle.
H: Charge of u is \(+\tfrac23 e\); charge of \(\bar{\mathrm{s}}\) is opposite to that of s.
A: (a) \(+\tfrac23 + \tfrac13\) [C1] \(= +1\). [A1] (b) It has one quark and one antiquark. [B1] (c) The antiparticle is \(\bar{\mathrm{u}}\mathrm{s}\) (an anti-up quark and a strange quark). [B1]
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A 5.0 MeV α-particle is fired directly at a gold nucleus (79 protons). The α-particle stops momentarily at its distance of closest approach and then rebounds. (a) Show that its kinetic energy is \(8.0 \times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}\). (b) Use energy conservation to estimate the distance of closest approach. The electric potential energy of two charges is \(\dfrac{Q_1 Q_2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r}\), with \(\dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99 \times 10^{9}\,\mathrm{m\,F^{-1}}\). (c) The radius of a gold nucleus is about \(7 \times 10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\). Comment on your answer.
H: \(1\,\mathrm{eV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-19}\,\mathrm{J}\), so \(1\,\mathrm{MeV} = 1.60 \times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}\).
H: At closest approach all the kinetic energy has become electric potential energy. The charges are \(2e\) and \(79e\).
A: (a) \(E_K = 5.0 \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13}\) [C1] \(= 8.0 \times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{J}\). [A1] (b) \(\dfrac{(2e)(79e)}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = 8.0 \times 10^{-13}\) [M1] so
\[r = \frac{8.99 \times 10^{9} \times 2 \times 79 \times (1.60 \times 10^{-19})^{2}}{8.0 \times 10^{-13}} = 4.5 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\] [A1]
(c) This is about six times the nuclear radius, so the α-particle stays outside the nucleus. [B1] The nucleus must therefore be smaller than \(4.5 \times 10^{-14}\,\mathrm{m}\), which supports the conclusion that it is very small. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Decide whether each of these nuclear reactions conserves both charge and nucleon number, and identify any that does not. (i) \(^{239}_{\;93}\mathrm{Np} \rightarrow {}^{239}_{\;94}\mathrm{Pu} + {}^{\;0}_{-1}\mathrm{e} + \bar{\nu}\). (ii) \(^{15}_{\;8}\mathrm{O} \rightarrow {}^{15}_{\;7}\mathrm{N} + {}^{\;0}_{+1}\mathrm{e} + \nu\). (iii) \(^{210}_{\;84}\mathrm{Po} \rightarrow {}^{206}_{\;82}\mathrm{Pb} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha\). (iv) \(^{238}_{\;92}\mathrm{U} \rightarrow {}^{236}_{\;90}\mathrm{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\alpha\).
H: Add the top numbers on each side, then the bottom numbers.
A: (i) \(239 = 239 + 0 + 0\) and \(93 = 94 - 1 + 0\): both conserved. [B1] (ii) \(15 = 15\) and \(8 = 7 + 1\): both conserved. [B1] (iii) \(210 = 206 + 4\) and \(84 = 82 + 2\): both conserved. [B1] (iv) The nucleon number: \(236 + 4 = 240 \neq 238\), so it does not conserve nucleon number. (The charge, \(90 + 2 = 92\), is conserved, and the thorium produced should be \(^{234}_{\;90}\mathrm{Th}\).) [B1]
:::

:::quiz
? What did the few α-particles deflected through angles greater than 90° show?
+ The positive charge and most of the mass of an atom are in a tiny nucleus.
- Atoms are mostly empty space.
- Electrons orbit the nucleus.
- The nucleus contains neutrons.
> Large deflections need a strong repulsion from a concentrated, massive charge. "Mostly empty space" is the conclusion from the particles that went straight through, and the experiment says nothing about neutrons or orbiting electrons.
? How many neutrons are there in a nucleus of \(^{238}_{\;92}\mathrm{U}\)?
- 92
- 238
+ 146
- 330
> \(N = A - Z = 238 - 92 = 146\). The number 92 is the protons and 238 is all the nucleons. Adding them (330) is a common slip.
? Which pair are isotopes of the same element?
+ \(^{12}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\)
- \(^{14}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{\;7}\mathrm{N}\)
- \(^{12}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{12}_{\;7}\mathrm{N}\)
- \(^{13}_{\;6}\mathrm{C}\) and \(^{14}_{\;7}\mathrm{N}\)
> Isotopes have the same proton number and different nucleon numbers. The other pairs have different proton numbers, so they are different elements.
? Which particles are emitted in β⁻ decay?
+ An electron and an electron antineutrino
- A positron and an electron neutrino
- An electron and an electron neutrino
- A helium nucleus and an electron antineutrino
> β⁻ decay turns a neutron into a proton, emitting an electron with an antineutrino. A positron and a neutrino go together in β⁺ decay.
? What is the quark composition of a proton?
- udd
+ uud
- uus
- u\(\bar{\mathrm{d}}\)
> Charges: \(+\tfrac23 + \tfrac23 - \tfrac13 = +1\). Udd is the neutron. Uus also has charge +1 but contains a strange quark, so it is a different particle. The last option is a meson.
? Which of these is a meson?
+ u\(\bar{\mathrm{d}}\)
- uud
- udd
- uds
> A meson has one quark and one antiquark. The other three are baryons, with three quarks each.
? Why do β-particles from a single isotope have a range of energies, while α-particles do not?
+ In β decay the energy is shared with an (anti)neutrino in varying proportions.
- β-particles lose energy escaping from the atom.
- The nucleus is in a different state each time.
- β-particles are much lighter than α-particles.
> Two products (α and daughter) share the energy in a fixed ratio, but three products (β, antineutrino and daughter) can share it in many ways. Mass alone does not explain the continuous spectrum.
? What is the charge of a strange antiquark?
+ \(+\tfrac13 e\)
- \(-\tfrac13 e\)
- \(+\tfrac23 e\)
- \(-\tfrac23 e\)
> A strange quark has charge \(-\tfrac13 e\), and its antiquark has the opposite charge, \(+\tfrac13 e\).

:::recap
- The α-scattering experiment: most go straight through (the atom is mostly empty space); a few are deflected through more than 90° (a small, dense, positive nucleus). Nucleus about \(10^{-15}\,\mathrm{m}\), atom about \(10^{-10}\,\mathrm{m}\).
- Nuclide \(^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\): \(A\) nucleons, \(Z\) protons, \(N = A - Z\) neutrons. Isotopes: same \(Z\), different \(N\).
- α = helium nucleus (+2e, 4 u); β⁻ = electron; β⁺ = positron; γ = photon. An antiparticle has the same mass and opposite charge.
- β⁻ decay emits an electron and an antineutrino; β⁺ decay emits a positron and a neutrino. α-particles have discrete energies; β-particles have a continuous range because the energy is shared with the (anti)neutrino.
- Decay equations balance nucleon number and charge. α: \(A \to A - 4\), \(Z \to Z - 2\). β⁻: \(Z \to Z + 1\). β⁺: \(Z \to Z - 1\). γ: no change.
- \(1\,\mathrm{u} = 1.66 \times 10^{-27}\,\mathrm{kg}\), one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
- Six quarks: u, c, t (\(+\tfrac23 e\)); d, s, b (\(-\tfrac13 e\)); antiquarks opposite. Proton uud, neutron udd. Baryon = three quarks; meson = quark + antiquark. Electrons and neutrinos are leptons.
- β⁻: d → u + e⁻ + antineutrino. β⁺: u → d + e⁺ + neutrino.
:::
