## phys.induction: Electromagnetic induction
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 5–7 hours` · `Next: [Alternating currents](#phys.ac)`
Before you start: [Magnetic fields](#phys.magnetic) (B, field patterns, F = BIL), [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc) (e.m.f.).
Warm-up: from Magnetic fields (define the tesla; field of a solenoid), D.C. circuits (define e.m.f.), Energy
(P = Fv).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 20.5.1 define magnetic flux as the product of the magnetic flux density and the cross-sectional area perpendicular to the direction of the magnetic flux density
- 20.5.2 recall and use Φ = BA
- 20.5.3 understand and use the concept of magnetic flux linkage
- 20.5.4 understand and explain experiments that demonstrate: that a changing magnetic flux can induce an e.m.f. in a circuit; that the induced e.m.f. is in such a direction as to oppose the change producing it; the factors affecting the magnitude of the induced e.m.f.
- 20.5.5 recall and use Faraday's and Lenz's laws of electromagnetic induction

### Sections (suggested)
1. Magnetic flux Φ = BA (A perpendicular to B; for a coil at angle, use the component: Φ = BA cos θ with θ between
   B and the normal, shown as an extension of the definition); the weber (1 Wb = 1 T m²). Flux linkage NΦ (Wb or
   Wb-turns).
2. Experiments (20.5.4), each a short `:::lab`: (a) a magnet pushed into and pulled out of a coil connected to a
   sensitive centre-zero meter: deflection only while moving, opposite when withdrawn, bigger when faster, with more
   turns, with a stronger magnet; (b) a straight wire moved through the field between magnets (cutting the field
   lines); (c) two coils on an iron core: switching the current in one induces a pulse in the other (no movement,
   just changing flux).
3. Faraday's law: the magnitude of the induced e.m.f. equals the rate of change of flux linkage, E = Δ(NΦ)/Δt.
   Lenz's law: the induced e.m.f. (and current) is in the direction that opposes the change producing it, which
   is conservation of energy (otherwise the magnet would be pulled in faster and faster: energy from nothing).
   Combined form E = −d(NΦ)/dt, with the minus sign meaning Lenz.
4. A rod moving through a field: E = BLv (`:::proof` plain: area swept per second Lv, flux change per second
   BLv); direction by Lenz (or Fleming's right-hand rule, optional); the current produces a force BIL that opposes
   the motion, so work must be done: mechanical power in = electrical power out (Fv = EI).
5. Graphs: flux linkage against time and the induced e.m.f. as its negative gradient (a coil moving into, through
   and out of a field region; a magnet falling through a coil: two pulses of opposite sign, the second larger and
   shorter because the magnet is faster).
6. Applications (qualitative): generator (a coil rotating in a field: sinusoidal e.m.f., link
   [Alternating currents](#phys.ac); E₀ = BANω as an A* extra), transformers (changing flux in the core; link to
   transmission of power), eddy currents and magnetic braking, induction hobs, a magnet falling slowly through a
   copper pipe.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Magnetic flux: "the product of the magnetic flux density and the cross-sectional area perpendicular to the direction of the magnetic flux density" (Φ = BA).
- The weber: "the magnetic flux through an area of 1 m² perpendicular to a uniform magnetic flux density of 1 T".
- Magnetic flux linkage: "the product of the magnetic flux through a coil and the number of turns of the coil" (NΦ).
- Faraday's law: "the magnitude of the induced e.m.f. is proportional to (equal to) the rate of change of magnetic flux linkage".
- Lenz's law: "the direction of the induced e.m.f. is such as to oppose the change (in flux) producing it".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Coil of 200 turns, area 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², field rises from 0 to 0.25 T in 0.050 s: E = 0.40 V.
- Aircraft wings 30 m across flying at 250 m s⁻¹ in a vertical field component of 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T: e.m.f. 0.30 V
  (and why no current flows round a circuit containing the wings).
- Coil pulled out of a field at constant speed: flux linkage–time and e.m.f.–time graphs.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define magnetic flux [1] and flux linkage [1]; state Faraday's law [2]; use Lenz's law to
  find a current direction and explain it with energy [3]; E from a flux–time graph gradient [3]; rod on rails with
  a resistor: E, I, force needed to keep it moving at constant speed, power [6]; ★★★ magnet falling through a coil:
  sketch and explain the e.m.f.–time graph (two pulses, sizes, signs, areas equal) [5]; ★★★ a rotating coil:
  maximum e.m.f. and the orientation where it happens [5].
- Stretch: a square loop entering, crossing and leaving a field region at constant speed: e.m.f. and force graphs,
  energy dissipated; or terminal speed of a rod sliding down rails on a slope in a field.
- Spot the error: e.m.f. induced by a steady (non-changing) field, or flux linkage with N forgotten.

### Pitfalls
Flux vs flux density; e.m.f. needs change; area perpendicular to B; Lenz opposes the **change**, not the field;
mixing up which rule (left-hand for motor force, Lenz/right-hand for induced current).

### Figures
Magnet and coil with a centre-zero meter (hand SVG). Rod on rails in a field (⊗) with v, I and the opposing force
arrows. Flux linkage–time and e.m.f.–time graphs (stacked, shared time axis: hand SVG or two `:::plot`s).

### Board box
AQA and OCR use the same laws and also require E = BANω sin ωt for a rotating coil and the transformer equation;
Cambridge covers the generator and transformer only qualitatively in this topic.

## phys.ac: Alternating currents
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 4–6 hours` · `Next: [Quantum physics](#phys.quantum)`
Before you start: [Electromagnetic induction](#phys.induction), [Capacitance](#phys.capacitance) (discharge, τ = RC),
[Electricity](#phys.electricity) (diode characteristic, P = I²R). The syllabus assumes IGCSE knowledge of why power is
transmitted at high voltage: recap in two sentences. Warm-up: from Capacitance (τ = RC), Electricity (P = V²/R),
Oscillations (ω = 2πf).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 21.1.1 understand and use the terms period, frequency and peak value as applied to an alternating current or voltage
- 21.1.2 use equations of the form x = x₀ sin ωt representing a sinusoidally alternating current or voltage
- 21.1.3 recall and use the fact that the mean power in a resistive load is half the maximum power for a sinusoidal alternating current
- 21.1.4 distinguish between root-mean-square (r.m.s.) and peak values and recall and use I_r.m.s. = I₀/√2 and V_r.m.s. = V₀/√2 for a sinusoidal alternating current
- 21.2.1 distinguish graphically between half-wave and full-wave rectification
- 21.2.2 explain the use of a single diode for the half-wave rectification of an alternating current
- 21.2.3 explain the use of four diodes (bridge rectifier) for the full-wave rectification of an alternating current
- 21.2.4 analyse the effect of a single capacitor in smoothing, including the effect of the values of capacitance and the load resistance

### Sections (suggested)
1. Alternating current and voltage: period, frequency, peak value, peak-to-peak; V = V₀ sin ωt with ω = 2πf; reading
   them from a graph or an oscilloscope trace (link [Waves](#phys.waves)).
2. Power in a resistor: P = I²R = I₀²R sin²ωt; the mean of sin² over a cycle is ½ (`:::proof` plain: sin² + cos² = 1
   and the two have the same mean), so mean power = ½ peak power.
3. R.m.s. values: definition (the steady direct current/p.d. that dissipates the same mean power in a resistor);
   I_rms = I₀/√2, V_rms = V₀/√2 (`:::proof` plain from the mean power); mean power = I_rms V_rms; mains values are
   r.m.s. (UK 230 V → peak 325 V; also 50 Hz). Mean current over a cycle is zero, which is why r.m.s. is used.
4. Rectification: half-wave with one diode (output only in half-cycles), full-wave with a bridge of four diodes
   (current through the load in the same direction in both half-cycles; trace the two paths on the circuit figure
   in two colours `c1` and `c2`). Output graphs of each.
5. Smoothing: a capacitor in parallel with the load charges up to the peak and then discharges through the load
   between peaks; ripple; a larger RC (time constant much longer than the period) gives less ripple; full-wave
   rectification halves the time between peaks, so smoothing is better for the same RC. Embed **phys-ac**.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Period, frequency, peak value (of an alternating current or voltage).
- Root-mean-square current: "the value of the steady direct current that would dissipate energy at the same average rate (mean power) in a resistor as the alternating current".
- Rectification: "the conversion of alternating current into direct (one-direction) current".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- V = 325 sin(100πt): peak, frequency (50 Hz), r.m.s. (230 V), value at t = 2.5 ms (230 V).
- A 60 W (r.m.s. rating) lamp on 230 V mains: r.m.s. current, peak current, peak power (120 W).
- Smoothing: load 2.0 kΩ, capacitor 470 μF, 50 Hz full-wave: τ = 0.94 s ≫ 0.010 s between peaks; estimate the
  fractional ripple (≈ t/τ ≈ 1%).
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define r.m.s. current [2]; peak and r.m.s. from a CRO trace [3]; mean power in a resistor
  [3]; sketch half-wave and full-wave outputs [2]; explain the bridge rectifier (current paths) [4]; effect of
  doubling C or R on ripple [2]; ★★★ smoothing design: choose C for a given maximum ripple [5]; ★★★ an a.c. and a d.c.
  supply giving the same heating: find the peak of the a.c. [4].
- Stretch: the r.m.s. value of a square wave or a half-wave rectified sine (V₀/2); or show that the mean of sin² is
  ½ using cos 2θ.
- Spot the error: dividing peak power by √2 instead of 2, or using peak values for mains voltage.

### Pitfalls
r.m.s. vs mean (mean of a sine is zero); √2 only for sinusoids; peak vs peak-to-peak; mean power = ½ peak power
(not 1/√2); rectified output frequency doubles with full-wave.

### Figures
`:::plot` of V = V₀ sin ωt with V₀ and V_rms lines (`--hline`); power against time (sin², mean line at ½). Bridge
rectifier circuit (hand SVG with four diodes, copy the gold chapter's diode symbol) with the two current paths.
Half-wave, full-wave and smoothed outputs (three small `:::plot`s or one hand SVG).

### Board box
AQA and OCR treat a.c. mainly through oscilloscope use and r.m.s. values; rectification and smoothing are a Cambridge
emphasis. Content-only.

## Widget to build: `phys-ac` (widgets/phys-ac.js)
Embed in phys.ac: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-ac" data-v0="10" data-f="50" data-mode="full"></div>`.
- Attributes: `data-v0` (peak, V, 1–20), `data-f` (Hz, 10–100), `data-mode="none|half|full"` (rectification),
  `data-c` (μF, 0 = no capacitor, up to 2200), `data-r` (load, kΩ, 0.1–10).
- Tabs: **No rectifier**, **Half-wave**, **Full-wave**; a smoothing toggle with C and R sliders.
- Canvas: input sine (faint), output p.d. across the load (bold `sc`) over 3–4 cycles; when smoothing is on, simulate
  the capacitor: it follows the rectified input while that is above the capacitor p.d., otherwise decays as
  V e^(−Δt/RC) (ignore the diode drop or include 0.7 V per conducting diode as a toggle, labelled). Mark the
  ripple ΔV. Optional: a small bridge-rectifier diagram highlighting the conducting diodes for the current
  half-cycle.
- Readout: V₀, V_rms = V₀/√2 (for the unrectified sine), period, mean power in R for the unrectified supply
  (V_rms²/R), τ = RC, ripple ΔV and ripple as a % of the peak.
- Probe `data-mode="banana"` (fall back to none), `data-c="0"`.
