## phys.temperature: Temperature
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 3–5 hours` · `Next: [Ideal gases](#phys.gases)`
Before you start: [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy), [Electricity](#phys.electricity) (resistance of a metal;
the thermistor). Warm-up: from Energy (power = energy/time), Electricity (P = VI), Solids or Dynamics (a unit
conversion: g to kg).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 14.1.1 understand that (thermal) energy is transferred from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature
- 14.1.2 understand that regions of equal temperature are in thermal equilibrium
- 14.2.1 understand that a physical property that varies with temperature may be used for the measurement of temperature and state examples of such properties, including the density of a liquid, volume of a gas at constant pressure, resistance of a metal, e.m.f. of a thermocouple
- 14.2.2 understand that the scale of thermodynamic temperature does not depend on the property of any particular substance
- 14.2.3 convert temperatures between kelvin and degrees Celsius and recall that T/K = θ/°C + 273.15
- 14.2.4 understand that the lowest possible temperature is zero kelvin on the thermodynamic temperature scale and that this is known as absolute zero
- 14.3.1 define and use specific heat capacity
- 14.3.2 define and use specific latent heat and distinguish between specific latent heat of fusion and specific latent heat of vaporisation

### Sections (suggested)
1. Temperature vs thermal energy (a bath at 40 °C holds more energy than a cup of tea at 80 °C). Energy flows
   from hotter to colder; thermal equilibrium (no net energy transfer) — the basis of every thermometer
   (it must reach equilibrium with the object).
2. Thermometric properties: the four the syllabus names (liquid density/expansion, gas volume at constant
   pressure, resistance of a metal, thermocouple e.m.f.) plus the thermistor. Comparison table (range,
   sensitivity, response time, size, remote reading). Different property-based scales agree only at the fixed
   points, because the properties do not all vary linearly.
3. The thermodynamic (Kelvin) scale: absolute, independent of any substance; absolute zero (0 K, −273.15 °C) is
   the lowest possible temperature (minimum internal energy; link to the extrapolated gas law in the next
   chapter). T/K = θ/°C + 273.15; a temperature **difference** is the same in K and °C.
4. Specific heat capacity: definition, E = mcΔθ (units J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹), typical values (water 4200, aluminium 900,
   copper 390, given as typical). `:::lab` electrical method: block or liquid, immersion heater, V and I (or a
   joulemeter), timer, thermometer, insulation; plot θ against t, gradient = P/(mc); heat losses make c too large;
   reduce with insulation, a lid, or by starting below room temperature and ending as far above. Method of
   mixtures (hot metal into water, energy lost = energy gained).
5. Specific latent heat: definition, E = mL (units J kg⁻¹); fusion vs vaporisation; why L_v ≫ L_f (molecules
   separated completely, much more potential energy gained, plus work pushing back the atmosphere); heating and
   cooling curves (temperature constant during a change of state: energy goes into potential energy, not kinetic
   energy, of the molecules). `:::lab` for latent heat: heater in a funnel of ice, with a control funnel without a
   heater to remove the effect of the room; or a boiling kettle on a balance (two powers to cancel losses, as an A*
   extra).

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Thermal equilibrium: "a state in which there is no net transfer of thermal energy between regions (they are at the same temperature)".
- Absolute zero: "the lowest possible temperature, zero kelvin, at which a substance has minimum internal energy".
- Specific heat capacity: "the energy required per unit mass to raise the temperature by one degree (one kelvin)" (c = E/(mΔθ)).
- Specific latent heat of fusion: "the energy required per unit mass to change a substance from solid to liquid without any change in temperature". Of vaporisation: "… from liquid to gas without any change in temperature".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- 0.50 kg of water from 20 °C to 100 °C (168 kJ); time for a 2.0 kW kettle (84 s, ignoring losses).
- Energy to turn 0.20 kg of ice at −10 °C into water at 20 °C (c_ice 2100, L_f 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹,
  c_water 4200): 4.2 + 66.8 + 16.8 = 87.8 kJ.
- Electrical method data: 50 W heater, 1.0 kg aluminium block, θ–t gradient 0.050 K s⁻¹ → c = 1000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
  (higher than the true value, why).
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): convert 37 °C to K [1]; define specific latent heat [2]; why temperature stays
  constant while melting [2]; mixtures problem [4]; L_v from a kettle on a balance [4]; ★★★ two-power method to
  eliminate heat losses [6]; ★★★ comparing thermometers for a given job, reasons [4].
- Stretch: a shower heater: water flow rate, temperature rise, required power; or the time for an ice cube
  to melt in a drink, combining c and L.
- Spot the error: Δθ converted to kelvin by adding 273.

### Pitfalls
Adding 273 to a temperature difference; mixing g and kg; confusing temperature with energy; latent heat
"increases kinetic energy".

### Figures
Heating curve for water (hand SVG or a piecewise `:::plot` alternative: hand SVG with labelled sections: solid
warming, melting at 0 °C, liquid warming, boiling at 100 °C, gas). Electrical method apparatus.

### Board box
AQA and OCR include the same content; AQA also uses the specific heat capacity equation Q = mcΔθ and the
continuous-flow method.

## phys.gases: Ideal gases
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Thermodynamics](#phys.thermo)`
Before you start: [Temperature](#phys.temperature), [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics) (force = rate of
change of momentum), [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces) (pressure = F/A).
Warm-up: from Temperature (°C → K), Dynamics (Δp for a rebound), Forces (pressure).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 15.1.1 understand that amount of substance is an SI base quantity with the base unit mol
- 15.1.2 use molar quantities where one mole of any substance is the amount containing a number of particles of that substance equal to the Avogadro constant N_A
- 15.2.1 understand that a gas obeying pV ∝ T, where T is the thermodynamic temperature, is known as an ideal gas
- 15.2.2 recall and use the equation of state for an ideal gas expressed as pV = nRT, where n = amount of substance (number of moles) and as pV = NkT, where N = number of molecules
- 15.2.3 recall that the Boltzmann constant k is given by k = R/N_A
- 15.3.1 state the basic assumptions of the kinetic theory of gases
- 15.3.2 explain how molecular movement causes the pressure exerted by a gas and derive and use the relationship pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩, where ⟨c²⟩ is the mean-square speed (a simple model considering one-dimensional collisions and then extending to three dimensions using ⅓⟨c²⟩ = ⟨c_x²⟩ is sufficient)
- 15.3.3 understand that the root-mean-square speed c_r.m.s. is given by √⟨c²⟩
- 15.3.4 compare pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ with pV = NkT to deduce that the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule is (3/2)kT, and recall and use this expression

### Data (data sheet)
N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹, 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Molar masses are given
in questions (e.g. nitrogen 0.028 kg mol⁻¹, helium 0.004 kg mol⁻¹, oxygen 0.032 kg mol⁻¹).

### Sections (suggested)
1. The mole and the Avogadro constant; number of molecules N = nN_A; molar mass; mass of one molecule m = M/N_A.
2. The gas laws as experimental facts: Boyle (pV constant at constant T), Charles (V ∝ T at constant p), pressure
   law (p ∝ T at constant V); extrapolating V–θ or p–θ graphs to −273 °C (absolute zero). Together: pV ∝ T for a
   fixed mass: an **ideal gas** is one that obeys this exactly (real gases approach it at low pressure and high
   temperature).
3. Equation of state: pV = nRT and pV = NkT; k = R/N_A (the gas constant per molecule). Units: p in Pa, V in m³
   (1 litre = 10⁻³ m³), T in K. "Before and after" problems: p₁V₁/T₁ = p₂V₂/T₂ for a fixed amount; leaking or
   pumped containers (n changes). Density of a gas from pV = (m/M)RT.
4. Kinetic theory assumptions (list in exam wording: a large number of molecules in random motion; the
   volume of the molecules is negligible compared with the volume of the container; no intermolecular forces except
   during collisions; collisions are elastic; the time of a collision is negligible compared with the time between
   collisions; (Newton's laws apply)).
5. Pressure from molecular motion: qualitative explanation (each collision with a wall changes momentum, so the
   wall exerts a force; many collisions per second give a steady force per unit area). Then
   `:::proof examinable` pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩: one molecule in a cube of side L, momentum change 2mc_x per collision,
   time between collisions with the same wall 2L/c_x, average force mc_x²/L, pressure mc_x²/L³; sum over N
   molecules: p = Nm⟨c_x²⟩/V; random motion gives ⟨c_x²⟩ = ⟨c_y²⟩ = ⟨c_z²⟩ = ⅓⟨c²⟩ (because c² = c_x² + c_y² + c_z²);
   so pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩. Note ρ form: p = ⅓ρ⟨c²⟩.
6. Mean-square and root-mean-square speed: a numerical example with five molecules (mean speed vs r.m.s. speed:
   they differ). Embed **phys-gas** here.
7. Temperature and molecular kinetic energy: `:::proof examinable` ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ = NkT → ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT. So the
   mean translational KE of a molecule depends only on T (not on the gas): at the same temperature, lighter
   molecules move faster; c_r.m.s. = √(3kT/m). Total translational KE of n moles = (3/2)nRT (link
   [Thermodynamics](#phys.thermo): internal energy of an ideal gas).

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Mole: "the amount of substance that contains as many particles as there are atoms in 0.012 kg of carbon-12" (equivalently N_A particles).
- Avogadro constant: "the number of particles in one mole of a substance".
- Ideal gas: "a gas that obeys pV ∝ T (pV = nRT) at all pressures, volumes and temperatures".
- Boltzmann constant: k = R/N_A.
- Root-mean-square speed: "the square root of the mean of the squares of the speeds of the molecules".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Number of molecules in 1.0 m³ of air at 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and 20 °C (2.5 × 10²⁵).
- Cylinder of helium: pressure after heating; mass of gas from pV = nRT.
- Tyre pumped from 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa at 10 °C to a hot day 35 °C at constant volume.
- r.m.s. speed of nitrogen molecules at 300 K (m from 0.028/N_A: ≈ 517 m s⁻¹); compare helium (≈ 1370 m s⁻¹).
- Mean KE of a molecule at 27 °C (6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J).
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): state two assumptions of kinetic theory [2]; pV = nRT calculation [3]; explain
  pressure in terms of molecules [3]; derivation of pV = ⅓Nm⟨c²⟩ [5]; r.m.s. speed [3]; show that mean KE ∝ T [3];
  ★★★ two connected flasks at different temperatures, find the final common pressure [6]; ★★★ escape of gas from
  a planet's atmosphere: compare c_r.m.s. with escape speed (link [Gravitational fields](#phys.gravity)) [5].
- Stretch: a gas mixture of He and Ar at the same T: ratio of r.m.s. speeds (√(40/4)); or the pressure of a gas in
  terms of its density and c_r.m.s., and the speed of air molecules from atmospheric data.
- Spot the error: temperature in °C in pV = nRT, or ⟨c⟩² used instead of ⟨c²⟩.

### Pitfalls
°C instead of K; litres or cm³ instead of m³; n vs N; mean speed ≠ r.m.s. speed; mass of one molecule in g mol⁻¹;
the assumptions stated vaguely ("molecules are small").

### Figures
The cube derivation (hand SVG: a cube with one molecule's velocity components). `:::plot` of p against V (Boyle
curves at two temperatures) and of V against θ/°C extrapolating to −273 °C.

### Board box
AQA and OCR use the same equations; AQA also asks for the historical development of the gas laws and Brownian
motion. Content-only.

## phys.thermo: Thermodynamics
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 3–5 hours` · `Next: [Oscillations](#phys.oscillations)`
Before you start: [Ideal gases](#phys.gases), [Temperature](#phys.temperature), [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
The syllabus assumes IGCSE energy ideas. Warm-up: from Ideal gases (mean KE = 3/2 kT), Temperature (latent heat
and molecular PE), Energy (W = Fs).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 16.1.1 understand that internal energy is determined by the state of the system and that it can be expressed as the sum of a random distribution of kinetic and potential energies associated with the molecules of a system
- 16.1.2 relate a rise in temperature of an object to an increase in its internal energy
- 16.2.1 recall and use W = pΔV for the work done when the volume of a gas changes at constant pressure and understand the difference between the work done by the gas and the work done on the gas
- 16.2.2 recall and use the first law of thermodynamics ΔU = q + W expressed in terms of the increase in internal energy, the heating of the system (energy transferred to the system by heating) and the work done on the system

### Sections (suggested)
1. Internal energy: sum of the random kinetic and potential energies of the molecules; a function of state
   (depends only on p, V, T now, not on history). Solids/liquids: both KE and PE; ideal gas: no intermolecular
   forces, so no PE: U = (3/2)nRT for a monatomic ideal gas, U ∝ T. Temperature rise ↔ increase in mean molecular
   KE ↔ increase in U; melting: U increases with no temperature change (PE increases).
2. Work done by and on a gas: `:::proof` (plain) W = pΔV for a piston (F = pA, W = FΔx = pAΔx). Expansion: the gas
   does work; compression: work is done on the gas. Area under a p–V graph = work (for changing pressure).
3. The first law (Cambridge form, **ΔU = q + W**, W = work done ON the system): sign table (q positive when
   energy enters by heating; W positive when the gas is compressed; for expansion W = −pΔV).
4. Processes, each with a p–V sketch and a sign table: constant volume (W = 0, ΔU = q), constant pressure
   (W = −pΔV), isothermal for an ideal gas (ΔU = 0, q = −W), adiabatic / rapid (q = 0, ΔU = W: a bicycle pump
   warms; a gas expanding from an aerosol cools), a full cycle (ΔU = 0 over the cycle, net work = enclosed area).
   Real contexts: a car engine cycle, a fridge (qualitative).

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Internal energy: "the sum of a random distribution of kinetic and potential energies of the molecules of a system".
- First law of thermodynamics: "the increase in internal energy of a system is equal to the sum of the energy supplied to the system by heating and the work done on the system" (ΔU = q + W).

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Gas at 1.5 × 10⁵ Pa expands from 2.0 × 10⁻³ m³ to 5.0 × 10⁻³ m³ at constant pressure: work done by the gas
  450 J, so W = −450 J; with 1200 J supplied by heating, ΔU = +750 J.
- A cycle A → B → C → A given in a table with some entries missing: fill in q, W and ΔU (Cambridge-style table).
- Ideal gas heated at constant volume: q = ΔU; with n and ΔT, ΔU = (3/2)nRΔT for a monatomic gas.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define internal energy [2]; state the first law with the meaning of each term [3];
  work done at constant pressure [2]; explain why an ideal gas's U depends only on T [3]; the table for a cycle [6];
  explain using the first law why a gas cools when it expands rapidly [3]; ★★★ melting ice: q, W (small volume
  decrease) and ΔU, and why ΔU ≈ q [4]; ★★★ p–V cycle with a triangle: net work from the area, energy in and out [6].
- Stretch: compressing a gas in a bicycle pump quickly and then slowly: compare temperature changes using the first
  law; or show that for a cycle the net heating equals the net work done by the gas.
- Spot the error: using ΔU = q − W with W as work done on the gas (sign slip).

### Pitfalls
Sign of W (on vs by); "heat" vs internal energy; claiming temperature constant means no energy transfer;
using °C in U = (3/2)nRT; ΔV in cm³.

### Figures
Piston and cylinder with F = pA (hand SVG). p–V diagrams for the four processes (one figure, four small panels)
and a cycle with the enclosed area shaded.

### Board box
AQA writes the first law as Q = ΔU + W, where W is the work done **by** the gas. (An earlier note also claimed OCR's
form; it could not be verified and was left out at REVIEW P7–P8: keep it out.) Always check the sign convention.

## Widget to build: `phys-gas` (widgets/phys-gas.js)
Embed in phys.gases: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-gas" data-n="60" data-t="300"></div>`.
- Attributes: `data-n` (number of drawn particles, 10–200), `data-t` (temperature, K, 50–1000), `data-gas="he|n2|o2"`.
- Canvas: a box (with a movable right wall = volume control) with particles moving in 2D, elastic wall collisions
  (particle–particle collisions optional); speeds drawn from a 2D Maxwell–Boltzmann-like distribution scaled so
  that the r.m.s. speed ∝ √T (purely visual speed scale, shown as such). Flash the wall when hit; count wall hits
  per second (rises with T, rises when the box is smaller).
- Sliders: temperature T, volume (box width), particle count.
- Readout: state the model in the `.w-note` ("each drawn particle stands for 10²⁰ molecules; the box is 0.10 m deep"). Show N, V, p from pV = NkT, c_r.m.s. = √(3kT/m) for the chosen gas (He 0.004, N₂ 0.028, O₂ 0.032 kg mol⁻¹, m = M/N_A) and the mean KE (3/2)kT.
- A small histogram of particle speeds under the box, with the r.m.s. speed marked.
- Reduced motion: no animation; draw a frozen snapshot with velocity arrows.
- Probe edge cases `data-t="-5"` (clamp with a message), `data-n="5000"` (clamp to 200).
