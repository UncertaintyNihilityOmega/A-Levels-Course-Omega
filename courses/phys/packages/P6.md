## phys.circular: Motion in a circle
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 4–6 hours` · `Next: [Gravitational fields](#phys.gravity)`
Before you start: [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics) (F = ma), [Circular measure](#math.p1.circular) (radians).
Warm-up: from Dynamics (resultant force and acceleration in the same direction), Kinematics (velocity is a vector),
Units (components of a force).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 12.1.1 define the radian and express angular displacement in radians
- 12.1.2 understand and use the concept of angular speed
- 12.1.3 recall and use ω = 2π/T and v = rω
- 12.2.1 understand that a force of constant magnitude that is always perpendicular to the direction of motion causes centripetal acceleration
- 12.2.2 understand that centripetal acceleration causes circular motion with a constant angular speed
- 12.2.3 recall and use a = rω² and a = v²/r
- 12.2.4 recall and use F = mrω² and F = mv²/r

### Sections (suggested)
1. Radians: definition, 2π rad = 360°, converting; arc length s = rθ. Angular displacement.
2. Angular speed ω (rad s⁻¹), ω = 2π/T = 2πf; v = rω (`:::proof` plain from s = rθ). Examples: the Earth's
   rotation, a hard disk or a washing-machine drum, the second hand of a clock.
3. Why circular motion is accelerated: velocity changes direction at constant speed; acceleration towards the
   centre. `:::proof` (plain, A* insight) of a = v²/r using a velocity vector triangle for a small angle Δθ:
   Δv ≈ vΔθ, so a = vω = v²/r = rω².
4. Centripetal force: F = mv²/r = mrω², always towards the centre; it is **not** a new kind of force but the
   resultant of real forces (tension, friction, gravity, normal contact force, magnetic force). A force of
   constant magnitude perpendicular to the velocity does no work, so the speed is constant.
5. Problems: car rounding a bend on a level road (friction provides F; maximum speed), a mass on a string in a
   horizontal circle on a table, a conical pendulum (T cos θ = mg, T sin θ = mrω²), a bucket or a ball on a string
   in a vertical circle (tension greatest at the bottom: T − mg = mv²/r; least at the top: T + mg = mv²/r; minimum
   speed at the top v = √(gr)), a car over a hump-back bridge (loses contact when v² = gr), banked track (stretch),
   charged particles in magnetic fields (link [Magnetic fields](#phys.magnetic)), satellites (link to the next
   chapter).
6. The "centrifugal force" misconception: when the string breaks the object moves off along the tangent.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Radian: "the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius".
- Angular displacement: "the angle through which an object has moved around a circle, measured from a reference line".
- Angular speed: "the rate of change of angular displacement" (ω = Δθ/Δt).
- Centripetal acceleration: "the acceleration of an object moving in a circle, directed towards the centre".
- Centripetal force: "the resultant force towards the centre of the circle needed for circular motion".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Earth's rotation: ω = 7.27 × 10⁻⁵ rad s⁻¹; speed of a point on the Equator (R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m): 463 m s⁻¹.
- Car of 1200 kg rounding a bend of radius 50 m at 15 m s⁻¹: friction force needed (5400 N).
- Conical pendulum: string 0.80 m at 30° to the vertical: speed and period.
- Ball of 0.20 kg on a 0.60 m string in a vertical circle at 4.0 m s⁻¹ at the bottom: tension at the bottom;
  speed at the top by energy; tension at the top.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define the radian [1]; angular speed of a wheel [2]; explain why an object in uniform
  circular motion accelerates [2]; car on a bend with maximum friction [3]; conical pendulum [5]; vertical circle
  with energy conservation [6]; ★★★ a fairground ride (rotor: friction holds riders against the wall; minimum ω for
  a given coefficient — give the maximum friction force instead of a coefficient, since coefficients are not in the
  syllabus) [6].
- Stretch: show that on a banked track of angle θ with no friction, v² = rg tan θ; or prove a = v²/r from the
  vector triangle.
- Spot the error: drawing "centripetal force" as an extra force on a free-body diagram, or using degrees in v = rω.

### Pitfalls
Degrees in ω; "centrifugal force outwards"; forgetting that weight also acts in a vertical circle; using the
diameter for r; F = mv²/r is the resultant, not one more force.

### Figures
Radian definition (arc = radius). Velocity vectors around a circle with acceleration arrows to the centre
(`c1` velocity, `c3` acceleration). Free-body diagrams: car on a bend (top-down view), conical pendulum,
ball at top and bottom of a vertical circle.

### Board box
All boards: same content. AQA and OCR give the formulas in the same form; some textbooks write ω = v/r first.

### Links (verified)
none needed; optional Physics & Maths Tutor notes.

## phys.gravity: Gravitational fields
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Temperature](#phys.temperature)`
Before you start: [Motion in a circle](#phys.circular), [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy) (ΔE_P = mgΔh).
Warm-up: from Circular (F = mrω²; ω from T), Energy (ΔE_P = mgΔh), Dynamics (weight = mg).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 13.1.1 understand that a gravitational field is an example of a field of force and define gravitational field as force per unit mass
- 13.1.2 represent a gravitational field by means of field lines
- 13.2.1 understand that, for a point outside a uniform sphere, the mass of the sphere may be considered to be a point mass at its centre
- 13.2.2 recall and use Newton's law of gravitation F = Gm₁m₂/r² for the force between two point masses
- 13.2.3 analyse circular orbits in gravitational fields by relating the gravitational force to the centripetal acceleration it causes
- 13.2.4 understand that a satellite in a geostationary orbit remains at the same point above the Earth's surface, with an orbital period of 24 hours, orbiting from west to east, directly above the Equator
- 13.3.1 derive, from Newton's law of gravitation and the definition of gravitational field, the equation g = GM/r² for the gravitational field strength due to a point mass
- 13.3.2 recall and use g = GM/r²
- 13.3.3 understand why g is approximately constant for small changes in height near the Earth's surface
- 13.4.1 define gravitational potential at a point as the work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to the point
- 13.4.2 use φ = −GM/r for the gravitational potential in the field due to a point mass
- 13.4.3 understand how the concept of gravitational potential leads to the gravitational potential energy of two point masses and use E_P = −GMm/r

### Data to use (put the values in the questions, as Cambridge does)
G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻² (data sheet); Earth: M = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m; Moon: M = 7.35 × 10²² kg,
R = 1.74 × 10⁶ m, Earth–Moon distance 3.84 × 10⁸ m; Sun: M = 1.99 × 10³⁰ kg, Earth–Sun distance 1.50 × 10¹¹ m;
geostationary period 24 h = 8.64 × 10⁴ s (the syllabus uses 24 hours).

### Sections (suggested)
1. Fields of force: a region where a mass feels a force; gravitational field strength g = F/m (N kg⁻¹ = m s⁻²).
   Field lines: radial (towards the centre) around a planet, nearly parallel and equally spaced close to the surface
   (uniform field). A comparison of field-line pictures.
2. Newton's law of gravitation: F = Gm₁m₂/r² (r between centres; uniform spheres act as point masses from
   outside); always attractive; inverse-square behaviour (double r → quarter F). Tiny between everyday objects.
3. g = GM/r²: `:::proof examinable` (F = GMm/r² and g = F/m). Graph of g against r: constant-ish near the surface
   (13.3.3: increase of height of a few km is a tiny fraction of 6.37 × 10⁶ m, so r² hardly changes), then 1/r²
   outside (inside the planet not required; mention only if drawn).
4. Orbits: GMm/r² = mv²/r = mrω² → v = √(GM/r), T² = (4π²/GM) r³ (Kepler's third law, `:::proof` plain
   derivation); orbital speed falls as r increases; satellites, the Moon, planets around the Sun; mass of a
   planet from a moon's orbit.
5. Geostationary orbits: all four conditions; calculate the radius (≈ 4.22 × 10⁷ m, height ≈ 3.6 × 10⁴ km);
   uses (communications, weather); low Earth orbits (ISS ≈ 400 km, period ≈ 92 min) for imaging; polar orbits as
   a contrast.
6. Gravitational potential: definition (work done per unit mass bringing a small test mass from infinity); zero
   at infinity, negative everywhere else, because the force is attractive (work is done **by** the field on the way
   in). φ = −GM/r; equipotentials (spheres; no work moving along one). Potential difference and ΔE_P = mΔφ;
   link to mgΔh near the surface.
7. Gravitational potential energy of two masses: E_P = −GMm/r; changes in E_P when a satellite changes orbit;
   escape speed v = √(2GM/R) as an energy application (not in the outcomes; teach as an "A* extra" in an `:::eg`
   or stretch). Field strength = −(gradient of potential), shown on a graph (an insight, not a syllabus outcome
   for gravity; the electric-field version is required in [Electric fields](#phys.efields)).
Embed **phys-orbit** after section 5.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Gravitational field: "a region of space in which a mass experiences a force".
- Gravitational field strength: "the gravitational force per unit mass" (at a point).
- Newton's law of gravitation: "the force between two point masses is proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of their separation".
- Geostationary orbit: "an orbit in which a satellite stays above the same point on the Equator: period 24 hours, moving west to east, in the equatorial plane".
- Gravitational potential (at a point): "the work done per unit mass in bringing a small test mass from infinity to the point".
- Gravitational potential energy (of two point masses): "the work done in bringing the masses from infinite separation to their positions" (E_P = −GMm/r).

### Worked examples and question ideas
- g at the Earth's surface from G, M, R (9.81 N kg⁻¹ — compute and compare); g at the height of the ISS (408 km).
- Force between the Earth and the Moon (1.98 × 10²⁰ N).
- Radius of a geostationary orbit (4.22 × 10⁷ m).
- Mass of Jupiter from Io: r = 4.22 × 10⁸ m, T = 1.77 days (≈ 1.90 × 10²⁷ kg).
- Energy needed to move a 500 kg satellite from the surface to geostationary orbit (ΔE_P only, then with KE).
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define gravitational potential [2]; explain why potential is negative [2]; derive
  g = GM/r² [2]; g at a height [3]; orbital period of a satellite [4]; geostationary conditions and radius [5];
  ★★★ change in total energy of a satellite moving to a higher orbit (E_K = GMm/2r, E_total = −GMm/2r) [6];
  ★★★ point between Earth and Moon where the field is zero (quadratic or ratio of square roots) [5].
- Stretch: escape speed from the Earth (1.12 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹) and why it does not depend on direction; or show that
  for any circular orbit E_K = −½E_P.
- Spot the error: using the height above the surface as r, or giving E_P as positive.

### Pitfalls
r is measured from the centre; potential is a scalar (add with signs), field is a vector; g ≠ 9.81 away from the
surface; mixing 24 h and seconds; "no gravity in orbit" (astronauts are in free fall); E_P = mgh only for small
heights.

### Figures
Radial field lines around a planet and uniform lines near the surface (hand SVG, `c1` lines with arrows towards
the planet). `:::plot` of g against r (1/r² outside the surface, with R marked) and of φ against r (−1/r curve).
Orbit diagram: Earth with a geostationary satellite and the Equator.

### Board box
AQA writes gravitational potential with V (V = −GM/r) and uses g = −ΔV/Δr explicitly; Edexcel IAL and OCR use
the same results. Content-only.

## Widget to build: `phys-orbit` (widgets/phys-orbit.js)
Embed in phys.gravity: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-orbit" data-body="earth" data-r="42200"></div>`.
- Attributes: `data-body="earth|moon|mars|jupiter"` (masses: Earth 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg, R 6.37 × 10⁶ m; Moon
  7.35 × 10²² kg, R 1.74 × 10⁶ m; Mars 6.42 × 10²³ kg, R 3.39 × 10⁶ m; Jupiter 1.90 × 10²⁷ kg, R 6.99 × 10⁷ m),
  `data-r` (orbital radius in km, from 1.05 R to 100 R).
- Controls: body tabs, orbital radius slider (logarithmic: slider over log10 r, show km), satellite mass
  (100–10 000 kg).
- Canvas (square-ish, ≤ 320 px tall): the planet to scale with the orbit; the satellite moving at a rate scaled to
  its real period (all periods sped up by the same factor so the comparison is honest; show the factor); for Earth,
  a marker on the surface rotating once per 24 h (same speed-up) so the student can see the geostationary case
  stay above one point; a "Snap to geostationary" button (Earth only).
- Readout: r, height above surface, orbital speed v = √(GM/r), period T (s and h/days), g at the orbit, potential
  φ, E_K, E_P, total energy; mark "geostationary" when |T − 86400 s| < 1%.
- Second small canvas or a toggle: T² against r³ (straight line through the origin) with the current point.
- Reduced motion: no animation, draw the orbit with the satellite at a fixed position.
