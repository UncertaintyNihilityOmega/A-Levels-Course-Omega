## phys.waves: Waves
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 (AS)` · `@time 7–9 hours` · `Next: [Superposition](#phys.superposition)`
Before you start: [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics) (speed, graphs), [Trigonometry](#math.p1.trig) for sine curves.
The syllabus assumes IGCSE colour (the visible spectrum in order). Warm-up: from Kinematics (speed = distance/time),
Units (convert 650 nm to m), Energy (power = energy/time).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 7.1.1 describe what is meant by wave motion as illustrated by vibration in ropes, springs and ripple tanks
- 7.1.2 understand and use the terms displacement, amplitude, phase difference, period, frequency, wavelength and speed
- 7.1.3 understand the use of the time-base and y-gain of a cathode-ray oscilloscope (CRO) to determine frequency and amplitude
- 7.1.4 derive, using the definitions of speed, frequency and wavelength, the wave equation v = fλ
- 7.1.5 recall and use v = fλ
- 7.1.6 understand that energy is transferred by a progressive wave
- 7.1.7 recall and use intensity = power/area and intensity ∝ (amplitude)² for a progressive wave
- 7.2.1 compare transverse and longitudinal waves
- 7.2.2 analyse and interpret graphical representations of transverse and longitudinal waves
- 7.3.1 understand that when a source of sound waves moves relative to a stationary observer, the observed frequency is different from the source frequency (understanding of the Doppler effect for a stationary source and a moving observer is not required)
- 7.3.2 use the expression f_o = f_s v/(v ± v_s) for the observed frequency when a source of sound waves moves relative to a stationary observer
- 7.4.1 state that all electromagnetic waves are transverse waves that travel with the same speed c in free space
- 7.4.2 recall the approximate range of wavelengths in free space of the principal regions of the electromagnetic spectrum from radio waves to γ-rays
- 7.4.3 recall that wavelengths in the range 400–700 nm in free space are visible to the human eye
- 7.5.1 understand that polarisation is a phenomenon associated with transverse waves
- 7.5.2 recall and use Malus's law (I = I₀cos²θ) to calculate the intensity of a plane-polarised electromagnetic wave after transmission through a polarising filter or a series of polarising filters (calculation of the effect of a polarising filter on the intensity of an unpolarised wave is not required)

### Sections (suggested)
1. What a wave is: a progressive wave transfers energy (not matter) by oscillations; rope, slinky spring,
   ripple tank (straight/circular wavefronts, rays perpendicular to wavefronts).
2. Describing a wave: displacement, amplitude, wavelength, period, frequency, speed, phase difference (in degrees
   and in radians, 360° = 2π rad; points in phase are a whole number of wavelengths apart; antiphase: 180°).
   Two different graphs: displacement–**distance** (snapshot; wavelength on the x-axis) vs displacement–**time**
   (one particle; period on the x-axis). Finding the direction a particle moves next from a snapshot and the
   wave's direction.
3. The wave equation: `:::proof examinable` v = fλ (in one period T the wave travels one wavelength:
   v = λ/T and f = 1/T).
4. The oscilloscope: screen grid, time-base (s cm⁻¹ or ms div⁻¹) gives the period, y-gain (V cm⁻¹) gives the
   amplitude (peak-to-peak ÷ 2). Hand SVG of a CRO trace on a grid with settings given; worked example.
5. Energy and intensity: intensity = power / area (W m⁻²); from a point source spreading in all directions
   I = P/(4πr²) (inverse square); intensity ∝ amplitude². Examples: doubling amplitude → 4× intensity; doubling
   distance from a point source → ¼ intensity → amplitude halves.
6. Transverse vs longitudinal: comparison table (direction of oscillation, examples, compressions/rarefactions,
   can be polarised or not). Graph of a longitudinal wave: displacement–distance graph and where compressions
   lie (where displacement changes from + to −, going along the direction of positive displacement; use a
   figure that shows particle positions above the graph).
7. Doppler effect for sound (moving source, stationary observer): wavefronts bunched ahead, spread behind
   (figure of circles with shifted centres). f_o = f_s v/(v − v_s) approaching, v/(v + v_s) receding.
   Contexts: ambulance siren, racing car, speed guns (qualitative), link to redshift in
   [Astronomy and cosmology](#phys.astro). A `:::proof` (plain) deriving the formula from the shortened
   wavelength is a good A* extra.
8. The electromagnetic spectrum: transverse, same speed c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in free space; table of approximate
   wavelength ranges (orders of magnitude): radio > 10⁻¹ m (up to 10⁴ m and more); microwaves 10⁻³ – 10⁻¹ m;
   infrared 7 × 10⁻⁷ – 10⁻³ m; visible 4 × 10⁻⁷ – 7 × 10⁻⁷ m (400–700 nm, violet to red); ultraviolet
   10⁻⁸ – 4 × 10⁻⁷ m; X-rays 10⁻¹³ – 10⁻⁸ m; γ-rays < 10⁻¹¹ m (X-ray and γ ranges overlap; they differ in origin).
   Give these as approximate, as the syllabus does.
9. Polarisation: only transverse waves can be polarised; plane polarised (oscillations in one plane containing the
   direction of travel); polarising filters; Polaroid sunglasses, LCD screens, microwaves with a metal grille.
   Malus's law I = I₀cos²θ for plane-polarised light through one filter at angle θ to its plane, and through two or
   three filters (the classic: crossed polarisers transmit nothing; insert a third at 45° and light gets through:
   I = I₀ × cos²45° × cos²45° = I₀/4). Note: the syllabus does not require the effect on unpolarised light.
Embed **phys-wave** (mode `progressive`) after section 2, with text saying what to try.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Displacement (of a wave): "the distance of a point on the wave from its equilibrium position, in a stated direction".
- Amplitude: "the maximum displacement from the equilibrium position".
- Wavelength: "the distance between two adjacent points on a wave that are in phase" (e.g. two crests).
- Period: "the time taken for one complete oscillation". Frequency: "the number of oscillations per unit time".
- Wave speed: "the distance travelled by the wave energy (wavefront) per unit time".
- Phase difference: "the fraction of a cycle by which one oscillation leads or lags another, expressed in degrees or radians".
- Progressive wave: "a wave that transfers energy from one place to another".
- Intensity: "the power per unit area incident at right angles to the direction of travel".
- Transverse wave: "oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer". Longitudinal wave: "oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer".
- Doppler effect: "the change in the observed frequency when a source of waves moves relative to the observer".
- Plane polarised: "oscillations in one plane only, containing the direction of travel of the wave".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- CRO: time-base 2.0 ms cm⁻¹, one cycle spans 2.5 cm → T = 5.0 ms, f = 200 Hz; y-gain 0.50 V cm⁻¹, peak-to-peak
  3.0 cm → amplitude 0.75 V.
- Radio station at 98.5 MHz: wavelength (3.05 m). Sound at 340 m s⁻¹ and 256 Hz: wavelength.
- Phase difference between two points 0.30 m apart on a wave of wavelength 1.2 m (90°, π/2 rad).
- Intensity: a 60 W point source at 2.0 m and 4.0 m (assume all power radiated equally; 1.19 W m⁻² and 0.298 W m⁻²).
- Ambulance at 25 m s⁻¹ with a 700 Hz siren, v = 340 m s⁻¹: approaching 756 Hz, receding 652 Hz.
- Malus: I₀ = 8.0 W m⁻² through a filter at 30° (6.0 W m⁻²); three-filter problem.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define wavelength [1]; v = fλ from a CRO trace [4]; compare transverse and
  longitudinal [3]; Doppler from approach and recession frequencies, find the source speed (★★★: two
  equations, eliminate f_s) [5]; intensity and amplitude ratio [3]; Malus's law with two filters [3];
  ★★★ from a displacement–distance and a displacement–time graph of the same wave find v, phase difference and
  the direction of motion of a marked particle [6].
- Stretch: derive the Doppler formula from the wavefront spacing; or energy flow from a ripple source spreading
  in circles (intensity ∝ 1/r, amplitude ∝ 1/√r).
- Spot the error: reading the wavelength off a displacement–time graph (it shows the period), or using
  v + v_s for an approaching source.

### Pitfalls
Confusing the two graphs; amplitude is not peak-to-peak; phase difference in degrees vs radians; "the wave
carries the particles"; intensity ∝ amplitude² (not amplitude); Doppler sign; EM waves "need a medium".

### Figures
Displacement–distance and displacement–time graphs (`:::plot` sine curves with `--xlabel "x / m"` and
`--xlabel "t / ms"`, mark λ and T). A longitudinal wave: a row of particle positions (dots `fi`) with
compressions C and rarefactions R labelled, above its displacement–distance graph. Doppler wavefronts (circles
`ln thin` with shifting centres). CRO screen grid trace. Two polarising filters (simple boxes with line hatching).

### Board box
AQA and OCR treat the Doppler effect for light (Δf/f = v/c) at A Level astrophysics; Cambridge AS covers sound
with a moving source only. Edexcel IAL and AQA include unpolarised light halving through a filter; Cambridge does
not examine it.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `wave-on-a-string`, `waves-intro`, `sound-waves`.

## phys.superposition: Superposition
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 · P3 (AS)` · `@time 7–9 hours` · `Next: [Electricity](#phys.electricity)`
Before you start: [Waves](#phys.waves). Warm-up: from Waves (v = fλ; phase difference of two points; amplitude
from intensity) and Units (small-angle use of tan θ ≈ y/L).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 8.1.1 explain and use the principle of superposition
- 8.1.2 show an understanding of experiments that demonstrate stationary waves using microwaves, stretched strings and air columns (it will be assumed that end corrections are negligible; knowledge of the concept of end corrections is not required)
- 8.1.3 explain the formation of a stationary wave using a graphical method, and identify nodes and antinodes
- 8.1.4 understand how wavelength may be determined from the positions of nodes or antinodes of a stationary wave
- 8.2.1 explain the meaning of the term diffraction
- 8.2.2 show an understanding of experiments that demonstrate diffraction including the qualitative effect of the gap width relative to the wavelength of the wave; for example diffraction of water waves in a ripple tank
- 8.3.1 understand the terms interference and coherence
- 8.3.2 show an understanding of experiments that demonstrate two-source interference using water waves in a ripple tank, sound, light and microwaves
- 8.3.3 understand the conditions required if two-source interference fringes are to be observed
- 8.3.4 recall and use λ = ax/D for double-slit interference using light
- 8.4.1 recall and use d sin θ = nλ
- 8.4.2 describe the use of a diffraction grating to determine the wavelength of light (the structure and use of the spectrometer are not included)

### Sections (suggested)
1. The principle of superposition: displacements add (vector sum) where waves meet; constructive and
   destructive superposition; path difference and phase difference.
2. Stationary waves: two waves of the same frequency (and amplitude) travelling in opposite directions; graphical
   method: draw the two waves at t = 0, T/8, T/4 and add them (a 4-panel figure, or the widget's stationary mode).
   Nodes (zero amplitude) and antinodes (maximum); node-to-node λ/2. Comparison table stationary vs progressive
   (energy transfer, amplitude along the wave, phase between points: same phase within a loop, antiphase in
   adjacent loops).
3. Stationary wave experiments: stretched string with a vibration generator and pulley (harmonics:
   L = nλ/2; changing frequency or tension); microwaves reflected from a metal plate, detector moved along the
   line (adjacent nodes λ/2 apart, so λ = 2 × node spacing; count several nodes); air columns: tube closed at one
   end (node at closed end, antinode at open end: L = λ/4, 3λ/4, …), open at both ends (L = λ/2, λ, …); resonance
   tube with a tuning fork to find the speed of sound (two successive resonance lengths differ by λ/2, which
   removes the need for end corrections). Embed **phys-wave** with `data-mode="stationary"`.
4. Diffraction: spreading through a gap or around an obstacle; most when gap ≈ wavelength; ripple tank figure
   for narrow and wide gaps (wavelength unchanged). Why you hear round corners but do not see round them; radio
   long waves round hills.
5. Interference and coherence: definitions; conditions for observable two-source fringes (coherent sources,
   similar amplitudes, (for light) same polarisation, sources close together); constructive where path
   difference = nλ, destructive where (n + ½)λ (for sources in phase). Experiments: ripple tank with two dippers;
   two loudspeakers driven by one signal generator (walk across: loud/quiet); microwaves through a double slit;
   light: Young's double slit with a laser.
6. Double-slit formula λ = ax/D (a = slit separation, x = fringe separation, D = slit-to-screen distance, valid
   when D ≫ a). `:::proof` (plain, A* insight): path difference a sin θ ≈ a x/D for small angles. Measuring x
   across several fringes; effect of changing a, D, λ; white light fringes (central white, coloured edges).
   Embed **phys-fringes** (mode `double`).
7. Diffraction grating d sin θ = nλ (d = 1/N, N lines per metre); `:::proof` (plain) from path difference between
   adjacent slits; maximum order n ≤ d/λ (round down); sharper, brighter maxima than double slit; measuring λ:
   laser through a grating, measure the distance between the two first-order spots and the grating–screen
   distance, θ = tan⁻¹(y/L), use higher orders for precision; white light spectra (violet deviated least).
   Embed **phys-fringes** with `data-mode="grating"`.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Principle of superposition: "when two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement is the sum of the displacements of the individual waves".
- Stationary wave; node ("a point of zero amplitude"); antinode ("a point of maximum amplitude").
- Diffraction: "the spreading of a wave as it passes through a gap or around an edge".
- Interference: "the superposition of two or more waves to give a resultant wave whose amplitude depends on the phase difference".
- Coherence: "two sources are coherent if they have a constant phase difference" (hence the same frequency).
- Path difference: "the difference in distance travelled by two waves from their sources to a point".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- String 1.20 m long, third harmonic at 150 Hz: wavelength 0.80 m, speed 120 m s⁻¹.
- Microwaves: distance between 5 successive nodes is 5.6 cm → λ = 2.8 cm, f ≈ 1.07 × 10¹⁰ Hz.
- Resonance tube: successive resonances at 0.163 m and 0.493 m with a 512 Hz fork: λ = 0.660 m, v = 338 m s⁻¹.
- Double slit: a = 0.50 mm, D = 2.0 m, 10 fringe spacings span 24 mm: λ = 600 nm.
- Grating 300 lines per mm and 589 nm: angles of orders 1 and 2 (10.2°, 20.7°), highest order (5).
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define coherence [1]; explain how a stationary wave forms [3]; nodes/antinodes in a
  closed pipe, find f of the first two harmonics [4]; double-slit with changes (halve a, what happens to x?) [3];
  two loudspeakers: path difference at a point and whether loud or quiet [4]; grating highest order and angle [4];
  ★★★ describe determining λ with a grating, with a sample calculation and the main uncertainty [6]; ★★★ two
  wavelengths through one grating: find where orders overlap [5].
- Stretch: two loudspeakers 1.5 m apart in phase, listener moving parallel: find the positions of the first
  minima using exact path differences (no small-angle approximation) and compare with λD/a; or a grating with
  white light: show that the second- and third-order spectra overlap.
- Spot the error: using node-to-node distance as λ (it is λ/2), or d in lines per mm instead of the slit spacing.

### Pitfalls
Interference needs coherence, not just "two sources"; λ = ax/D only for small angles; a vs d confusion; the
number of orders includes both sides and the zero order (2n_max + 1 maxima); diffraction does not change
wavelength or frequency; end corrections are ignored (say so).

### Figures
Graphical formation of a stationary wave (hand SVG, two waves `c1` `c2`, resultant `c3`, nodes N and antinodes A
labelled; or several `:::plot` panels). String harmonics (first three) in one figure. Closed-pipe air column
with displacement envelope. Ripple-tank diffraction (narrow vs wide gap). Double-slit geometry with a, D, x.
Grating geometry with θ and the orders.

### Board box
AQA and OCR use the same equations (AQA writes the double-slit equation as w = λD/s). AQA includes the speed
of a wave on a string formula as well; Cambridge does not need it.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `wave-interference`, `wave-on-a-string`.

## Widget to build: `phys-wave` (widgets/phys-wave.js)
Embeds: phys.waves `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-wave" data-mode="progressive" data-a="2" data-lambda="4" data-f="0.5"></div>`;
phys.superposition `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-wave" data-mode="stationary" data-n="3"></div>`.
- Attributes: `data-mode="progressive|stationary"` (default progressive), `data-a` (amplitude, cm, 0.5–5),
  `data-lambda` (m, 0.5–10), `data-f` (Hz, 0.1–2, slow enough to watch), `data-n` (harmonic 1–6),
  `data-view="transverse|longitudinal"`.
- **Progressive**: canvas shows the displacement–distance curve (`sc`) moving right; a toggle shows the same wave
  as a longitudinal row of particles (dots displaced along x, compressions visible) under the curve; two marked
  particles P and Q (draggable along x, `W.drag`) with their phase difference shown in degrees and radians; a
  second small canvas shows the displacement–time graph of particle P. Readout: v = fλ, T = 1/f, phase difference.
- **Stationary**: string fixed at both ends; harmonic slider n; shows the two travelling components (faint `sc`
  and `sc2`, toggleable) and their sum (`red`/`ink`), nodes N and antinodes A labelled; readout L = nλ/2.
- Play/pause button; `W.animate`; under reduced motion show a still frame plus a "Step" button that advances
  T/8 at a time.
- `.w-note` explaining what to try. Probe edge cases (`data-n="9"`, `data-mode="x"`).

## Widget to build: `phys-fringes` (widgets/phys-fringes.js)
Embeds: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-fringes" data-mode="double" data-a="0.5" data-d="2.0" data-lambda="600"></div>`
and `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-fringes" data-mode="grating" data-n="300" data-lambda="589"></div>`.
- **Double**: sliders slit separation a (0.1–1.0 mm), slit–screen distance D (0.5–4 m), wavelength λ (400–700 nm,
  the screen colour follows the wavelength via a simple wavelength-to-RGB mapping; text label always shown so it
  never relies on colour alone). Canvas: an intensity graph across the screen (cos² fringes under a single-slit
  envelope is optional) and a strip showing the fringe pattern; fringe spacing x marked. Readout: x = λD/a in mm.
- **Grating**: lines per mm (100–1000), λ; a top-view diagram with the zero order and the orders at angles θₙ
  (rays drawn to a semicircular screen); readout: d, θ for each order, n_max = floor(d/λ). A "white light" toggle
  draws each order as a spectrum band (400–700 nm) to show overlap of orders 2 and 3.
- Probe: both modes, plus `data-lambda="1200"` (clamp to 700 with a note).
