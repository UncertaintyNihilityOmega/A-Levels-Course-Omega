## phys.oscillations: Oscillations
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Electric fields](#phys.efields)`
Before you start: [Motion in a circle](#phys.circular) (ω, radians), [Waves](#phys.waves) (period, frequency,
phase difference), [Trigonometry](#math.p1.trig). Warm-up: from Circular (ω = 2π/T), Waves (phase difference
of points λ/4 apart), Solids (½kx²).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 17.1.1 understand and use the terms displacement, amplitude, period, frequency, angular frequency and phase difference in the context of oscillations, and express the period in terms of both frequency and angular frequency
- 17.1.2 understand that simple harmonic motion occurs when acceleration is proportional to displacement from a fixed point and in the opposite direction
- 17.1.3 use a = −ω²x and recall and use, as a solution to this equation, x = x₀ sin ωt
- 17.1.4 use the equations v = v₀ cos ωt and v = ±ω√(x₀² − x²)
- 17.1.5 analyse and interpret graphical representations of the variations of displacement, velocity and acceleration for simple harmonic motion
- 17.2.1 describe the interchange between kinetic and potential energy during simple harmonic motion
- 17.2.2 recall and use E = ½mω²x₀² for the total energy of a system undergoing simple harmonic motion
- 17.3.1 understand that a resistive force acting on an oscillating system causes damping
- 17.3.2 understand and use the terms light, critical and heavy damping and sketch displacement–time graphs illustrating these types of damping
- 17.3.3 understand that resonance involves a maximum amplitude of oscillations and that this occurs when an oscillating system is forced to oscillate at its natural frequency

### Data sheet (Paper 4 page 3)
a = −ω²x · v = v₀ cos ωt · v = ±ω√(x₀² − x²). (x = x₀ sin ωt and E = ½mω²x₀² must be recalled.)

### Sections (suggested)
1. Oscillations around us (pendulum, mass on a spring, guitar string, atoms in a solid, a boat bobbing); free
   oscillation; equilibrium position; the terms with ω = 2πf = 2π/T; phase difference between two oscillators
   (in radians, from a time offset: Δφ = 2πΔt/T).
2. Simple harmonic motion: definition (a ∝ −x); the defining equation a = −ω²x; the restoring force. Show that a
   mass on a spring (F = −kx ⇒ a = −(k/m)x, ω² = k/m) and a small-angle pendulum (a ≈ −(g/l)x) are SHM; the
   periods T = 2π√(m/k) and T = 2π√(l/g) are **not** syllabus formulas: they appear in questions as given
   equations, so treat them as applications.
3. Solutions: x = x₀ sin ωt (starting at equilibrium) or x = x₀ cos ωt (starting at maximum displacement; say
   which one fits the start condition), v = v₀ cos ωt with v₀ = ωx₀, v = ±ω√(x₀² − x²) (`:::proof` plain from
   sin² + cos² = 1), a_max = ω²x₀. Note that ωt is in radians: set the calculator to radians.
4. Graphs: x–t, v–t, a–t stacked with a shared time axis (v leads x by π/2, a is in antiphase with x); a–x (a
   straight line through the origin with negative gradient −ω²); v–x (an ellipse). Reading ω from the gradient of
   an a–x graph.
5. Energy: KE = ½mω²(x₀² − x²), PE = ½mω²x² (for a spring-type system), total E = ½mω²x₀² constant; graphs of
   KE, PE and total against x (two parabolas and a line) and against t (KE and PE oscillate at twice the
   frequency). Embed **phys-shm** (mode `shm`).
6. Damping: resistive forces remove energy, amplitude decreases (light damping: roughly exponential decay with the
   period almost unchanged); light, critical (returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without oscillating) and
   heavy (returns slowly, no oscillation) damping, sketched on one x–t figure. Uses: car suspension (critical),
   door closers, measuring instruments.
7. Forced oscillations and resonance: driving frequency vs natural frequency; amplitude–driving-frequency graph,
   peak at the natural frequency; with more damping the peak is lower, broader and (slightly) at a lower frequency.
   Useful resonance: pushing a child on a swing, musical instruments (an air column or a string driven at its
   natural frequency), tuning a radio circuit. Harmful resonance: a bridge driven by wind or footsteps, an engine
   part vibrating at certain speeds, buildings in earthquakes; prevented by damping or by changing the natural
   frequency. Keep every claim simple and correct (no folk stories). Embed **phys-shm** (mode `resonance`).

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Displacement (of an oscillator): "the distance from the equilibrium position in a stated direction".
- Amplitude: "the maximum displacement from the equilibrium position".
- Period: "the time for one complete oscillation". Frequency: "the number of oscillations per unit time".
- Angular frequency: ω = 2πf, "2π times the frequency" (rad s⁻¹).
- Simple harmonic motion: "motion in which the acceleration is proportional to the displacement from a fixed point and is always directed towards that point (in the opposite direction to the displacement)".
- Damping: "the loss of energy from an oscillating system, caused by resistive forces, so that the amplitude decreases".
- Critical damping: "damping in which the system returns to its equilibrium position in the shortest possible time without oscillating".
- Natural frequency: "the frequency at which a system oscillates when it is displaced and then left to oscillate freely".
- Forced oscillation; resonance: "the large increase in amplitude of a forced oscillation when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Mass on a spring: amplitude 4.0 cm, period 0.50 s: ω = 12.6 rad s⁻¹, v_max = 0.503 m s⁻¹, a_max = 6.32 m s⁻²;
  speed at x = 2.0 cm; time to go from x = 0 to x = 2.0 cm (t = T/12).
- Energy: 0.20 kg mass, ω = 10 rad s⁻¹, x₀ = 0.050 m: E = 0.025 J; KE at x = 0.030 m.
- Reading ω from the gradient of an a–x graph; finding x₀ and T from an x–t graph.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define SHM [2]; use a = −ω²x from a graph [3]; v at a given x [3]; sketch v–t from
  x–t [3]; energy at a displacement [3]; sketch light/critical/heavy damping [3]; resonance curve with more
  damping [3]; ★★★ a loaded test tube bobbing in water: show the motion is SHM from the upthrust change (link
  [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces)) and find T [6].
- Stretch: show that the pendulum is SHM only for small angles (sin θ ≈ θ), and estimate the error at 20°;
  or two springs in series vs parallel: compare periods.
- Spot the error: calculator in degrees for x = x₀ sin ωt, or v = ωx instead of ω√(x₀² − x²).

### Pitfalls
Degrees vs radians; ω vs f; x₀ is amplitude not peak-to-peak; KE max at equilibrium; damping changes amplitude
not (much) the period; "resonance is when the frequency is high".

### Figures
`:::plot` panels for x, v, a against t (sin, cos, −sin, with --xlabel "t / s"); KE/PE against x (two parabolas
and a line); a damped x–t trio (hand SVG or `:::plot` with exp·cos functions); amplitude against driving frequency
for light and heavier damping (`:::plot` with two `--f` curves of the form 1/√((1−x²)² + (bx)²)).

### Board box
AQA and OCR give T = 2π√(m/k) and T = 2π√(l/g) as required equations and include the derivation of the
pendulum's SHM; Cambridge gives them in questions when needed.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `pendulum-lab`, `masses-and-springs`.

## phys.efields: Electric fields
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Capacitance](#phys.capacitance)`
Before you start: [Gravitational fields](#phys.gravity) (the same field ideas), [Electricity](#phys.electricity)
(charge, p.d.), [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics) (projectiles). Warm-up: from Gravity (g = GM/r²; potential is
work per unit mass from infinity), Kinematics (horizontal projectile), Electricity (V = W/Q).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 18.1.1 understand that an electric field is an example of a field of force and define electric field as force per unit positive charge
- 18.1.2 recall and use F = qE for the force on a charge in an electric field
- 18.1.3 represent an electric field by means of field lines
- 18.2.1 recall and use E = ΔV/Δd to calculate the field strength of the uniform field between charged parallel plates
- 18.2.2 describe the effect of a uniform electric field on the motion of charged particles
- 18.3.1 understand that, for a point outside a spherical conductor, the charge on the sphere may be considered to be a point charge at its centre
- 18.3.2 recall and use Coulomb's law F = Q₁Q₂/(4πε₀r²) for the force between two point charges in free space
- 18.4.1 recall and use E = Q/(4πε₀r²) for the electric field strength due to a point charge in free space
- 18.5.1 define electric potential at a point as the work done per unit positive charge in bringing a small test charge from infinity to the point
- 18.5.2 recall and use the fact that the electric field at a point is equal to the negative of potential gradient at that point
- 18.5.3 use V = Q/(4πε₀r) for the electric potential in the field due to a point charge
- 18.5.4 understand how the concept of electric potential leads to the electric potential energy of two point charges and use E_P = Qq/(4πε₀r)

### Data
ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ m F⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, m_e = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg,
m_p = 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (data sheet). V = Q/(4πε₀r) and E_P = Qq/(4πε₀r) are on the Paper 4 formula page.

### Sections (suggested)
1. Electric fields: a region where a charge feels a force; E = F/q (N C⁻¹ = V m⁻¹, show the equivalence);
   direction = force on a positive charge. Field lines: radial out of +, into −; between a + and − point charge;
   between parallel plates (uniform, with edge effects); lines never cross; closer lines = stronger field.
2. Uniform field between parallel plates: E = ΔV/Δd (`:::proof` plain: work done moving charge q across the gap,
   qV = Fd = qEd). Example: 2.0 kV across 5.0 mm (4.0 × 10⁵ V m⁻¹).
3. Charged particles in a uniform field: along the field (accelerated; gain KE = qV: an electron gun, link the
   electronvolt in [Quantum physics](#phys.quantum)); across the field (constant horizontal velocity, constant
   vertical acceleration a = qE/m: a parabola, exactly like a projectile; deflection of an electron beam,
   inkjet printer, particle detectors). Gravity is negligible for electrons and protons (compare qE with mg).
4. Coulomb's law F = Q₁Q₂/(4πε₀r²); like charges repel, unlike attract; a charged conducting sphere acts as a
   point charge at its centre (from outside). Comparison with gravitation (both inverse square; electric force
   can repel; electric ≫ gravitational between particles: e.g. two protons, ratio ≈ 10³⁶).
5. Field of a point charge E = Q/(4πε₀r²); field from two charges (vector sum; point of zero field between two
   like charges).
6. Electric potential: definition; V = Q/(4πε₀r) (positive near a positive charge, negative near a negative one;
   zero at infinity); potential is a scalar (add with signs); equipotentials (perpendicular to field lines; no
   work along them). Field = −potential gradient, E = −dV/dr; graphs of E and V against r for a charged sphere
   (constant V inside the conductor and E = 0 inside, then 1/r and 1/r² outside); E from the gradient of a V–r
   graph (tangent).
7. Electric potential energy of two point charges E_P = Qq/(4πε₀r) (positive for like charges); energy changes
   when charges move; closest approach of an α particle to a nucleus (link [Particle physics](#phys.particles)).
8. A comparison table: gravitational vs electric fields (definitions, equations, signs, attractive/repulsive).

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Electric field: "a region of space in which a charge experiences a force".
- Electric field strength: "the force per unit positive charge (acting on a small stationary test charge)".
- Coulomb's law: "the force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of their separation".
- Electric potential (at a point): "the work done per unit positive charge in bringing a small test charge from infinity to the point".
- Equipotential: "a surface on which every point has the same potential".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Electron between plates 2.0 cm apart with 500 V: force, acceleration (≈ 4.4 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻²), and a horizontal
  beam entering at 3.0 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ through plates 5.0 cm long: vertical deflection on exit.
- Force between two 2.0 nC charges 30 mm apart (4.0 × 10⁻⁵ N).
- Field and potential 0.10 m from a sphere carrying 5.0 nC; the potential at the surface of a Van de Graaff dome.
- The point between charges +4Q and +Q where the field is zero: its distances from them are in the ratio 2 : 1.
- Speed of a proton accelerated from rest through 2.0 kV.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define electric field strength [1]; field lines for two charges [3]; E between
  plates and the force on an oil drop that is held still (qE = mg) [4]; Coulomb's law [3]; explain why the potential
  near a negative charge is negative [2]; E from a V–x graph gradient [3]; ★★★ electron deflection between plates
  (projectile analogy) [7]; ★★★ closest approach of an α particle to a gold nucleus (energy) [5].
- Stretch: the potential at a point due to two charges and the work to bring a third charge there; or an electron
  in a uniform field entering at an angle (full projectile analysis).
- Spot the error: adding potentials as vectors or fields as scalars; using r from the surface.

### Pitfalls
Potential vs potential energy; E is a vector (directions when adding), V is a scalar; units N C⁻¹ = V m⁻¹;
r from centre; forgetting that the electron's charge is negative (force opposite to E).

### Figures
Field-line patterns: isolated + and − charges, a + and − pair (dipole), two + charges, parallel plates with edge
effects (hand SVGs). Electron path between plates (parabola, `c1`). `:::plot` of V and E against r outside a
charged sphere.

### Board box
AQA and OCR use the same Coulomb law and potential definitions; AQA writes k = 1/(4πε₀) and also covers
equipotential sketches in more detail. Content-only.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `charges-and-fields`, `coulombs-law`.

## Widget to build: `phys-shm` (widgets/phys-shm.js)
Embeds in phys.oscillations: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-shm" data-mode="shm" data-x0="0.05" data-f="0.5"></div>`
and `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-shm" data-mode="resonance"></div>`.
- **SHM mode**: a mass on a vertical spring animated beside three stacked graphs (x–t, v–t, a–t) drawn as time
  advances, with a moving cursor; sliders amplitude x₀ (0.01–0.10 m), frequency f (0.2–2 Hz), mass (0.1–2 kg),
  damping (none, light, critical, heavy: tabs or a slider b with labelled regions); energy bars (KE, PE, total)
  beside the spring; readout ω, T, v_max = ωx₀, a_max = ω²x₀, E = ½mω²x₀².
- **Resonance mode**: a driven oscillator: driving-frequency slider (0.1–3 × natural frequency), damping slider;
  canvas shows the steady-state amplitude curve A(f) (for two or three damping values faintly, current one bold)
  with a dot at the current driving frequency, and a small animation of the oscillator at that frequency.
  Amplitude model: A = F₀/m / √((ω₀² − ω²)² + (bω)²).
- Play/pause; `W.animate`; reduced motion: no animation, static graphs.
- Probe `data-mode="shm"`, `data-mode="resonance"`, and `data-f="0"` (clamp).
