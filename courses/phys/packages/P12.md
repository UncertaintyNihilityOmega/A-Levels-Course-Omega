## phys.medical: Medical physics
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Astronomy and cosmology](#phys.astro)`
Before you start: [Waves](#phys.waves) (intensity, speed), [Quantum physics](#phys.quantum) (photons, eV),
[Nuclear physics](#phys.nuclear) (β⁺ decay, exponential decay), [Particle physics](#phys.particles) (positrons).
Warm-up: from Waves (v = fλ for 3.0 MHz in tissue), Quantum (photon energy of 0.10 nm X-rays), Nuclear (fraction left
after two half-lives).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 24.1.1 understand that a piezo-electric crystal changes shape when a p.d. is applied across it and that the crystal generates an e.m.f. when its shape changes
- 24.1.2 understand how ultrasound waves are generated and detected by a piezoelectric transducer
- 24.1.3 understand how the reflection of pulses of ultrasound at boundaries between tissues can be used to obtain diagnostic information about internal structures
- 24.1.4 define the specific acoustic impedance of a medium as Z = ρc, where c is the speed of sound in the medium
- 24.1.5 use I_R/I₀ = (Z₁ − Z₂)²/(Z₁ + Z₂)² for the intensity reflection coefficient of a boundary between two media
- 24.1.6 recall and use I = I₀e^(−μx) for the attenuation of ultrasound in matter
- 24.2.1 explain that X-rays are produced by electron bombardment of a metal target and calculate the minimum wavelength of X-rays produced from the accelerating p.d.
- 24.2.2 understand the use of X-rays in imaging internal body structures, including an understanding of the term contrast in X-ray imaging
- 24.2.3 recall and use I = I₀e^(−μx) for the attenuation of X-rays in matter
- 24.2.4 understand that computed tomography (CT) scanning produces a 3D image of an internal structure by first combining multiple X-ray images taken in the same section from different angles to obtain a 2D image of the section, then repeating this process along an axis and combining 2D images of multiple sections
- 24.3.1 understand that a tracer is a substance containing radioactive nuclei that can be introduced into the body and is then absorbed by the tissue being studied
- 24.3.2 recall that a tracer that decays by β⁺ decay is used in positron emission tomography (PET scanning)
- 24.3.3 understand that annihilation occurs when a particle interacts with its antiparticle and that mass–energy and momentum are conserved in the process
- 24.3.4 explain that, in PET scanning, positrons emitted by the decay of the tracer annihilate when they interact with electrons in the tissue, producing a pair of gamma-ray photons travelling in opposite directions
- 24.3.5 calculate the energy of the gamma-ray photons emitted during the annihilation of an electron-positron pair
- 24.3.6 understand that the gamma-ray photons from an annihilation event travel outside the body and can be detected, and an image of the tracer concentration in the tissue can be created by processing the arrival times of the gamma-ray photons

### Data (give typical values in questions, labelled typical)
Speed of sound: soft tissue ≈ 1540 m s⁻¹, air 330 m s⁻¹, bone ≈ 4000 m s⁻¹; densities: soft tissue ≈ 1060 kg m⁻³,
air 1.3 kg m⁻³, bone ≈ 1900 kg m⁻³. m_e = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, c, e, h from the data sheet: the annihilation photon energy
from data-sheet values is m_e c² = 8.20 × 10⁻¹⁴ J = 0.512 MeV (the precise value is 0.511 MeV; use the data-sheet
result in calculations and mention the precise value once).

### Sections (suggested)
1. Ultrasound: frequencies above 20 kHz (medical: 1–15 MHz); the piezoelectric effect (both directions); the
   transducer: an a.c. p.d. at the crystal's resonant frequency makes it vibrate (generation, short pulses with damping
   backing) and returning echoes make it vibrate and produce an e.m.f. (detection): the same crystal does both.
2. Pulse-echo imaging: time of echo → depth (d = vt/2); A-scan (amplitude against time, one line) and B-scan
   (many A-scans from a moving/array probe → 2D image) described simply; why high frequency gives better resolution
   but more attenuation; safety (non-ionising) and uses (pregnancy, heart, soft tissue).
3. Acoustic impedance: Z = ρc (kg m⁻² s⁻¹); reflection coefficient I_R/I₀ = (Z₁ − Z₂)²/(Z₁ + Z₂)²; big mismatch
   (air–skin) reflects almost everything: hence the coupling gel; bone–tissue reflects a lot (shadows behind bone).
4. Attenuation: I = I₀e^(−μx) (μ the attenuation coefficient, m⁻¹ or cm⁻¹); combine with reflection for an echo's
   intensity (there and back). Half-value thickness x½ = ln 2/μ (A* extra).
5. X-ray production: thermionic emission from a heated filament, electrons accelerated through p.d. V (tens of kV)
   to a tungsten target (high melting point; rotating anode; oil cooling; ≈ 1% of the energy becomes X-rays, the rest
   heat); spectrum (continuous braking radiation plus characteristic lines) described; minimum wavelength when one
   electron's whole energy eV becomes one photon: λ_min = hc/(eV) (`:::proof` plain). Effect of increasing V
   (shorter λ_min, more penetrating) and the tube current (more photons, same λ_min).
6. X-ray imaging: attenuation differs between tissues (bone absorbs much more than soft tissue): the image is a
   shadow; contrast (difference in intensity/brightness between regions) and how to improve it (contrast media such
   as barium or iodine with high attenuation, suitable kV); I = I₀e^(−μx) for X-rays; comparing transmitted
   intensities through bone and tissue paths.
7. CT scanning: the X-ray tube and detectors rotate around the patient; many images of one thin section (slice) from
   different angles are combined by computer into a 2D image of that section; the patient moves along the axis and
   the process repeats; the 2D sections are combined into a 3D image. Advantages (3D, soft tissue detail) and the
   higher dose.
8. PET scanning: tracer (e.g. fluorine-18 in a glucose-like molecule, absorbed by active tissue, link the β⁺ equation
   in [Particle physics](#phys.particles)); β⁺ decay; positron travels about a millimetre, annihilates with an
   electron: mass–energy and momentum conserved, so two γ photons of equal energy in opposite directions (total
   momentum zero); photon energy = m_e c² each (when the kinetic energies are negligible); a ring of detectors; the
   difference in arrival times of a pair locates the annihilation along the line between the detectors; many events
   build an image of tracer concentration. Short half-lives (F-18 ≈ 110 min) and why.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Piezoelectric effect (as in 24.1.1).
- Specific acoustic impedance: "the product of the density of a medium and the speed of sound in it" (Z = ρc).
- Intensity reflection coefficient: "the ratio of the reflected intensity to the incident intensity at a boundary".
- Attenuation (coefficient μ): "the gradual loss of intensity as a beam passes through a medium"; I = I₀e^(−μx).
- Contrast (X-ray imaging): "the difference in the degree of blackening (brightness) between different regions of the image".
- Tracer; annihilation ("the interaction of a particle with its antiparticle in which their total mass is converted into energy, as photons").

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Echo after 65 μs in soft tissue (1540 m s⁻¹): depth 5.0 cm.
- Z for soft tissue and bone with typical values; reflection coefficient at tissue–bone and air–tissue boundaries
  (≈ 1, explain the gel).
- Ultrasound through 4.0 cm of tissue with μ = 23 m⁻¹ (there and back: x = 8.0 cm): fraction remaining.
- X-ray tube at 80 kV: λ_min (1.55 × 10⁻¹¹ m with data-sheet values); max photon energy 80 keV.
- X-rays through 3.0 cm of bone (μ = 600 m⁻¹) vs 3.0 cm of tissue (μ = 20 m⁻¹ … give typical values): ratio of
  transmitted intensities → contrast.
- PET: energy and wavelength of each annihilation photon from data-sheet values; why they travel in opposite directions.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define acoustic impedance [1]; explain how the transducer produces and detects
  ultrasound [4]; reflection coefficient and the need for gel [4]; λ_min of X-rays [3]; explain contrast and one way to
  improve it [3]; CT process [4]; PET annihilation energy with momentum argument [5]; ★★★ combined ultrasound
  question: echo intensity after reflection and attenuation there and back [6].
- Stretch: compare the dose and information of CT and PET for a given diagnosis (qualitative), or find the
  thickness of tissue that halves an X-ray beam and the thickness of lead that does the same.
- Spot the error: using d = vt (forgetting the echo travels there and back), or using one photon's energy as 2m_ec².

### Pitfalls
Time of the echo covers twice the depth; Z units; reflection coefficient is for intensity, not amplitude; λ_min uses eV
of the accelerating p.d.; annihilation gives two photons each m_e c²; μ units consistent with x.

## phys.astro: Astronomy and cosmology
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 5–7 hours` · `Next: [Practical skills: measurement, tables, graphs and uncertainty](#phys.practical)`
Before you start: [Waves](#phys.waves) (Doppler effect, EM spectrum), [Quantum physics](#phys.quantum) (line spectra),
[Temperature](#phys.temperature) (kelvin). Warm-up: from Waves (intensity = power/area), Quantum (absorption lines),
Gravity (orbits) or Waves (Doppler).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 25.1.1 understand the term luminosity as the total power of radiation emitted by a star
- 25.1.2 recall and use the inverse square law for radiant flux intensity F in terms of the luminosity L of the source F = L/(4πd²)
- 25.1.3 understand that an object of known luminosity is called a standard candle
- 25.1.4 understand the use of standard candles to determine distances to galaxies
- 25.2.1 recall and use Wien's displacement law λmax ∝ 1/T to estimate the peak surface temperature of a star
- 25.2.2 use the Stefan–Boltzmann law L = 4πσr²T⁴
- 25.2.3 use Wien's displacement law and the Stefan–Boltzmann law to estimate the radius of a star
- 25.3.1 understand that the lines in the emission and absorption spectra from distant objects show an increase in wavelength from their known values
- 25.3.2 use Δλ/λ ≈ Δf/f ≈ v/c for the redshift of electromagnetic radiation from a source moving relative to an observer
- 25.3.3 explain why redshift leads to the idea that the Universe is expanding
- 25.3.4 recall and use Hubble's law v ≈ H₀d and explain how this leads to the Big Bang theory (candidates will only be required to use SI units)

### Data (give in questions; label typical)
σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (data sheet). Wien constant 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K (not on the data sheet: questions give it or give
a reference star). Sun: L = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W, r = 6.96 × 10⁸ m, surface temperature ≈ 5800 K (5778 K), λmax ≈ 500 nm;
Earth–Sun distance 1.50 × 10¹¹ m (the solar constant ≈ 1.35 × 10³ W m⁻² follows from these); light-year
9.46 × 10¹⁵ m; parsec 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m (not required; may appear in context); H₀ ≈ 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ (≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹;
SI only in calculations, as the syllabus says).

### Sections (suggested)
1. Luminosity (W); radiant flux intensity F = L/(4πd²) (W m⁻²), the inverse square law with a sphere figure; the
   solar constant from the Sun's luminosity (≈ 1.35 kW m⁻² from the data above).
2. Standard candles: definition; examples (Cepheid variable stars, whose period tells their luminosity; type Ia
   supernovae, which all peak at about the same luminosity) — describe each in one or two sentences, no extra theory;
   measure F, know L, find d = √(L/(4πF)); the distance ladder idea.
3. Stellar temperatures: black-body radiation curves (intensity against wavelength) for three temperatures;
   Wien's law λmax T = constant; colour of stars (red cool, blue hot).
4. Stefan–Boltzmann law L = 4πσr²T⁴ (surface area × σT⁴); estimating radius: T from Wien, L from F and d, then r.
   Compare stars by ratios (twice the temperature → 16 times the power per unit area).
5. Redshift: spectral lines from distant galaxies are shifted to longer wavelengths; Δλ/λ ≈ Δf/f ≈ v/c (valid for
   v ≪ c); a receding source; link to the Doppler effect for sound in [Waves](#phys.waves).
6. Hubble's law v ≈ H₀d: nearly all galaxies recede, faster the further away: the Universe is expanding (space
   itself; no centre). Running the expansion backwards → the Universe began in a hot, dense state: the Big Bang;
   age ≈ 1/H₀ (≈ 4.3 × 10¹⁷ s ≈ 14 billion years, as an estimate assuming constant expansion). Mention the cosmic
   microwave background as further evidence in one sentence (not a syllabus outcome).
Embed **phys-star** in section 4.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Luminosity: "the total power of electromagnetic radiation emitted by a star".
- Radiant flux intensity: "the radiant power passing normally through unit area" (W m⁻²).
- Standard candle: "an astronomical object of known luminosity".
- Wien's displacement law: "the wavelength at which the intensity of radiation from a black body is maximum is inversely proportional to its thermodynamic temperature".
- Stefan–Boltzmann law: as L = 4πσr²T⁴ for a star (spherical black body).
- Redshift: "the increase in the observed wavelength of radiation from a source moving away from the observer".
- Hubble's law: "the recession speed of a galaxy is (approximately) proportional to its distance".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Solar constant from L and d (1.35 × 10³ W m⁻² with the data above; compute exactly in checks).
- A Cepheid of luminosity 3.0 × 10³⁰ W observed at 1.2 × 10⁻¹² W m⁻²: distance.
- Star with λmax = 290 nm (T ≈ 1.0 × 10⁴ K) and L = 4.0 × 10²⁸ W: radius.
- Hydrogen line 656.3 nm observed at 663.0 nm: v (3.06 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹), then d from H₀.
- Age of the Universe from H₀.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define luminosity [1]; standard candle and its use [3]; inverse square law [2]; Wien
  temperature [2]; Stefan–Boltzmann ratio of radii of two stars [4]; redshift speed [3]; explain how redshift data
  lead to the Big Bang [4]; ★★★ full radius estimate from F, d and λmax [6].
- Stretch: compare two stars with the same luminosity but different temperatures (radius ratio ∝ 1/T²); or why
  Δλ/λ = v/c fails for very distant quasars (qualitative: v approaching c), with the syllabus limit stated.
- Spot the error: using diameter instead of radius in 4πr², or Wien's law with T in °C.

### Pitfalls
F vs L; r of the star vs d to the star; T⁴ makes small errors large; redshift Δλ is observed − emitted; Hubble constant
in SI (s⁻¹); the Universe expanding "from a point in space" misconception.

## Widget to build: `phys-star` (widgets/phys-star.js)
Embed in phys.astro: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-star" data-t="5800" data-r="1"></div>`.
- Attributes: `data-t` (surface temperature, K, 2500–40 000), `data-r` (radius in solar radii, 0.01–1000),
  `data-d` (distance in light-years, optional, 1–10⁹).
- Canvas: black-body spectral curve (Planck's law, relative intensity against wavelength 0–3000 nm, normalised to
  the curve's peak or to a fixed reference so that hotter stars are visibly higher: offer a "same scale" toggle),
  visible band 400–700 nm shaded with a rainbow strip, λmax marked; a star disc drawn in the approximate colour of a
  black body at that temperature, size ∝ log radius.
- Readout: λmax (Wien, b = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K), L = 4πσr²T⁴ (W and in solar luminosities with L☉ = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W),
  surface power per unit area σT⁴, and if a distance is set, F = L/(4πd²).
- Presets: Sun, Betelgeuse-like red supergiant, Sirius-A-like white star, a white dwarf (label "approximate").
- `.w-note`: "Double the temperature: the peak moves to half the wavelength and the luminosity rises 16 times."
