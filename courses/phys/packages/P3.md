## phys.energy: Work, energy and power
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 (AS)` · `@time 5–7 hours` · `Next: [Deformation of solids](#phys.solids)`
Before you start: [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics) (the equations of motion), [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics).
The syllabus assumes IGCSE energy stores and transfers: recap them in a short table (kinetic, gravitational
potential, elastic potential, thermal/internal, chemical, nuclear, electrical work, radiation).
Warm-up: from Kinematics (v² = u² + 2as), Dynamics (F = ma), Forces (component of a force).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 5.1.1 understand the concept of work, and recall and use work done = force × displacement in the direction of the force
- 5.1.2 recall and apply the principle of conservation of energy
- 5.1.3 recall and understand that the efficiency of a system is the ratio of useful energy output from the system to the total energy input
- 5.1.4 use the concept of efficiency to solve problems
- 5.1.5 define power as work done per unit time
- 5.1.6 solve problems using P = W/t
- 5.1.7 derive P = Fv and use it to solve problems
- 5.2.1 derive, using W = Fs, the formula ΔE_P = mgΔh for gravitational potential energy changes in a uniform gravitational field
- 5.2.2 recall and use the formula ΔE_P = mgΔh for gravitational potential energy changes in a uniform gravitational field
- 5.2.3 derive, using the equations of motion, the formula for kinetic energy E_K = ½mv²
- 5.2.4 recall and use E_K = ½mv²

### Sections (suggested)
1. Work: W = Fs cos θ (the component of force along the displacement); joule defined; zero work when the force is
   perpendicular to the motion (carrying a bag horizontally, circular orbit); negative work by friction/drag.
   Work as the area under a force–displacement graph (link to solids).
2. Conservation of energy: statement; energy transfer diagrams; "lost" energy is dissipated as thermal energy.
3. Gravitational potential energy: `:::proof examinable` ΔE_P = mgΔh from W = Fs (lifting at constant speed,
   force = weight). Only valid where g is uniform (near the Earth's surface; link
   [Gravitational fields](#phys.gravity) for the general case).
4. Kinetic energy: `:::proof examinable` E_K = ½mv² (a constant resultant force F accelerates mass m from rest
   through s: W = Fs = mas and v² = 2as, so W = ½mv²).
5. Energy problems: falling objects, roller coaster, pendulum (speed at the bottom independent of mass), a car
   braking (work done by friction = KE lost), sliding down a rough slope (GPE → KE + work against friction).
6. Efficiency = useful energy output / total energy input (= useful power out / total power in); as a ratio or
   a percentage, never more than 1; Sankey diagram (optional hand SVG).
7. Power: P = W/t (watt defined); `:::proof examinable` P = Fv (W = Fs, divide by t, s/t = v, force constant and
   along the motion). Car at constant top speed: driving force = drag, so P = F_drag × v; lifting a load with a
   motor at constant speed; a cyclist; kWh as a unit of energy (link [Electricity](#phys.electricity)).

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Work done: "the product of the force and the displacement in the direction of the force".
- The joule: "the work done when a force of 1 N moves its point of application 1 m in the direction of the force".
- Principle of conservation of energy: "energy cannot be created or destroyed; it can only be transferred from one form to another; the total energy of a closed system is constant".
- Efficiency: "the ratio of useful energy (or power) output to the total energy (or power) input".
- Power: "work done (or energy transferred) per unit time". The watt: "one joule per second".
- Gravitational potential energy: "the energy a mass has because of its position in a gravitational field".
- Kinetic energy: "the energy an object has because of its motion".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Pushing a 25 kg crate 8.0 m with a 120 N force at 30° below the horizontal: work done (831 J).
- Roller-coaster car from 35 m: speed at the bottom with no friction (26.2 m s⁻¹); with 20% of the energy lost.
- Car of 1100 kg with maximum power 60 kW and top speed 50 m s⁻¹: drag at top speed (1.2 kN); acceleration at
  25 m s⁻¹ if drag ∝ speed (simple model).
- Lift motor raises 800 kg through 20 m in 25 s with efficiency 65%: input power.
- Hydroelectric: water falling 120 m at 50 m³ s⁻¹ (ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³), 85% efficient: electrical power out.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define power [1]; work done at an angle [2]; derive P = Fv [3]; efficiency of a
  motor [3]; energy conservation on a slope with friction [5]; ★★★ car on a hill: power to climb at constant
  speed against drag and gravity (component mg sin θ) [6]; ★★★ synoptic with momentum: bullet into a block, find
  the KE lost and where it went [6].
- Stretch: a bungee jumper (GPE → KE → elastic PE, with ½kx², link solids): maximum extension from a quadratic;
  or the power of a wind turbine from ½(ρAv)v² per second, P ∝ v³.
- Spot the error: using E_P = mgh with h measured to the wrong reference, or W = Fs with the whole force
  instead of its component.

### Pitfalls
Work is zero for a perpendicular force; "energy is used up" (it is transferred); efficiency above 100%;
using g = 10; forgetting to square v; mass in grams; power in kW vs W.

### Figures
A force at an angle to the displacement (hand SVG, `c1` force, dashed components); a roller-coaster track with
energy bar charts at three points (hand SVG, bars `f1`, `f2`); optional Sankey diagram.

### Board box
All boards: same content. Some UK specifications write the work done as W = Fs cos θ explicitly.

## phys.solids: Deformation of solids
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 · P3 (AS)` · `@time 4–6 hours` · `Next: [Waves](#phys.waves)`
Before you start: [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy) (work = area under a force–displacement graph),
[Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces) (pressure: same unit as stress).
Warm-up: from Energy (work done by a force), Forces (pressure = F/A), Units (area of a circle of diameter 0.50 mm in m²).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 6.1.1 understand that deformation is caused by tensile or compressive forces (forces and deformations will be assumed to be in one dimension only)
- 6.1.2 understand and use the terms load, extension, compression and limit of proportionality
- 6.1.3 recall and use Hooke's law
- 6.1.4 recall and use the formula for the spring constant k = F/x
- 6.1.5 define and use the terms stress, strain and the Young modulus
- 6.1.6 describe an experiment to determine the Young modulus of a metal in the form of a wire
- 6.2.1 understand and use the terms elastic deformation, plastic deformation and elastic limit
- 6.2.2 understand that the area under the force–extension graph represents the work done
- 6.2.3 determine the elastic potential energy of a material deformed within its limit of proportionality from the area under the force–extension graph
- 6.2.4 recall and use E_P = ½Fx = ½kx² for a material deformed within its limit of proportionality

### Sections (suggested)
1. Tension and compression; load, extension, compression.
2. Hooke's law and the spring constant; limit of proportionality on a force–extension graph. Springs in series
   and parallel are not in the syllabus outcomes but are common in questions: include a short subsection
   (series: 1/k = 1/k₁ + 1/k₂; parallel: k = k₁ + k₂, each with a two-line reason).
3. Stress, strain, Young modulus (E = σ/ε = FL/(Ax)); typical values (steel about 2 × 10¹¹ Pa, copper about
   1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa, rubber about 10⁷ Pa, give them as "typical"). Why stress and strain (they describe the material,
   not the sample).
4. Measuring the Young modulus of a wire (6.1.6): `:::lab` with steps: long wire (≈ 2–3 m) clamped, over a pulley
   with a marker against a fixed rule (or a Searle's apparatus with a reference wire); measure original length
   with a metre rule/tape, diameter with a micrometer at several places and orientations (mean); add loads in
   steps, record extension, unload to check no permanent extension; plot F against x (straight line in the
   proportional region), E = gradient × L/A. Why a long thin wire (larger extension, smaller percentage
   uncertainty); safety goggles (wire may snap) and a box under the masses.
5. Elastic and plastic deformation: elastic limit (may be just beyond the limit of proportionality); permanent
   extension after plastic deformation; loading and unloading lines (unloading parallel to the loading line for a
   metal stretched past the elastic limit). Ductile (copper), brittle (glass, cast iron), polymeric (rubber) curves
   for comparison, briefly.
6. Work done and elastic potential energy: area under F–x graph; E_P = ½Fx = ½kx² (`:::proof` plain: the area of
   the triangle); for a non-linear graph count squares. Energy per unit volume ½σε is optional stretch.
Embed **phys-stretch** after section 5.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Load: "the force applied to deform the object". Extension: "the increase in length". Compression: "the decrease in length".
- Limit of proportionality: "the point beyond which the extension is no longer proportional to the load".
- Hooke's law: "the extension of an object is proportional to the load applied, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded".
- Spring constant: "the force per unit extension" (k = F/x, N m⁻¹).
- Stress: "force per unit cross-sectional area" (σ = F/A, Pa). Strain: "extension per unit original length" (ε = x/L, no unit).
- Young modulus: "the ratio of stress to strain" (within the limit of proportionality).
- Elastic deformation: "deformation in which the object returns to its original shape and size when the load is removed".
  Plastic deformation: "deformation in which the object does not return to its original shape when the load is removed".
- Elastic limit: "the maximum load (or stress) beyond which the object will not return to its original length when the load is removed".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Spring of k = 40 N m⁻¹ with a 0.50 kg mass: extension (0.123 m); energy stored.
- Steel wire 2.5 m long, diameter 0.40 mm, load 25 N, extension 2.5 mm: stress, strain, E (≈ 1.99 × 10¹¹ Pa).
- From an F–x graph with a curve: energy stored by counting squares.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define Young modulus [1]; stress for a given wire [2]; E from a graph gradient [4];
  describe the Young modulus experiment [6]; energy from a curved F–x graph [3]; ★★★ two wires of different
  materials joined end to end with the same load: ratio of extensions [5]; ★★★ a mass dropped onto a spring
  (energy conservation with ½kx², quadratic) [6].
- Stretch: uncertainty in E for the wire experiment (diameter dominates: its percentage doubles);
  or springs in series and parallel combined.
- Spot the error: using the diameter instead of the radius in A = πr², or the extended length instead of the
  original length in strain.

### Pitfalls
mm to m (and mm² to m²: diameter 0.40 mm → A = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m²); strain has no unit; E_P = ½Fx only within the
limit of proportionality; confusing limit of proportionality with elastic limit; Young modulus is a property of
the material, the spring constant of the object.

### Figures
`:::plot` of a straight F–x line with the energy area shaded (`--shade`). Hand SVG for a metal loading curve:
proportional region, limit of proportionality P, elastic limit E, plastic region, unloading line parallel to the
start, permanent extension marked. Searle's/pulley apparatus as a simple labelled SVG.

### Board box
AQA and OCR also use the terms "yield point", "breaking stress" and "ultimate tensile strength", and AQA expects
the energy per unit volume ½σε. Cambridge needs only the terms above.

## Widget to build: `phys-stretch` (widgets/phys-stretch.js)
A stretching-a-wire lab showing loading, unloading and stored energy. Embed in phys.solids:
`<div class="widget" data-w="phys-stretch" data-material="copper" data-l="2.0" data-d="0.50"></div>`
- Attributes: `data-material="steel|copper|rubber"` (default copper), `data-l` (original length, m, 0.5–5),
  `data-d` (diameter, mm, 0.1–2). Validate and clamp.
- Material models (label them "typical values"): steel E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa, limit of proportionality 2.5 × 10⁸ Pa,
  elastic limit 2.7 × 10⁸ Pa, breaking stress 4.0 × 10⁸ Pa; copper E = 1.2 × 10¹¹ Pa, limit 7 × 10⁷ Pa,
  elastic limit 8 × 10⁷ Pa, breaking 2.2 × 10⁸ Pa; rubber: non-linear, no plastic region (stress–strain curve:
  a smooth S-shaped force–extension curve of your choice, steep at first, flatter, then stiffening; label it "qualitative only"),
  returns to its original length.
- Controls: a load slider (0 → the breaking load for the chosen wire; computed from breaking stress × area),
  tabs Steel / Copper / Rubber, `W.sliderNum` for length and diameter, a **Remove load** button that draws the
  unloading line (parallel to the initial line for metals beyond the elastic limit, ending at a permanent
  extension; back to zero within the elastic limit).
- Canvas: force (N) against extension (mm) with the curve up to the current load, a dot at the current point, the
  limit of proportionality marked, the area under the curve (work done) shaded in a soft fill
  (`ctx.globalAlpha`), and the "snapped!" state at breaking load.
- Readout: load F, extension x, stress σ, strain ε, Young modulus (only within the limit of proportionality,
  else "beyond the limit of proportionality"), energy stored ½Fx (within the limit) or "area under the curve"
  (computed numerically), permanent extension after unloading.
- `.w-note`: what to try (go past the limit of proportionality, then remove the load).
- Probe edge cases: `data-d="0"`, `data-material="glass"` (fall back to copper with a message).
