## phys.electricity: Electricity
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 · P3 (AS)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc)`
Before you start: [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units), [Work, energy and power](#phys.energy).
Warm-up: from Energy (power = energy/time), Units (prefixes: 25 mA, 4.7 kΩ), Forces (area of a circle of a
wire's cross-section).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 9.1.1 understand that an electric current is a flow of charge carriers
- 9.1.2 understand that the charge on charge carriers is quantised
- 9.1.3 recall and use Q = It
- 9.1.4 use, for a current-carrying conductor, the expression I = Anvq, where n is the number density of charge carriers
- 9.2.1 define the potential difference across a component as the energy transferred per unit charge
- 9.2.2 recall and use V = W/Q
- 9.2.3 recall and use P = VI, P = I²R and P = V²/R
- 9.3.1 define resistance
- 9.3.2 recall and use V = IR
- 9.3.3 sketch the I–V characteristics of a metallic conductor at constant temperature, a semiconductor diode and a filament lamp
- 9.3.4 explain that the resistance of a filament lamp increases as current increases because its temperature increases
- 9.3.5 state Ohm's law
- 9.3.6 recall and use R = ρL/A
- 9.3.7 understand that the resistance of a light-dependent resistor (LDR) decreases as the light intensity increases
- 9.3.8 understand that the resistance of a thermistor decreases as the temperature increases (it will be assumed that thermistors have a negative temperature coefficient)

### Sections (suggested)
1. Current as a flow of charge carriers (electrons in metals, ions in electrolytes and in a spark); conventional
   current direction vs electron flow.
2. Charge: the coulomb (1 C = 1 A s), Q = It; quantisation (charge comes in multiples of e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C;
   a "charge of 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C" is impossible). Number of electrons = Q/e.
3. I = Anvq: `:::proof` (plain; the syllabus says "use"): charge passing a cross-section in time t is the charge in
   a length vt. Drift speed in copper (n ≈ 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³, value given in questions): fractions of a mm per second;
   why lamps light at once anyway. Comparison of metal vs semiconductor (much smaller n, larger v for the same I).
   Current in series parts of different thickness: same I, so v ∝ 1/A.
4. Potential difference: definition, V = W/Q, the volt (1 V = 1 J C⁻¹). (e.m.f. is introduced in
   [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc): mention it only as "coming next".)
5. Power: P = VI and its two other forms (`:::proof` plain: substitute V = IR); kWh and the cost of electricity.
6. Resistance: definition R = V/I, the ohm; Ohm's law (statement, and what "ohmic" means); R is V/I at a point,
   **not** the gradient of the I–V graph.
7. I–V characteristics (sketch, both quadrants): metal wire at constant temperature (straight line through the
   origin), filament lamp (S-shaped curve, symmetric, resistance rising with current: explain with lattice ions
   vibrating more and electrons colliding more often), semiconductor diode (very high resistance in reverse bias;
   in forward bias little current until about 0.6 V, then current rises steeply). Embed **phys-iv**. A `:::lab`:
   measuring an I–V characteristic with a potential divider supply (so V can go to zero), ammeter in series,
   voltmeter in parallel, reverse the supply for negative values.
8. Resistivity: definition and R = ρL/A (ρ in Ω m; it depends on the material and temperature, not the shape);
   typical values (copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m, nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m, as "typical"). `:::lab` (Paper 3 favourite):
   measuring the resistivity of a wire: micrometer for diameter at several points, measure R (or V and I) for
   several lengths, plot R against L, gradient = ρ/A. Doubling length/diameter questions.
9. Sensors: LDR (resistance falls as light intensity rises) and NTC thermistor (resistance falls as temperature
   rises), with sketch graphs of R against light intensity and R against temperature. Say that they are used in
   potential dividers in the next chapter.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Electric current: "the rate of flow of charge" (I = Q/t).
- The coulomb: "the charge that passes a point when a current of 1 A flows for 1 s".
- Potential difference: "the energy transferred per unit charge" (from electrical to other forms between two points).
- The volt: "one joule per coulomb".
- Resistance: "the ratio of the potential difference across a component to the current in it" (R = V/I).
- The ohm: "one volt per ampere".
- Ohm's law: "the current in a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided its temperature (and other physical conditions) remain constant".
- Resistivity: ρ = RA/L, "a property of the material: the resistance of a sample of unit length and unit cross-sectional area".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- 3.0 A for 5.0 min: charge 900 C; number of electrons 5.63 × 10²¹.
- Drift speed in a copper wire of diameter 1.0 mm carrying 2.0 A (n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³): ≈ 1.9 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹.
- 2.0 kW kettle on 230 V: current 8.7 A, resistance 26 Ω, energy in 3.0 min.
- Lamp marked 12 V 36 W: current and resistance at normal brightness; why its resistance is lower when cold.
- Resistivity: nichrome wire 1.5 m long, diameter 0.30 mm: R ≈ 23 Ω; what happens to R if the same volume of
  wire is drawn to twice the length (×4).
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define p.d. [1]; state Ohm's law [2]; Q = It and number of electrons [3]; sketch and
  explain a filament lamp characteristic [4]; resistance from a diode characteristic at two points [3];
  I = Anvq in two wires in series [4]; ★★★ resistivity experiment analysis from a table of R and L with a graph
  gradient [6]; ★★★ a heater: power, resistance, required length of nichrome wire for a given diameter [6].
- Stretch: a wire stretched to 1.1 × its length at constant volume: percentage change in resistance (21%);
  or comparing drift speeds in a metal and a semiconductor carrying the same current.
- Spot the error: resistance taken as the gradient of a curved I–V graph, or area from the diameter as πd².

### Pitfalls
R = V/I at the point, not the gradient; diode "conducts in both directions"; current "used up" in a lamp;
mm² → m²; quantisation (non-integer number of electrons); P = V²/R with the wrong V.

### Figures
I–V characteristics (hand SVG with three panels, axes through the origin, or `:::plot` for the resistor line and the
diode's exponential). Wire segment with charge carriers drifting (a cylinder of length vt and area A) for I = Anvq.
The I–V measuring circuit (copy the gold chapter's circuit-symbol patterns).

### Board box
All boards: same content. AQA and OCR also cover superconductivity (zero resistivity below a critical
temperature); Cambridge does not.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `ohms-law`, `resistance-in-a-wire`.

## phys.particles: Particle physics
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 (AS)` · `@time 5–7 hours` · `Next: [Motion in a circle](#phys.circular)`
Before you start: [Electricity](#phys.electricity) (charge e), [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics).
Warm-up: from Electricity (charge of an electron), Units (1 u in kg; standard form), Dynamics (momentum
conservation).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 11.1.1 infer from the results of the α-particle scattering experiment the existence and small size of the nucleus
- 11.1.2 describe a simple model for the nuclear atom to include protons, neutrons and orbital electrons
- 11.1.3 distinguish between nucleon number and proton number
- 11.1.4 understand that isotopes are forms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons in their nuclei
- 11.1.5 understand and use the notation ᴬ_Z X for the representation of nuclides
- 11.1.6 understand that nucleon number and charge are conserved in nuclear processes
- 11.1.7 describe the composition, mass and charge of α-, β- and γ-radiations (both β⁻ (electrons) and β⁺ (positrons) are included)
- 11.1.8 understand that an antiparticle has the same mass but opposite charge to the corresponding particle, and that a positron is the antiparticle of an electron
- 11.1.9 state that (electron) antineutrinos are produced during β⁻ decay and (electron) neutrinos are produced during β⁺ decay
- 11.1.10 understand that α-particles have discrete energies but that β-particles have a continuous range of energies because (anti)neutrinos are emitted in β-decay
- 11.1.11 represent α- and β-decay by a radioactive decay equation of the form ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂α
- 11.1.12 use the unified atomic mass unit (u) as a unit of mass
- 11.2.1 understand that a quark is a fundamental particle and that there are six flavours (types) of quark: up, down, strange, charm, top and bottom
- 11.2.2 recall and use the charge of each flavour of quark and understand that its respective antiquark has the opposite charge (no knowledge of any other properties of quarks is required)
- 11.2.3 recall that protons and neutrons are not fundamental particles and describe protons and neutrons in terms of their quark composition
- 11.2.4 understand that a hadron may be either a baryon (consisting of three quarks) or a meson (consisting of one quark and one antiquark)
- 11.2.5 describe the changes to quark composition that take place during β⁻ and β⁺ decay
- 11.2.6 recall that electrons and neutrinos are fundamental particles called leptons

### Sections (suggested)
1. The α-scattering experiment (Geiger and Marsden, Rutherford): apparatus (source, thin gold foil, detector,
   vacuum), observations (most pass straight through; a few deflected through small angles; very few, about 1 in
   several thousand, deflected by more than 90°) and inferences (atom mostly empty space; mass and positive charge
   concentrated in a tiny nucleus). Figure of paths near a nucleus. Size: nucleus ~10⁻¹⁵ m, atom ~10⁻¹⁰ m.
2. The nuclear atom: protons, neutrons (nucleons), electrons; proton number Z, nucleon number A, neutron number
   N = A − Z; notation ᴬ_Z X; isotopes (same Z, different N; same chemistry).
3. Radiation: table of α (helium nucleus, 2p + 2n, charge +2e, mass ≈ 4 u, strongly ionising, stopped by paper),
   β⁻ (electron, −e, ≈ 1/1840 u), β⁺ (positron, +e, same mass), γ (photon, no charge, no mass, weakly ionising).
   Antiparticles: same mass, opposite charge. Neutrinos: electron antineutrino with β⁻, electron neutrino with β⁺;
   nearly massless, uncharged, very weakly interacting.
4. Energy spectra: α particles from one decay have discrete energies (two bodies share the energy in a fixed
   way); β energies form a continuous range up to a maximum because the energy is shared with the (anti)neutrino
   (the reason the neutrino was proposed). Sketch both (number against kinetic energy).
5. Decay equations: balance nucleon number and charge (proton number); α, β⁻ (with ⁰₋₁e and ν̄), β⁺ (⁰₊₁e and ν),
   γ (no change in A or Z). Decay chains. Conservation in all nuclear processes.
6. The unified atomic mass unit: 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg (data sheet); masses of particles in u.
7. Fundamental particles: quarks (six flavours, charges +⅔e for u, c, t and −⅓e for d, s, b; antiquarks opposite);
   proton uud (+1), neutron udd (0); hadrons = baryons (qqq) or mesons (q q̄); leptons: electron and neutrinos are
   fundamental. Embed **phys-quarks**.
8. β decay at quark level: β⁻: a down quark becomes an up quark (udd → uud), d → u + e⁻ + ν̄; β⁺: an up quark
   becomes a down quark (uud → udd), u → d + e⁺ + ν. Check charge conservation in each.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Nucleon; nucleon number ("the number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus"); proton number ("the number of protons in a nucleus").
- Isotopes: "nuclei of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons".
- Nuclide: "a particular type of nucleus with a specific number of protons and neutrons".
- Antiparticle: "a particle with the same mass as its corresponding particle but opposite charge".
- Unified atomic mass unit: "one twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12".
- Hadron, baryon, meson, lepton, quark (fundamental, six flavours).

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Number of protons, neutrons, electrons in ²³⁵₉₂U and in ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺.
- Complete decay equations: ²²⁶₈₈Ra α decay; ¹⁴₆C β⁻ decay; ¹⁸₉F β⁺ decay (the PET tracer, link
  [Medical physics](#phys.medical)).
- Which quark combinations are hadrons, and their charges: uds (0), sss (−1), ūūd̄ (−1), us̄ (+1); uu is not allowed.
- Mass of an α particle in kg from 4.00 u; ratio of α charge to mass vs proton.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define isotopes [1]; α-scattering observations and conclusions [4]; complete a β⁻
  equation including the antineutrino [2]; why β energies are continuous [2]; quark composition of a neutron and
  its charge check [2]; quark change in β⁺ decay [2]; ★★★ a decay chain from U-238 to a named nuclide, count the
  α and β decays [5]; ★★★ a meson made of u and an anti-strange: charge, baryon or meson, and its antiparticle [4].
- Stretch: estimate the closest approach of a 5.0 MeV α particle to a gold nucleus (energy conservation with
  Coulomb potential energy, link [Electric fields](#phys.efields); give 1/(4πε₀) and the energy in J); or decide
  whether a proposed reaction conserves charge and nucleon number.
- Spot the error: β⁻ decay equation with the nucleon number decreasing, or a neutrino in β⁻ instead of an
  antineutrino.

### Pitfalls
Mass number vs atomic mass; electrons are not in the nucleus (β⁻ electrons are created at decay); γ changes
nothing in A or Z; antineutrino vs neutrino; quarks charges ⅔ and −⅓ of e (not of 1 C); mesons are not baryons.

### Figures
α-scattering paths near a gold nucleus (hand SVG: nucleus `fa`, paths `c1`, one rebounding path `c3`).
α and β energy spectra (two small sketches: lines vs a continuous curve). A quark-composition diagram of proton and
neutron (three small circles `f1`/`f2` with u, u, d labels).

### Board box
AQA and OCR teach a wider particle zoo (strangeness, lepton number, baryon number, Feynman diagrams); Cambridge
asks only for the six quarks, their charges, hadrons and the quark changes in β decay.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `rutherford-scattering`, `build-an-atom`, `beta-decay`, `alpha-decay`.

## Widget to build: `phys-iv` (widgets/phys-iv.js)
Embed in phys.electricity: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-iv" data-c="lamp"></div>`.
- Tabs: **Resistor**, **Filament lamp**, **Diode**, **Thermistor** (`data-c="resistor|lamp|diode|thermistor"`).
- A p.d. slider from −6 V to +6 V. Canvas: I (mA or A) against V with both quadrants, the characteristic curve in
  `sc`, the current operating point in `red`, a dashed line from the origin to the point (its gradient is 1/R) and
  a tangent toggle (to show that R is not 1/gradient of the tangent).
- Models: resistor 100 Ω; filament lamp: I = 0.25 × sign(V) × (|V|/6)^0.6 A (resistance rises with current; fine to
  label "model lamp, 6 V 0.25 A"); diode: I = 1 × 10⁻⁹ (e^{V/0.05} − 1) A capped at 0.5 A (label "model diode");
  thermistor: an extra temperature slider (0–100 °C) with R = 10 kΩ · exp(3950 (1/T − 1/298.15)), straight line
  whose gradient changes with temperature.
- Readout: V, I, R = V/I (show "—" at V = 0), and a sentence on what is happening ("the filament is hotter, so its
  resistance is higher").
- `.w-note`: what to try. Probe `data-c="banana"` (fall back to resistor).

## Widget to build: `phys-quarks` (widgets/phys-quarks.js)
Embed in phys.particles: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-quarks"></div>`.
- Twelve buttons: u, d, s, c, t, b and their antiquarks ū, d̄, s̄, c̄, t̄, b̄ (with charges shown: +⅔, −⅓ …).
  Clicking adds the quark to a "hadron" slot area (max 3); a Clear button; click a slot to remove it.
- Output: total charge in units of e (as a fraction and decimal), the type ("baryon: three quarks", "antibaryon:
  three antiquarks", "meson: one quark and one antiquark", or "not a hadron in this syllabus: …" with the reason),
  and the name when it is one of: proton (uud), neutron (udd), antiproton, antineutron, π⁺ (ud̄), π⁻ (dū),
  K⁺ (us̄), K⁻ (sū) (label mesons "for interest").
- A second tab **β decay**: shows the neutron udd → proton uud with one d highlighted turning into u, and the
  emitted e⁻ and ν̄; toggle to β⁺ (u → d, e⁺ and ν). Plain DOM, no canvas needed.
- Use `aria-pressed`/labels on buttons; works at 360 px (buttons wrap in `.w-row`).
