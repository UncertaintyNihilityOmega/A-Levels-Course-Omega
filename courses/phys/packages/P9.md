## phys.capacitance: Capacitance
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 · P5 (A Level)` · `@time 5–7 hours` · `Next: [Magnetic fields](#phys.magnetic)`
Before you start: [Electric fields](#phys.efields) (potential of a sphere), [D.C. circuits](#phys.dc)
(Kirchhoff's laws), [Logarithms and exponentials](#math.p3.logs). Warm-up: from Electric fields
(V = Q/(4πε₀r)), D.C. circuits (series/parallel resistors), Logarithms (solve e^(−x) = 0.25).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 19.1.1 define capacitance, as applied to both isolated spherical conductors and to parallel plate capacitors
- 19.1.2 recall and use C = Q/V
- 19.1.3 derive, using C = Q/V, formulae for the combined capacitance of capacitors in series and in parallel
- 19.1.4 use the capacitance formulae for capacitors in series and in parallel
- 19.2.1 determine the electric potential energy stored in a capacitor from the area under the potential–charge graph
- 19.2.2 recall and use W = ½QV = ½CV²
- 19.3.1 analyse graphs of the variation with time of potential difference, charge and current for a capacitor discharging through a resistor
- 19.3.2 recall and use τ = RC for the time constant for a capacitor discharging through a resistor
- 19.3.3 use equations of the form x = x₀e^(−t/RC) where x could represent current, charge or potential difference for a capacitor discharging through a resistor

### Data sheet (Paper 4 page 3)
1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + … (series) · C = C₁ + C₂ + … (parallel) · x = x₀e^(−t/RC). C = Q/V, W = ½QV = ½CV² and τ = RC
must be recalled.

### Sections (suggested)
1. What a capacitor is (two conducting plates separated by an insulator, the dielectric); charging moves electrons
   from one plate to the other through the circuit: +Q and −Q, net charge zero, "the charge on a capacitor" means
   Q. Circuit symbol (copy from the gold chapter's symbol chart).
2. Capacitance C = Q/V, the farad (and μF, nF, pF in practice). For parallel plates: C depends on area, separation
   and the dielectric (qualitative only; C = εA/d is not in the syllabus). Isolated sphere: from V = Q/(4πε₀r),
   C = 4πε₀r (`:::proof` plain); capacitance of the Earth as a sphere (≈ 7 × 10⁻⁴ F: surprisingly small).
3. Series and parallel: `:::proof examinable` for both (parallel: same V, charges add; series: same charge Q on each,
   p.d.s add). Note they are "the other way round" from resistors. Networks.
4. Energy stored: the V–Q graph is a straight line; the work done charging = area under it = ½QV
   (`:::proof examinable`-style explanation: adding charge δQ at p.d. V costs VδQ, a thin strip), then ½CV² and
   Q²/2C. Half the energy from the supply is dissipated in the resistance during charging (A* insight).
   Contexts: camera flash, defibrillator, back-up power, keyboard keys.
5. Discharge through a resistor: the current is proportional to the p.d., so the rate of loss of charge is
   proportional to the charge left: exponential decay. Q = Q₀e^(−t/RC), V = V₀e^(−t/RC), I = I₀e^(−t/RC)
   (current direction opposite to charging). Time constant τ = RC (units: Ω × F = s): after τ, 37% left; after 5τ,
   under 1%; half-life of discharge t½ = RC ln 2 ≈ 0.693RC. Graphs of V, Q, I against t; ln V against t is a
   straight line with gradient −1/RC (`:::lab`: discharge with a voltmeter and stopwatch or data logger, plot
   ln V against t, find RC; link [Planning, analysis and evaluation](#phys.planning)).
   Charging curves (V rising to the supply value) are not in the outcomes; show them once, for comparison only.
   Embed **phys-rc**.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Capacitance: "the charge stored per unit potential difference" (C = Q/V); for an isolated sphere, the p.d. is its
  potential (relative to infinity, or earth); for parallel plates, the p.d. between the plates.
- The farad: "one coulomb per volt".
- Time constant: "the time taken for the charge (or p.d., or current) to fall to 1/e (about 37%) of its initial
  value during discharge"; τ = RC.

### Worked examples and question ideas
- 470 μF charged to 12 V: charge (5.64 × 10⁻³ C) and energy (3.38 × 10⁻² J).
- 2.0 μF and 3.0 μF in series and in parallel (1.2 μF and 5.0 μF); the p.d. across each in series on a 10 V supply
  (6.0 V across the 2.0 μF, 4.0 V across the 3.0 μF).
- Discharge: 100 μF through 50 kΩ from 9.0 V: τ = 5.0 s; V after 3.0 s (4.94 V); time to reach 2.0 V (7.52 s).
- Energy lost when two capacitors share charge (one charged, one empty, connected in parallel): energy decreases.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define capacitance [1]; derive the parallel formula [3]; energy from a V–Q graph [3];
  network capacitance and charges [4]; τ from a discharge graph [3]; V after a time [3]; ★★★ a defibrillator:
  energy, average power over the discharge time, and the time for the energy to halve (t = τ ln2 / 2 for energy) [6];
  ★★★ ln V against t data: find RC and the capacitance with uncertainty [6].
- Stretch: show that the time for the stored energy to halve is half the time for the charge to halve; or two
  charged capacitors connected together: final p.d. and energy lost.
- Spot the error: using the series formula for parallel capacitors, or e^(−t/RC) with t in ms and RC in s.

### Pitfalls
μF to F; "a capacitor stores charge" (it stores energy: net charge is zero); half-life of discharge vs time constant;
energy halves at τ ln2/2, not τ ln2; inverted series/parallel rules.

### Figures
Capacitor in a charge/discharge circuit with a two-way switch (hand SVG, gold-chapter symbols). V–Q graph with the
triangle area shaded (`:::plot` with `--shade`). Discharge curves (`:::plot` of exp(−x)) with τ marked and the 37%
level; ln V against t straight line.

### Board box
AQA and OCR include charging equations (V = V₀(1 − e^(−t/RC))) and C = ε₀ε_rA/d with permittivity and dielectric
action; Cambridge requires only discharge and the definitions above.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `capacitor-lab-basics`.

## phys.magnetic: Magnetic fields
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Electromagnetic induction](#phys.induction)`
Before you start: [Electric fields](#phys.efields), [Motion in a circle](#phys.circular), [Electricity](#phys.electricity)
(I = Anvq). Warm-up: from Electric fields (F = qE; velocity of a particle after acceleration), Circular
(F = mv²/r), Electricity (I = Anvq).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 20.1.1 understand that a magnetic field is an example of a field of force produced either by moving charges or by permanent magnets
- 20.1.2 represent a magnetic field by field lines
- 20.2.1 understand that a force might act on a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field
- 20.2.2 recall and use the equation F = BIL sin θ, with directions as interpreted by Fleming's left-hand rule
- 20.2.3 define magnetic flux density as the force acting per unit current per unit length on a wire placed at right-angles to the magnetic field
- 20.3.1 determine the direction of the force on a charge moving in a magnetic field
- 20.3.2 recall and use F = BQv sin θ
- 20.3.3 understand the origin of the Hall voltage and derive and use the expression V_H = BI/(ntq), where t = thickness
- 20.3.4 understand the use of a Hall probe to measure magnetic flux density
- 20.3.5 describe the motion of a charged particle moving in a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the direction of motion of the particle
- 20.3.6 explain how electric and magnetic fields can be used in velocity selection
- 20.4.1 sketch magnetic field patterns due to the currents in a long straight wire, a flat circular coil and a long solenoid
- 20.4.2 understand that the magnetic field due to the current in a solenoid is increased by a ferrous core
- 20.4.3 explain the origin of the forces between current-carrying conductors and determine the direction of the forces

### Sections (suggested)
1. Magnetic fields: produced by moving charges (currents) or permanent magnets; field lines (N to S outside a
   magnet, never crossing; uniform between flat poles); the ⊙ (out of the page) and ⊗ (into the page) notation.
2. Fields due to currents (20.4.1–2): long straight wire (concentric circles, right-hand grip rule), flat circular
   coil, long solenoid (uniform inside, like a bar magnet outside); a ferrous (iron) core increases the field
   (electromagnets, relays). Formulas for B near wires are NOT required: do not teach them as syllabus.
3. Force on a current-carrying conductor: F = BIL sin θ (θ between the wire and the field); Fleming's left-hand rule
   (first finger field, second finger current, thumb motion/force); zero force when parallel. Definition of
   magnetic flux density and of the tesla (1 T = 1 N A⁻¹ m⁻¹). Measuring B with a current balance (a wire on a top-pan
   balance between magnets: reading change gives F). The motor effect (a coil in a field: a couple, link
   [Forces, density and pressure](#phys.forces)).
4. Force on a moving charge: F = BQv sin θ (`:::proof` plain from F = BIL with I = nAvq: force on one carrier);
   direction (left-hand rule with conventional current: for an electron, current is opposite to its velocity).
5. Charged particle in a uniform field perpendicular to its velocity: force perpendicular to velocity → circular
   path, speed constant, r = mv/(BQ) (`:::proof` plain from BQv = mv²/r); the period does not depend on speed
   (A* insight); applications: deflection of beams, mass spectrometer idea (qualitative), particle tracks in a
   bubble chamber (spirals as particles lose energy).
6. Velocity selection: crossed E and B fields; undeflected when qE = Bqv → v = E/B (independent of charge and
   mass); faster particles are deflected one way, slower the other.
7. The Hall effect: charge carriers moving through a slab in a perpendicular field are pushed to one side; charge
   builds up until the electric force balances the magnetic force: qV_H/d = Bqv; with I = ntdvq (n carriers per
   unit volume, slab width d, thickness t) → V_H = BI/(ntq) (`:::proof examinable`, careful with the symbols:
   t = thickness measured parallel to B). Why V_H is larger in semiconductors (smaller n); Hall probe: calibrated,
   held with its face perpendicular to the field, reading ∝ B; sign of V_H shows the sign of the carriers.
8. Forces between currents: each wire sits in the other's field; parallel currents in the same direction attract,
   opposite directions repel; forces equal and opposite (Newton's third law) even if the currents differ.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Magnetic field: "a region of space in which a moving charge (or a current-carrying conductor, or a magnetic pole) experiences a force".
- Magnetic flux density: "the force acting per unit current per unit length on a wire placed at right angles to the magnetic field".
- The tesla: "the magnetic flux density when a wire carrying a current of 1 A, at right angles to the field, experiences a force of 1 N per metre of its length".
- Hall voltage: "the potential difference produced across a conductor carrying a current in a magnetic field, perpendicular to both the current and the field".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Wire 0.050 m in a 0.20 T field carrying 3.0 A at 90° and at 30° (0.030 N and 0.015 N).
- Electron at 2.0 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ in a 5.0 mT field: force and radius of its path (2.28 cm).
- Velocity selector with E = 3.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹ and B = 0.15 T: selected speed 2.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹.
- Hall voltage in a semiconductor slab: B = 0.10 T, I = 20 mA, n = 1.0 × 10²² m⁻³, t = 0.50 mm (V_H = 2.5 × 10⁻³ V);
  compare with copper.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define the tesla [2]; F = BIL with an angle [2]; direction by Fleming's rule [1];
  sketch the field of a solenoid [2]; explain the Hall voltage [4]; radius of a proton's path [3]; ★★★ velocity
  selector followed by a magnetic field region: radius for particles of two masses (separation) [7]; ★★★ two
  parallel wires: direction of forces and why they are equal [3].
- Stretch: show that the time for a half-circle in a magnetic field does not depend on the speed (so faster particles
  take larger circles in the same time: the idea behind the cyclotron); or a current balance experiment: find B from
  readings.
- Spot the error: using the right-hand rule for force, or forgetting that the electron's current is opposite to its
  velocity.

### Pitfalls
F = BIL sin θ with θ between wire and field (not the angle to the normal); direction rules; magnetic force does no
work on a moving charge; t in the Hall formula is the thickness parallel to B; V_H is tiny in metals.

### Figures
Field patterns (hand SVG): bar magnet, long straight wire (concentric circles with ⊙ current), flat coil (side view),
solenoid. Fleming's left-hand rule diagram (three labelled arrows `c1`, `c2`, `c3`). Electron path in a field into the
page (⊗ grid of crosses, circular arc). Hall slab with B, I, carriers and V_H labelled. Velocity selector.

### Board box
AQA and OCR also use F = BQv and the Hall probe; AQA applies the circular-path result to the cyclotron. Cambridge
requires the Hall voltage derivation, which most UK specifications do not.

explore: PhET `magnet-and-compass`, `faradays-electromagnetic-lab`.

## Widget to build: `phys-rc` (widgets/phys-rc.js)
Embed in phys.capacitance: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-rc" data-c="100" data-r="50" data-v="9"></div>`.
- Attributes: `data-c` (μF, 1–2200), `data-r` (kΩ, 1–1000), `data-v` (initial p.d., V, 1–20),
  `data-mode="discharge|charge"` (default discharge; charge only for comparison).
- Controls: `W.sliderNum` for C, R, V₀; tabs **V**, **Q**, **I**, **ln V** (what to plot); a time cursor slider
  (0–6τ).
- Canvas: the chosen quantity against time with τ, 2τ, 3τ marked on the time axis, a dashed 37% (1/e) level and the
  half-value time t½ = 0.693RC; the cursor point in `red`. For **ln V**, a straight line with its gradient −1/RC
  labelled.
- Readout: τ = RC (s), t½ (s), V, Q, I and stored energy ½CV² at the cursor time, fraction of the initial energy
  remaining.
- `.w-note`: "Double R or C and the time constant doubles; the curve has the same shape, stretched in time."
- Probe `data-c="0"` (clamp), `data-r="abc"` (default with a message).
