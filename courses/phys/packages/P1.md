## phys.units: Physical quantities, units and uncertainty
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 · P3 (AS)` · `@time 5–7 hours` · `Next: [Kinematics](#phys.kinematics)`
Before you start: [Physics from zero](#phys.f.basics), [Indices, surds and standard form](#math.f.powers).
Warm-up (3 questions, from Physics from zero and the maths foundations): convert 3.5 km to m in standard form;
rearrange v = s/t for t; find the hypotenuse of a 3–4–5 triangle (name the chapter each comes from).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 1.1.1 understand that all physical quantities consist of a numerical magnitude and a unit
- 1.1.2 make reasonable estimates of physical quantities included within the syllabus
- 1.2.1 recall the SI base quantities and units: mass (kg), length (m), time (s), current (A), temperature (K)
- 1.2.2 express derived units as products or quotients of the SI base units; use derived units for syllabus quantities
- 1.2.3 use SI base units to check the homogeneity of physical equations
- 1.2.4 recall and use the prefixes p, n, μ, m, c, d, k, M, G, T for multiples and submultiples of base and derived units
- 1.3.1 understand and explain the effects of systematic errors (including zero errors) and random errors
- 1.3.2 understand the distinction between precision and accuracy
- 1.3.3 assess the uncertainty in a derived quantity by simple addition of absolute or percentage uncertainties
- 1.4.1 understand the difference between scalar and vector quantities, with syllabus examples
- 1.4.2 add and subtract coplanar vectors
- 1.4.3 represent a vector as two perpendicular components

### Sections (suggested)
1. Physical quantities: number × unit ("5.0" is meaningless, "5.0 m" is a length). Symbol / unit convention
   (`v / m s⁻¹` in tables and on axes, as Cambridge uses).
2. SI base units: the five in the syllabus outcome, plus the mole (amount of substance, met again in Ideal gases,
   15.1.1). Derived units from defining equations: N = kg m s⁻², J = kg m² s⁻², W = kg m² s⁻³,
   Pa = kg m⁻¹ s⁻², C = A s, V = kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹, Ω = kg m² s⁻³ A⁻². Teach the method (start from a defining
   equation), not a list to memorise. A table of quantity → defining equation → derived unit → base units.
3. Homogeneity: both sides of a correct equation have the same base units. Worked example: check
   s = ut + ½at². Key point: homogeneity is necessary but not sufficient (it cannot detect the ½ or a missing
   dimensionless factor; it cannot check equations with sums of mismatched terms). Exam favourite: "show that
   the equation is homogeneous" and "find the base units of k in F = kv²" (k: kg m⁻¹).
4. Prefixes: table pico 10⁻¹² to tera 10¹². Pitfalls: mm² → m² (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²), cm³ → m³ (10⁻⁶),
   μ vs m, "ms" (millisecond) vs "m s" (metre second). Embed the **units** widget here.
5. Estimates: a table of reference values a student should know to within a factor of 3 (mass of an adult 70 kg,
   apple 0.1 kg, height of a room 3 m, walking speed 1.5 m s⁻¹, car on a motorway 30 m s⁻¹, speed of sound
   340 m s⁻¹, diameter of an atom 10⁻¹⁰ m, nucleus 10⁻¹⁵ m, wavelength of visible light 400–700 nm, power of a
   kettle 2 kW, a lamp 10–60 W, mains 230 V, a current of 0.1–10 A in a household device, atmospheric
   pressure 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa, density of water 1000 kg m⁻³, of air 1.2 kg m⁻³). One worked Paper 1-style estimate
   (e.g. kinetic energy of a sprinter, ~4 kJ) showing the order-of-magnitude reasoning.
6. Errors and uncertainties: random errors (scatter; reduced by repeating and averaging, never removed),
   systematic errors (all readings shifted the same way; zero error, wrongly calibrated meter, reaction time
   in the same direction; repeating does not help, correct by checking the zero or calibrating). Precision
   (how close repeated readings are to each other; small scatter) vs accuracy (how close the result is to the
   true value). Target/dartboard figure with four panels (precise and accurate, precise not accurate, …).
   Absolute, fractional, percentage uncertainty. Uncertainty of repeated readings = half the range.
   Combining (Cambridge: simple addition, never quadrature): sum or difference → add absolute uncertainties;
   product or quotient → add percentage (or fractional) uncertainties; power n → multiply the percentage by |n|;
   a constant (π, 2) has no uncertainty. Final answer: uncertainty to 1 (or 2) s.f., value to the same
   decimal place: (2.70 ± 0.05) g cm⁻³.
7. Scalars and vectors: lists (scalars: distance, speed, mass, time, energy, power, temperature, charge,
   p.d., pressure; vectors: displacement, velocity, acceleration, force, weight, momentum, electric and
   gravitational field strength). Adding vectors: tip to tail, scale drawing, and by calculation (Pythagoras for
   perpendicular, cosine and sine rules otherwise). Subtracting: a − b = a + (−b); change in velocity
   Δv = v − u for a ball bouncing or a car turning a corner. Components: F cos θ and F sin θ with θ measured from
   the direction of the component you want ("the cosine is next to the angle"). Embed `resultant` (maths widget,
   no attributes) if it helps.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Base quantity / base unit; derived unit ("a unit formed by multiplying and/or dividing base units").
- Homogeneous equation: "an equation in which every term has the same base units".
- Systematic error: "an error that makes all readings differ from the true value by the same amount or in the
  same proportion, in the same direction; it cannot be reduced by repeating readings".
- Random error: "an error that causes readings to be scattered unpredictably about the true value; its effect is
  reduced by repeating readings and averaging".
- Zero error; precision; accuracy; uncertainty ("the range within which the true value is expected to lie");
  scalar ("a quantity with magnitude only"); vector ("a quantity with magnitude and direction").

### Worked examples and question ideas (write your own numbers; checks recompute all of them)
- Base units of the pascal and of the volt (from P = W/(tI)... use V = W/Q = W/(It)).
- Homogeneity of p = ρgh and of v² = u² + 2as; find the base units of the constant in F = kAv² (drag).
- Uncertainty in the density of a metal cylinder from m ± Δm, d ± Δd (micrometer), l ± Δl (calipers): note d²
  doubles its percentage; give the answer with its absolute uncertainty.
- Period from timing 20 oscillations: 20T = 31.2 s ± 0.2 s → T = 1.56 s ± 0.01 s (why timing many swings helps).
- Resultant of two forces at 90° and at 60°; components of a 250 N tow-rope force at 25°; Δv for a ball that
  hits a wall at 8.0 m s⁻¹ and rebounds at 6.0 m s⁻¹ (14 m s⁻¹, not 2 m s⁻¹).
- Exam practice ★→★★★: base units / homogeneity [3]; estimate the power of a student running upstairs [3];
  systematic vs random with a zero-error reading [3]; percentage uncertainty in g from a pendulum
  g = 4π²l/T² [4]; resultant velocity of an aircraft in a crosswind with bearing [5]; ★★★ uncertainty in a
  Young-modulus-style formula E = 4FL/(πd²x) with four measured quantities [6].
- Stretch for A*: find the base units of G and check Kepler's T² = 4π²r³/(GM); or uncertainty when two nearly
  equal readings are subtracted (percentage explodes), with the lesson for experiment design.
- Spot the error: adding percentage uncertainties for a difference (should add absolute uncertainties).

### Pitfalls to warn about
"kg" is the base unit of mass (not g); temperature in K for base units; uncertainties are added, never
subtracted, even for a difference; percentage of a power multiplies; vectors cannot be added as plain numbers;
writing "m/s" is accepted in words but use m s⁻¹; too many s.f. in an uncertainty.

### Figures
Four-panel precision/accuracy target figure (hand SVG, `fm` panels, dots `fi`); a vector triangle (tip to tail,
`c1`, `c2`, resultant `c3` with `#ahr`); a component diagram (force at angle θ with dashed `ln-m dash`
components). A micrometer/calipers reading figure is welcome but optional (keep it simple and correct).

### Board box
AQA, OCR and Edexcel IAL teach the same SI system; UK boards often also combine uncertainties by simple
addition at A Level. Keep it short and content-only.

### Links (verified)
Optional: the Physics & Maths Tutor CAIE revision notes page (COURSE.md list).

## phys.kinematics: Kinematics
`@level as` · `@paper CAIE P1 · P2 · P3 (AS)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Dynamics and momentum](#phys.dynamics)`
Before you start: [Physical quantities, units and uncertainty](#phys.units) (vectors, components),
[Graphs and straight lines](#math.f.graphs). Warm-up: from phys.units (resolve a velocity into components;
convert 72 km h⁻¹ to m s⁻¹; scalar or vector?).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 2.1.1 define and use distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration
- 2.1.2 use graphical methods to represent distance, displacement, speed, velocity and acceleration
- 2.1.3 determine displacement from the area under a velocity–time graph
- 2.1.4 determine velocity using the gradient of a displacement–time graph
- 2.1.5 determine acceleration using the gradient of a velocity–time graph
- 2.1.6 derive, from the definitions of velocity and acceleration, equations that represent uniformly accelerated motion in a straight line
- 2.1.7 solve problems using equations that represent uniformly accelerated motion in a straight line, including the motion of bodies falling in a uniform gravitational field without air resistance
- 2.1.8 describe an experiment to determine the acceleration of free fall using a falling object
- 2.1.9 describe and explain motion due to a uniform velocity in one direction and a uniform acceleration in a perpendicular direction

### Sections (suggested)
1. Describing motion: distance vs displacement, speed vs velocity (average and instantaneous), acceleration
   (deceleration = negative acceleration in the chosen positive direction). Sign conventions from the start.
2. Motion graphs: s–t (gradient = velocity; tangent for instantaneous velocity), v–t (gradient = acceleration,
   area = displacement, area below the axis is negative displacement), a–t (area = change in velocity).
   Distance–time vs displacement–time for a ball thrown up and caught. Converting between graphs.
3. The equations of uniformly accelerated motion: v = u + at, s = ½(u + v)t, s = ut + ½at², v² = u² + 2as
   (the last two are on the data sheet; the others must be recalled). `:::proof examinable` deriving all four
   from a = (v − u)/t and the area under the v–t graph (2.1.6). A "suvat" method box: list the five symbols,
   tick the three you know, choose the equation without the one you do not want.
4. Free fall: g = 9.81 m s⁻², choose up or down as positive and keep to it; ball thrown up from a cliff
   (quadratic in t: use the formula, reject the negative root with a reason).
5. Measuring g (2.1.8): `:::lab` with steps. Method: steel ball released from an electromagnet, falling a
   measured height h through a trapdoor or two light gates, time t from an electronic timer; repeat for 6+
   heights; plot h against t² (straight line through the origin, gradient = g/2), or 2h/t against t with two light
   gates. Sources of error: release delay (systematic), measuring h to the bottom of the ball, air resistance
   (small for a dense ball). Mention the alternative: a picket fence through a light gate.
6. Projectiles (2.1.9): horizontal velocity constant, vertical acceleration g, the two are independent. Horizontal
   launch (time from the vertical fall alone), angled launch (components u cos θ, u sin θ; time of flight,
   maximum height, range on level ground), landing velocity by combining components. Parabolic path figure with
   velocity arrows at several points (horizontal arrow the same length each time). Air resistance is treated
   qualitatively in Dynamics; here assume none, and say so.
Embed the maths widgets `vt` (in section 2) and `projectile` (in section 6), each as
`<div class="widget" data-w="projectile"></div>` with no data attributes, and say what to try in the text.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Displacement: "the distance in a specified direction from a fixed point" (vector).
- Speed: "distance travelled per unit time". Velocity: "rate of change of displacement" (or "change in
  displacement per unit time").
- Acceleration: "rate of change of velocity".
- Uniform acceleration: "acceleration that is constant in magnitude and direction".

### Formulas
`:::formula Equations of uniformly accelerated motion` with all four, every symbol explained, and the condition
"only for constant acceleration in a straight line".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Car accelerating from rest at 2.5 m s⁻² for 8.0 s: v and s; then braking to rest in 40 m (deceleration).
- Reading a piecewise v–t graph (hand SVG): acceleration of each stage, total displacement by areas, average
  velocity. A lift or a train journey are good contexts.
- Stopping distance = thinking distance (constant speed during reaction time) + braking distance;
  how each scales with speed (×2 speed → ×2 thinking, ×4 braking).
- Ball thrown up at 15 m s⁻¹ from a 20 m cliff: time to hit the sea, speed on impact.
- Parcel dropped from a plane flying horizontally at 60 m s⁻¹ at 500 m: time, horizontal distance, impact speed
  and angle.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define acceleration [1]; s from a v–t area [2]; suvat for a braking car [3];
  ball thrown vertically with sign convention [4]; "show that" derivation of s = ut + ½at² from a v–t graph [3];
  horizontal projectile from a table [5]; ★★★ angled projectile landing on a raised platform, with a graph to
  sketch [7]; ★★★ analysing given h, t data to find g and comment on the intercept (a systematic delay) [6].
- Stretch for A*: two objects, one dropped and one thrown up at the same time from different heights: when and
  where they meet; or show the range on level ground is (u² sin 2θ)/g and is greatest at 45°.
- Spot the error: using v² = u² + 2as with g positive downward but the upward initial velocity also positive.

### Pitfalls
Mixing sign conventions within one problem; using suvat when the acceleration is not constant; reading the area
of a v–t graph as distance when part is below the axis; thinking the velocity at the top of a projectile path is
zero (only the vertical component is); confusing the gradient of a distance–time graph (speed) with velocity;
using 10 instead of 9.81.

### Figures
`:::plot` for s–t and v–t curves where a single formula fits (e.g. s = 5t²). Hand SVG for a piecewise v–t graph
with shaded areas (`f1`, `f2` fills) and for the projectile path with velocity components at three points (use
`c1` for horizontal, `c2` for vertical arrows, `ln` for the path). The light-gate free-fall apparatus as a simple
labelled SVG.

### Board box
All boards teach the same kinematics. AQA and OCR use the same four equations; some UK textbooks write
s = vt − ½at² as a fifth. Content-only.

### Links (verified)
explore: [Projectile Motion](https://phet.colorado.edu/en/simulations/projectile-motion) · PhET.

## Widget to build: `units` (widgets/units.js)
The Toolbox calculators page (tools.calc) already embeds `<div class="widget" data-w="units"></div>`, and other
courses (chemistry) will use it too, so make it general and robust. Embed it in phys.units.

- Attributes: `data-tab="convert|prefix|base"` (default `convert`); `data-q="length|mass|time|volume|energy|pressure|temperature"` (default `length`).
- Tabs (`.w-tabs`): **Convert**, **Prefixes**, **Base units**.
- **Convert**: quantity select; a number field (accept "4.7e3", "4.7×10^3", "4.7 x 10^-3"; friendly `.w-err` on
  bad input); "from" and "to" unit selects; the result in normal and standard form to 4 s.f.; a line of working
  ("4.7 kΩ × 10³ = 4.7 × 10³ Ω"). Units:
  - length: pm, nm, μm, mm, cm, m, km (and light-year 9.461 × 10¹⁵ m, AU 1.496 × 10¹¹ m as extras)
  - mass: μg, mg, g, kg, tonne (10³ kg), u (1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, the 9702 data value)
  - time: ns, μs, ms, s, min, h, day, year (3.156 × 10⁷ s)
  - volume: mm³, cm³, dm³, L (= dm³), mL (= cm³), m³
  - energy: J, kJ, MJ, eV (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, the 9702 data value; note "exact SI: 1.602 176 634 × 10⁻¹⁹ J"),
    keV, MeV, kWh (3.6 × 10⁶ J)
  - pressure: Pa, kPa, MPa, atm (101 325 Pa), mmHg (133.322 Pa), bar (10⁵ Pa)
  - temperature: °C and K (T/K = θ/°C + 273.15); refuse values below absolute zero with a friendly message.
- **Prefixes**: a table p, n, μ, m, c, d, (none), k, M, G, T with symbol, power of ten, and an example;
  plus a "standard form helper": type any number, see it in standard form and with the best prefix
  (0.000 47 m → 4.7 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.47 mm = 470 μm).
- **Base units**: select a derived unit (N, J, W, Pa, C, V, Ω, Hz, T, Wb, F) and show its defining equation and
  SI base units (N → F = ma → kg m s⁻²; V → V = W/Q → kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹; T → F = BIL → kg s⁻² A⁻¹;
  Wb = T m² → kg m² s⁻² A⁻¹; F = C/V → kg⁻¹ m⁻² s⁴ A²; Hz → s⁻¹). Use `W.sup` for superscripts.
- No canvas needed. Everything in one card, works at 360 px. Test: `widget units --probe`, and
  `widget units 'data-tab="base"' --probe`, and bad input such as `data-q="banana"` (fall back to length).
