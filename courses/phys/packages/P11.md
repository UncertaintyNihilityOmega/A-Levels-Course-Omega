## phys.quantum: Quantum physics
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Nuclear physics](#phys.nuclear)`
Before you start: [Waves](#phys.waves) (EM spectrum), [Superposition](#phys.superposition) (diffraction, interference),
[Electric fields](#phys.efields) (an electron accelerated through a p.d. gains energy eV).
Warm-up: from Waves (c = fλ for 500 nm), Superposition (what diffraction shows), Electric fields (energy gained by
an electron through 100 V).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 22.1.1 understand that electromagnetic radiation has a particulate nature
- 22.1.2 understand that a photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy
- 22.1.3 recall and use E = hf
- 22.1.4 use the electronvolt (eV) as a unit of energy
- 22.1.5 understand that a photon has momentum and that the momentum is given by p = E/c
- 22.2.1 understand that photoelectrons may be emitted from a metal surface when it is illuminated by electromagnetic radiation
- 22.2.2 understand and use the terms threshold frequency and threshold wavelength
- 22.2.3 explain photoelectric emission in terms of photon energy and work function energy
- 22.2.4 recall and use hf = Φ + ½mv²max
- 22.2.5 explain why the maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons is independent of intensity, whereas the photoelectric current is proportional to intensity
- 22.3.1 understand that the photoelectric effect provides evidence for a particulate nature of electromagnetic radiation while phenomena such as interference and diffraction provide evidence for a wave nature
- 22.3.2 describe and interpret qualitatively the evidence provided by electron diffraction for the wave nature of particles
- 22.3.3 understand the de Broglie wavelength as the wavelength associated with a moving particle
- 22.3.4 recall and use λ = h/p
- 22.4.1 understand that there are discrete electron energy levels in isolated atoms (e.g. atomic hydrogen)
- 22.4.2 understand the appearance and formation of emission and absorption line spectra
- 22.4.3 recall and use hf = E₁ − E₂

### Data
h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, m_e = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg (data sheet). Work functions
and energy levels are given in questions (typical: caesium 2.1 eV, sodium 2.3 eV, zinc 4.3 eV; hydrogen levels
−13.6, −3.40, −1.51, −0.85 eV).

### Sections (suggested)
1. Photons: EM radiation comes in packets (quanta) of energy E = hf = hc/λ; a beam's intensity is the photon
   energy × photon flux per unit area. Number of photons per second from a laser pointer (≈ 10¹⁵–10¹⁶ s⁻¹).
2. The electronvolt: definition; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; photon energies in eV (visible light ≈ 1.8–3.1 eV).
3. Photon momentum p = E/c = h/λ; radiation pressure and solar sails as context.
4. The photoelectric effect: the gold-leaf electroscope with a zinc plate (UV discharges a negatively charged plate;
   visible light, however bright, does not); observations (threshold frequency; emission is instantaneous; max KE
   depends on frequency not intensity; current ∝ intensity) and why the wave theory fails to explain them;
   Einstein's explanation: one photon gives all its energy to one electron; work function Φ; hf = Φ + ½mv²max;
   threshold frequency f₀ = Φ/h and threshold wavelength λ₀ = hc/Φ. Graph of KE_max against f: straight line,
   gradient h, x-intercept f₀, y-intercept −Φ (same gradient for every metal). Intensity: more photons per second →
   more electrons per second → larger current, same KE_max. Stopping potential (eV_s = KE_max) as a measurement tool
   (not a syllabus term, introduce it as a useful idea). Embed **phys-photo**.
5. Wave–particle duality: photoelectric effect → particles; interference and diffraction → waves. Electron
   diffraction: a beam of electrons through a thin graphite film gives rings on a fluorescent screen (only waves
   diffract; ring spacing matches the de Broglie wavelength comparable to atomic spacing ~10⁻¹⁰ m; higher
   accelerating voltage → faster electrons → shorter λ → smaller rings). De Broglie λ = h/p = h/(mv); for an electron
   accelerated through V: p = √(2meV). Why we do not see a cricket ball diffract (λ ~10⁻³⁴ m).
6. Energy levels and line spectra: discrete levels in isolated atoms (negative energies, zero = ionised); excitation
   and de-excitation; a photon emitted or absorbed with hf = E₁ − E₂; emission spectrum (bright lines on dark) from
   a hot low-pressure gas; absorption spectrum (dark lines in a continuous spectrum) when white light passes through a
   cool gas (absorbed photons are re-emitted in all directions); counting possible lines from level n (n(n − 1)/2);
   ionisation energy. Hydrogen level diagram figure.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Photon: "a quantum (discrete packet) of electromagnetic energy".
- Electronvolt: "the energy gained by an electron when it is accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V".
- Work function: "the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the surface of a metal".
- Threshold frequency: "the minimum frequency of electromagnetic radiation that can cause photoelectric emission from a metal". Threshold wavelength: "the maximum wavelength …".
- De Broglie wavelength: "the wavelength associated with a moving particle", λ = h/p.
- Energy level: "one of the discrete values of energy that an electron in an isolated atom can have".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Photon energy of 450 nm light in J and eV (4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, 2.76 eV); photons per second from a 5.0 mW laser at
  650 nm (1.6 × 10¹⁶ s⁻¹); momentum of one photon.
- Sodium (Φ = 2.3 eV) lit by 400 nm light: KE_max and v_max; threshold wavelength (540 nm).
- Graph of KE_max against f from given data: h from the gradient, Φ from the intercept.
- Electron accelerated through 150 V: de Broglie wavelength (≈ 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m), comparable to atomic spacing.
- Hydrogen: photon for n = 3 → n = 2 (1.89 eV, 658 nm, red); which transitions give visible light.
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define the work function [1]; photon energy in eV [2]; explain why KE_max does not
  depend on intensity [3]; threshold wavelength [2]; photoelectric equation with v_max [3]; electron diffraction as
  evidence [3]; de Broglie wavelength of an accelerated electron [4]; ★★★ energy-level diagram: number of lines,
  longest and shortest wavelengths, which are visible [6]; ★★★ KE_max–f graph analysis with h and Φ [5].
- Stretch: compare the momentum of a photon and an electron with the same wavelength, and their energies; or the force
  of a laser beam on a perfect mirror (2P/c).
- Spot the error: using eV directly in J in hf = Φ + KE, or λ = h/(mv) with the electron's energy instead of momentum.

### Pitfalls
eV ↔ J conversions; threshold depends on frequency, not intensity; "brighter light gives faster electrons";
KE_max (not every electron); energy levels are negative; λ = h/p with p = √(2mE) (E in J).

### Figures
Photoelectric arrangement (hand SVG: light on a metal plate in a vacuum tube, collector, ammeter). `:::plot` of
KE_max against f for two metals (parallel lines, intercepts f₀). Hydrogen energy-level diagram (horizontal lines,
downward arrows `c1` for emission). Electron diffraction tube (simple labelled SVG with rings).

### Board box
AQA includes the stopping potential and how it is measured; OCR uses LEDs to estimate h. All boards: same core
equations. Nothing else unless you can verify it.

### Links (verified)
explore: PhET `photoelectric`, `models-of-the-hydrogen-atom`.

## phys.nuclear: Nuclear physics
`@level a2` · `@paper CAIE P4 (A Level)` · `@time 6–8 hours` · `Next: [Medical physics](#phys.medical)`
Before you start: [Particle physics](#phys.particles) (nuclide notation, decay equations, u), [Capacitance](#phys.capacitance)
(exponential decay), [Logarithms and exponentials](#math.p3.logs). Warm-up: from Particles (complete a β⁻ equation),
Capacitance (x = x₀e^(−t/RC)), Quantum (convert MeV to J).

### Syllabus outcomes (cover every one)
- 23.1.1 understand the equivalence between energy and mass as represented by E = mc² and recall and use this equation
- 23.1.2 represent simple nuclear reactions by nuclear equations of the form ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + ¹₁H
- 23.1.3 define and use the terms mass defect and binding energy
- 23.1.4 sketch the variation of binding energy per nucleon with nucleon number
- 23.1.5 explain what is meant by nuclear fusion and nuclear fission
- 23.1.6 explain the relevance of binding energy per nucleon to nuclear reactions, including nuclear fusion and nuclear fission
- 23.1.7 calculate the energy released in nuclear reactions using E = c²Δm
- 23.2.1 understand that fluctuations in count rate provide evidence for the random nature of radioactive decay
- 23.2.2 understand that radioactive decay is both spontaneous and random
- 23.2.3 define activity and decay constant, and recall and use A = λN
- 23.2.4 define half-life
- 23.2.5 use λ = 0.693/t½
- 23.2.6 understand the exponential nature of radioactive decay, and sketch and use the relationship x = x₀e^(−λt), where x could represent activity, number of undecayed nuclei or received count rate

### Data
c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ (data sheet).
**Compute 1 u × c² from the data-sheet values** (1.494 × 10⁻¹⁰ J ≈ 934 MeV); do not quote 931.5 MeV unless a
question gives it, or the checks will disagree. Masses for questions (give them in the question): proton
1.007 276 u, neutron 1.008 665 u, ⁴₂He nucleus 4.001 506 u.

### Sections (suggested)
1. Mass–energy equivalence: E = mc²; mass and energy are two aspects of one conserved quantity; tiny mass changes in
   chemistry, large ones in nuclear reactions. Converting u ↔ kg ↔ J ↔ MeV.
2. Nuclear equations: balance A and Z (the syllabus example ¹⁴₇N + ⁴₂He → ¹⁷₈O + ¹₁H, Rutherford's first
   transmutation); neutron-induced fission of U-235 as a second example.
3. Mass defect and binding energy: the mass of a nucleus is less than the total mass of its separate nucleons;
   binding energy = Δm c² = the energy needed to separate the nucleus into its nucleons. Worked example for He-4
   (Δm = 0.030 376 u → ≈ 28.4 MeV with the data-sheet conversion; 7.1 MeV per nucleon).
4. Binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number: sketch (rises steeply through light nuclei with a spike at
   He-4, broad maximum ≈ 8.8 MeV near A ≈ 56 (iron/nickel), slow fall to ≈ 7.6 MeV for uranium). Larger BE per
   nucleon = more stable.
5. Fusion and fission: definitions; both release energy because the products have a higher binding energy per nucleon
   than the reactants (move towards the peak); energy released = (mass of reactants − mass of products)c². Fusion in
   the Sun (high temperature and pressure needed to overcome electrostatic repulsion); fission in reactors (chain
   reaction, neutrons). Energy released in a given reaction from given masses.
6. Radioactive decay is spontaneous (not affected by external conditions such as temperature or pressure) and
   random (cannot predict which nucleus decays or when; each nucleus has a constant probability per unit time).
   Evidence: count-rate fluctuations from a G-M tube and counter (repeat counts differ). Background count and
   subtracting it.
7. Activity A = λN (becquerel); decay constant (probability of decay per unit time); half-life; λ = 0.693/t½
   (`:::proof` plain from N = N₀e^(−λt) with N = N₀/2: ln 2 = λt½). Exponential decay x = x₀e^(−λt) for N, A or count
   rate; sketching (equal fractions in equal times); ln A against t is a straight line with gradient −λ (data analysis,
   link [Planning, analysis and evaluation](#phys.planning)). Embed **phys-halflife**. Contexts: carbon dating,
   medical tracers (technetium-99m, 6 h), smoke detectors (americium-241, 432 years), choosing a half-life for a job.

### Definitions (`:::def`, exam wording)
- Mass defect: "the difference between the total mass of the separate nucleons and the mass of the nucleus".
- Binding energy: "the minimum energy required to separate a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons" (equivalently, the energy released when the nucleus is formed from them).
- Binding energy per nucleon.
- Nuclear fusion: "the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus". Nuclear fission: "the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter nuclei of similar mass (and neutrons)".
- Spontaneous: "not affected by external factors such as temperature or pressure". Random: "it cannot be predicted when a particular nucleus will decay, but each nucleus has a constant probability of decay per unit time".
- Activity: "the number of nuclei that decay per unit time". Decay constant: "the probability that an individual nucleus will decay per unit time".
- Half-life: "the time taken for the number of undecayed nuclei (or the activity) of an isotope to fall to half of its initial value".

### Worked examples and question ideas
- Energy equivalent of 1.0 g of mass (9.0 × 10¹³ J).
- Binding energy and BE per nucleon of He-4 (as above); comparison with Fe-56 and U-235 (values given).
- Energy released in a D–T fusion or a U-235 fission from given masses; energy per kg compared with burning coal.
- Activity of 1.0 μg of a nuclide with given half-life (number of nuclei from the molar mass and N_A).
- Iodine-131 (t½ = 8.0 days): fraction left after 20 days (0.177); time for the activity to fall to 5%.
- Carbon dating: activity per gram now vs living (t½ = 5730 years).
- Exam practice ★→★★★ (6–8): define binding energy [2]; sketch the BE per nucleon curve and mark fusion/fission regions
  [3]; energy released in a reaction [4]; explain why fission releases energy using the curve [3]; define decay
  constant [1]; A = λN with a mass sample [4]; ★★★ ln(count rate) against t data: find λ and t½ with background
  correction [6]; ★★★ choosing a medical tracer: activity after a delay and dose considerations [5].
- Stretch: two-isotope sample with different half-lives (activity curve not a single exponential); or the
  energy released per nucleon in fusion vs fission from the curve.
- Spot the error: using the atomic mass (with electrons) for one item and the nuclear mass for another; or
  λ = 0.693 × t½.

### Pitfalls
Mass defect with nuclear vs atomic masses; MeV ↔ J; half-life of the activity equals that of N; "after two half-lives
it's all gone"; λ units (s⁻¹ vs year⁻¹: keep consistent); count rate vs activity.

### Figures
Binding energy per nucleon curve (hand SVG with plotted points/curve, Fe-56 peak labelled, arrows for fusion and
fission moving towards the peak). Decay curve `:::plot` of N₀e^(−λt) with half-lives marked (`--vline`), and ln A
against t straight line.

### Board box

requires the content above.

## Widget to build: `phys-photo` (widgets/phys-photo.js)
Embed in phys.quantum: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-photo" data-metal="sodium" data-lambda="400"></div>`.
- Metals (label "approximate work functions"): caesium 2.1 eV, potassium 2.3 eV, sodium 2.3 eV, calcium 2.9 eV,
  zinc 4.3 eV, copper 4.7 eV. Attribute `data-metal`, `data-lambda` (100–800 nm), `data-i` (intensity 0–100%).
- Controls: metal select, wavelength slider (the light beam coloured by wavelength when visible, labelled "UV" or
  "IR" otherwise), intensity slider.
- Canvas 1: a metal plate in a tube with photons arriving (number per frame ∝ intensity) and electrons leaving when
  hf > Φ, with speed ∝ √KE_max (random fraction of max for each electron); no electrons below threshold whatever
  the intensity (the key lesson).
- Canvas 2: KE_max (eV) against frequency (10¹⁴ Hz) for the chosen metal: straight line of gradient h from f₀, with
  the current point; a faint line for another metal for comparison.
- Readout: photon energy (J and eV), f, threshold frequency and wavelength, KE_max (eV, J), v_max, relative
  current (∝ intensity, or zero).
- Reduced motion: static picture with arrows.

## Widget to build: `phys-halflife` (widgets/phys-halflife.js)
Embed in phys.nuclear: `<div class="widget" data-w="phys-halflife" data-n0="400" data-thalf="5"></div>`.
- Attributes: `data-n0` (number of nuclei, 50–400), `data-thalf` (half-life in arbitrary time units, 1–20).
- A grid of N₀ dots (nuclei) that decay at random with probability λΔt per step (seeded `W.rng`), changing colour when
  decayed; a canvas graph of N against t showing the simulated count (steps) over the smooth curve N₀e^(−λt), with
  dashed markers at t½, 2t½, 3t½ and the N₀/2, N₀/4, N₀/8 levels.
- Controls: Run/Pause, Reset (new seed), N₀ and t½ sliders, a time cursor.
- Readout: λ = 0.693/t½, N at the cursor (smooth model) and the simulated count, activity A = λN, fraction remaining.
- `.w-note`: "Each run is different (decay is random), but the larger N₀ is, the closer the steps follow the smooth
  curve."
- Reduced motion: compute the run instantly and draw the final state.
