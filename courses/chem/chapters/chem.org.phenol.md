You will learn how phenol is made, why it is a weak acid but stronger than water and ethanol, how to rank carboxylic acids, phenols, water and alcohols (and chloro-substituted acids) by acidity, why phenol's ring reacts under mild conditions, and how phenol couples with diazonium salts to make azo dyes.

:::why
Phenol is a raw material for plastics and medicines, and it was one of the first antiseptics: in the 1860s Joseph Lister used carbolic acid, which is phenol, to keep surgical wounds clean. It is also the best example of how an OH group and a benzene ring change each other. The OH group makes the ring far more reactive, and the ring makes the OH group acidic. The same idea explains the ranking of acids, the speed of ring reactions and the colour of azo dyes.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know the structure and reactions of benzene from [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes), why alcohols are weaker acids than water from [Alcohols](#chem.org.alcohols), and what Ka and pKa mean from [pH, buffers, solubility product and partition](#chem.acids2).

:::try Warm-up
Q: Why is ethanol a weaker acid than water? (from [Alcohols](#chem.org.alcohols))
A: The ethyl group pushes electron density onto the oxygen of the ethoxide ion, \(\ce{CH3CH2O-}\), making the negative charge more intense and the ion less stable than \(\ce{OH-}\).
Q: In which positions does an \(\ce{-OH}\) or \(\ce{-NH2}\) group direct an incoming electrophile, and does it activate or deactivate the ring? (from [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes))
A: The 2- and 4-positions (and 6). It activates the ring, which reacts faster.
Q: A weak acid HA has pKa 4.76. What is its Ka? (from [pH, buffers, solubility product and partition](#chem.acids2))
A: \(K_a = 10^{-4.76} = 1.74 \times 10^{-5}\) mol dm⁻³. A smaller pKa means a larger Ka and a stronger acid.
:::

## What is phenol?

Phenol, \(\ce{C6H5OH}\), has an OH group bonded **directly to a benzene ring**. That makes it quite different from an alcohol, in which OH is bonded to a saturated carbon of an alkyl group. In the name, phenol is the stem and the carbon carrying the OH group is carbon 1 (see the nine compounds in [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes)).

:::def Phenol
A compound in which a hydroxyl group is bonded directly to a carbon atom of a benzene ring. The simplest example is phenol itself, \(\ce{C6H5OH}\).
:::

Pure phenol is a colourless crystalline solid that is only slightly soluble in water, because the large ring is non-polar even though the OH group forms hydrogen bonds. Phenol is toxic and damages skin on contact, so it is handled with gloves and goggles. Other phenols have two or more OH groups or other groups on the ring, and naphthol, which has two fused rings, is met later in this chapter.

## Making phenol from phenylamine

Phenol is made in the laboratory from phenylamine, \(\ce{C6H5NH2}\). The route is in two steps, and the details of phenylamine itself are in [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen).

**Step 1: diazotisation.** Phenylamine is treated with nitrous acid, \(\ce{HNO2}\), at a temperature **below 10 °C**. Nitrous acid is unstable, so it is made in the mixture from sodium nitrite and dilute hydrochloric acid. The product is a **benzenediazonium salt**:

\[\ce{NaNO2 + HCl -> HNO2 + NaCl}\]

\[\ce{C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl -> C6H5N2+Cl- + 2H2O}\]

**Step 2: warming with water.** The diazonium salt in water is warmed. The N₂ group leaves as nitrogen gas and is replaced by OH:

\[\ce{C6H5N2+ + H2O -> C6H5OH + N2 + H+}\]

The first step must be kept cold because a diazonium salt is unstable above about 10 °C: it reacts with water at once and the yield of useful product falls. In the second step, that same instability is put to use.

:::steps
1. Benzene is nitrated (concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) and concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C) to nitrobenzene.
2. Nitrobenzene is reduced (hot tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, then NaOH(aq)) to phenylamine.
3. Phenylamine, \(\ce{NaNO2}\) and dilute \(\ce{HCl}\) below 10 °C give the benzenediazonium salt.
4. Warming the salt with water gives phenol and nitrogen.
:::

## Phenol is a weak acid

An alcohol is hardly acidic at all, but phenol is. It is still a **weak acid**: it is only slightly ionised in water. Its reactions show this.

**With sodium hydroxide.** Phenol is only slightly soluble in water, but it dissolves in NaOH(aq) to give a colourless solution of sodium phenoxide, because the acid–base reaction goes to completion. Alcohols do not react with NaOH(aq).

\[\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5ONa + H2O}\]

**With sodium metal.** Sodium reacts with phenol (molten, or dissolved in an inert solvent) to give sodium phenoxide and bubbles of hydrogen, as it does with water and alcohols:

\[\ce{2C6H5OH + 2Na -> 2C6H5ONa + H2}\]

**With carbonates.** Phenol is **too weak an acid** to react with sodium carbonate or sodium hydrogencarbonate to release carbon dioxide. A carboxylic acid does, so this is a simple way to tell the two apart: add the compound to sodium carbonate solution; a carboxylic acid fizzes, and phenol does not.

:::key Reactions that show phenol is acidic
Phenol reacts with Na(s) (giving \(\ce{H2}\)) and with NaOH(aq), forming sodium phenoxide, \(\ce{C6H5O^-Na+}\). It does not react with \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) or \(\ce{NaHCO3}\) to give \(\ce{CO2}\).
:::

:::warn Phenol is not an alcohol
Phenol has an OH group, but it does not behave as an alcohol: it is not neutral, and it does not give an aldehyde, ketone or acid on oxidation. Write "phenol", never "an aromatic alcohol". Its acidity is also not explained by "OH is more polar". The explanation is about the stability of the phenoxide ion, as below.
:::

:::eg Write equations for the reactions of phenol with (a) aqueous sodium hydroxide and (b) sodium metal, and state one observation in each.
(a) \(\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5ONa + H2O}\). The sparingly soluble phenol dissolves to give a colourless solution. [B1]

(b) \(\ce{2C6H5OH + 2Na -> 2C6H5ONa + H2}\). Bubbles of hydrogen gas are given off. [B1]
:::

## Why phenol is a stronger acid than water and ethanol

An acid \(\ce{HA}\) loses \(\ce{H+}\) to leave an anion, and the position of the equilibrium depends on how stable the anion is. The more stable the anion, the more the equilibrium lies to the right, and the stronger the acid. For phenol:

\[\ce{C6H5OH <=> C6H5O- + H+}\]

In the phenoxide ion the negative charge sits on the oxygen atom, as in the ethoxide ion. But oxygen in phenoxide is bonded to a benzene ring. A lone pair in a p orbital on the oxygen atom lies parallel to the p orbitals of the ring, so it overlaps sideways with them and is partly drawn into the **delocalised π system**.

In the phenoxide ion, then, the negative charge is **spread over the oxygen atom and the ring** instead of being concentrated on oxygen. A charge that is spread out is more stable than a charge held on one atom. The phenoxide ion is therefore more stable than the ethoxide ion (where the alkyl group pushes charge onto oxygen and makes it worse), so phenol loses \(\ce{H+}\) more readily than ethanol does.

:::key Why phenol is a stronger acid than ethanol and water
The phenoxide ion is stabilised because a lone pair on oxygen overlaps with the ring's π system, delocalising the negative charge over the ring. The alkoxide ion is destabilised by the electron-donating alkyl group. Order: phenol > water > ethanol.
:::

Water has no alkyl group to push electrons onto its oxygen, so the hydroxide ion is less destabilised than ethoxide: water is a stronger acid than ethanol, and phenol is stronger than both. The acid strengths of phenol, water and ethanol are, however, still far below those of carboxylic acids.

Phenol is still weak. With a pKa of about 10, in 0.1 mol dm⁻³ solution only a tiny fraction of the molecules are ionised. Ethanol and water, with pKa values close to 16, are about a million times weaker still.

## Ranking carboxylic acids, phenols, water and alcohols

The full order of acid strength is:

\[\text{carboxylic acid} > \text{phenol} > \text{water} > \text{alcohol}\]

| Compound | Anion left behind | What happens to the negative charge | Acidity |
|---|---|---|---|
| ethanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) | ethanoate \(\ce{CH3COO-}\) | shared equally by **two** oxygen atoms | strongest (pKa 4.76) |
| phenol, \(\ce{C6H5OH}\) | phenoxide \(\ce{C6H5O-}\) | spread over the oxygen and the ring | weaker (pKa about 10) |
| water, \(\ce{H2O}\) | hydroxide \(\ce{OH-}\) | on one oxygen, no alkyl group | weaker still |
| ethanol, \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) | ethoxide \(\ce{CH3CH2O-}\) | on one oxygen, made worse by the alkyl group | weakest (pKa about 16) |

In the carboxylate ion the charge is delocalised across the O–C–O group: both C–O bonds are the same length, and each oxygen carries half of the charge. Two oxygen atoms, both electronegative, hold the charge between them, and this stabilises the anion much more than the ring does in phenoxide, where the charge is shared with carbon atoms. That is why carboxylic acids are stronger than phenols. You can see this ranking in what the compounds do:

| Reagent | Ethanol | Water | Phenol | Ethanoic acid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na(s), gives \(\ce{H2}\) | yes (slowly) | yes | yes | yes |
| NaOH(aq) | no | not applicable | yes | yes |
| \(\ce{Na2CO3}\), gives \(\ce{CO2}\) | no | no | **no** | yes |

:::eg (Exam style) Place the following in order of increasing acidity, and explain the order: ethanol, phenol, ethanoic acid, water. [4 marks]
Increasing acidity: ethanol < water < phenol < ethanoic acid. [B1]

Ethanol is weakest because the alkyl group donates electrons to the oxygen and destabilises the ethoxide ion, \(\ce{CH3CH2O-}\). [B1]

Phenol is stronger than water because a lone pair on the oxygen of phenoxide overlaps with the π system of the ring, delocalising the charge. [B1]

Ethanoic acid is the strongest because the charge in the ethanoate ion is delocalised equally over two oxygen atoms. [B1]
:::

### Chlorine-substituted carboxylic acids

Replace hydrogen atoms in the \(\ce{CH3}\) group of ethanoic acid with chlorine, and the acid gets stronger:

| Acid | pKa (approximate) |
|---|---|
| ethanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) | 4.76 |
| chloroethanoic acid, \(\ce{CH2ClCOOH}\) | 2.86 |
| dichloroethanoic acid, \(\ce{CHCl2COOH}\) | about 1.3 |
| trichloroethanoic acid, \(\ce{CCl3COOH}\) | about 0.7 |

Only the order matters for the exam, and it follows from the stability of the carboxylate ion. Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon, so each chlorine atom **withdraws electron density** along the σ bonds (a negative inductive effect). This pulls charge away from the \(\ce{COO-}\) group and spreads the negative charge further, which stabilises the anion. The more chlorine atoms, and the nearer they are to the \(\ce{COOH}\) group, the stronger the effect and the stronger the acid.

:::key Acidity of chlorine-substituted ethanoic acids
\(\ce{CH3COOH} < \ce{CH2ClCOOH} < \ce{CHCl2COOH} < \ce{CCl3COOH}\) in acid strength. Chlorine is electron-withdrawing, so it spreads the negative charge of the carboxylate ion and stabilises it. More Cl atoms, or Cl nearer the COOH group, give a stronger acid.
:::

Notice that this is the opposite of the alkyl group, which donates electrons and makes an acid weaker: ethanoic acid is weaker than methanoic acid for the same reason that ethanol is weaker than water.

:::eg (Exam style) Chloroethanoic acid has pKa 2.86 and ethanoic acid has pKa 4.76. Calculate by what factor chloroethanoic acid has the larger \(K_a\), and explain why it is the stronger acid.
\(K_a = 10^{-\mathrm{p}K_a}\), so the ratio is \(\dfrac{K_a(\ce{CH2ClCOOH})}{K_a(\ce{CH3COOH})} = 10^{4.76 - 2.86} = 10^{1.90}\). [M1]

The ratio is 79, so chloroethanoic acid has a \(K_a\) about 79 times as large. [A1]

The electronegative chlorine atom withdraws electron density, spreading the negative charge on the carboxylate ion and stabilising it, so the acid ionises more. [B1]
:::

:::eg Place in order of increasing acid strength: \(\ce{CH3CHClCOOH}\), \(\ce{CH3CH2COOH}\), \(\ce{CH2ClCH2COOH}\). Explain the order.
\(\ce{CH3CH2COOH} < \ce{CH2ClCH2COOH} < \ce{CH3CHClCOOH}\). [B1]

Chlorine withdraws electrons and stabilises the carboxylate ion. In \(\ce{CH3CHClCOOH}\) the chlorine is on the carbon next to \(\ce{COOH}\), and in \(\ce{CH2ClCH2COOH}\) it is one carbon further away, so the effect is weaker there. \(\ce{CH3CH2COOH}\) has no chlorine at all. [B1]
:::

## The ring of phenol is very reactive

The same lone pair that stabilises the phenoxide ion has another effect. In phenol, the oxygen lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring, so the ring has a **higher electron density**, especially at the 2-, 4- and 6-positions (the positions next to, and opposite to, the OH group). The OH group is an **activating** group that directs to those positions, as you saw in [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes).

:::html

:::

A ring that is richer in electrons is attacked by weaker electrophiles. Phenol needs **no catalyst** and much milder conditions than benzene:

| Reaction | Benzene | Phenol |
|---|---|---|
| Bromination | \(\ce{Br2}\) with an \(\ce{AlBr3}\) catalyst | \(\ce{Br2}\)(aq), room temperature, no catalyst |
| Nitration | concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) + concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C | dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\)(aq), room temperature |
| Product positions | one substituent | 2-, 4- (and 6-) positions |

### Bromine water

When bromine water is added to a solution of phenol, the **orange bromine water is decolourised** and a **white precipitate** of 2,4,6-tribromophenol forms at once. All three activated positions are substituted without any catalyst, and the product is so poorly soluble that it separates at once:

\[\ce{C6H5OH + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr}\]

2,4,6-Tribromophenol has Br atoms at carbons 2, 4 and 6 and the OH at carbon 1 (see the figure in [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes)). Benzene gives no reaction with bromine water at all, which is a good illustration of the activating effect of OH.

### Nitration

With **dilute nitric acid at room temperature**, phenol is nitrated to a mixture of 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol. Benzene needs a concentrated mixture of both acids and a higher temperature to do the same job.

\[\ce{C6H5OH + HNO3 -> C6H4(NO2)OH + H2O}\]

With concentrated nitric acid, all three activated positions are nitrated and 2,4,6-trinitrophenol (picric acid) forms.

:::eg Describe what you would see when bromine water is added to phenol, and give the equation.
The orange colour of the bromine water disappears (the solution becomes colourless), [B1] and a white precipitate forms. [B1]

\[\ce{C6H5OH + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr}\] [B1]

The precipitate is 2,4,6-tribromophenol. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why phenol reacts with bromine water at room temperature but benzene does not. [3 marks]
A lone pair of electrons on the oxygen of the OH group overlaps with (is delocalised into) the π system of the ring. [B1]

This increases the electron density of the ring, so the ring is more strongly attracted to (and polarises) bromine molecules. [B1]

Phenol is therefore attacked by bromine without a catalyst. In benzene the ring is less electron-rich, so a catalyst is needed to make a strong electrophile. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 4.70 g of phenol is treated with an excess of bromine water. Calculate the maximum mass of 2,4,6-tribromophenol that could be formed.
\(M_r(\ce{C6H5OH}) = 6(12.0) + 6(1.0) + 16.0 = 94.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C6H2Br3OH}) = 6(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 3(79.9) + 16.0 = 330.7\). [B1]

\(n(\ce{phenol}) = 4.70 / 94.0 = 0.0500\) mol, and 1 mol of phenol gives 1 mol of product. [M1]

Maximum mass \(= 0.0500 \times 330.7 = 16.5\) g. [A1]
:::

## Azo coupling and dyes

Phenol is the nucleophilic partner in a reaction that makes **azo compounds**, which are the basis of the dyes that colour textiles, food and inks. In **coupling**, a benzenediazonium salt reacts with phenol dissolved in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C. The diazonium ion, \(\ce{C6H5N2+}\), is a weak electrophile, so it needs a very reactive ring. In sodium hydroxide the phenol is changed to the phenoxide ion, whose O⁻ group is a much stronger electron donor than OH, so the ring is activated enough to react. The diazonium ion attacks the ring at the **4-position**, opposite the OH group, and the two rings become joined by an **azo group**, \(\ce{-N=N-}\).

The product, 4-hydroxyazobenzene, \(\ce{HOC6H4N=NC6H5}\), is a yellow or orange compound. In an azo compound the π systems of the two rings are joined through the \(\ce{N=N}\) group into one large **delocalised system**, which absorbs light in the visible region, and the compound appears coloured. Many such azo compounds are used as dyes, and changing the groups on either ring changes the colour. Dyes are made in the same way from other coupling partners.

### Naphthol

The syllabus asks you to apply this to other phenols. 2-Naphthol has two fused rings with an OH group on carbon 2. It couples with benzenediazonium salts in the same way, at carbon 1 next to the OH group, to give an intensely coloured orange-red azo dye.

The same reasoning works for any phenol: find the free 2-, 4- and 6-positions relative to each OH group, and remember that the OH group still makes the compound a weak acid.

:::eg (Exam style) Predict the organic product when (a) 4-methylphenol, \(\ce{CH3C6H4OH}\), is shaken with excess bromine water, (b) 2-naphthol is shaken with NaOH(aq), and (c) the solution from (b) is added to benzenediazonium chloride below 10 °C. [5 marks]
(a) The OH group activates the 2-, 4- and 6-positions, but position 4 already carries the \(\ce{CH3}\) group, so only positions 2 and 6 are substituted. The product is 2,6-dibromo-4-methylphenol. [B1] The bromine water is decolourised. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3C6H4OH + 2Br2 -> CH3C6H2Br2OH + 2HBr}\] [B1]
(b) 2-Naphthol is a phenol, so it is acidic enough to react with the alkali: it dissolves as sodium 2-naphthoxide, \(\ce{C10H7ONa}\), and water. [B1]
(c) The diazonium ion attacks carbon 1, next to the OH group, and an orange-red azo dye forms, \(\ce{C6H5N=NC10H6OH}\). [B1]
:::

:::key Azo coupling
Benzenediazonium salt + phenol (or 2-naphthol) in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C → azo compound, Ar–N=N–Ar′, coloured (yellow or orange for phenol). The diazonium ion attacks the 4-position of phenol (opposite OH). The azo group joins the two rings into one delocalised system that absorbs visible light.
:::

An azo compound (4-hydroxyazobenzene), formed by coupling. [B1]

Q: Give the reagents and conditions for each step of the conversion of phenylamine into phenol.
A: \(\ce{NaNO2}\) and dilute \(\ce{HCl}\) (making \(\ce{HNO2}\)), below 10 °C, to give the benzenediazonium salt; then warm the salt with water.
Q: Write the equation for the reaction of phenol with sodium hydroxide, and name the organic product.
A: \(\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5ONa + H2O}\). Sodium phenoxide.
Q: A solution of phenol and a solution of ethanoic acid are tested with sodium carbonate. Which one gives carbon dioxide?
A: Ethanoic acid. Phenol is too weak an acid to release \(\ce{CO2}\) from a carbonate.
Q: Name the product of phenol with excess bromine water and state two observations.
A: 2,4,6-tribromophenol. The orange bromine colour disappears and a white precipitate forms.
Q: Name the products of the reaction of phenol with dilute nitric acid at room temperature.
A: 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol.
Q: Put in order of increasing acid strength: \(\ce{CCl3COOH}\), \(\ce{CH3COOH}\), \(\ce{CH2ClCOOH}\).
A: \(\ce{CH3COOH} < \ce{CH2ClCOOH} < \ce{CCl3COOH}\). More chlorine atoms withdraw more electron density and stabilise the anion more.
Q: The \(K_a\) of phenol is \(1.0 \times 10^{-10}\) mol dm⁻³. Calculate the pH of a 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution. (A Level link: [pH, buffers, solubility product and partition](#chem.acids2))
A: For a weak acid \([\ce{H+}] = \sqrt{K_a c} = \sqrt{1.0 \times 10^{-10} \times 0.100} = 3.16 \times 10^{-6}\) mol dm⁻³, so pH \(= -\log_{10}(3.16 \times 10^{-6}) = 5.50\).
Q: Why is a solution of benzenediazonium chloride kept below 10 °C?
A: Above that temperature it reacts with water, giving phenol and nitrogen, so the diazonium ion is lost before it can be used.
:::

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [2 marks] ★ Write an equation for the reaction between phenol and sodium metal, and state what you would see.
A: \(\ce{2C6H5OH + 2Na -> 2C6H5ONa + H2}\) [B1]
Bubbles of gas (hydrogen) and the sodium disappears. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ Describe a chemical test that distinguishes phenol from ethanoic acid, with the result for each.
A: Add sodium carbonate solution (or sodium hydrogencarbonate) to each. [B1] Ethanoic acid gives effervescence (\(\ce{CO2}\)). [B1] Phenol gives no effervescence. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain why phenol is a stronger acid than ethanol but a weaker acid than ethanoic acid.
H: Compare the three anions. Where can each carry its negative charge?
A: In the phenoxide ion a lone pair on oxygen overlaps with the π system of the ring, delocalising the charge over the ring. [B1]
In the ethoxide ion the charge stays on one oxygen atom and the electron-donating alkyl group destabilises it. [B1] So phenol is more acidic than ethanol. [B1]
In the ethanoate ion the charge is delocalised equally over two electronegative oxygen atoms, which is more stable than phenoxide. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ 2.35 g of phenol is treated with excess bromine water and 7.31 g of 2,4,6-tribromophenol is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.
A: \(M_r(\ce{C6H5OH}) = 94.0\), \(M_r(\ce{C6H2Br3OH}) = 330.7\). [B1]
\(n = 2.35 / 94.0 = 0.0250\) mol [M1]
Maximum mass \(= 0.0250 \times 330.7 = 8.27\) g [M1]
Yield \(= 7.31 / 8.27 \times 100 = 88.4\,\%\) [A1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ Calculate the volume of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ bromine water needed to react completely with 0.235 g of phenol.
H: How many moles of \(\ce{Br2}\) react with one mole of phenol?
A: \(n(\ce{phenol}) = 0.235 / 94.0 = 0.00250\) mol; 3 mol of \(\ce{Br2}\) react with each mole of phenol, so \(n(\ce{Br2}) = 0.00750\) mol. [M1]
Volume \(= 0.00750 / 0.0500 = 0.150\) dm³ [M1] \(= 150\) cm³. [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain why (a) phenol reacts with dilute nitric acid at room temperature whereas benzene needs a concentrated mixture of nitric and sulfuric acids at a higher temperature, and (b) the nitrophenols formed are 2- and 4-nitrophenol.
A: (a) A lone pair on oxygen is delocalised into the ring, increasing its electron density, so the ring is more reactive towards electrophiles. [B1] A weaker electrophile (from dilute acid) is enough. [B1]
(b) The OH group directs incoming groups to the 2-, 4- and 6-positions, so the product is the 2- and 4-isomer. [B1] [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain why trichloroethanoic acid, \(\ce{CCl3COOH}\), is a stronger acid than ethanoic acid, and why 2-chloropropanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CHClCOOH}\), is a stronger acid than 3-chloropropanoic acid, \(\ce{CH2ClCH2COOH}\).
H: Think about what chlorine does to the carboxylate ion, and how its effect changes with distance.
A: Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon, so it withdraws electron density along the σ bonds (negative inductive effect). [B1]
This spreads the negative charge of the carboxylate ion and stabilises it. [B1] Three chlorine atoms stabilise it much more than none, so \(\ce{CCl3COOH}\) is the stronger acid. [B1]
The effect weakens with distance: Cl on the carbon next to COOH withdraws more than Cl one carbon further away, so 2-chloropropanoic acid is stronger. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Phenylamine is converted into the yellow-orange azo compound \(\ce{HOC6H4N=NC6H5}\) in two steps. (a) Give the reagents and conditions for step 1, and name the organic product. (b) Give the reagent and conditions for step 2, and name the type of reaction.
H: Step 1 forms a diazonium salt. Step 2 happens in alkaline solution.
A: (a) \(\ce{NaNO2}\) and dilute \(\ce{HCl}\) (which make \(\ce{HNO2}\)) [B1] at a temperature below 10 °C [B1]. The product is benzenediazonium chloride (a diazonium salt). [B1]
(b) Phenol dissolved in NaOH(aq) [B1] at a temperature below 10 °C. [B1] The reaction is coupling (electrophilic substitution on the ring of phenol, at the 4-position). [B1]
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ 4-nitrophenol is a stronger acid than phenol, whereas 4-methylphenol is a weaker acid than phenol. Explain both observations in terms of the stability of the anion.
H: The nitro group withdraws electrons from the ring; the methyl group donates them.
A: The \(\ce{NO2}\) group withdraws electron density from the ring, helping to spread the negative charge of the phenoxide ion further. [B1] This stabilises the anion, so 4-nitrophenol ionises more and is the stronger acid. [B1] The \(\ce{CH3}\) group donates electron density, which intensifies the charge on the ring and oxygen and destabilises the anion. [B1] So 4-methylphenol is a weaker acid. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A compound Y, \(\ce{C6H6O}\), dissolves in NaOH(aq), gives a white precipitate with bromine water and does not fizz with sodium carbonate. Compound Z, \(\ce{C7H8O}\), does not react with NaOH(aq) but reacts with sodium metal to give hydrogen, and does not decolourise bromine water. Identify Y and Z and explain each result.
H: Think of one phenol and one alcohol in which the OH group is not on the ring.
A: Y is phenol. [B1] It is acidic enough to react with NaOH but too weak to react with sodium carbonate. [B1] The ring is activated by the OH group, so bromine water gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. [B1]
Z is phenylmethanol, \(\ce{C6H5CH2OH}\), an alcohol whose OH is not on the ring. [B1] It is not acidic enough for NaOH, reacts with Na like an alcohol, and the ring is not activated, so bromine water is not decolourised. [B1]
:::

:::recap
- Phenol, \(\ce{C6H5OH}\): OH bonded directly to a benzene ring. Made from phenylamine: \(\ce{NaNO2}\) + dilute \(\ce{HCl}\) below 10 °C gives the diazonium salt; warming with water gives phenol + \(\ce{N2}\).
- Phenol is a weak acid: reacts with Na(s) (\(\ce{H2}\)) and NaOH(aq) (sodium phenoxide + water), not with carbonates (no \(\ce{CO2}\)).
- The phenoxide ion is stabilised by delocalisation of the oxygen lone pair into the ring. Acidity: carboxylic acid > phenol > water > alcohol. The ethoxide ion is destabilised by the alkyl group; the carboxylate ion shares its charge equally over two oxygen atoms.
- Chlorine withdraws electrons: \(\ce{CH3COOH} < \ce{CH2ClCOOH} < \ce{CHCl2COOH} < \ce{CCl3COOH}\); more Cl or Cl nearer COOH means a stronger acid.
- The oxygen lone pair activates the ring and directs to the 2-, 4- and 6-positions: bromine water (room temperature, no catalyst) gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol; dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\) (room temperature) gives 2- and 4-nitrophenol.
- Azo coupling: benzenediazonium salt + phenol (or 2-naphthol) in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C, gives a coloured azo compound with the –N=N– group, at the 4-position of phenol.
:::
