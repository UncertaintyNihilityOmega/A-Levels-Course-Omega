Q: Propan-1-ol is heated under reflux with an excess of acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Name the organic product. (from [Alcohols](#chem.org.alcohols))
A: Propanoic acid. Under reflux the aldehyde formed first cannot escape and is oxidised further.

A: It is only partly ionised in water: most molecules stay as \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) and only a few give \(\ce{H+}\) and \(\ce{CH3COO-}\), unlike a strong acid such as HCl, which ionises completely.

## The carboxyl group and naming

A carboxylic acid has the **carboxyl group**, \(\ce{-COOH}\): a carbonyl group, C=O, and a hydroxyl group, OH, on the same carbon atom. The name comes from the two parts, *carbonyl* and *hydroxyl*.

:::def Carboxylic acid
A compound containing the carboxyl group, \(\ce{-COOH}\). The general formula of a saturated acid with one carboxyl group is \(\ce{RCOOH}\), where R is an alkyl group or H.
:::

:::def Ester
A compound with the group \(\ce{-COO-}\), in which the hydrogen of a carboxylic acid is replaced by an alkyl group: the general formula is \(\ce{RCOOR'}\).
:::

Carboxylic acids are named with the ending **-oic acid**, and the carbon of the COOH group is always carbon 1. The salt of an acid, formed when the \(\ce{H+}\) is lost, has the ending **-oate**.

| Acid | Formula | Carboxylate ion | Salt with sodium |
|---|---|---|---|
| methanoic acid | \(\ce{HCOOH}\) | \(\ce{HCOO-}\) | sodium methanoate |
| ethanoic acid | \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) | \(\ce{CH3COO-}\) | sodium ethanoate |
| propanoic acid | \(\ce{CH3CH2COOH}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2COO-}\) | sodium propanoate |
| butanoic acid | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COO-}\) | sodium butanoate |

### Naming esters

An ester has two parts. It is made from an acid and an alcohol, and its name has two words in the same order: the **alkyl group of the alcohol** first, then the **-oate** from the acid. The acid part contains the C=O carbon.

So \(\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}\) is **ethyl ethanoate** (ethanoic acid part, ethyl from ethanol), \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\) is methyl propanoate and \(\ce{HCOOCH2CH2CH3}\) is propyl methanoate. Reading the formula backwards gives a different ester: \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\) and \(\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}\) are isomers (both \(\ce{C4H8O2}\)), but methyl propanoate and ethyl ethanoate are different compounds.

:::warn Esters the wrong way round
The name starts with the group on the **oxygen** atom (from the alcohol) and ends with the part that contains the C=O (from the acid). \(\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}\) is ethyl ethanoate, never "ethanoyl ethanoate" or "ethanoate ethyl". To check, ask which side of the \(\ce{-COO-}\) the C=O carbon is on: that side is the acid.
:::

(a) The C=O carbon is on the left with three carbons before the oxygen: butanoate. The group on the oxygen is \(\ce{CH3}\): methyl butanoate. [B1]

(b) The acid part is \(\ce{CH3CO-}\), ethanoate. The group on the oxygen is propyl: propyl ethanoate. [B1]

(c) Propan-1-ol gives the "propyl" and ethanoic acid gives "ethanoate": propyl ethanoate, \(\ce{CH3COOCH2CH2CH3}\). The same ester as (b). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2CH2OH <=>[H2SO4] CH3COOCH2CH2CH3 + H2O}\]
:::

## Physical properties

Carboxylic acids have an O–H bond and a C=O bond. They form strong hydrogen bonds with each other, and two molecules can link as a pair with two hydrogen bonds between them. The result is a high boiling point for the size of the molecule. Esters have no O–H, so their molecules cannot hydrogen-bond to each other. Their only attractions are pd-pd and id-id forces, and they are volatile.

| Compound | \(M_r\) | Forces between its molecules | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| ethanol | 46.0 | hydrogen bonding | 78 |
| ethanoic acid | 60.0 | hydrogen bonding (stronger, pairs of molecules) | 118 |
| propanoic acid | 74.0 | hydrogen bonding | 141 |
| methyl ethanoate | 74.0 | pd-pd and id-id only | 57 |

Propanoic acid and methyl ethanoate have the same relative molecular mass and the same molecular formula, \(\ce{C3H6O2}\), but their boiling points differ by 84 °C. That is the direct effect of hydrogen bonding. The small acids (up to butanoic) mix with water, since their COOH group hydrogen-bonds to water molecules. Esters of low \(M_r\) dissolve only a little.

## Making carboxylic acids

There are three routes to learn, and each starts from a different family:

| Route | Starting compound | Reagent and conditions |
|---|---|---|
| (a) oxidation | primary alcohol or aldehyde | acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) or \(\ce{KMnO4}\), heat under reflux with excess oxidant |
| (b) hydrolysis | nitrile | dilute acid (or dilute alkali) and heat, then acidify |
| (c) hydrolysis | ester | dilute acid (or dilute alkali) and heat, then acidify |

**(a) Oxidation.** Reflux makes any aldehyde formed drip back into the flask, so it is oxidised all the way to the acid ([Alcohols](#chem.org.alcohols)).

\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3COOH + H2O}\]
\[\ce{CH3CHO + [O] -> CH3COOH}\]

The orange dichromate turns green. This is the reaction that turns wine into vinegar when air oxidises the ethanol (with the help of enzymes), and the aldehyde route is the second stage of the alcohol route in [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl).

**(b) Hydrolysis of nitriles** and **(c) hydrolysis of esters** are covered in their own sections below. In both, an alkali gives the salt of the acid, and you must **acidify** (add a dilute strong acid) to release the free carboxylic acid.

## Reactions of carboxylic acids

### Acid reactions

In water, a carboxylic acid is a **weak acid**: it is partly ionised, and the equilibrium lies well to the left.

\[\ce{CH3COOH(aq) <=> CH3COO-(aq) + H+(aq)}\]

It still shows the typical reactions of an acid with metals, alkalis and carbonates. In every case the product is a carboxylate salt.

:::key Carboxylic acids as acids
| Reacts with | Products | Example |
|---|---|---|
| reactive metal, e.g. Mg | salt + hydrogen (a redox reaction) | \(\ce{2CH3COOH + Mg -> (CH3COO)2Mg + H2}\) |
| alkali, e.g. NaOH | salt + water | \(\ce{CH3COOH + NaOH -> CH3COONa + H2O}\) |
| carbonate, e.g. \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) | salt + water + carbon dioxide | \(\ce{2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 -> 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2}\) |
:::

With magnesium, the metal is oxidised and \(\ce{H+}\) is reduced: \(\ce{Mg + 2H+ -> Mg^2+ + H2}\), so the reaction is a redox reaction as well as an acid reaction. The same acid with calcium carbonate is the descaling reaction in a kettle. In ionic form the carbonate equation is \(\ce{2CH3COOH + CO3^2- -> 2CH3COO- + H2O + CO2}\), and with a hydrogencarbonate it is \(\ce{CH3COOH + HCO3- -> CH3COO- + H2O + CO2}\).

:::tip The test for a carboxylic acid
Add sodium carbonate solution (or sodium hydrogencarbonate) to the compound. **Effervescence** of carbon dioxide, which turns limewater cloudy, shows a carboxylic acid (or a stronger acid). Alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and esters give no fizz. This test separates a carboxylic acid from an alcohol that also gives hydrogen with sodium.
:::

If you add the same amounts of ethanoic acid and hydrochloric acid of equal concentration to magnesium, the ethanoic acid reacts more slowly, because fewer \(\ce{H+}\) ions are present at the start. Both eventually give the same volume of hydrogen, because the weak acid is replaced as it is used up. Why a carboxylic acid is more acidic than an alcohol or phenol is explained at A Level in [Phenol and the acidity of organic compounds](#chem.org.phenol).

:::eg Write an equation for the reaction between ethanoic acid and sodium carbonate, then calculate the volume of carbon dioxide at room conditions given off when 5.00 g of ethanoic acid reacts with excess sodium carbonate.
\[\ce{2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 -> 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2}\] [B1]
\(M_r(\ce{CH3COOH}) = 2(12.0) + 4(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 60.0\) [B1]
\[n(\ce{CH3COOH}) = \frac{5.00}{60.0} = 0.08333\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
2 mol of acid gives 1 mol of \(\ce{CO2}\), so \(n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.08333 / 2 = 0.04167\,\mathrm{mol}\). [M1]
\[V = 0.04167 \times 24.0 = 1.00\,\mathrm{dm^3}\] [A1]
(The molar volume of a gas at room conditions is 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.)
:::

### Reduction

Lithium tetrahydridoaluminate, \(\ce{LiAlH4}\), in dry ether reduces a carboxylic acid to a **primary alcohol**. Sodium tetrahydridoborate, \(\ce{NaBH4}\), is too weak to reduce a carboxylic acid.

\[\ce{CH3COOH + 4[H] -> CH3CH2OH + H2O}\]

The aldehyde is formed on the way, but it is reduced faster than the acid, so it cannot be isolated.

## Esters

### Making an ester

An ester is made by **condensation** of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol: the two molecules join and a small molecule, water, is lost. The mixture is heated under reflux with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid, which acts as a catalyst.

:::def Condensation reaction
A reaction in which two molecules join to form a larger molecule and a small molecule, such as water, is eliminated.
:::

\[\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH <=>[H2SO4][heat] CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}\]

The OH of the acid and the H of the alcohol's OH group form the water; the rest of the acid and the alcohol join through the new C–O bond. The reaction is reversible: ester and water react back to acid and alcohol (this is the hydrolysis below). A mixture of all four substances is always present at equilibrium, so the yield is never 100%.

:::lab Making ethyl ethanoate
Put ethanol and ethanoic acid in a round-bottomed flask with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid and anti-bumping granules. Heat under reflux (using a water bath or an electric heating mantle, never a flame, because ethanol is flammable). Then rearrange the apparatus for distillation and collect the liquid that distils at about the boiling point of the ester. Shake the distillate with sodium carbonate solution to remove acid (effervescence shows acid is still present; keep adding until the fizzing stops), then separate the ester layer, which floats on the water. Concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive: wear eye protection and gloves.
:::

You can raise the yield by Le Chatelier's principle ([Equilibria](#chem.equilibria)). Use an excess of the cheaper reactant, so the equilibrium moves right. Concentrated sulfuric acid also absorbs water as it forms, so the equilibrium moves right again. Distilling the ester off as it forms is a third way, if the ester boils at a lower temperature than everything else in the flask.

:::warn Sulfuric acid is a catalyst, not a reagent
Do not write \(\ce{H2SO4}\) as a reactant in the equation. It is not used up. Write it above the arrow, as a catalyst, with "heat" or "reflux". The reaction is also reversible, so use \(\ce{<=>}\), not a plain arrow.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 6.00 g of ethanoic acid is heated with excess ethanol and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid, and 5.72 g of ethyl ethanoate is collected. Calculate the percentage yield.
The equation is \(\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH <=> CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}\): 1 mol of acid gives 1 mol of ester. [B1]

\(M_r(\ce{CH3COOH}) = 60.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{CH3COOC2H5}) = 4(12.0) + 8(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 88.0\). [B1]
\[n(\ce{CH3COOH}) = \frac{6.00}{60.0} = 0.1000\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[\text{maximum mass of ester} = 0.1000 \times 88.0 = 8.80\,\mathrm{g}\] [M1]
\[\text{percentage yield} = \frac{5.72}{8.80} \times 100 = 65.0\,\%\] [A1]
The yield is below 100% because the reaction is an equilibrium and some ester is lost in purification.
:::

### Uses of esters

Esters of low relative molecular mass are volatile and many have fruity smells, so they are used as flavourings in food and in perfumes. Ethyl ethanoate is a solvent in glues and nail-varnish remover. Natural fats and vegetable oils are also esters: each molecule is an ester of the alcohol propane-1,2,3-triol (three OH groups) with three long-chain carboxylic acids. Polyesters, the fibre in many clothes, are formed by joining many ester links (see [Polymers](#chem.org.polymers)).

## Hydrolysis of esters

**Hydrolysis** is the breaking of a bond by reaction with water. An ester is hydrolysed back to its parent acid and alcohol, and the reaction is done in two ways.

:::def Hydrolysis
The breaking of a bond in a molecule by reaction with water. Hydrolysis of an ester gives a carboxylic acid (or its salt) and an alcohol.
:::

:::cols
:::col With dilute acid, heat under reflux
The reverse of esterification. It is **reversible** and does not go to completion, because the acid and alcohol formed react back to give ester and water. The products are the **carboxylic acid** and the alcohol. A large excess of water pushes the equilibrium to the right.
\[\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O <=>[H+] CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH}\]
:::
:::col With dilute alkali, heat under reflux
The reaction **goes to completion**, because the acid made is immediately turned into its carboxylate salt, which cannot react back with the alcohol. The products are the **carboxylate salt** and the alcohol.
\[\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3 + OH- -> CH3COO- + CH3CH2OH}\]
:::
:::

To obtain the free acid after alkaline hydrolysis, **acidify** the mixture with a dilute strong acid:

\[\ce{CH3COO- + H+ -> CH3COOH}\]

### Soap

Hydrolysis of a fat or vegetable oil with hot aqueous sodium hydroxide is called **saponification**. Each molecule of fat has three ester links, so three \(\ce{OH-}\) ions are used per molecule. The products are propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) and the sodium salts of three long-chain carboxylic acids. These sodium salts are soap.

\[\ce{(RCOO)3C3H5 + 3NaOH -> 3RCOONa + C3H5(OH)3}\]

:::board
AQA also teaches biodiesel: a mixture of methyl esters of long-chain carboxylic acids, made by reacting vegetable oils with methanol in the presence of a catalyst. Biodiesel is not in the Cambridge 9701 syllabus.
:::

## Nitriles and amines from halogenoalkanes

[Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen) react with nucleophiles, and two of those reactions make new functional groups.

**Nitriles.** A halogenoalkane heated with KCN in ethanol gives a nitrile, with one more carbon atom in the chain:

\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + KCN -> CH3CH2CN + KBr}\]

The other route to a carbon–carbon bond is in [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl): HCN adds to an aldehyde or ketone and gives a hydroxynitrile, \(\ce{R-CH(OH)-CN}\), with KCN as a catalyst and heat. Both types of nitrile are hydrolysed in the same way.

**Primary amines.** A halogenoalkane heated with excess ammonia in ethanol, in a sealed tube under pressure, replaces the halogen with an \(\ce{NH2}\) group:

\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br}\]

The product, ethylamine, is a **primary amine**. The excess of ammonia gives the amine a better chance of surviving, because most of the halogenoalkane then meets ammonia and not the amine product. Classifying amines as primary, secondary and tertiary and their properties are A Level topics ([Nitrogen compounds](#chem.org.nitrogen)).

### Hydrolysis of nitriles

A nitrile is hydrolysed to a carboxylic acid by heating under reflux with dilute acid or dilute alkali. The nitrogen leaves as ammonium ions in acid and as ammonia in alkali.

:::key Hydrolysis of nitriles
| Conditions | Products | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| dilute acid (e.g. \(\ce{HCl}\)), heat | carboxylic acid + ammonium ion | \(\ce{RCN + 2H2O + H+ -> RCOOH + NH4+}\) |
| dilute alkali (e.g. NaOH), heat | carboxylate ion + ammonia | \(\ce{RCN + H2O + OH- -> RCOO- + NH3}\) |

After alkaline hydrolysis, acidify (dilute strong acid) to obtain the carboxylic acid.
:::

:::warn Acid and alkali products are not swapped
In acid, the nitrogen is an ammonium ion, \(\ce{NH4+}\), and the organic product is the acid. In alkali, the nitrogen is the gas ammonia, \(\ce{NH3}\) (alkaline conditions cannot give the \(\ce{NH4+}\) ion), and the organic product is the carboxylate ion. The ammonia can be detected by its smell and by its action on damp red litmus paper, which turns blue.
:::

Hydrolysing a hydroxynitrile gives a **hydroxy acid**. Ethanal with HCN gives 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, and hydrolysis gives 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid, the acid in sour milk):

\[\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN + 2H2O + H+ -> CH3CH(OH)COOH + NH4+}\]

:::eg (Exam style) A monobasic carboxylic acid \(\ce{RCOOH}\), 0.370 g, is dissolved in water and neutralised exactly by 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide. Deduce the relative molecular mass of the acid and identify it.
\(n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.200 \times 25.0 / 1000 = 0.00500\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
The acid is monobasic (one COOH), so \(n(\text{acid}) = 0.00500\,\mathrm{mol}\). [M1]
\[M_r = \frac{0.370}{0.00500} = 74.0\] [A1]

(a) Heat under reflux with excess acidified potassium dichromate(VI) (or acidified potassium manganate(VII)). [B1]

(c) \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether. [B1] (\(\ce{NaBH4}\) is not strong enough.)

Q: Describe a test that distinguishes ethanoic acid from ethanol and state the observation for each.
A: Add sodium carbonate solution (or solid). Ethanoic acid gives effervescence (\(\ce{CO2}\), which turns limewater cloudy); ethanol gives no visible change.
Q: Name the ester made from butanoic acid and methanol, and give the conditions.
A: Methyl butanoate. Heat under reflux with a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid as catalyst.
Q: Write the equation for the alkaline hydrolysis of methyl propanoate with NaOH(aq), and state the extra step needed to obtain the acid.
A: \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3 + NaOH -> CH3CH2COONa + CH3OH}\). Add dilute strong acid (e.g. HCl) to turn sodium propanoate into propanoic acid.
Q: Give the formula of the organic product when butanenitrile is heated with dilute sulfuric acid, and when it is heated with dilute sodium hydroxide.
A: With acid: butanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}\) (with \(\ce{NH4+}\)). With alkali: the butanoate ion, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COO-}\) (with \(\ce{NH3}\)), which gives butanoic acid on acidification.
Q: How is propylamine made from 1-bromopropane?
A: Heat 1-bromopropane with excess ammonia in ethanol in a sealed tube under pressure: \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2CH2NH2 + NH4Br}\).
Q: Why is concentrated sulfuric acid used in making esters, and why is the yield never 100%?
A: It is a catalyst, and it absorbs water, which shifts the equilibrium towards the ester. The reaction is reversible, so some acid and alcohol always remain at equilibrium.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [2 marks] ★ Give the reagents and conditions to convert propanoic acid into ethyl propanoate.
A: Ethanol and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid (as catalyst) [B1], heat under reflux. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ A sample of butanoic acid is added to magnesium ribbon. State what you would see, name the gas formed and write an equation.
A: Effervescence (bubbles), and the magnesium dissolves. [B1] The gas is hydrogen. [B1]
\[\ce{2CH3CH2CH2COOH + Mg -> (CH3CH2CH2COO)2Mg + H2}\] [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Propanoic acid has a boiling point of 141 °C, but methyl ethanoate, which has the same molecular formula, boils at 57 °C. Explain the difference.
H: Draw both and look for an O–H bond.
A: Propanoic acid has an O–H group, so its molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other. [B1]
Methyl ethanoate has no O–H bond, so it cannot form hydrogen bonds with itself. [B1]
Its molecules are held by weaker pd-pd and id-id forces. [B1]
Less energy is needed to overcome the intermolecular forces in methyl ethanoate, so it boils at the lower temperature. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Compound E, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOCH3}\), is heated under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide. (a) Name E. (b) Name the two organic products of the reaction. (c) Explain why a further reagent is needed to obtain butanoic acid.
A: (a) methyl butanoate [B1]
(b) sodium butanoate and methanol [B1] [B1]
(c) In alkaline conditions the acid is formed as its salt; adding dilute strong acid supplies \(\ce{H+}\), which converts butanoate ions into butanoic acid. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Butanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}\), is hydrolysed in two different ways. (a) State the reagents and conditions, and write equations, for hydrolysis with dilute acid and with dilute alkali. (b) Name the gas given off in the alkaline hydrolysis.
A: (a) Heat under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN + 2H2O + H+ -> CH3CH2CH2COOH + NH4+}\] [B1]
Heat under reflux with dilute sodium hydroxide. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN + H2O + OH- -> CH3CH2CH2COO- + NH3}\] [B1]
(b) Ammonia. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) 2.40 g of methanol is heated with excess propanoic acid and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid. 4.95 g of methyl propanoate is collected. Calculate the percentage yield.
H: 1 mol of methanol gives 1 mol of ester. The ester is \(\ce{C4H8O2}\).
A: \(M_r(\ce{CH3OH}) = 32.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}) = 88.0\). [B1]
\(n(\ce{CH3OH}) = 2.40 / 32.0 = 0.07500\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
maximum mass \(= 0.07500 \times 88.0 = 6.60\,\mathrm{g}\) [M1]
percentage yield \(= 4.95 / 6.60 \times 100 = 75.0\,\%\) [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) A student dissolves 0.880 g of an unbranched monobasic carboxylic acid in water and makes the solution up to 250 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ sample needs 20.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide for neutralisation. Calculate the relative molecular mass of the acid and name it.
H: Work out the moles in the 25.0 cm³ sample, then scale up to 250 cm³.
A: \(n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.0500 \times 20.0 / 1000 = 0.001000\,\mathrm{mol}\), so 25.0 cm³ of acid contains 0.001000 mol. [M1]
In 250 cm³: \(0.001000 \times 10 = 0.01000\,\mathrm{mol}\). [M1]
\(M_r = 0.880 / 0.01000 = 88.0\) [A1]
The acid is butanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}\) (\(M_r = 4(12.0) + 8(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 88.0\)). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Describe a three-step synthesis of butanoic acid from propan-1-ol. Give the reagents and conditions for each step and the structure or name of each intermediate.
H: The product has one more carbon than the starting material, so the route needs a cyanide step. First turn the OH into a halogen.
A: Step 1: propan-1-ol with \(\ce{PCl5}\) (or \(\ce{SOCl2}\)) gives 1-chloropropane, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Cl}\). [B1] [B1]
Step 2: heat 1-chloropropane with KCN in ethanol to give butanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}\). [B1] [B1]
Step 3: heat butanenitrile under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid to give butanoic acid. [B1]
The route adds a carbon atom through the \(\ce{CN-}\) nucleophile (the nucleophilic substitution step). [B1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ Ester X, \(\ce{C5H10O2}\), is hydrolysed by heating with dilute sulfuric acid to give an acid P with \(M_r = 60.0\) and an alcohol Q. Q gives a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine. When Q is heated with acidified potassium dichromate(VI), the product R gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH but no silver mirror with Tollens' reagent. (a) Identify P, Q, R and X. (b) State why the hydrolysis does not go to completion.
H: Which acid has \(M_r\) 60.0? Subtract it from the ester formula to find Q.
H: Which type of alcohol is oxidised to a compound that does not give a silver mirror?
A: (a) P is ethanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3COOH}\) (\(M_r = 60.0\)). [B1]
The alcohol has 3 carbons (X has 5, P has 2): \(\ce{C3H7OH}\). [B1]
The yellow precipitate shows Q contains \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\), so Q is propan-2-ol. [B1]
R is propanone: the orange precipitate shows C=O, and no silver mirror shows a ketone. [B1]
X is propan-2-yl ethanoate, \(\ce{CH3COOCH(CH3)2}\). [B1]
(b) The acid-catalysed hydrolysis is reversible, [B1] so acid, alcohol, ester and water are all present at equilibrium. [B1]
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Plan a synthesis of 2-hydroxypropanoic acid from ethanol in three steps, giving reagents, conditions and the structure of each intermediate. Explain why the product is a racemic mixture and not optically active.
H: Ethanol has two carbons and the product three. Which aldehyde is formed first?
H: The product has a chiral centre. From which sides can \(\ce{CN-}\) attack a planar C=O?
A: Step 1: warm ethanol with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) and distil off ethanal, \(\ce{CH3CHO}\). [B1] [B1]
Step 2: add HCN with KCN as catalyst, heat, to give 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}\). [B1]
Step 3: heat under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid to give 2-hydroxypropanoic acid. [B1]
In step 2 the planar C=O carbon is attacked equally from both faces by \(\ce{CN-}\), [B1] giving equal amounts of the two enantiomers, so the mixture does not rotate plane-polarised light. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A fat is an ester of propane-1,2,3-triol and three molecules of stearic acid, \(\ce{C17H35COOH}\) (so the fat is \(\ce{(C17H35COO)3C3H5}\), \(M_r = 890.0\)). A sample of 8.90 g of the fat is heated with excess aqueous sodium hydroxide. Write the equation, and calculate the mass of sodium stearate (soap) produced. (\(M_r\) of \(\ce{C17H35COONa}\) = 306.0)
H: Three ester links break in each molecule.
A: \(\ce{(C17H35COO)3C3H5 + 3NaOH -> 3C17H35COONa + C3H5(OH)3}\) [B1]
\(n(\text{fat}) = 8.90 / 890.0 = 0.01000\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
\(n(\text{soap}) = 3 \times 0.01000 = 0.03000\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
mass \(= 0.03000 \times 306.0 = 9.18\,\mathrm{g}\) [A1]
The other product is propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol). [B1]

:::quiz
? Ethyl ethanoate is heated under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide. What are the organic products?
- ethanoic acid and ethanol
+ sodium ethanoate and ethanol
- sodium ethanoate and ethanoic acid
- ethanol and ethanal
> The alkali converts the acid into its salt as it forms. Acid appears only after a further acidification step.
? What is the role of concentrated sulfuric acid in making an ester?
- It is a reactant.
- It is an oxidising agent.
+ It is a catalyst, and it absorbs water.
- It is a nucleophile.
> The H₂SO₄ is not used up. It speeds up the reaction and, by absorbing water, helps move the equilibrium towards the ester.
? What is the correct name of \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOCH2CH3}\)?
- butyl ethanoate
+ ethyl butanoate
- ethyl propanoate
- ethanoyl butanoate
> The acid part contains the C=O carbon and has four carbons (butanoate); the group on the oxygen is ethyl. The name goes alkyl first, then the acid part.
? Nitrile \(\ce{RCN}\) is heated under reflux with dilute NaOH. Which pair of products forms?
- \(\ce{RCOOH}\) and \(\ce{NH4+}\)
+ \(\ce{RCOO-}\) and \(\ce{NH3}\)
- \(\ce{RCOO-}\) and \(\ce{NH4+}\)
- \(\ce{RCH2NH2}\) and water
> In alkali the carboxylic acid is a carboxylate ion and the nitrogen is released as ammonia gas. \(\ce{RCOOH}\) with \(\ce{NH4+}\) are the products with dilute acid.
? 3.00 g of ethanoic acid (\(M_r\) = 60.0) reacts with excess sodium carbonate. What volume of \(\ce{CO2}\) is released at room conditions (24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹)?
- 1.20 dm³
+ 0.600 dm³
- 2.40 dm³
- 1.80 dm³
> \(n(\text{acid}) = 3.00/60.0 = 0.0500\,\mathrm{mol}\); 2 mol acid give 1 mol \(\ce{CO2}\), so \(n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.0250\,\mathrm{mol}\) and \(V = 0.0250 \times 24.0 = 0.600\,\mathrm{dm^3}\). The 1.20 dm³ option forgets the 2 : 1 ratio.
? Which test shows that a compound is a carboxylic acid and not an alcohol?
- It reacts with sodium metal to give hydrogen.
+ It gives effervescence with sodium carbonate solution.
- It turns acidified dichromate(VI) green.
- It gives a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine.
> Alcohols also react with sodium and may turn dichromate green. Only the acid is acidic enough to release \(\ce{CO2}\) from a carbonate.
? Why is the acid hydrolysis of an ester incomplete, while alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion?
- The acid is a weak catalyst.
+ Acid hydrolysis is an equilibrium; in alkali the acid is removed as its carboxylate salt.
- Alkali is a stronger oxidising agent.
- Esters do not react with acids.
> The acid-catalysed reaction is the reverse of esterification, so ester and products coexist. With alkali the carboxylate ion cannot react back with the alcohol.
:::

:::recap
- Carboxylic acid \(\ce{RCOOH}\) (-oic acid), carboxylate \(\ce{RCOO-}\) (-oate). Ester \(\ce{RCOOR'}\): named alkyl (alcohol part) then alkanoate (acid part).
- Acids hydrogen-bond, so they boil much higher than esters of the same \(M_r\). Esters cannot hydrogen-bond to themselves.
- Made by: oxidation of primary alcohols and aldehydes (acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) or \(\ce{KMnO4}\), reflux); hydrolysis of nitriles or esters (dilute acid or alkali, heat, then acidify).
- Weak acid reactions: metal gives salt + \(\ce{H2}\); alkali gives salt + water; carbonate gives salt + water + \(\ce{CO2}\) (effervescence is the test). \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether reduces the acid to a primary alcohol.
- Esterification: acid + alcohol, concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, reflux. A reversible condensation. Excess reactant and removal of water raise the yield.
- Ester hydrolysis: dilute acid, heat, is reversible and gives acid + alcohol. Dilute alkali, heat, goes to completion and gives carboxylate salt + alcohol; acidify to get the acid. Saponification of fats gives soap.
- Nitriles: halogenoalkane + KCN in ethanol, heat (one more carbon); hydroxynitriles from aldehydes and ketones with HCN and KCN. Hydrolysis: dilute acid gives \(\ce{RCOOH}\) + \(\ce{NH4+}\); dilute alkali gives \(\ce{RCOO-}\) + \(\ce{NH3}\), then acidify.
- Primary amines: halogenoalkane + excess \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure.
:::
