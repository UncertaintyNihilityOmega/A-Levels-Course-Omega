Q: What is the oxidation number of sulfur in \(\ce{H2SO4}\)? (from [Redox and oxidation numbers](#chem.redox))
A: Hydrogen is +1 and oxygen is −2. The oxidation numbers add up to zero: \(2(+1) + x + 4(-2) = 0\), so \(x = +6\).
Q: Which intermolecular force acts between the non-polar molecules in liquid argon, and why does it get stronger when the atoms have more electrons? (from [Shapes of molecules and intermolecular forces](#chem.shapes))
A: Instantaneous dipole–induced dipole (id–id) forces. More electrons give a larger electron cloud, which is more easily distorted, so the instantaneous dipoles and the attractions between them are larger.
Q: Why is a longer covalent bond usually a weaker one? (from [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding))
A: In a longer bond the shared pair of electrons is further from both nuclei, so the attraction between the nuclei and the shared pair is weaker and less energy is needed to break the bond.
:::

## The halogens

Group 17 is the column of the Periodic Table between Group 16 and the noble gases. Its elements are fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine (radioactive, and rarely studied). The syllabus concentrates on chlorine, bromine and iodine. The name **halogen** means "salt-former": these elements react with metals to make salts such as sodium chloride.

:::def Halogen
An element in Group 17 of the Periodic Table. Halogen atoms have seven electrons in their outer shell, and the elements exist as diatomic molecules, \(\ce{X2}\) (\(\ce{F2}\), \(\ce{Cl2}\), \(\ce{Br2}\), \(\ce{I2}\)).
:::

:::def Halide ion
The ion formed when a halogen atom gains one electron to complete its outer shell: \(\ce{F-}\), \(\ce{Cl-}\), \(\ce{Br-}\), \(\ce{I-}\). A compound containing one is a halide, such as sodium chloride.
:::

:::warn Halogen or halide?
\(\ce{Cl2}\) is a **halogen** (the element, a molecule with two atoms, oxidation number 0). \(\ce{Cl-}\) is a **halide ion** (oxidation number −1). The two behave in opposite ways: halogens are oxidising agents, halide ions are reducing agents. Mixing up the words is the most common way to lose a mark in this topic, so write the formula as well as the name.
:::

### Colour and state

Look at the three halogens at room temperature and you see a clear pattern.

| Halogen | Appearance at room temperature | Colour of the vapour | Electrons per molecule | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{F2}\) | pale yellow gas | pale yellow | 18 | −188 |
| \(\ce{Cl2}\) | pale green (yellow-green) gas | pale green | 34 | −34 |
| \(\ce{Br2}\) | red-brown liquid | orange | 70 | 59 |
| \(\ce{I2}\) | grey-black solid | purple | 106 | 184 |

Fluorine is in the table only for comparison. Bromine is the only non-metallic element that is a liquid at room temperature, and it gives off a dense orange vapour even below its boiling point. Iodine does not melt easily: when you warm the grey-black crystals gently, purple vapour rises straight off the solid. A solid that turns directly into a gas is said to **sublime**.

:::warn Safety
Chlorine is a toxic gas and bromine is corrosive, with toxic vapour. Both are used only in a fume cupboard, and the volumes are kept small. Iodine stains skin and its vapour irritates the lungs.
:::

### Volatility and id–id forces

:::def Volatility
How easily a substance turns from a liquid or solid into a gas. A volatile substance has a low boiling point because its particles are only weakly attracted to each other.
:::

Chlorine is a gas, bromine a liquid and iodine a solid, so the volatility falls steadily down the group. The reason is the strength of the forces *between* the molecules, not the strength of the bond *within* them. When bromine boils, the \(\ce{Br2}\) molecules move apart but each Br–Br bond stays intact.

Halogen molecules are non-polar because the two atoms are identical, so the only attractions between them are id–id forces. Each molecule down the group has more electrons (34, 70, 106), a larger electron cloud that is more easily distorted, and so larger instantaneous dipoles and stronger id–id attractions. More energy is needed to pull the molecules apart, and the boiling point rises.

:::eg Explain why the boiling point of iodine is much higher than that of chlorine.
Both halogens are simple molecules with a non-polar covalent bond, so the attractions between molecules are id–id forces. [B1]

An \(\ce{I2}\) molecule has 106 electrons and a \(\ce{Cl2}\) molecule has 34, so the electron cloud of \(\ce{I2}\) is larger and more easily distorted. This gives larger instantaneous dipoles and stronger id–id forces. [B1]

More energy is needed to overcome the id–id forces between \(\ce{I2}\) molecules (the I–I bonds do not break), so iodine has a much higher boiling point. [B1]
:::

### The strength of the X–X bond

The bond energy of a halogen molecule is the energy needed to break one mole of X–X bonds in the gas phase. The Data booklet values are:

| Bond | F–F | Cl–Cl | Br–Br | I–I |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 158 | 242 | 193 | 151 |

From chlorine to iodine the bond energy falls. The atoms get larger, so the bond gets longer, the shared pair is further from both nuclei and the larger outer orbitals overlap less well. The attraction between the nuclei and the shared pair is weaker, and the bond is easier to break.

:::note Stretch: why is F–F so weak?
Fluorine breaks the pattern: F–F (158) is weaker than Cl–Cl (242). The F atoms are so small that the F–F bond is very short, and the lone pairs on the two atoms are close enough to repel each other strongly. This repulsion weakens the bond. You will not be asked to explain it in a Paper 1 or Paper 2 question, but it is a good example of an exception that has a reason.
:::

:::warn A weaker bond does not make a stronger oxidising agent
Iodine has the weakest X–X bond, yet it is the *least* reactive of the three as an oxidising agent. How good a halogen is at oxidising something depends on how strongly its atoms attract an extra electron, not on how easily the \(\ce{X2}\) molecule splits. Keep the two ideas separate.
:::

## Halogens as oxidising agents

An **oxidising agent** accepts electrons and is itself reduced (see [Redox and oxidation numbers](#chem.redox)). A halogen molecule accepts two electrons, one for each atom, to give two halide ions:

\[\ce{X2 + 2e- -> 2X-}\]

The halogens differ in how willing they are to do this. You find out by mixing a halogen with a solution containing a *different* halide ion. A more reactive halogen takes electrons from the halide ion of a less reactive one and so displaces it.

:::def Displacement reaction
A reaction in which a more reactive element takes the place of a less reactive element in a compound. A halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from a solution of its halide.
:::

### What you see

Aqueous chlorine ("chlorine water") is pale green, aqueous bromine is orange (a yellow-orange when dilute) and aqueous iodine is brown. A displacement therefore shows up as a colour change.

\[\ce{Cl2(aq) + 2Br-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + Br2(aq)}\]
\[\ce{Cl2(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + I2(aq)}\]
\[\ce{Br2(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> 2Br-(aq) + I2(aq)}\]

| Add this halogen (aq) | to \(\ce{Cl-}\)(aq) | to \(\ce{Br-}\)(aq) | to \(\ce{I-}\)(aq) |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{Cl2}\) | – | solution turns orange (\(\ce{Br2}\) formed) | solution turns brown (\(\ce{I2}\) formed) |
| \(\ce{Br2}\) | no reaction | – | solution turns brown (\(\ce{I2}\) formed) |
| \(\ce{I2}\) | no reaction | no reaction | – |

When a lot of iodine forms, some of it comes out as a grey-black solid. To make the results easier to read, shake the mixture with a few drops of a hydrocarbon solvent such as cyclohexane. Halogens dissolve better in the non-polar solvent than in water, so they move into the upper layer, where the colours are clearer: bromine is orange (or orange-red) and iodine is purple. Chlorine stays pale green. The cyclohexane layer is the one to look at. A drop of starch solution also turns blue-black when iodine is present.

:::lab Displacement reactions
In a fume cupboard, put about 2 cm³ of potassium bromide solution in one test tube and 2 cm³ of potassium iodide solution in another. Add about 2 cm³ of chlorine water to each and note the colour. Add about 1 cm³ of cyclohexane and shake gently. Then repeat with bromine water in place of chlorine water, and with iodine solution added to potassium chloride and to potassium bromide. No flames: cyclohexane is flammable.
:::

The results give the order of oxidising power: chlorine is the strongest of the three, then bromine, then iodine. A halogen oxidises the halide ions *below* it in the group and none above.

:::key Halogens as oxidising agents
The oxidising power decreases down the group: \(\ce{Cl2}\) > \(\ce{Br2}\) > \(\ce{I2}\). A halogen displaces any halide below it in the group. Chlorine displaces bromide and iodide; bromine displaces iodide; iodine displaces neither.
:::

### Why the trend?

To oxidise something a halogen atom must pull an electron into its outer shell. A chlorine atom does this more strongly than an iodine atom because:

- the chlorine atom is smaller, so the incoming electron enters a shell closer to the nucleus;
- the iodine atom has more inner shells of electrons, so the outer shell is more shielded from the nuclear charge;
- the attraction between the nucleus and the incoming electron is therefore greater for chlorine than for iodine.

The same two factors, distance and shielding, make the first ionisation energy fall down the group, which you can see below.

:::note The numbers behind it
The Data booklet gives the standard electrode potential for each \(\ce{X2/X-}\) pair: \(\ce{F2}\) +2.87 V, \(\ce{Cl2}\) +1.36 V, \(\ce{Br2}\) +1.07 V, \(\ce{I2}\) +0.54 V. The more positive the value, the stronger the oxidising agent, so the order is the same. You will use these values properly in [Electrochemistry: electrolysis and electrode potentials](#chem.electrochem). Fluorine (+2.87 V) is the strongest oxidising agent of all: it even oxidises water, and it is too dangerous to use in school.

:::eg Chlorine water is added to aqueous potassium iodide. Describe what you see, write an ionic equation and explain why the reaction happens.
The colourless solution turns brown (iodine forms; a grey-black solid may appear if there is a lot). [B1]
\[\ce{Cl2(aq) + 2I-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + I2(aq)}\] [B1]
Chlorine is reduced (oxidation number 0 → −1) and iodide is oxidised (−1 → 0). Chlorine is a stronger oxidising agent than iodine, so it takes electrons from the iodide ions. [B1]
:::

## Reactions with hydrogen and the stability of the hydrogen halides

The halogens combine with hydrogen to make hydrogen halides, \(\ce{H2 + X2 -> 2HX}\), but the conditions needed change a great deal down the group.

| Halogen | Reaction with hydrogen |
|---|---|
| \(\ce{F2}\) | explodes, even in the cold and in the dark |
| \(\ce{Cl2}\) | no reaction in the dark; explodes in bright sunlight (or with a flame) |
| \(\ce{Br2}\) | the vapour needs heating before it reacts, and the reaction is less violent |
| \(\ce{I2}\) | slow even when heated with a catalyst; the reaction does not go to completion and is reversible |

\[\ce{H2(g) + Cl2(g) -> 2HCl(g)}\]
\[\ce{H2(g) + I2(g) <=> 2HI(g)}\]

You can explain the pattern with bond energies. The reaction breaks one H–H bond and one X–X bond and makes two H–X bonds:

\[\Delta H = (\text{H–H} + \text{X–X}) - 2(\text{H–X})\]

Using the Data booklet values (H–H 436, H–F 562, H–Cl 431, H–Br 366, H–I 299) gives the table below. A negative value means that the reaction gives out heat. The formation of HF releases a great deal of energy, which is why fluorine is so violent. The formation of HI is only just exothermic, so little energy is released to keep the reaction going and the equilibrium mixture contains a lot of unreacted \(\ce{H2}\) and \(\ce{I2}\).

| \(\ce{H2 + X2 -> 2HX}\) | \(\Delta H\) / kJ mol⁻¹ (per mole of equation) |
|---|---|
| X = F | −530 |
| X = Cl | −184 |
| X = Br | −103 |
| X = I | −11 |

### Thermal stability

The same figures, reversed, tell you how stable the hydrogen halides are when heated. The decomposition \(\ce{2HX -> H2 + X2}\) has the opposite sign of \(\Delta H\): +184, +103 and +11 kJ for HCl, HBr and HI. The H–X bond energy falls (H–Cl 431, H–Br 366, H–I 299) because the halogen atoms get bigger and the bond gets longer, so the shared pair is held less strongly.

:::key Thermal stability of the hydrogen halides
Thermal stability decreases down the group: HCl > HBr > HI. The H–X bond becomes longer and weaker (H–Cl 431, H–Br 366, H–I 299 kJ mol⁻¹), so less energy is needed to break it. Hydrogen iodide is decomposed easily by heating, which you see as purple iodine vapour.
:::

\[\ce{2HI(g) <=> H2(g) + I2(g)}\]

Hydrogen chloride is not decomposed when heated in the laboratory, hydrogen bromide lies in between, and hydrogen iodide breaks down readily.

:::eg (Exam style) Use bond energies to calculate \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{2HCl -> H2 + Cl2}\) and \(\ce{2HI -> H2 + I2}\), and state what your answers show.
For \(\ce{2HCl -> H2 + Cl2}\): bonds broken are 2 × H–Cl, \(2 \times 431 = 862\) kJ. [M1]
Bonds made are H–H and Cl–Cl, \(436 + 242 = 678\) kJ. \(\Delta H = 862 - 678 = +184\) kJ mol⁻¹. [A1]

For \(\ce{2HI -> H2 + I2}\): bonds broken \(2 \times 299 = 598\) kJ, bonds made \(436 + 151 = 587\) kJ. [M1]
\(\Delta H = 598 - 587 = +11\) kJ mol⁻¹. [A1]

HCl needs far more energy to decompose than HI, so HCl is much more stable to heat: thermal stability falls down the group because the H–X bond gets weaker. [B1]
:::

## Halide ions as reducing agents

A **reducing agent** donates electrons and is itself oxidised. A halide ion donates its extra electron: \(\ce{2X- -> X2 + 2e-}\). The order of reducing power is the reverse of the order of oxidising power of the halogens:

:::key Halide ions as reducing agents
The reducing power increases down the group: \(\ce{I-}\) > \(\ce{Br-}\) > \(\ce{Cl-}\). A halide ion is the weaker reducing agent when the halogen it makes is the stronger oxidising agent.
:::

The explanation is again size and shielding. In an iodide ion the outer electrons are in the fifth shell, far from the nucleus and shielded by many inner electrons. They are held weakly, so they are given up easily. In a chloride ion the outer electrons are in the third shell and are held much more strongly.

:::eg Explain why iodide ions are stronger reducing agents than chloride ions.
The iodide ion is larger than the chloride ion: its outer electrons are in a shell further from the nucleus. [B1]
There are more inner shells of electrons in iodine, so the outer electrons are more shielded from the nuclear charge. [B1]
The attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker, so \(\ce{I-}\) loses an electron more easily than \(\ce{Cl-}\). [B1]
:::

### Halide ions and concentrated sulfuric acid

The best evidence comes from adding concentrated sulfuric acid to a **solid** sodium halide. Two things can happen. In every case the acid first gives away \(\ce{H+}\) and makes a hydrogen halide:

\[\ce{NaX(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HX(g)}\]

The hydrogen halide gas fumes in moist air: you see steamy fumes. This is an acid–base reaction, not a redox one. Next, the sulfuric acid may also act as an oxidising agent on the halide ion. Whether it does depends on how good a reducing agent the halide is.

**Chloride.** Chloride ions are such weak reducing agents that concentrated sulfuric acid cannot oxidise them. The only reaction is the one above, and the only observation is steamy fumes of hydrogen chloride.

\[\ce{NaCl(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HCl(g)}\]

**Bromide.** The \(\ce{HBr}\) formed is oxidised to bromine by the sulfuric acid, which is reduced from oxidation number +6 to +4 and forms sulfur dioxide. You see steamy fumes, an orange-brown vapour (bromine) and a colourless gas with a sharp smell (sulfur dioxide).

\[\ce{NaBr(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HBr(g)}\]
\[\ce{2HBr + H2SO4 -> Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O}\]

**Iodide.** Iodide ions are the strongest reducing agents of the three and reduce the sulfur much further. Along with the steamy fumes and iodine (purple fumes, and a black solid in the mixture) you get a mixture of reduction products of sulfur: sulfur dioxide, a yellow solid (sulfur) and hydrogen sulfide, which smells of bad eggs.

\[\ce{NaI(s) + H2SO4(l) -> NaHSO4(s) + HI(g)}\]
\[\ce{2HI + H2SO4 -> I2 + SO2 + 2H2O}\]
\[\ce{6HI + H2SO4 -> 3I2 + S + 4H2O}\]
\[\ce{8HI + H2SO4 -> 4I2 + H2S + 4H2O}\]

The oxidation number of sulfur falls from +6 to +4, to 0 and finally to −2. The larger the fall, the more electrons the halide has given to the sulfur: 2 for each sulfur in the first reaction, 6 in the second and 8 in the third. The greater the fall, the stronger the reducing agent.

:::lab Concentrated sulfuric acid and halides
Concentrated sulfuric acid is corrosive: wear gloves and eye protection. The gases (hydrogen halides, sulfur dioxide, hydrogen sulfide and bromine) are toxic, so the test is done in a fume cupboard with a small spatula of the solid halide. Never add water to the acid in the tube.
:::

:::tip The acid is the wrong choice for making HBr and HI
Because concentrated sulfuric acid oxidises HBr and HI, you cannot use it to make pure samples of those gases. Concentrated phosphoric acid, \(\ce{H3PO4}\), is not an oxidising agent, so it gives the hydrogen halide without the redox side reactions. This is why the halogenoalkane chapter lists "KCl with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) or \(\ce{H3PO4}\)" (see [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen)).
:::

Bromide ions are oxidised: \(\ce{2Br- -> Br2 + 2e-}\) (oxidation number −1 → 0). [B1]
Sulfur is reduced from +6 in \(\ce{H2SO4}\) to +4 in \(\ce{SO2}\): \(\ce{H2SO4 + 2H+ + 2e- -> SO2 + 2H2O}\). [B1]
Both half-equations involve 2 electrons, so they add directly:
\[\ce{2Br- + H2SO4 + 2H+ -> Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O}\] [B1]
In terms of hydrogen bromide, this is \(\ce{2HBr + H2SO4 -> Br2 + SO2 + 2H2O}\). [B1]

## Testing for halide ions with silver nitrate

Silver ions react with halide ions to form silver halides, which are insoluble in water. Each has its own colour, and each behaves differently when you add aqueous ammonia. This gives a reliable test for which halide ion is present.

:::steps
1. Dissolve the sample in water in a test tube.
2. Add dilute nitric acid. This removes ions such as carbonate, which would form a precipitate with silver ions and give a false result.
3. Add a few drops of silver nitrate solution, \(\ce{AgNO3(aq)}\). A precipitate shows that a halide is present. Note its colour.
4. Add ammonia solution, \(\ce{NH3(aq)}\), to the precipitate and see whether it dissolves.
:::

\[\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}\]
\[\ce{Ag+(aq) + Br-(aq) -> AgBr(s)}\]
\[\ce{Ag+(aq) + I-(aq) -> AgI(s)}\]

Ammonia dissolves silver chloride because its molecules bond to the silver ion and make a soluble complex ion. You are not required to know its formula, but you may meet it: \(\ce{AgCl(s) + 2NH3(aq) -> [Ag(NH3)2]+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}\). Silver bromide is less soluble to begin with and dissolves only partly. :::story Silver bromide and photography
Light turns silver ions in silver bromide into tiny specks of silver metal. Black-and-white photographic film is a layer of silver bromide crystals in gelatin: where light falls on the film, specks of silver form and, after developing, make the dark parts of the picture. The same test tube chemistry (a cream precipitate that is only partly soluble in ammonia) is behind the film.
:::

## Chlorine and sodium hydroxide

Chlorine reacts with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and the product depends on the temperature. In both cases the reaction is a **disproportionation**: the same element is both oxidised and reduced (see [Redox and oxidation numbers](#chem.redox), where the number line for chlorine is drawn).

**Cold, dilute sodium hydroxide** gives sodium chloride and sodium chlorate(I), \(\ce{NaClO}\):

\[\ce{Cl2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + NaClO(aq) + H2O(l)}\]

The ionic equation is \(\ce{Cl2 + 2OH- -> Cl- + ClO- + H2O}\). The oxidation number of chlorine goes from 0 to −1 in \(\ce{Cl-}\) (reduced) and from 0 to +1 in \(\ce{ClO-}\) (oxidised). A solution of sodium chlorate(I) is sold as household bleach: it is a good oxidising agent and disinfectant.

**Hot sodium hydroxide** gives sodium chloride and sodium chlorate(V), \(\ce{NaClO3}\):

\[\ce{3Cl2(g) + 6NaOH(aq) -> 5NaCl(aq) + NaClO3(aq) + 3H2O(l)}\]

Here chlorine goes from 0 to −1 in \(\ce{Cl-}\) (reduced) and from 0 to +5 in \(\ce{ClO3-}\) (oxidised). Chlorate(I) itself disproportionates on heating, \(\ce{3ClO- -> 2Cl- + ClO3-}\), which is why heating the cold mixture ends up with chlorate(V).

:::warn Name the ion from its oxidation number
\(\ce{ClO-}\) is **chlorate(I)** and \(\ce{ClO3-}\) is **chlorate(V)**. The Roman numeral is the oxidation number of chlorine. Writing "chlorate(V)" for bleach is a common slip, and "chlorite" is a different ion. You may meet the older name "hypochlorite" for \(\ce{ClO-}\), but write chlorate(I).
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Chlorine reacts with hot aqueous sodium hydroxide: \(\ce{3Cl2 + 6NaOH -> 5NaCl + NaClO3 + 3H2O}\). Show, using oxidation numbers, that this is a disproportionation and that the equation is balanced for electrons.
Oxidation numbers of chlorine: \(\ce{Cl2}\) 0; \(\ce{NaCl}\) −1; \(\ce{NaClO3}\) +5 (since \(+1 + x + 3(-2) = 0\)). [B1]
The same element, chlorine, is reduced (0 → −1) and oxidised (0 → +5), so the reaction is a disproportionation. [B1]
Each chlorine atom that forms \(\ce{Cl-}\) gains 1 electron and each that forms \(\ce{ClO3-}\) loses 5. Five \(\ce{Cl-}\) (5 electrons gained) balance one \(\ce{ClO3-}\) (5 electrons lost). [M1]
So six chlorine atoms (three \(\ce{Cl2}\)) are needed, which gives the ratio 5 \(\ce{NaCl}\) : 1 \(\ce{NaClO3}\) in the equation. [A1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 4.80 dm³ of chlorine gas, measured at room temperature and pressure, is passed into an excess of cold dilute sodium hydroxide solution. Calculate the mass of sodium chlorate(I) formed.
\(\ce{Cl2 + 2NaOH -> NaCl + NaClO + H2O}\): 1 mol of \(\ce{Cl2}\) gives 1 mol of NaClO. [B1]
\(n(\ce{Cl2}) = \dfrac{4.80}{24.0} = 0.200\) mol, so \(n(\ce{NaClO}) = 0.200\) mol. [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{NaClO}) = 23.0 + 35.5 + 16.0 = 74.5\) [B1]
\[m(\ce{NaClO}) = 0.200 \times 74.5 = 14.9\,\mathrm{g}\] [A1]
:::

## Chlorine in water

Chlorine dissolves in water and reacts reversibly with it. This is also a disproportionation:

\[\ce{Cl2(aq) + H2O(l) <=> HCl(aq) + HOCl(aq)}\]

Chlorine (0) becomes chloride in HCl (−1) and chlorine(I) in HOCl (+1). The acid \(\ce{HOCl}\) is weak, so it is only partly ionised:

\[\ce{HOCl(aq) <=> H+(aq) + ClO-(aq)}\]

The two chlorine-containing species that matter are **HOCl** and **ClO⁻**. Both are strong oxidising agents that kill bacteria and other micro-organisms, and this is how chlorine makes drinking water safe and keeps swimming pools clean. Water companies add a small amount of chlorine to the water supply, and swimming pools use a chlorate(I) salt such as sodium chlorate(I) or calcium chlorate(I), which in water gives the same two active species (\(\ce{ClO- + H2O <=> HOCl + OH-}\)). The disease that chlorination prevents, such as cholera, spreads when water is contaminated by sewage.

:::key Chlorine and water purification
\(\ce{Cl2 + H2O <=> HCl + HOCl}\) and \(\ce{HOCl <=> H+ + ClO-}\). Chlorine disproportionates in water (0 → −1 in HCl, 0 → +1 in HOCl). The active species that kill bacteria are HOCl and \(\ce{ClO-}\).
:::

The equilibrium responds to changes as Le Chatelier's principle predicts (see [Chemical equilibria](#chem.equilibria)). Adding alkali removes \(\ce{H+}\) from the right-hand side, so the position moves right and more HOCl and \(\ce{ClO-}\) form: this is why cold NaOH(aq) turns chlorine into bleach. In water, adding acid pushes \(\ce{HOCl <=> H+ + ClO-}\) to the left, so more of the chlorine is present as HOCl.

:::eg (Real context) A water works treats 2.0 × 10⁶ dm³ of water with chlorine at a concentration of 0.50 mg dm⁻³. (a) Explain, with equations, how chlorine makes the water safe to drink. (b) Calculate the volume of chlorine gas, at room temperature and pressure, needed.
(a) Chlorine reacts reversibly with water: \(\ce{Cl2 + H2O <=> HCl + HOCl}\). [B1]
The weak acid HOCl ionises partly: \(\ce{HOCl <=> H+ + ClO-}\). [B1]
HOCl and \(\ce{ClO-}\) are the active species and kill bacteria in the water. [B1]
(b) Mass of chlorine \(= 0.50 \times 2.0 \times 10^{6} = 1.0 \times 10^{6}\) mg \(= 1.0 \times 10^{3}\) g. [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{Cl2}) = 71.0\), so \(n = \dfrac{1.0 \times 10^{3}}{71.0} = 14.08\) mol. [B1] [M1]
\[V = 14.08 \times 24.0 = 338\,\mathrm{dm^{3}}\] [A1]
The data are given to 2 significant figures, so quote 340 dm³.
:::

Q: State the colour and physical state at room temperature of (a) chlorine, (b) bromine, (c) iodine.
A: (a) pale green (yellow-green) gas. (b) red-brown liquid (with an orange vapour). (c) grey-black solid (which gives a purple vapour on warming).
Q: Explain why the boiling points of the halogens increase from chlorine to iodine.
A: The molecules have more electrons down the group, so the id–id forces between them are stronger and more energy is needed to separate them.
Q: Which of these mixtures will react: (a) \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) and \(\ce{KCl(aq)}\), (b) \(\ce{Cl2(aq)}\) and \(\ce{KBr(aq)}\), (c) \(\ce{I2(aq)}\) and \(\ce{KBr(aq)}\)? Write an ionic equation for any reaction.
A: Only (b). A halogen displaces a halide lower in the group, and chlorine is above bromine. \(\ce{Cl2(aq) + 2Br-(aq) -> 2Cl-(aq) + Br2(aq)}\). In (a) and (c) the halogen is below the halide, so no reaction.
Q: What happens to the thermal stability of the hydrogen halides from HCl to HI, and why?
A: It decreases. The H–X bond becomes longer and weaker (431, 366, 299 kJ mol⁻¹), so less energy is needed to break it.
Q: Give the colour of the precipitate formed, and the effect of aqueous ammonia, for (a) \(\ce{Cl-}\), (b) \(\ce{Br-}\), (c) \(\ce{I-}\) with silver nitrate.
A: (a) white, dissolves. (b) cream, partially dissolves. (c) pale yellow, does not dissolve.

A: \[\ce{Cl2 + 2NaOH -> NaCl + NaClO + H2O}\]
Sodium chlorate(I), \(\ce{NaClO}\).
Q: What is the oxidation number of chlorine in (a) \(\ce{HOCl}\), (b) \(\ce{ClO3-}\)?
A: (a) +1: \(+1 + x + (-2) = 0\). (b) +5: \(x + 3(-2) = -1\), so \(x = +5\).

:::recap
- Cl₂ pale green gas; Br₂ red-brown liquid, orange vapour; I₂ grey-black solid, purple vapour. Volatility falls down the group because the molecules have more electrons, so the id–id forces are stronger.
- X–X bond energy: Cl–Cl 242 > Br–Br 193 > I–I 151 kJ mol⁻¹ (F–F 158 is anomalously weak because of lone-pair repulsion).
- Oxidising power: Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. A halogen displaces halides lower in the group. Colours: bromine orange and iodine brown in water; in cyclohexane bromine is orange(-red) and iodine purple.
- With H₂: Cl₂ explodes in sunlight, Br₂ needs heating, I₂ is slow, needs a catalyst and is reversible. Thermal stability HCl > HBr > HI because the H–X bond energy falls (431, 366, 299 kJ mol⁻¹); HI gives purple iodine vapour on heating.
- Reducing power of halide ions: I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ (bigger ion, outer electron further away and more shielded).
- Concentrated H₂SO₄ with solid halides: all give steamy HX. Cl⁻: no redox. Br⁻: Br₂ (orange-brown) and SO₂ (S +6 → +4). I⁻: I₂ (purple fumes, black solid), SO₂, S and H₂S (S +6 → +4, 0, −2; bad eggs).
- Ag⁺(aq) then NH₃(aq): AgCl white, soluble; AgBr cream, partially soluble; AgI pale yellow, insoluble. Acidify with dilute nitric acid first.
- Cl₂ + cold NaOH(aq) → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O; hot: 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O. Both are disproportionations (0 → −1 and +1, or 0 → −1 and +5). ClO⁻ is chlorate(I), ClO₃⁻ is chlorate(V).
- Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HOCl; HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + ClO⁻. HOCl and ClO⁻ kill bacteria in water purification.
