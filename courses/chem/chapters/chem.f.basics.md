## Matter and particles

Everything around you is **matter**: it has mass and takes up space. Matter is made of tiny **particles** that are far too small to see. Chemistry explains how substances behave by asking what kind of particles they contain and how those particles are arranged and joined.

The smallest piece of an element that still has the properties of that element is an **atom**. There are only about a hundred different kinds of atom, and all matter is built from them.

:::def Element
A substance made of only one kind of atom. An element cannot be broken down into anything simpler by chemical reactions. Examples are hydrogen, iron and sulfur.
:::

:::def Compound
A substance made of two or more different elements chemically joined together, in a fixed ratio. Water, \(\ce{H2O}\), always has two hydrogen atoms for every oxygen atom. A compound has different properties from the elements it is made from.
:::

:::def Molecule
A group of two or more atoms joined together by covalent bonds, which behaves as a single particle. The atoms may be the same, as in oxygen, \(\ce{O2}\), or different, as in water, \(\ce{H2O}\).
:::

:::def Mixture
Two or more substances that are together but not chemically joined. They keep their own properties and can be separated by physical methods. Air is a mixture of nitrogen, oxygen and other gases.
:::

The difference between a compound and a mixture is the most useful idea here. Iron filings and sulfur powder stirred together make a mixture: a magnet pulls the iron out. Heat them and the atoms join to make iron sulfide, a compound with its own properties. A magnet no longer separates it, because there is no iron left as iron.

Mixtures are separated by physical methods that use a difference in properties. Filtration separates an insoluble solid from a liquid. Evaporation recovers a dissolved solid, such as salt from sea water. Distillation separates liquids with different boiling points. Chromatography separates substances that travel at different speeds through a paper or other material.

A substance is a **solid**, a **liquid** or a **gas**, depending on how close its particles are and how freely they move.

|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | regular, closely packed | random, closely packed | random, far apart |
| Movement | vibrate about fixed positions | slide past each other | move quickly and freely in all directions |
| Shape and volume | fixed shape, fixed volume | takes the shape of the container, fixed volume | fills the container, easily compressed |

Changes of state happen when the particles gain or lose energy. Heat a solid and its particles vibrate more strongly, until at the **melting point** they can break free of their fixed positions and the solid becomes a liquid. Keep heating and the particles move faster, until at the **boiling point** they escape from the liquid completely and form a gas. Cooling reverses the changes: a gas **condenses** to a liquid, and a liquid **freezes** to a solid. A few solids, such as solid carbon dioxide ("dry ice"), turn straight into a gas without becoming a liquid. That change is **sublimation**.

The temperature stays the same while a pure substance melts or boils, because the energy you supply is used to separate the particles, not to make them move faster. The stronger the forces between the particles, the more energy is needed and the higher the melting and boiling points. [States of matter](#chem.states) and [Shapes of molecules and intermolecular forces](#chem.shapes) take this much further.

## The atom in one picture

An atom has a tiny, dense centre called the **nucleus**, with electrons moving around it. Nearly all the mass is in the nucleus, and the rest of the atom is mostly empty space. Three kinds of particle build every atom:

| Particle | Where | Relative charge | Relative mass |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | nucleus | +1 | 1 |
| neutron | nucleus | 0 | 1 |
| electron | in shells around the nucleus | −1 | very small (less than 1/1000 of a proton) |

An atom has no overall charge, so **the number of protons equals the number of electrons**. The number of protons decides which element the atom is.

:::def Proton number (atomic number)
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom, written \(Z\). Every atom of a given element has the same proton number.
:::

:::def Nucleon number (mass number)
The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom, written \(A\). The number of neutrons is \(A - Z\).
:::

Chemists write both numbers beside the symbol: \({}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}\) is a sodium atom with nucleon number 23 and proton number 11. It has 11 protons, 11 electrons and \(23 - 11 = 12\) neutrons. Atoms of the same element can have different numbers of neutrons. These are **isotopes**, and [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms) explains them properly.

The electrons are arranged in **shells**, which you can picture as rings around the nucleus. The first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the second up to 8 and the third up to 8 for the first 20 elements. Electrons fill the innermost shell first, then the next, so sodium, with 11 electrons, has the arrangement 2, 8, 1.

## The Periodic Table

The **Periodic Table** lists all the elements in order of increasing proton number, arranged so that elements with similar properties line up. The rows are **periods** and the columns are **groups**. The Cambridge table numbers the groups 1 to 18.

- **Period number** = number of occupied shells. Sodium (2, 8, 1) has three shells and is in Period 3.
- **Group number** tells you how many outer-shell electrons an atom has in the "tall" groups. Groups 1 and 2 have 1 and 2 outer electrons. For groups 13 to 17 the number of outer electrons is the group number minus 10, so group 13 has 3, group 14 has 4, up to group 17 with 7. The noble gases in group 18 have a full outer shell (8 electrons, or 2 for helium).
- The block of metals in the middle, groups 3 to 12, are the **d-block** elements, which include the transition elements such as iron and copper.

The elements on the left and in the middle are **metals**: shiny, they conduct electricity and heat, and they form positive ions. Those on the right are **non-metals**, and a diagonal band of **metalloids** lies between. Some groups have names worth learning: group 1 the alkali metals, group 2 the alkaline earth metals, group 17 the halogens and group 18 the noble gases.

## Ions and ionic formulas

Atoms with a full outer shell are very stable, which is why the noble gases hardly react. Other atoms can reach a full outer shell by losing or gaining electrons. An atom that has lost or gained electrons is no longer neutral: it is an **ion**.

:::def Ion
An atom or group of atoms that has gained or lost electrons, so that it carries an overall electric charge.
:::

Sodium, 2, 8, 1, loses its one outer electron and becomes \(\ce{Na+}\), with the arrangement 2, 8 (the same as neon). Chlorine, 2, 8, 7, gains one electron and becomes \(\ce{Cl-}\), with 2, 8, 8 (the same as argon). A positive ion is a **cation** and a negative ion is an **anion**. **Metals lose electrons to form positive ions, and non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions.**

The charge follows from the group number:

| Group | 1 | 2 | 13 | 15 | 16 | 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ion formed | \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{K+}\) | \(\ce{Mg^2+}\), \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) | \(\ce{Al^3+}\) | \(\ce{N^3-}\) | \(\ce{O^2-}\), \(\ce{S^2-}\) | \(\ce{Cl-}\), \(\ce{Br-}\) |
| Charge | 1+ | 2+ | 3+ | 3− | 2− | 1− |
| Electrons lost or gained | lose 1 | lose 2 | lose 3 | gain 3 | gain 2 | gain 1 |

Transition elements such as iron and copper form ions with different charges (iron forms \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) and \(\ce{Fe^3+}\)), so their charges must be given or learned; [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles) shows how Roman numerals are used for this.

Oppositely charged ions attract each other strongly. This attraction is **ionic bonding**, and it holds the ions of a compound such as sodium chloride in a giant regular pattern called a lattice.

:::def Ionic bonding
The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
:::

Because an ionic compound is a huge lattice and not a small molecule, its formula gives only the simplest ratio of the ions. The formula \(\ce{NaCl}\) means one \(\ce{Na+}\) for each \(\ce{Cl-}\).

### Ions made of more than one atom

Some ions contain several atoms joined together and carry one charge between them. These **polyatomic ions** stay together in reactions. Learn this list now: you will use it in every chapter.

| Name | Formula | Name | Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| hydroxide | \(\ce{OH-}\) | ammonium | \(\ce{NH4+}\) |
| nitrate | \(\ce{NO3-}\) | zinc | \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) |
| hydrogencarbonate | \(\ce{HCO3-}\) | silver | \(\ce{Ag+}\) |
| carbonate | \(\ce{CO3^2-}\) | copper(II) | \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) |
| sulfate | \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) | hydrogen | \(\ce{H+}\) |
| phosphate | \(\ce{PO4^3-}\) | | |

Zinc and silver are not polyatomic, but their charges are fixed and they are easy to forget, so they are in the list. Names help: a single non-metal atom's ion ends in "-ide" (chloride, oxide), and an ion with oxygen in it usually ends in "-ate" (sulfate, nitrate, carbonate, phosphate). The hydroxide ion is the exception that ends in "-ide" yet contains oxygen.

### Writing the formula of an ionic compound

A compound has no overall charge. So the positive and negative charges must cancel.

:::key Charges add to zero
In an ionic formula, (number of cations × charge) + (number of anions × charge) = 0. Choose the smallest numbers of each ion that make the total charge zero. Write the cation first. Put a small number after an ion for how many are needed, and put **brackets** around a polyatomic ion if you need more than one.
:::

:::steps
1. Write each ion with its charge.
2. Find the smallest numbers that make the positive and negative charges equal.
3. Write the formula, with brackets around a polyatomic ion that needs a number.
4. Check that the charges cancel.
:::

:::eg Write the formula of calcium hydroxide.
The ions are \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and \(\ce{OH-}\).

One \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) has charge +2, and each \(\ce{OH-}\) has charge −1, so two hydroxide ions are needed to cancel it: \(+2 + 2(-1) = 0\).

Put brackets round the hydroxide ion because two are needed: \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\).
:::

:::eg Write the formula of ammonium sulfate.
The ions are \(\ce{NH4+}\) and \(\ce{SO4^2-}\).

One sulfate ion has charge −2, so two ammonium ions are needed: \(2(+1) + (-2) = 0\).

The ammonium ion is polyatomic, so it takes brackets: \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\). The sulfate ion needs no brackets because only one is present.
:::

:::eg Write the formula of aluminium sulfate.
The ions are \(\ce{Al^3+}\) and \(\ce{SO4^2-}\).

The charges are 3+ and 2−. The smallest total charge that both 3 and 2 divide into is 6, so use two aluminium ions (\(2 \times 3 = 6\)) and three sulfate ions (\(3 \times 2 = 6\)).

The formula is \(\ce{Al2(SO4)3}\). Check: \(2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0\).
:::

:::warn Do not mix up the charge and the subscript
The charge of an ion goes up and to the right (\(\ce{Mg^2+}\)). Once the ions are put into a formula, the charges are not written, and the small numbers after the symbols count atoms: \(\ce{MgCl2}\) has one magnesium ion and two chloride ions. Also, \(\ce{Mg+}\) does not exist as an ordinary ion: a magnesium atom has 2 outer electrons and loses both.
:::

## Covalent molecules

Non-metal atoms do not usually give away electrons to each other, because each wants to gain them. Instead they **share** electrons. Each shared pair of electrons is one covalent bond.

:::def Covalent bond
The electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons between them.
:::

Hydrogen has one electron and needs one more to fill its first shell, so two hydrogen atoms share a pair: \(\ce{H2}\). Oxygen has 6 outer electrons and needs two more, so it can make two bonds. Nitrogen needs three and makes three bonds. Carbon needs four and makes four. A hydrogen or halogen atom makes one. This number of bonds an atom normally forms is its **valency**.

| Atom | H | Cl (and F, Br, I) | O | N | C |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonds usually made | 1 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |

From the valencies you can predict the formula of a simple molecule. In water, an oxygen atom (2 bonds) joins two hydrogen atoms (1 bond each): \(\ce{H2O}\). In ammonia, nitrogen joins three hydrogen atoms: \(\ce{NH3}\). In methane, carbon joins four: \(\ce{CH4}\). In carbon dioxide, each oxygen makes a **double bond** (two shared pairs) to carbon: \(\ce{CO2}\), drawn O=C=O.

Some elements exist as molecules, not as single atoms. Seven elements are **diatomic**: they are always written with two atoms. Their formulas are \(\ce{H2}\), \(\ce{N2}\), \(\ce{O2}\), \(\ce{F2}\), \(\ce{Cl2}\), \(\ce{Br2}\) and \(\ce{I2}\).

:::tip Remember the seven diatomic elements
"Have No Fear Of Ice Cold Beer": **H**ydrogen, **N**itrogen, **F**luorine, **O**xygen, **I**odine, **C**hlorine, **B**romine. When one of these elements appears in an equation as an element, write it with the subscript 2.
:::

Compounds made only of non-metals, such as water and carbon dioxide, are made of molecules held together by covalent bonds. A compound of a metal with a non-metal, such as sodium chloride, is usually ionic. This is a good first guide to which kind of bonding a compound has. [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding) covers the exceptions.

## Equations

A chemical reaction changes substances into new ones, and an **equation** records it. The substances you start with are the **reactants** and the new substances are the **products**. The atoms are rearranged, but none is created or destroyed, so an equation must have the same number of atoms of each element on both sides. This is the idea of conservation of mass, and it is why equations must be **balanced**.

You can write an equation in words first:

\[\text{magnesium} + \text{oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{magnesium oxide}\]

Then replace each name with its formula. Remember that oxygen is diatomic, and the formula of magnesium oxide comes from its ions, \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\):

\[\ce{Mg + O2 -> MgO}\]

This is not yet balanced, because there are two oxygen atoms on the left and one on the right. The only thing you may change is the **coefficient**, the number placed in front of a formula, which says how many of that formula there are.

:::steps
1. Write the word equation, then the correct formula of every substance (use the ion charges, the valencies and the diatomic elements).
2. Count the atoms of each element on both sides. Count a polyatomic ion in brackets as a unit if it appears unchanged on both sides.
3. Change a coefficient to fix one element. Leave hydrogen and oxygen until last if they appear in several formulas.
4. Recount everything, because changing one coefficient changes the others.
5. Check that the coefficients are the smallest whole numbers, then add state symbols.
:::

For magnesium, put 2 in front of \(\ce{MgO}\) to give two oxygen atoms on the right. That makes two magnesium atoms on the right, so put 2 in front of \(\ce{Mg}\):

\[\ce{2Mg + O2 -> 2MgO}\]

:::warn Never change a formula to balance an equation
Writing \(\ce{MgO2}\) instead of \(\ce{MgO}\) would balance the oxygen, but \(\ce{MgO2}\) is a different substance that does not exist. Only coefficients in front of formulas may be changed, never the small numbers inside a formula.
:::

**State symbols** tell you the physical state of each substance: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas and (aq) aqueous, meaning dissolved in water. A complete equation for magnesium burning is:

\[\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}\]

:::eg Balance the equation for the burning of methane (natural gas): \(\ce{CH4 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}\).
Count the atoms. Left: 1 C, 4 H, 2 O. Right: 1 C, 2 H, 3 O (2 from \(\ce{CO2}\) and 1 from \(\ce{H2O}\)).

Carbon is already balanced. Put 2 in front of \(\ce{H2O}\) to balance hydrogen: 4 H on each side. Now the right has \(2 + 2 = 4\) O atoms, so put 2 in front of \(\ce{O2}\).

\[\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}\]

Check: left 1 C, 4 H, 4 O; right 1 C, 4 H, \(2 + 2 = 4\) O. Balanced.
:::

:::eg Balance the burning of propane, \(\ce{C3H8 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}\), and the burning of aluminium, \(\ce{Al + O2 -> Al2O3}\).
**Propane.** Balance carbon first: 3 C on the left, so 3 \(\ce{CO2}\). Balance hydrogen: 8 H on the left, so 4 \(\ce{H2O}\). Now count oxygen on the right: \(3 \times 2 + 4 \times 1 = 10\) atoms, so we need \(5\,\ce{O2}\):

\[\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}\]

**Aluminium.** The right has 2 Al and 3 O, and the left has 2 O. The smallest number of oxygen atoms that both 2 (in \(\ce{O2}\)) and 3 (in \(\ce{Al2O3}\)) divide into is 6, so use \(3\,\ce{O2}\) and \(2\,\ce{Al2O3}\). Now the right has 4 Al, so put 4 in front of \(\ce{Al}\):

\[\ce{4Al + 3O2 -> 2Al2O3}\]
:::

:::eg Spot the error: Write a balanced equation, with state symbols, for the burning of methane in oxygen. [3 marks]
A student wrote:

\[\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + H2O(l)}\]

All the formulas and state symbols are correct, so the formulas mark is earned [B1] and the state symbols mark is earned [B1]. **The equation is not balanced: the left has 4 hydrogen atoms and the right only 2, and the left has 4 oxygen atoms and the right only 3. The balancing mark is lost [B0].**

The correct equation has \(2\,\ce{H2O}\) and would have earned the balancing mark: \(\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)}\). [B1]

The student scores 2 out of 3. Always recount every element at the end, as a final check on your coefficients.
:::

## Acids, bases and salts in a nutshell

An **acid** is a substance that releases hydrogen ions, \(\ce{H+}\), when it dissolves in water. Hydrochloric acid, \(\ce{HCl}\), sulfuric acid, \(\ce{H2SO4}\), nitric acid, \(\ce{HNO3}\), and ethanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3COOH}\), are the ones you meet most. A **base** is a substance that reacts with an acid to give a salt and water, such as a metal oxide or hydroxide. A base that dissolves in water is an **alkali**, and it releases hydroxide ions, \(\ce{OH-}\), in solution. Sodium hydroxide, NaOH, is an alkali.

The **pH scale** measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is. It runs from 0 to 14: below 7 is acidic, exactly 7 is neutral (pure water) and above 7 is alkaline. Universal indicator turns red or orange in strong acids, green when neutral and blue or purple in strong alkalis.

When an acid reacts with a base, the products are a **salt** and water. This is **neutralisation**. A salt is a compound in which the hydrogen of an acid has been replaced by a metal or by the ammonium ion. The salt's name comes from the acid: hydrochloric acid makes chlorides, sulfuric acid makes sulfates, nitric acid makes nitrates.

:::key Acid reactions to learn
- acid + metal → salt + hydrogen
- acid + metal oxide or hydroxide (a base) → salt + water
- acid + carbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide
:::

\[\ce{Mg(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> MgSO4(aq) + H2(g)}\]
\[\ce{CuO(s) + 2HNO3(aq) -> Cu(NO3)2(aq) + H2O(l)}\]
\[\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\]

In reactions of acids with alkalis, the real change is the same every time: \(\ce{H+(aq)}\) from the acid combines with \(\ce{OH-(aq)}\) from the alkali to make water.

\[\ce{H+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> H2O(l)}\]

Not every metal reacts with dilute acids: copper does not. Gases can be identified by simple tests. Hydrogen makes a lighted splint go "pop". Carbon dioxide gives a white precipitate when bubbled through limewater (calcium hydroxide solution), which is why the limewater turns cloudy.

(Real context.) Indigestion tablets are bases. They neutralise excess hydrochloric acid in the stomach, for example \(\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)}\). The full theory of acids and bases is in [Acids and bases](#chem.acids).

:::eg (Exam style) Zinc reacts with dilute sulfuric acid. Write the balanced equation with state symbols and state the test for the gas. [3 marks]
Zinc is \(\ce{Zn}\) and sulfuric acid is \(\ce{H2SO4}\). The salt is zinc sulfate: \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) and \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) give \(\ce{ZnSO4}\). The gas is hydrogen.

\[\ce{Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)}\]
Formulas of all four substances correct and balanced. [B1]
State symbols correct: (s), (aq), (aq), (g). [B1]

Test for hydrogen: a lighted splint makes a "pop". [B1]
:::

## Numbers chemists use

### Standard form and significant figures

Chemistry has very large and very small numbers, so you must write them in **standard form**: a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten. For example, \(0.00042 = 4.2 \times 10^{-4}\) and \(56\,000 = 5.6 \times 10^{4}\). To enter a number such as \(6.02 \times 10^{23}\) on a calculator, use the key labelled EXP or \(\times 10^x\); do not type "× 10" first. [Calculators](#tools.calc) shows how.

**Significant figures** (s.f.) count the digits that carry information. Count from the first non-zero digit.

- Zeros at the start do not count: 0.0042 has 2 s.f.
- Zeros between other digits do count: 4.005 has 4 s.f.
- Zeros after the decimal point at the end do count: 0.250 has 3 s.f., and 25.0 has 3 s.f.

To round to a given number of significant figures, look at the digit just after the last one you keep. If it is 5 or more, round up; if it is 4 or less, leave the last kept digit alone.

:::key The Cambridge rule for answers
Give a calculated answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data in the question, or one more. If a question gives masses to 3 s.f., give the answer to 3 s.f. (4 s.f. is also acceptable). Never round in the middle of a calculation: keep extra digits in your working, and round only the final answer.
:::

:::eg Round 0.0042516 to 3 significant figures, then write it in standard form.
The first non-zero digit is 4, so the significant figures are 4, 2 and 5. The next digit is 1, which is less than 5, so the 5 stays as it is.

\(0.0042516 = 0.00425\) to 3 s.f. In standard form this is \(4.25 \times 10^{-3}\).
:::

### Units and conversions

| Quantity | Units used | Conversion |
|---|---|---|
| volume | cm³ and dm³ | \(1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) |
| mass | g and kg | \(1\,\mathrm{kg} = 1000\,\mathrm{g}\) |
| energy | J and kJ | \(1\,\mathrm{kJ} = 1000\,\mathrm{J}\) |
| temperature | °C and K | \(T/\mathrm{K} = \theta/{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 273\) |

Chemists measure small volumes in cm³ but use dm³ in calculations, so converting between them is a skill you will use constantly. To go from cm³ to dm³, divide by 1000. To go from dm³ to cm³, multiply by 1000.

:::eg Convert 250 cm³ to dm³.
Divide by 1000: \(\dfrac{250}{1000} = 0.250\,\mathrm{dm^3}\).

A quick check: a dm³ is a larger unit than a cm³, so the number must get smaller.
:::

### A first look at moles

Atoms are far too small to count one at a time, so chemists count them in huge, fixed groups called **moles**, as a baker counts eggs in dozens. The mass of one mole of a substance in grams is its **molar mass**, \(M\), in g mol⁻¹. You find it by adding the relative atomic masses from the Periodic Table: for example, \(M(\ce{NaCl}) = 23.0 + 35.5 = 58.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). The amount of substance, \(n\), is linked to the mass, \(m\), by

\[n = \frac{m}{M}\]

:::eg (Preview) How many moles are there in 5.85 g of sodium chloride?
\(M(\ce{NaCl}) = 23.0 + 35.5 = 58.5\,\mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).

\[n = \frac{5.85}{58.5} = 0.100\,\mathrm{mol}\]
:::

This is only a preview. [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles) develops the mole into the central tool of chemistry.

## Safety and the lab

Chemistry experiments use hazardous substances, so a hazard label comes before the reaction. Learn to read the hazard symbols by their names:

| Hazard | What it means |
|---|---|
| corrosive | destroys skin, eyes and metals on contact (strong acids and alkalis) |
| toxic | can cause serious harm or death if swallowed, breathed in or absorbed through skin |
| flammable | catches fire easily (ethanol, many organic solvents) |
| oxidising | helps other materials burn, and can start a fire with fuels |
| irritant | can cause redness or discomfort of skin, eyes or lungs |

Wear eye protection throughout. Tie hair back. Never taste anything in the lab. Keep flammable liquids away from a flame. Use a fume cupboard for any reaction that makes a toxic gas. Wash off spills with plenty of water and tell your teacher at once.

The apparatus you will meet most:

| Apparatus | Used for |
|---|---|
| beaker | holding and heating liquids, not for accurate measurement |
| conical flask | mixing and titrating, because swirling does not splash |
| measuring cylinder | measuring a volume roughly |
| pipette | measuring one fixed volume of liquid accurately |
| burette | delivering a measured, variable volume of liquid accurately, as in a titration |
| test tube | small-scale reactions and tests |
| Bunsen burner | heating with a flame |

The accurate measuring instruments (pipette, burette, balance) are explained in [Practical skills and qualitative analysis](#chem.practical).

## Exam practice and revision

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in (a) \({}^{23}_{11}\mathrm{Na}\), (b) \({}^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}^{-}\), (c) \({}^{27}_{13}\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\).
A: (a) 11 protons, \(23 - 11 = 12\) neutrons, 11 electrons. (b) 17 protons, \(35 - 17 = 18\) neutrons, 18 electrons (one extra for the 1− charge). (c) 13 protons, \(27 - 13 = 14\) neutrons, 10 electrons (three fewer for the 3+ charge).
Q: An atom of phosphorus has proton number 15. Give its shell arrangement, its group and its period.
A: 15 electrons: 2, 8, 5. It has 3 shells, so Period 3, and 5 outer electrons, so group \(5 + 10 = 15\).
Q: Write the formula of (a) magnesium nitrate, (b) aluminium hydroxide, (c) potassium phosphate, (d) ammonium carbonate.
A: (a) \(\ce{Mg(NO3)2}\). (b) \(\ce{Al(OH)3}\). (c) \(\ce{K3PO4}\): three \(\ce{K+}\) balance one \(\ce{PO4^3-}\). (d) \(\ce{(NH4)2CO3}\).
Q: Write the formula of (a) zinc chloride, (b) silver sulfate, (c) calcium oxide, (d) sodium sulfate.
A: (a) \(\ce{ZnCl2}\). (b) \(\ce{Ag2SO4}\). (c) \(\ce{CaO}\): \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\) cancel in a 1:1 ratio. (d) \(\ce{Na2SO4}\).
Q: Balance (a) \(\ce{N2 + H2 -> NH3}\), (b) \(\ce{Na + H2O -> NaOH + H2}\), (c) \(\ce{C2H6 + O2 -> CO2 + H2O}\).
A: (a) \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}\). (b) \(\ce{2Na + 2H2O -> 2NaOH + H2}\). (c) \(\ce{2C2H6 + 7O2 -> 4CO2 + 6H2O}\).
Q: Balance (a) \(\ce{Fe + O2 -> Fe2O3}\), (b) \(\ce{Al + HCl -> AlCl3 + H2}\), (c) \(\ce{Ca(OH)2 + HNO3 -> Ca(NO3)2 + H2O}\).
A: (a) \(\ce{4Fe + 3O2 -> 2Fe2O3}\). (b) \(\ce{2Al + 6HCl -> 2AlCl3 + 3H2}\). (c) \(\ce{Ca(OH)2 + 2HNO3 -> Ca(NO3)2 + 2H2O}\).
Q: Write a balanced equation with state symbols for (a) calcium carbonate with dilute nitric acid, (b) magnesium hydroxide with dilute hydrochloric acid.
A: (a) \(\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HNO3(aq) -> Ca(NO3)2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\). (b) \(\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)}\).
Q: Convert (a) 35.0 cm³ to dm³, (b) 0.0125 dm³ to cm³, (c) 2.5 kg to g, (d) 48 kJ to J, (e) 25 °C to K.
A: (a) \(35.0 / 1000 = 0.0350\,\mathrm{dm^3}\). (b) \(0.0125 \times 1000 = 12.5\,\mathrm{cm^3}\). (c) 2500 g. (d) 48 000 J. (e) \(25 + 273 = 298\,\mathrm{K}\).
Q: Round (a) 0.0067845 to 3 s.f., (b) 1234.5 to 3 s.f., (c) 0.0003059 to 2 s.f.
A: (a) 0.00678 (the next digit is 4). (b) 1230 (the next digit is 4, and the zero holds the place). (c) 0.00031 (the significant figures start at the 3, and the next digit 5 rounds up).
Q: Write (a) 0.000042 and (b) 0.0302 in standard form, and (c) \(7.5 \times 10^{3}\) as an ordinary number.
A: (a) \(4.2 \times 10^{-5}\). (b) \(3.02 \times 10^{-2}\). (c) 7500.
Q: Using \(n = m/M\), find the amount in (a) 9.00 g of water, \(\ce{H2O}\), and (b) 25.0 g of calcium carbonate, \(\ce{CaCO3}\).
A: (a) \(M = 2(1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0\), so \(n = 9.00 / 18.0 = 0.500\,\mathrm{mol}\). (b) \(M = 40.1 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 100.1\), so \(n = 25.0 / 100.1 = 0.250\,\mathrm{mol}\).
Q: Name the change of state when (a) ice turns into water and (b) solid carbon dioxide turns straight into gas. Why can a gas be compressed but a liquid hardly can?
A: (a) melting. (b) sublimation. In a gas the particles are far apart with a lot of empty space between them, so they can be pushed closer. In a liquid they already touch.

:::recap
- An element has one kind of atom, a compound has different atoms chemically joined in a fixed ratio, and a mixture is not chemically joined. Solids, liquids and gases differ in how close the particles are and how they move.
- Atoms contain protons (+1) and neutrons in the nucleus, and electrons (−1) in shells of 2, 8 and 8. Proton number = protons = electrons in a neutral atom; nucleon number = protons + neutrons.
- Period number = number of shells. For groups 1, 2 and 13–17 the outer electrons are 1, 2 and (group − 10). Noble gases have a full outer shell.
- Metals lose electrons to form positive ions and non-metals gain electrons to form negative ions. The total charge in an ionic formula is zero. Learn \(\ce{NO3-}\), \(\ce{CO3^2-}\), \(\ce{SO4^2-}\), \(\ce{OH-}\), \(\ce{NH4+}\), \(\ce{Zn^2+}\), \(\ce{Ag+}\), \(\ce{HCO3-}\), \(\ce{PO4^3-}\).
- A covalent bond is a shared pair of electrons. Valencies: H 1, Cl 1, O 2, N 3, C 4. The diatomic elements are \(\ce{H2}\), \(\ce{N2}\), \(\ce{O2}\), \(\ce{F2}\), \(\ce{Cl2}\), \(\ce{Br2}\), \(\ce{I2}\).
- Balance by changing coefficients only, never subscripts. State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq).
- Acid + metal gives salt + hydrogen; acid + base gives salt + water; acid + carbonate gives salt + water + carbon dioxide.
- Standard form, significant figures, \(1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3}\), \(T/\mathrm{K} = \theta/{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C} + 273\) and \(n = m/M\).
:::
