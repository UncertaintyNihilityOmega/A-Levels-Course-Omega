:::why
The Periodic Table is chemistry's map, and Period 3 is the part of the map that is easiest to read: eight elements in a row, from a soft metal to a gas, with every kind of structure and bonding along the way. If you can explain why sodium oxide makes an alkali, silicon dioxide does nothing and sulfur trioxide makes a strong acid, you can reason about elements you have never met. That is exactly what exam questions on this topic ask, and the same reasoning carries into [Group 2](#chem.group2), [Group 17](#chem.group17) and the transition elements.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know why atomic radius falls across a period from [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms), what electronegativity and bond polarity are from [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding), and the four types of structure from [States of matter](#chem.states). Oxidation numbers are in [Redox and oxidation numbers](#chem.redox).

:::try Warm-up
Q: Explain why the atomic radius of chlorine is smaller than that of sodium. (from [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms))
A: Both have their outer electrons in the third shell, but chlorine has 17 protons against 11. The shielding is about the same, so the nuclear attraction on the outer electrons is stronger in chlorine and pulls them closer.
Q: Pauling electronegativities are Na 0.9 and Cl 3.0. What kind of bonding does sodium chloride have, and why? (from [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding))
A: Ionic. The electronegativity difference (2.1) is large, so sodium gives its outer electron to chlorine completely.
Q: Why does diamond melt at a far higher temperature than iodine? (from [States of matter](#chem.states))
A: Diamond is a giant covalent structure, so strong covalent bonds must be broken. Iodine is simple molecular, so only weak id-id forces between the molecules must be overcome.
:::

## What periodicity means

:::def Periodicity
A repeating pattern in the properties of the elements when they are arranged in order of atomic number. The pattern repeats because the same outer-shell electron configurations come round again in each period.
:::

A **period** is a row of the Periodic Table. Sodium starts Period 3 and has one outer electron in the third shell, just as lithium starts Period 2 and has one outer electron in the second. Because the properties of an element depend mostly on its outer electrons, the pattern across Period 3 is close to the pattern across Period 2, and then to Period 4.

Period 3 holds eight elements. Look at their structures: this one table explains most of the chapter.

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Outer electrons | 3s¹ | 3s² | 3s² 3p¹ | 3s² 3p² | 3s² 3p³ | 3s² 3p⁴ | 3s² 3p⁵ | 3s² 3p⁶ |
| Number of outer electrons | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 |
| Structure | giant metallic | giant metallic | giant metallic | giant covalent | simple molecular, \(\ce{P4}\) | simple molecular, \(\ce{S8}\) | simple molecular, \(\ce{Cl2}\) | single atoms |

Everything in this chapter follows from two facts: the **nuclear charge rises** by one proton at each step, and the outer electrons are added to the **same shell**, so the shielding stays about the same. The bonding changes from metallic to giant covalent to simple molecular as the atoms grow better at attracting electrons.

## Atomic radius and ionic radius

You met the reason for the fall in atomic radius in [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms): across the period the nuclear charge increases, the extra electrons go into the same shell, and the shielding by inner electrons hardly changes. The outer electrons are pulled closer, so the atoms get **smaller** from sodium to chlorine. (Argon is left out because noble-gas atoms do not form bonds, so their radius is measured in a different way and cannot be compared directly.)

The ions follow a pattern with a break in the middle.

- The **positive ions** \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{Al^3+}\) have each lost the whole third shell, so they are much smaller than their atoms. They all have the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ (10 electrons), and the number of protons rises from 11 to 13, so the pull on the same 10 electrons gets stronger and the ions get smaller: \(\ce{Na+} > \ce{Mg^2+} > \ce{Al^3+}\).
- The **negative ions** \(\ce{P^3-}\), \(\ce{S^2-}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\) have gained electrons into the third shell, so they are larger than their atoms and larger than the positive ions, which have lost that shell. They all have the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (18 electrons), so again the larger nuclear charge shrinks them: \(\ce{P^3-} > \ce{S^2-} > \ce{Cl-}\).
- Silicon does not form a simple ion in compounds: gaining or losing four electrons takes far too much energy.

\(\ce{S^2-}\) has an extra shell of electrons and fewer protons than the number needed to pull a third shell in tightly, so it is larger. [B1]

## Melting point

The melting point is not a smooth trend. It rises, peaks at silicon, falls steeply, and then stays low. The shape comes straight from the structure of each element, so this is the place to use [States of matter](#chem.states).

### Sodium, magnesium and aluminium: giant metallic

In a metal, positive ions sit in a lattice surrounded by delocalised electrons, and melting means loosening the lattice, which needs the attraction between ions and electrons to be overcome. That attraction gets stronger from Na to Al for three reasons:

1. The ion charge rises: \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Mg^2+}\), \(\ce{Al^3+}\).
2. The number of delocalised electrons per atom rises: 1, 2, 3.
3. The ions get smaller, so the charge is concentrated on less volume (a higher charge density), and the electrons sit closer to the positive ions.

A bigger charge on the ions and more electrons in the "sea" give a stronger electrostatic attraction, so more energy is needed to melt the metal.

### Silicon: giant covalent

Silicon has the same structure as diamond: every atom is joined to four others by single covalent bonds in a tetrahedral arrangement, in a giant lattice. To melt it you must break a very large number of strong covalent bonds, which takes a lot of energy. Silicon has the **highest** melting point in the period.

### Phosphorus, sulfur, chlorine and argon: simple molecular

These elements exist as separate small units: \(\ce{P4}\) molecules, \(\ce{S8}\) rings, \(\ce{Cl2}\) molecules and single argon atoms. When they melt, only the weak **id-id forces** between the units are overcome. The covalent bonds inside the molecules are not touched. The strength of id-id forces rises with the number of electrons (more electrons, larger electron cloud, larger induced dipoles). Count them:

| Unit | \(\ce{S8}\) | \(\ce{P4}\) | \(\ce{Cl2}\) | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electrons per unit | 128 | 60 | 34 | 18 |
| Melting point order | highest | | | lowest |

So the order of melting point is \(\ce{S8} > \ce{P4} > \ce{Cl2} > \text{Ar}\).

:::warn Do not "break covalent bonds" to melt a molecule
"Sulfur has a low melting point because its S–S bonds are weak" is wrong. The S–S bonds in an \(\ce{S8}\) ring are strong and they do not break when sulfur melts. It is the weak forces **between** the rings that are overcome. For silicon the opposite is true: its covalent bonds are the whole structure, so they must be broken.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why the melting point of silicon is much higher than that of phosphorus. [3 marks]
Silicon has a giant covalent structure, with every atom joined to four others by strong covalent bonds. [B1]

Phosphorus is simple molecular, made of \(\ce{P4}\) molecules held together only by weak id-id forces. [B1]

Much more energy is needed to break the many strong covalent bonds in silicon than to overcome the weak id-id forces between \(\ce{P4}\) molecules. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why the melting point of sulfur is higher than that of chlorine, and why that of magnesium is higher than that of sodium. [4 marks]
Sulfur is \(\ce{S8}\) and chlorine is \(\ce{Cl2}\), both simple molecular. \(\ce{S8}\) has more electrons (128 against 34), so stronger id-id forces between the molecules. [B1]

More energy is needed to overcome the stronger id-id forces between \(\ce{S8}\) molecules. [B1]

Magnesium has \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) ions and 2 delocalised electrons per atom, against \(\ce{Na+}\) and 1 for sodium; the \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) ion is also smaller. [B1]

So the attraction between the positive ions and the delocalised electrons is stronger in magnesium, and more energy is needed to melt it. [B1]
:::

## Electrical conductivity

A substance conducts electricity if it contains charged particles that are free to move. The pattern across Period 3 is as follows.

| Element | Conductivity | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Na, Mg, Al | good, and increasing from Na to Al | delocalised electrons move; each Al atom contributes 3 electrons, each Na atom only 1 |
| Si | poor: a **semiconductor** | few electrons are free to move at room temperature; unlike a metal, it conducts better when warmed |
| P, S, Cl, Ar | none | simple molecules or atoms, with no free electrons or ions |

Silicon is the unusual one. Its electrons are all held in covalent bonds, so a pure sample is a poor conductor. A little energy (heat or light) frees a few of them, so its conductivity **rises** with temperature, the opposite of a metal. This is why silicon is the material of computer chips.

:::key Structure and bonding explain melting point and conductivity
Giant metallic (Na, Mg, Al): high melting point, rising across the period; conduct. Giant covalent (Si): highest melting point; semiconductor. Simple molecular (P₄, S₈, Cl₂, Ar): low melting point from weak id-id forces, higher for the molecules with more electrons; do not conduct.
:::

## First ionisation energy and electronegativity

Two more properties change across the period, and both explain the chemistry that follows. The first ionisation energy is the energy needed to remove the outermost electron from each atom in a mole of gaseous atoms. Its trend is explained in [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms): it **increases** across the period, because the nuclear charge rises while the shielding stays about the same, with two small dips (Mg to Al, because the 3p electron of aluminium is higher in energy and shielded by the 3s electrons, and P to S, because the sulfur 3p electron is repelled by the other electron in its orbital).

**Electronegativity** is the power of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond. It rises across the period for the same reason, and the trend is smooth.

| Element | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S | Cl | Ar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| First ionisation energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | 494 | 736 | 577 | 786 | 1060 | 1000 | 1260 | 1520 |
| Pauling electronegativity | 0.9 | 1.3 | 1.5 | 1.9 | 2.2 | 2.6 | 3.0 | no value |

Argon has no electronegativity because it forms no bonds. The key idea for everything below is the electronegativity of oxygen (3.5) and chlorine (3.0) compared with each Period 3 element. When the difference is large, the electrons transfer and the compound is **ionic**. When the difference is small, the electrons are shared and the compound is **covalent**.

| Oxide | \(\ce{Na2O}\) | \(\ce{MgO}\) | \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | \(\ce{SiO2}\) | \(\ce{P4O10}\) | \(\ce{SO2}\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity difference from O (3.5) | 2.6 | 2.2 | 2.0 | 1.6 | 1.3 | 0.9 |

| Chloride | NaCl | \(\ce{MgCl2}\) | \(\ce{AlCl3}\) | \(\ce{SiCl4}\) | \(\ce{PCl5}\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity difference from Cl (3.0) | 2.1 | 1.7 | 1.5 | 1.1 | 0.8 |

The difference falls steadily from left to right: the bonding changes from ionic to covalent. The change is gradual, and a few compounds in the middle (aluminium oxide and aluminium chloride) are between the two. Their **properties** show which side they are on.

## Reactions of the elements

### With oxygen

All the Period 3 elements except argon burn or react in oxygen to give an oxide. The metals give ionic oxides, and the non-metals give covalent oxides.

| Element | Observation | Equation | Oxide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na | burns with a bright yellow flame | \(\ce{4Na(s) + O2(g) -> 2Na2O(s)}\) | white solid |
| Mg | burns with a bright white flame | \(\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}\) | white solid |
| Al | a layer of oxide protects the metal; powder burns with a white flame | \(\ce{4Al(s) + 3O2(g) -> 2Al2O3(s)}\) | white solid |
| Si | reacts only at high temperature | \(\ce{Si(s) + O2(g) -> SiO2(s)}\) | white solid |
| P | burns with a white flame | \(\ce{P4(s) + 5O2(g) -> P4O10(s)}\) | white solid |
| S | burns with a blue flame | \(\ce{S(s) + O2(g) -> SO2(g)}\) | colourless, choking gas |

Sodium also forms some sodium peroxide, \(\ce{Na2O2}\), in excess oxygen, but the oxide to know is \(\ce{Na2O}\). Sulfur burns to **sulfur dioxide**, not sulfur trioxide. Sulfur trioxide, \(\ce{SO3}\), forms only when \(\ce{SO2}\) reacts with more oxygen using a catalyst (see the Contact process in [Nitrogen and sulfur](#chem.nitrogen)): \(\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}\).

:::warn Write P₄O₁₀, not P₂O₅
The formula \(\ce{P2O5}\) is the simplest ratio, but the oxide exists as \(\ce{P4O10}\) molecules. Write \(\ce{P4(s) + 5O2(g) -> P4O10(s)}\) and keep to \(\ce{P4O10}\) in every later equation.
:::

### With chlorine

The metals and non-metals burn or react on heating in chlorine to give chlorides.

| Element | Observation | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Na | burns with a bright yellow flame, white solid | \(\ce{2Na(s) + Cl2(g) -> 2NaCl(s)}\) |
| Mg | burns with a bright white flame, white solid | \(\ce{Mg(s) + Cl2(g) -> MgCl2(s)}\) |
| Al | heated in dry chlorine; a pale solid sublimes and collects | \(\ce{2Al(s) + 3Cl2(g) -> 2AlCl3(s)}\) |
| Si | heated in chlorine, a colourless liquid distils | \(\ce{Si(s) + 2Cl2(g) -> SiCl4(l)}\) |
| P | burns in excess chlorine, a pale yellow solid | \(\ce{2P(s) + 5Cl2(g) -> 2PCl5(s)}\) |

Phosphorus in a limited supply of chlorine makes the liquid \(\ce{PCl3}\) instead (phosphorus uses only 3 of its 5 outer electrons). The syllabus chloride is \(\ce{PCl5}\), made in excess chlorine.

### Oxidation numbers from outer electrons

In every reaction above, the element is **oxidised**: its oxidation number rises from 0. The rule is simple. When a Period 3 element burns in oxygen or chlorine it uses some or all of its outer electrons, and the oxidation number equals the number of outer electrons used (for a metal giving them away, or a non-metal sharing them with a more electronegative atom).

| Oxide | \(\ce{Na2O}\) | \(\ce{MgO}\) | \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | \(\ce{SiO2}\) | \(\ce{P4O10}\) | \(\ce{SO2}\) | \(\ce{SO3}\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation number of the element | +1 | +2 | +3 | +4 | +5 | +4 | +6 |

| Chloride | NaCl | \(\ce{MgCl2}\) | \(\ce{AlCl3}\) | \(\ce{SiCl4}\) | \(\ce{PCl5}\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxidation number of the element | +1 | +2 | +3 | +4 | +5 |

Oxygen is −2 and chlorine −1 in these compounds. Sulfur has two oxides because it can use 4 of its 6 outer electrons (SO₂) or all 6 (SO₃). The highest oxidation number an element reaches in these compounds is its number of outer electrons, which is its group number (apart from the last three elements, whose outer shells are nearly full).

:::eg Use the outer electrons to deduce the oxidation number of the Period 3 element in (a) \(\ce{MgCl2}\), (b) \(\ce{P4O10}\), (c) \(\ce{SO3}\). Check by balancing the charges.
(a) Magnesium has 2 outer electrons and loses both: Mg is +2. Check: \(+2 + 2(-1) = 0\).

(b) Phosphorus has 5 outer electrons and all are used: P is +5. Check with 4 P atoms and 10 O atoms: \(4(+5) + 10(-2) = 0\).

(c) Sulfur has 6 outer electrons and all are used with oxygen: S is +6. Check: \(+6 + 3(-2) = 0\).
:::

### With water

Only sodium and magnesium are on the syllabus here.

**Sodium** reacts vigorously with cold water. It floats, melts into a ball because the reaction gives out a lot of heat, moves about on the surface and fizzes. The solution is strongly alkaline.

\[\ce{2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g)}\]

:::lab Safety with sodium
Keep sodium under oil. Use a piece no bigger than a grain of rice, in a large trough of water behind a safety screen, with eye protection. Never touch it with wet hands.
:::

**Magnesium** reacts very slowly with cold water (a few bubbles form on the ribbon and the solution becomes weakly alkaline), but it burns in steam with a bright white flame.

\[\ce{Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g)}\ \text{(slow, cold water)}\]
\[\ce{Mg(s) + H2O(g) -> MgO(s) + H2(g)}\ \text{(steam)}\]

Aluminium does not react with water because the unbroken oxide layer protects it. Silicon, phosphorus and sulfur do not react with water. Chlorine reacts partly with water to make an acidic solution (see [Group 17: the halogens](#chem.group17)).

:::eg (Exam style) Magnesium reacts differently with cold water and with steam. Write an equation for each reaction and say which is faster. [3 marks]
The reaction with steam is much faster than with cold water. [B1]

\(\ce{Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g)}\) (cold water) [B1]

\(\ce{Mg(s) + H2O(g) -> MgO(s) + H2(g)}\) (steam) [B1]
:::

## The oxides

### Structure and bonding

| Oxide | \(\ce{Na2O}\) | \(\ce{MgO}\) | \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | \(\ce{SiO2}\) | \(\ce{P4O10}\) | \(\ce{SO2}\), \(\ce{SO3}\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonding | ionic | ionic | ionic with some covalent character | covalent | covalent | covalent |
| Structure | giant ionic | giant ionic | giant ionic | giant covalent | simple molecular | simple molecular |
| Melting point | high | very high | very high | very high | low | very low |
| Conducts when molten? | yes | yes | yes | no | no | no |

The properties give the bonding away. An oxide that conducts when molten contains free ions, so it is ionic. One that does not conduct has no ions, so it is covalent. A very high melting point means a giant structure and a low one means simple molecules. This is how the exam asks you to "suggest the bonding from the properties".

### With water and the likely pH

The oxides split into three groups by what they do with water.

| Oxide | Reaction with water | Likely pH of the mixture |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{Na2O}\) | \(\ce{Na2O(s) + H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq)}\) | about 13 to 14 (strongly alkaline) |
| \(\ce{MgO}\) | \(\ce{MgO(s) + H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s)}\), only slightly soluble | about 9 to 10 (weakly alkaline) |
| \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | insoluble, no reaction | 7 |
| \(\ce{SiO2}\) | insoluble, no reaction | 7 |
| \(\ce{P4O10}\) | \(\ce{P4O10(s) + 6H2O(l) -> 4H3PO4(aq)}\) | about 2 (acidic) |
| \(\ce{SO2}\) | \(\ce{SO2(g) + H2O(l) <=> H2SO3(aq)}\) | about 2 to 3 (acidic) |
| \(\ce{SO3}\) | \(\ce{SO3(g) + H2O(l) -> H2SO4(aq)}\) | about 1 (strongly acidic) |

The pH values are typical. The exact pH depends on the concentration, so write "about" and give a range. The reaction of ionic oxides is the oxide ion taking an \(\ce{H+}\) from water: \(\ce{O^2-(s) + H2O(l) -> 2OH-(aq)}\). The covalent oxides of the non-metals form acids because they react with water to give \(\ce{H+}\) ions.

:::warn Silicon dioxide does not make an acid with water
\(\ce{SiO2}\) is a giant covalent structure and is insoluble in water, so the pH stays at 7. It is acidic because it reacts with bases (below), not because it dissolves in water.
:::

:::eg A student dissolves 0.310 g of sodium oxide in water and makes the solution up to 250 cm³. Calculate the pH, taking \(K_w = 1.00 \times 10^{-14}\) mol² dm⁻⁶ at room temperature.
\(M_r(\ce{Na2O}) = 2(23.0) + 16.0 = 62.0\), so \(n = 0.310/62.0 = 0.005000\) mol. [B1]

Each \(\ce{Na2O}\) gives 2 \(\ce{OH-}\), so \(n(\ce{OH-}) = 0.01000\) mol, and \(c(\ce{OH-}) = 0.01000/0.250 = 0.04000\) mol dm⁻³. [M1]

\[[\ce{H+}] = \frac{K_w}{[\ce{OH-}]} = \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-14}}{0.04000} = 2.50 \times 10^{-13}\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}\] [M1]
\[\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}(2.50 \times 10^{-13}) = 12.60\] [A1]

This lies in the range given in the table. (See [Acids and bases](#chem.acids) for the method.)
:::

### Acid–base behaviour

An oxide is **basic** if it reacts with an acid to form a salt and water, and **acidic** if it reacts with a base to form a salt and water.

:::def Amphoteric
An amphoteric oxide or hydroxide is one that shows both acidic and basic behaviour: it reacts with both acids and bases to form salts.
:::

- **Basic: \(\ce{Na2O}\) and \(\ce{MgO}\).** They react with acids.
 \[\ce{Na2O(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l)}\]
 \[\ce{MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l)}\]
- **Amphoteric: \(\ce{Al2O3}\).** It reacts with acids like a base, and with sodium hydroxide like an acid. In excess aqueous NaOH it dissolves to form sodium tetrahydroxoaluminate, which contains the complex ion \(\ce{[Al(OH)4]-}\).
 \[\ce{Al2O3(s) + 6HCl(aq) -> 2AlCl3(aq) + 3H2O(l)}\]
 \[\ce{Al2O3(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 3H2O(l) -> 2NaAl(OH)4(aq)}\]
- **Acidic: \(\ce{SiO2}\), \(\ce{P4O10}\), \(\ce{SO2}\) and \(\ce{SO3}\).** They react with bases.
 \[\ce{SiO2(s) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2SiO3(aq) + H2O(l)}\ \text{(hot, concentrated NaOH)}\]
 \[\ce{P4O10(s) + 12NaOH(aq) -> 4Na3PO4(aq) + 6H2O(l)}\]
 \[\ce{SO2(g) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2SO3(aq) + H2O(l)}\]
 \[\ce{SO3(g) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2SO4(aq) + H2O(l)}\]

The behaviour changes from basic through amphoteric to acidic as the bonding changes from ionic to covalent. The oxides of the metals contain \(\ce{O^2-}\) ions, which are bases, and the covalent oxides of the non-metals react with water to give acids.

:::tip Why is aluminium oxide amphoteric?
\(\ce{Al^3+}\) is small and highly charged, so it pulls on the electrons of the oxide ion and the bonding in \(\ce{Al2O3}\) has some covalent character. The oxide is between the basic metal oxides and the acidic non-metal oxides, so it can act both ways.
:::

### The hydroxides

The hydroxides of sodium, magnesium and aluminium follow the same pattern.

| Hydroxide | \(\ce{NaOH}\) | \(\ce{Mg(OH)2}\) | \(\ce{Al(OH)3}\) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | strong base (alkali) | weak base | amphoteric |
| With acid | neutralised | neutralised | neutralised |
| With excess NaOH(aq) | no reaction | no reaction | dissolves |

\[\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)}\]
\[\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + 3HCl(aq) -> AlCl3(aq) + 3H2O(l)}\]
\[\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + NaOH(aq) -> NaAl(OH)4(aq)}\]

Sodium hydroxide is an alkali, so it is fully dissolved. Magnesium hydroxide is only sparingly soluble, which is why it is used as an antacid, "milk of magnesia": the small amount that dissolves is enough to neutralise stomach acid, but too little to be harmful. Aluminium hydroxide is the white precipitate formed when aluminium ions are mixed with sodium hydroxide solution, and it **dissolves in excess** to give a colourless solution.

:::eg Write equations for the reactions of aluminium hydroxide with (a) hydrochloric acid and (b) aqueous sodium hydroxide, and say what the two reactions show about it.
(a) \(\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + 3HCl(aq) -> AlCl3(aq) + 3H2O(l)}\)

(b) \(\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + NaOH(aq) -> NaAl(OH)4(aq)}\), or \(\ce{Al(OH)3(s) + OH-(aq) -> [Al(OH)4]-(aq)}\)

In (a) it reacts as a base and in (b) as an acid, so aluminium hydroxide is **amphoteric**. Magnesium hydroxide only reacts in (a), so it is basic.
:::

:::eg 0.780 g of aluminium hydroxide is dissolved completely in aqueous sodium hydroxide of concentration 1.00 mol dm⁻³. Calculate the minimum volume of sodium hydroxide solution needed.
The equation is \(\ce{Al(OH)3 + NaOH -> NaAl(OH)4}\), so 1 mol of \(\ce{Al(OH)3}\) needs 1 mol of NaOH.

\(M_r(\ce{Al(OH)3}) = 27.0 + 3(16.0 + 1.0) = 78.0\), so \(n = 0.780/78.0 = 0.0100\) mol. [B1]

\(n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.0100\) mol [M1]

\[V = \frac{n}{c} = \frac{0.0100}{1.00} = 0.0100\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 10.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}\] [A1]
:::

## The chlorides

### Structure, bonding and reaction with water

| Chloride | NaCl | \(\ce{MgCl2}\) | \(\ce{AlCl3}\) | \(\ce{SiCl4}\) | \(\ce{PCl5}\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bonding and structure | ionic, giant | ionic, giant | covalent, simple molecular (\(\ce{Al2Cl6}\) in the vapour) | covalent, simple molecular | covalent, simple molecular |
| Melting point | high | high | low (it sublimes) | very low (liquid) | low |
| Conducts when molten? | yes | yes | no | no | no |
| With water | dissolves | dissolves | hydrolysed | hydrolysed | hydrolysed |
| Likely pH | 7 | about 6.5 | about 3 | about 1 to 2 | about 1 to 2 |

**Sodium chloride** and **magnesium chloride** are ionic. Water molecules surround the ions and they dissolve with no chemical change. \(\ce{Na+}\) is large and carries a single charge, so the solution is neutral (pH 7). \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is smaller and carries a double charge, and its hydrated ion is a very weak acid, so a solution of magnesium chloride is very slightly acidic (pH about 6.5).

The other three chlorides are covalent and are **hydrolysed** by water: water reacts with them and the bonds to chlorine are broken. The chlorine ends up as hydrogen chloride, which makes the solution strongly acidic and gives steamy white fumes in moist air.

:::def Hydrolysis
A reaction in which a compound is broken down by water, forming new compounds.
:::

\[\ce{AlCl3(s) + 3H2O(l) -> Al(OH)3(s) + 3HCl(aq)}\]
\[\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}\]
\[\ce{PCl5(s) + 4H2O(l) -> H3PO4(aq) + 5HCl(aq)}\]

In a lot of water, aluminium chloride dissolves to give \(\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+}\) and chloride ions, and the hydrated ion is a weak acid (pH about 3): \(\ce{[Al(H2O)6]^3+(aq) <=> [Al(H2O)5(OH)]^2+(aq) + H+(aq)}\). The small, 3+ aluminium ion pulls so hard on the electrons in the water molecules that it makes them release \(\ce{H+}\). You will meet this idea again in [Transition elements](#chem.transition).

:::eg (Exam style) A sample of \(\ce{NaCl}\) and a sample of \(\ce{SiCl4}\) are each added to water. Describe the observations and give equations for any reactions. [5 marks]
Sodium chloride dissolves to give a neutral solution (pH 7); there is no reaction apart from dissolving. [B1]
\[\ce{NaCl(s) + aq -> Na+(aq) + Cl-(aq)}\] [B1]

Silicon tetrachloride reacts vigorously with water, giving steamy white fumes of hydrogen chloride and a white precipitate of silicon dioxide. [B1]
\[\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}\] [B1]

The solution is strongly acidic (pH about 1 to 2) because of the hydrogen chloride. [B1]
:::

:::eg 0.0100 mol of silicon tetrachloride is added to water and the mixture is made up to 1.00 dm³. Calculate the pH, assuming that the hydrogen chloride is fully ionised.
\(\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}\): 1 mol of \(\ce{SiCl4}\) gives 4 mol of HCl.

\(n(\ce{HCl}) = 4 \times 0.0100 = 0.0400\) mol, so \([\ce{H+}] = 0.0400\) mol dm⁻³, as HCl is a strong acid. [M1]

\[\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}(0.0400) = 1.40\] [A1]

The value agrees with the range "about 1 to 2".
:::

### Why the chlorides of Si and P are hydrolysed but NaCl is not

In an ionic chloride the bonding is the attraction between ions, and water simply separates and surrounds the ions. In a covalent chloride the Si–Cl or P–Cl bond is polar, \(\ce{Si^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}}\), and a lone pair on the oxygen of a water molecule can attack the \(\delta+\) atom. Silicon and phosphorus are in the third period and can accept extra electrons into low-energy empty orbitals. (Carbon tetrachloride, \(\ce{CCl4}\), is not hydrolysed: carbon is in Period 2 and has no such orbitals available, and the carbon atom is also crowded by the four large chlorine atoms.)

## Explaining the trends: bonding and electronegativity

You now have every observation. The explanation behind them is a single idea.

:::key From ionic to covalent across Period 3
The electronegativity of the elements rises from Na (0.9) to Cl (3.0), so the difference from oxygen or chlorine falls from left to right. Large differences give **ionic** bonding (Na₂O, MgO, NaCl, MgCl₂), which gives giant ionic structures that are basic oxides, that conduct when molten, and that dissolve in water without being hydrolysed. Small differences give **covalent** bonding (SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, SO₃, SiCl₄, PCl₅), giving acidic oxides, and chlorides that are hydrolysed by water. Aluminium is in the middle: the oxide and the chloride show properties of both.
:::

The table sums it up.

| | Na | Mg | Al | Si | P | S |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Oxide | \(\ce{Na2O}\) | \(\ce{MgO}\) | \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | \(\ce{SiO2}\) | \(\ce{P4O10}\) | \(\ce{SO2}\), \(\ce{SO3}\) |
| Bonding | ionic | ionic | ionic (some covalent) | covalent | covalent | covalent |
| With water | alkaline, pH 13 to 14 | weakly alkaline, pH 9 to 10 | insoluble | insoluble | acid, pH about 2 | acid, pH 1 to 3 |
| Acid–base nature | basic | basic | amphoteric | acidic | acidic | acidic |
| Chloride | NaCl | \(\ce{MgCl2}\) | \(\ce{AlCl3}\) | \(\ce{SiCl4}\) | \(\ce{PCl5}\) | |
| Chloride with water | dissolves, pH 7 | dissolves, pH 6.5 | hydrolysed, pH 3 | hydrolysed, pH 1 to 2 | hydrolysed, pH 1 to 2 | |

:::warn Aluminium chloride is not ionic
The electronegativity difference between Al (1.5) and Cl (3.0) is small, and the properties confirm it: \(\ce{AlCl3}\) sublimes easily, it does not conduct when molten, and the vapour is made of \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\) molecules (see [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding)). Writing "ionic aluminium chloride" loses the mark. For the oxide, "ionic with some covalent character" is the safe answer.
:::

## Suggesting the bonding from the properties

An exam question may give the properties of a Period 3 compound and ask for its structure and bonding. Work through the evidence in this order.

:::steps
1. **Melting point.** High means a giant structure (ionic, metallic or covalent); low means simple molecules.
2. **Electrical conductivity when molten.** If it conducts, it contains free ions (ionic) or free electrons (metallic). If not, the structure is covalent (giant covalent or simple molecular).
3. **Effect of water.** If it dissolves and gives a neutral or alkaline solution, it is probably ionic. If it reacts to give an acidic solution, it is a covalent compound of a non-metal, or an acidic oxide.
4. **Check against electronegativity.** A small difference supports covalent bonding.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Substance X is a white solid. It melts at a very high temperature, conducts electricity when molten, is insoluble in water, and dissolves in both dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide. Identify X (one of the Period 3 oxides), and state its bonding. [4 marks]
Conducts when molten and has a very high melting point, so it contains ions in a giant ionic lattice. [B1]

It dissolves in both an acid and an alkali, so it is amphoteric. [B1]

X is aluminium oxide, \(\ce{Al2O3}\). [B1]

Bonding: ionic, with some covalent character because of the small, highly charged \(\ce{Al^3+}\) ion. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Chlorides A, B and C are chlorides of three different Period 3 elements. A is a white solid with a high melting point that dissolves in water to give a neutral solution and conducts when molten. B is a colourless liquid that fumes in moist air and reacts with water to give a white solid and a strongly acidic solution. C is a white solid that sublimes easily and does not conduct when molten, and it gives an acidic solution (pH about 3) in water. Identify A, B and C. [3 marks]
A conducts when molten and dissolves without reacting, so it is an ionic chloride with a neutral solution: sodium chloride, NaCl. [B1]

B is a covalent liquid that is hydrolysed by water, giving HCl (the fumes and the acid) and a white solid, silicon(IV) oxide: B is silicon tetrachloride, \(\ce{SiCl4}\). [B1]

C is covalent (it sublimes and does not conduct when molten) but gives only a weakly acidic solution, pH about 3: aluminium chloride, \(\ce{AlCl3}\). [B1]
:::

## Predicting and deducing: the Periodic Table as a tool

Elements in the same group have the same number of outer electrons, so they behave in a similar way, with a trend down the group as the atoms get larger and the outer electrons less tightly held. To predict an element's properties, take the Period 3 member of its group as the pattern, then adjust for the trend: down a group the elements become more metallic, so their oxides become more basic and their compounds more ionic.

:::eg Germanium, Ge, is in Group 14 directly below silicon. Predict its structure, its electrical conductivity and the reaction of its chloride with water.
Germanium is in the same group as silicon, so it should also have a giant covalent structure with a high melting point.

Elements on the border between metals and non-metals are semiconductors, so germanium should be a semiconductor, conducting poorly at room temperature.

Its chloride should be \(\ce{GeCl4}\), a covalent, volatile liquid like \(\ce{SiCl4}\), and it should be hydrolysed by water to the oxide and hydrogen chloride: \(\ce{GeCl4 + 2H2O -> GeO2 + 4HCl}\).
:::

:::eg Selenium, Se, is in Group 16 directly below sulfur. Predict the formula and nature of its oxide, and what it does with water.
Selenium has 6 outer electrons like sulfur, so it forms an oxide \(\ce{SeO2}\), like \(\ce{SO2}\).

\(\ce{SO2}\) is a covalent, acidic oxide, so \(\ce{SeO2}\) should also be acidic, reacting with water to form an acid and with NaOH to form a salt.

\(\ce{SeO2 + H2O -> H2SeO3}\) (an acidic solution), by analogy with \(\ce{SO2 + H2O <=> H2SO3}\). This is a prediction by analogy: the acid is expected to be weaker than sulfurous acid, because the elements become more metallic down a group.
:::

### Deducing an unknown element from data

Data questions give you facts and ask you to place and name an element. The most useful data are the **successive ionisation energies**: the big jump shows how many electrons are in the outer shell, and therefore the group. Use the chart above (the successive tab) to practise.

:::eg (Exam style) The first four ionisation energies of element Z, in kJ mol⁻¹, are 577, 1820, 2740 and 11 600. Z is in Period 3. The oxide of Z is insoluble in water but dissolves in both hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide. (a) State the group of Z. (b) Identify Z and write an equation for the reaction of its oxide with hydrochloric acid. [4 marks]
(a) The largest jump comes between the third and fourth ionisation energies (from 2740 to 11 600). The first three electrons are in the outer shell, so Z has 3 outer electrons and is in Group 13. [B1]

(b) Group 13 in Period 3 is aluminium. [B1]

The oxide is amphoteric, as aluminium oxide is, which agrees. [B1]
\[\ce{Al2O3(s) + 6HCl(aq) -> 2AlCl3(aq) + 3H2O(l)}\] [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) An element Y is a yellow solid that burns in air with a blue flame to give a colourless, choking gas that dissolves in water to form an acidic solution. Y has a low melting point and does not conduct. Identify Y and give the equation for its reaction with oxygen. [3 marks]
Low melting point and non-conductor: a simple molecular non-metal. [B1]

Burns with a blue flame to give \(\ce{SO2}\) (choking, acidic): Y is sulfur. [B1]
\[\ce{S(s) + O2(g) -> SO2(g)}\] [B1]
:::

## Three more calculations

The ideas above often combine with moles from [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles).

:::eg (Exam style) 2.70 g of aluminium is burned completely in oxygen. Calculate the mass of aluminium oxide formed, and the volume of oxygen used, measured at room temperature and pressure. [4 marks]
\(4\ce{Al} + 3\ce{O2} \rightarrow 2\ce{Al2O3}\). \(M_r(\ce{Al2O3}) = 2(27.0) + 3(16.0) = 102.0\). [B1]

\(n(\ce{Al}) = 2.70/27.0 = 0.100\) mol, so \(n(\ce{Al2O3}) = 0.0500\) mol. [M1]

Mass of \(\ce{Al2O3} = 0.0500 \times 102.0 = 5.10\) g [A1]

\(n(\ce{O2}) = \tfrac{3}{4} \times 0.100 = 0.0750\) mol, and \(V = 0.0750 \times 24.0 = 1.80\) dm³ [A1]
:::

:::eg 1.60 g of sulfur is burned in excess oxygen. Calculate the volume of sulfur dioxide formed at room temperature and pressure (molar gas volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹).
\(\ce{S + O2 -> SO2}\): 1 mol of S gives 1 mol of \(\ce{SO2}\).

\(n(\ce{S}) = 1.60/32.1 = 0.04984\) mol, so \(n(\ce{SO2}) = 0.04984\) mol.

\(V = 0.04984 \times 24.0 = 1.196\) dm³, which is 1.20 dm³ to 3 significant figures.
:::

:::eg 6.20 g of phosphorus burns completely to \(\ce{P4O10}\). Calculate the mass of \(\ce{P4O10}\), and the amount of \(\ce{H3PO4}\) that forms when it dissolves in water.
\(\ce{P4(s) + 5O2(g) -> P4O10(s)}\); the oxide contains all the phosphorus atoms of the sample.

\(n(\ce{P}) = 6.20/31.0 = 0.200\) mol, so \(n(\ce{P4O10}) = 0.200/4 = 0.0500\) mol.

\(M_r(\ce{P4O10}) = 4(31.0) + 10(16.0) = 284.0\), so the mass is \(0.0500 \times 284.0 = 14.2\) g.

\(\ce{P4O10 + 6H2O -> 4H3PO4}\), so \(n(\ce{H3PO4}) = 4 \times 0.0500 = 0.200\) mol: every phosphorus atom ends up in one molecule of phosphoric acid.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: Explain why the melting point of sulfur is much lower than that of silicon. [3 marks]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Silicon has a giant covalent structure, with strong covalent bonds throughout.
Line 2: Sulfur has weak covalent bonds, so they are easy to break when it melts.
Line 3: So less energy is needed to melt sulfur.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: the S–S bonds inside an S₈ molecule are strong and are not broken when sulfur melts. The mark for the structure of sulfur is lost [B0].** Line 3 repeats the mistake, because the weak forces are not named [B0].

Correct lines 2 and 3: sulfur is simple molecular, made of \(\ce{S8}\) molecules held by weak id-id forces [B1], and only a small amount of energy is needed to overcome these forces, far less than to break the covalent bonds in silicon [B1].

The student scores 1 out of 3. Whenever a simple molecular substance melts or boils, name the forces **between** the molecules.
:::

pH \(= -\log_{10}(0.200) = 0.70\) [A1]
(The real pH is slightly lower, because \(\ce{H3PO4}\) also ionises, so the value from the HCl alone is an approximation.)
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The chlorides \(\ce{MgCl2}\), \(\ce{AlCl3}\) and \(\ce{SiCl4}\) are each added to water. (a) State the likely pH of each resulting solution. (b) Write an equation for the reaction of \(\ce{SiCl4}\) with water. (c) Explain why \(\ce{SiCl4}\) reacts with water but \(\ce{MgCl2}\) does not.
H: One of the two is ionic and one is covalent: which is attacked by water?
A: (a) \(\ce{MgCl2}\) about 6.5 (about 7), \(\ce{AlCl3}\) about 3, \(\ce{SiCl4}\) about 1 to 2. [B1] [B1]
(b) \(\ce{SiCl4(l) + 2H2O(l) -> SiO2(s) + 4HCl(aq)}\) [B1]
(c) \(\ce{MgCl2}\) is ionic: it dissolves, as the ions are surrounded by water molecules, and no bonds are broken. [B1]
\(\ce{SiCl4}\) is covalent, with polar \(\ce{Si^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}}\) bonds: a lone pair on oxygen of water attacks the \(\delta+\) silicon, so the Si–Cl bonds break (hydrolysis). [B1]
Hydrogen chloride is formed, which is a strong acid. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ The oxides of the elements sodium to sulfur show a trend in acid–base behaviour. (a) Describe the trend. (b) Explain it using bonding and the electronegativity of the elements. (c) Give one equation to show the acidic nature of an oxide of your choice.
H: Think about the electronegativity difference between each element and oxygen, and what \(\ce{O^2-}\) does in water.
A: (a) Basic (Na₂O, MgO), then amphoteric (Al₂O₃), then acidic (SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, SO₃). [B1]
(b) Electronegativity rises across the period, so the difference from oxygen falls. [B1]
Na₂O and MgO are ionic and contain \(\ce{O^2-}\) ions, which accept \(\ce{H+}\), so the oxides are basic. [B1]
The oxides of silicon, phosphorus and sulfur are covalent. They react with water to form acids, or with bases to form salts, so they are acidic. [B1]
Aluminium oxide is between the two, with some covalent character, so it is amphoteric. [B1]
(c) For example, \(\ce{SO3(g) + 2NaOH(aq) -> Na2SO4(aq) + H2O(l)}\). [B1]
:::

Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Oxides P, Q and R are oxides of three different Period 3 elements. P is a white solid that reacts with water to give a strongly alkaline solution of pH about 14, and it dissolves in dilute hydrochloric acid. Q is a white solid that is insoluble in water but dissolves in both dilute hydrochloric acid and aqueous sodium hydroxide. R is a colourless gas with a choking smell that dissolves in water to give an acidic solution of pH about 2 to 3. (a) Identify P, Q and R. (b) Write equations for the reaction of P with water, of Q with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and of R with water.
H: Which Period 3 oxide is amphoteric?
H: Sulfur burns to give a gas. Which oxide of sulfur does it give first?
A: (a) P is \(\ce{Na2O}\) [B1], Q is \(\ce{Al2O3}\) [B1] and R is \(\ce{SO2}\). [B1]
(b) \(\ce{Na2O(s) + H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq)}\) [B1]
\(\ce{Al2O3(s) + 2NaOH(aq) + 3H2O(l) -> 2NaAl(OH)4(aq)}\) [B1]
\(\ce{SO2(g) + H2O(l) <=> H2SO3(aq)}\) [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ The first four ionisation energies of element W are 786, 1580, 3230 and 4360 kJ mol⁻¹. (a) Explain why these four values do not show by themselves which group W is in. (b) W is in Period 3 and its chloride is a colourless liquid that fumes in moist air. Identify W, and explain why W has a very high melting point but its chloride has a very low one.
H: Look for a big jump. Is there one? If not, where must it come later?
H: Is the chloride ionic or covalent? What forces does a covalent liquid have to overcome?
A: (a) There is no big jump among the four values (the largest ratio is only about 2.0). [B1]
The big jump comes after the outer shell has been emptied, so W has at least four outer electrons and the jump is after the fourth. [B1]
(b) W is silicon, which is in Group 14, with four outer electrons; its chloride \(\ce{SiCl4}\) is a covalent liquid that is hydrolysed by water, which fumes. [B1]
Silicon has a giant covalent structure, so many strong covalent bonds must be broken to melt it. [B1]
\(\ce{SiCl4}\) is simple molecular, so only weak id-id forces between the molecules must be overcome. [B1]

- \(\ce{Al^3+}\) is the same size as \(\ce{P^3-}\), because both have the same charge.
> \(\ce{Na+}\) has lost its third shell, so it has two shells. \(\ce{Cl-}\) has three. \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is smaller than \(\ce{Na+}\) because more protons pull the same 10 electrons.
? Why does silicon have a higher melting point than sulfur?
- The covalent bonds in silicon are stronger than those in sulfur molecules.
+ Silicon has a giant covalent structure, so many covalent bonds must be broken, while sulfur needs only weak forces between molecules to be overcome.
- Silicon has more delocalised electrons.
- Silicon has stronger id-id forces.
> Melting sulfur overcomes weak id-id forces between \(\ce{S8}\) molecules and no covalent bonds break. Melting silicon breaks covalent bonds throughout the lattice.

:::recap
- Across Period 3 the nuclear charge rises and the shielding stays about the same, so atomic radius falls and electronegativity and (with two dips) first ionisation energy rise.
- Ionic radius: positive ions (Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺) are smaller than their atoms; negative ions (P³⁻ > S²⁻ > Cl⁻) are larger than their atoms; a big jump up between them.
- Melting point: rises Na < Mg < Al (stronger metallic bonding), highest for giant covalent Si, then low for simple molecular S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ > Ar (id-id forces rise with electrons). Na, Mg and Al conduct; Si is a semiconductor; the rest do not conduct.
- Oxygen: 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O; 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO; 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃; P₄ + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀; S + O₂ → SO₂. Chlorine gives NaCl, MgCl₂, AlCl₃, SiCl₄, PCl₅. Water: Na vigorously (NaOH + H₂), Mg slowly (Mg(OH)₂) or in steam (MgO).
- Oxidation numbers equal the outer electrons used: +1, +2, +3, +4, +5, +4 (SO₂), +6 (SO₃); Cl is −1 in chlorides.
- Oxides in water: Na₂O pH 13 to 14, MgO 9 to 10, Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ insoluble (pH 7), P₄O₁₀ about 2, SO₂ about 2 to 3, SO₃ about 1. Basic: Na₂O, MgO. Amphoteric: Al₂O₃, Al(OH)₃ (dissolve in acids and in excess NaOH to give NaAl(OH)₄). Acidic: SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, SO₃.
- Chlorides in water: NaCl pH 7; MgCl₂ about 6.5; AlCl₃ about 3; SiCl₄ and PCl₅ about 1 to 2 (hydrolysed, give HCl).
- The bonding changes from ionic to covalent because the electronegativity difference falls; the properties (melting point, conductivity when molten, reaction with water) show which it is.
:::
