Q: Explain why the first ionisation energy decreases down a group. (from [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms))

## The Group 2 elements

Group 2 is the second column of the Periodic Table. It is also called the **alkaline earth metals**, because the oxides were once known as "earths" and their hydroxides are alkaline. The syllabus covers magnesium, calcium, strontium and barium. (Beryllium, at the top, behaves differently in several ways, and radium at the bottom is radioactive.)

| Element | Symbol | Outer electrons |
|---|---|---|
| Magnesium | Mg | [Ne] 3s² |
| Calcium | Ca | [Ar] 4s² |
| Strontium | Sr | [Kr] 5s² |
| Barium | Ba | [Xe] 6s² |

All of them have **two outer electrons** in an s sub-shell. They lose both of them in reactions to form the \(\ce{M^2+}\) ion, so every Group 2 element has oxidation number **+2** in its compounds, and the reactions are all oxidations of the metal.

### Why +2, and why reactivity increases down the group

The successive ionisation energies from the Data booklet show why the ions are 2+. The third ionisation energy is much bigger than the second: the third electron would have to come from the inner shell, which is much closer to the nucleus.

| Element | 1st IE | 2nd IE | 3rd IE | 1st + 2nd |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mg | 736 | 1450 | 7740 | 2186 |
| Ca | 590 | 1150 | 4940 | 1740 |
| Sr | 548 | 1060 | 4120 | 1608 |
| Ba | 502 | 966 | 3390 | 1468 |

(All values in kJ mol⁻¹.) The energy needed to turn a gaseous atom into \(\ce{M^2+}\) is the sum of the first two ionisation energies, and that **falls** down the group. Each step down adds a shell, so the atomic radius increases and the two outer electrons are further from the nucleus and better shielded. The electrons are lost more easily, so the elements become **more reactive** down the group. You can see the first ionisation energy, and the other trends, in the chart.

:::key Group 2 in one sentence
Down Group 2 the atomic radius increases and the ionisation energies decrease, so the metals lose their two outer electrons more easily and become more reactive.
:::

The ionic radius follows the atomic radius: \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is smaller than \(\ce{Ca^2+}\), then \(\ce{Sr^2+}\), then \(\ce{Ba^2+}\), since each ion down the group has one more full shell of electrons. The **charge** is the same for every one of these ions, 2+, so what changes down the group is the **size**, and with it the charge density (the charge spread over the volume of the ion). Almost every trend in this chapter is a consequence of that one change.

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why the ions of Group 2 elements have a charge of 2+, using the ionisation energies of magnesium: 736, 1450 and 7740 kJ mol⁻¹. [3 marks]
The first two ionisation energies are relatively small, so the two outer electrons are removed fairly easily. [B1]

The third ionisation energy (7740) is more than five times the second (1450). [B1]

The third electron would have to come from the shell inside the outer shell, which is much closer to the nucleus and not shielded as much. So only two electrons are lost, and the ion is \(\ce{Mg^2+}\). [B1]
:::

## Reactions of the elements

### With oxygen

Each metal burns in oxygen to form the white solid oxide. The metals get more reactive down the group, but all of them react.

\[\ce{2M(s) + O2(g) -> 2MO(s)}\]

For magnesium: \(\ce{2Mg(s) + O2(g) -> 2MgO(s)}\). Magnesium burns with a very bright white flame and leaves a white ash of magnesium oxide. The oxide is an ionic solid, \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\).

### With water

The reactions get more vigorous down the group. The product is the metal hydroxide and hydrogen.

| Metal | Reaction with water | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| Mg | very slow with cold water; fast with steam | a few bubbles on the ribbon; with steam it burns with a bright white flame and gives MgO |
| Ca | moderate with cold water | steady bubbles of gas, the metal dissolves, the solution turns cloudy and warms |
| Sr | fast with cold water | faster bubbling than calcium |
| Ba | vigorous with cold water | rapid bubbling, the metal dissolves quickly |

\[\ce{Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g)}\ \text{(cold water, very slow)}\]
\[\ce{Mg(s) + H2O(g) -> MgO(s) + H2(g)}\ \text{(steam)}\]
\[\ce{Ca(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}\]
\[\ce{Sr(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Sr(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}\]
\[\ce{Ba(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Ba(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}\]

The calcium solution goes cloudy because calcium hydroxide is only slightly soluble, so some of it forms a white solid. The solution of each hydroxide is alkaline, and it becomes more strongly alkaline down the group because the hydroxides become more soluble.

:::lab Comparing the reactivity of the metals with water
Fill a trough with water, add a few drops of universal indicator, and drop in a small piece of calcium (never more than a lentil-sized piece, and never touch it with wet hands). Note the fizzing, the temperature and the colour of the indicator. Repeat with clean magnesium ribbon, which you should find reacts far more slowly. Collect the hydrogen over water in an inverted test tube and test it with a lighted splint: it burns with a squeaky pop. Wear eye protection and keep the metals in the containers they were supplied in until they are needed. Strontium and barium are too reactive to use in a school laboratory this way.
:::

:::warn Magnesium and cold water
Magnesium does **not** react vigorously with cold water. The reaction is very slow because the oxide or hydroxide formed on the surface, which is only slightly soluble, protects the metal. A common wrong answer says "Mg reacts vigorously with water". Magnesium needs steam to react quickly, and then it makes the oxide.
:::

### With dilute acids

All four metals react with dilute hydrochloric and sulfuric acids to make a salt and hydrogen, again faster down the group. This is the reaction of a metal above hydrogen with \(\ce{H+}\): the metal is oxidised and \(\ce{H+}\) is reduced.

\[\ce{Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)}\]
\[\ce{Ba(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> BaCl2(aq) + H2(g)}\]
\[\ce{Mg(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> MgSO4(aq) + H2(g)}\]

Magnesium dissolves quickly with fizzing and gives a colourless solution. The ionic equation is \(\ce{M(s) + 2H+(aq) -> M^2+(aq) + H2(g)}\).

Sulfuric acid is different for the heavier metals. Calcium sulfate is only slightly soluble, and strontium sulfate and barium sulfate are almost insoluble. As they form they coat the surface of the metal and stop the acid reaching it, so the reaction slows or stops. (Magnesium sulfate is soluble, so magnesium keeps reacting.)

:::eg Write equations for (a) the reaction of strontium with cold water and (b) the reaction of barium with dilute hydrochloric acid. State what you would see in (b).
(a) \(\ce{Sr(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Sr(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}\)

(b) \(\ce{Ba(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> BaCl2(aq) + H2(g)}\)

You would see the metal fizz rapidly and dissolve, giving off a colourless gas, and a colourless solution. The reaction is more vigorous than for magnesium.
:::

## Oxides, hydroxides and carbonates

The compounds follow a pattern too. All the oxides, hydroxides and carbonates are basic and react with acids.

### Oxides and hydroxides with water

Group 2 oxides are ionic. The oxide ion takes \(\ce{H+}\) from water, forming hydroxide ions:

\[\ce{O^2-(s) + H2O(l) -> 2OH-(aq)}\]

\[\ce{MgO(s) + H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s)}\]
\[\ce{CaO(s) + H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(s)}\ \text{(very exothermic)}\]
\[\ce{BaO(s) + H2O(l) -> Ba(OH)2(aq)}\]

Magnesium oxide reacts only a little (it hardly dissolves), so its mixture is only weakly alkaline. Calcium oxide, "quicklime", reacts vigorously and gives out a lot of heat; the solid product, calcium hydroxide or "slaked lime", dissolves slightly to give limewater. Barium oxide reacts to give a strongly alkaline solution. The solutions are more alkaline down the group because the hydroxides get more soluble (see below).

### With dilute acids

An oxide, a hydroxide or a carbonate is a **base**, so it neutralises an acid to form a salt and water (and carbon dioxide too for a carbonate).

\[\ce{MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l)}\]
\[\ce{Ca(OH)2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l)}\]
\[\ce{CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\]
\[\ce{MgO(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> MgSO4(aq) + H2O(l)}\]

The carbon dioxide from the carbonate fizzes out and turns limewater milky: \(\ce{Ca(OH)2(aq) + CO2(g) -> CaCO3(s) + H2O(l)}\). With dilute sulfuric acid, the carbonates of calcium, strontium and barium react only briefly, because an insoluble layer of sulfate forms on the solid, as with the metals: \(\ce{BaCO3(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> BaSO4(s) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\). In practice, hydrochloric acid is used to dissolve the carbonate in a lab test.

:::eg (Exam style) Write equations for the reactions of (a) calcium oxide with dilute hydrochloric acid and (b) magnesium carbonate with dilute sulfuric acid, and state one observation for (b). [4 marks]
(a) \(\ce{CaO(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l)}\) [B1] [B1]

(b) \(\ce{MgCO3(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> MgSO4(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g)}\) [B1]

The solid dissolves and a colourless gas (\(\ce{CO2}\)) fizzes off. [B1]
:::

## Thermal decomposition

When you heat a Group 2 carbonate or nitrate strongly it breaks down to the oxide. The reactions are examples of **thermal decomposition**.

:::def Thermal decomposition
A reaction in which a compound breaks down into simpler substances when it is heated.
:::

**Carbonates** give the oxide and carbon dioxide:

\[\ce{MCO3(s) -> MO(s) + CO2(g)}\]

**Nitrates** give the oxide, brown nitrogen dioxide gas, and oxygen:

\[\ce{2M(NO3)2(s) -> 2MO(s) + 4NO2(g) + O2(g)}\]

You see the white solid, brown fumes of \(\ce{NO2}\) and relight a glowing splint in the oxygen. Check the equation: \(\ce{2M(NO3)2}\) has 4 N and 12 O atoms, and the right side has 2 O in the oxides, 4 N and 8 O in \(\ce{4NO2}\) and 2 O in \(\ce{O2}\), which is 12 O in all.

:::lab Comparing the thermal stability of the carbonates
Put equal amounts (in moles) of each carbonate in a hard-glass test tube and heat strongly with a Bunsen burner, with a delivery tube leading to limewater. Time how long the limewater takes to turn cloudy. The shorter the time, the more easily the carbonate decomposes. Keep the amount of solid, the flame and the tube position the same. Take the delivery tube out of the limewater before you stop heating, or it will suck back and crack the tube. Wear eye protection.
:::

### The trend

:::key Thermal stability of Group 2 carbonates and nitrates
Thermal stability **increases** down the group. \(\ce{MgCO3}\) decomposes at a lower temperature than \(\ce{CaCO3}\), then \(\ce{SrCO3}\), then \(\ce{BaCO3}\), which needs the highest temperature. The nitrates follow the same trend.
:::

:::warn Stability goes up, not down
"Stability" here means resistance to heating. The compounds lower in the group are *more* stable, so they need *more* heat to decompose. Writing that thermal stability "decreases down the group" is a common slip, and it is the opposite of the facts. Check your sentence: barium carbonate needs a hotter flame than magnesium carbonate.
:::

Calcium carbonate is important in industry: limestone is heated in a kiln, \(\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}\), to make quicklime, which is used in cement and steel-making and to neutralise acidic soils.

:::eg (Real context) 20.0 g of calcium carbonate is heated in a lime kiln until it has all decomposed. Calculate the mass of calcium oxide made and the volume of carbon dioxide formed at room temperature and pressure.
\(M_r(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40.1 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 100.1\) and \(M_r(\ce{CaO}) = 40.1 + 16.0 = 56.1\).

\(n(\ce{CaCO3}) = 20.0/100.1 = 0.1998\) mol. The ratio \(\ce{CaCO3} : \ce{CaO} : \ce{CO2}\) is 1 : 1 : 1.

Mass of CaO \(= 0.1998 \times 56.1 = 11.2\) g.

Volume of \(\ce{CO2} = 0.1998 \times 24.0 = 4.80\) dm³.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 2.50 g of the carbonate of a Group 2 metal, M, is heated strongly until it has completely decomposed. The solid residue has a mass of 1.40 g. Calculate the relative atomic mass of M and identify the metal. [4 marks]
\(\ce{MCO3 -> MO + CO2}\). The mass lost is the mass of carbon dioxide: \(2.50 - 1.40 = 1.10\) g. [B1]

\(M_r(\ce{CO2}) = 12.0 + 2(16.0) = 44.0\), so \(n(\ce{CO2}) = 1.10/44.0 = 0.0250\) mol. [M1]

\(n(\ce{MCO3}) = 0.0250\) mol, so \(M_r(\ce{MCO3}) = 2.50/0.0250 = 100\). [M1]

\(A_r(\text{M}) = 100 - (12.0 + 3 \times 16.0) = 40\), so M is calcium (\(A_r\) 40.1). [A1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 5.23 g of barium nitrate, \(\ce{Ba(NO3)2}\), is heated until it has completely decomposed. Calculate the total volume of gas produced, measured at room temperature and pressure. [4 marks]
\(2\ce{Ba(NO3)2} \rightarrow 2\ce{BaO} + 4\ce{NO2} + \ce{O2}\), so 2 mol of the nitrate gives 5 mol of gas, or 2.5 mol of gas per mol of nitrate. [B1]

\(M_r(\ce{Ba(NO3)2}) = 137.3 + 2(14.0 + 3 \times 16.0) = 261.3\), so \(n = 5.23/261.3 = 0.02002\) mol. [B1]

\(n(\text{gas}) = 2.5 \times 0.02002 = 0.05004\) mol [M1]

\(V = 0.05004 \times 24.0 = 1.20\) dm³ [A1]
:::

## Solubility of the hydroxides and sulfates

Two solubility trends are in the syllabus, and they go in opposite directions.

:::key Solubility trends
- The hydroxides **increase** in solubility down the group: \(\ce{Mg(OH)2}\) is almost insoluble and \(\ce{Ba(OH)2}\) is fairly soluble.
- The sulfates **decrease** in solubility down the group: \(\ce{MgSO4}\) is soluble and \(\ce{BaSO4}\) is insoluble.
:::

| Compound | Mg | Ca | Sr | Ba |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydroxide, \(\ce{M(OH)2}\) | almost insoluble | slightly soluble | more soluble | fairly soluble |
| Sulfate, \(\ce{MSO4}\) | soluble | slightly soluble | almost insoluble | insoluble |

:::tip A way to remember the two trends
Think of barium, at the bottom: barium hydroxide is the most soluble hydroxide (its solution is the most alkaline), and barium sulfate is the least soluble sulfate (the white precipitate in the sulfate test).
:::

The carbonates are all insoluble in water.

The hydroxide trend explains why the solutions of the oxides become more alkaline down the group, and it shows in the tests for cations in the qualitative analysis notes: with aqueous sodium hydroxide, \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) gives a white precipitate that is insoluble in excess (magnesium hydroxide is the least soluble), \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) gives a white precipitate unless its concentration is very low, and \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) gives only a faint white precipitate (none if its concentration is very low) (see [Practical skills and qualitative analysis](#chem.practical)).

### Uses

- **Magnesium hydroxide** is the active ingredient of "milk of magnesia", an antacid. It neutralises stomach acid, \(\ce{Mg(OH)2 + 2HCl -> MgCl2 + 2H2O}\). Only a little dissolves, so the solution is never strongly alkaline.
- **Calcium hydroxide** (slaked lime) is spread on fields to neutralise acidic soil.
- **Barium sulfate** is insoluble, so it passes through the body unabsorbed. It is opaque to X-rays, so a patient drinks a "barium meal" and the gut shows up in the X-ray image. Soluble barium compounds are poisonous, which is why it is the *insolubility* that makes barium sulfate safe.
- **Test for sulfate ions.** Add dilute hydrochloric (or nitric) acid and then aqueous barium chloride (or nitrate). A white precipitate of barium sulfate shows sulfate ions: \(\ce{Ba^2+(aq) + SO4^2-(aq) -> BaSO4(s)}\). The acid is added first to remove carbonate ions and sulfite ions, which would also give a white precipitate with barium ions, but one that dissolves in the acid.

:::eg A student shakes calcium hydroxide with water, filters it, and titrates 25.0 cm³ of the clear saturated solution against hydrochloric acid of concentration 0.0500 mol dm⁻³. The mean titre is 19.5 cm³. Calculate the solubility of calcium hydroxide in mol dm⁻³ and in g dm⁻³.
\(\ce{Ca(OH)2 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + 2H2O}\): 1 mol of \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\) reacts with 2 mol of HCl.

\(n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.0500 \times 19.5/1000 = 9.75 \times 10^{-4}\) mol.

\(n(\ce{Ca(OH)2}) = 9.75 \times 10^{-4}/2 = 4.875 \times 10^{-4}\) mol in 25.0 cm³.

Solubility \(= 4.875 \times 10^{-4}/0.0250 = 0.0195\) mol dm⁻³.

\(M_r(\ce{Ca(OH)2}) = 40.1 + 2(16.0 + 1.0) = 74.1\), so the solubility is \(0.0195 \times 74.1 = 1.44\) g dm⁻³.
:::

:::eg 25.0 cm³ of a solution containing sulfate ions is treated with excess barium chloride solution. The precipitate of barium sulfate, after washing and drying, has a mass of 0.467 g. Calculate the concentration of sulfate ions in the original solution.
\(M_r(\ce{BaSO4}) = 137.3 + 32.1 + 4(16.0) = 233.4\)

\(n(\ce{BaSO4}) = 0.467/233.4 = 2.001 \times 10^{-3}\) mol. Each mole of sulfate ions gives 1 mol of \(\ce{BaSO4}\), so \(n(\ce{SO4^2-}) = 2.001 \times 10^{-3}\) mol.

\[c = \frac{2.001 \times 10^{-3}}{0.0250} = 0.0800\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}\]
:::

## Predicting properties

Because the trends are regular, you can predict the properties of an element or compound that you have not studied. Radium is the next element below barium, so it should continue every trend.

:::eg Predict, with reasons, the thermal stability of radium carbonate compared with barium carbonate, the reaction of radium with water, and the solubility of radium sulfate.
Radium is below barium in Group 2, so its trends continue.

\(\ce{RaCO3}\) should be **more** thermally stable than \(\ce{BaCO3}\): it should decompose at a higher temperature, since the stability increases down the group.

Radium should react with cold water even more vigorously than barium, giving \(\ce{Ra(OH)2}\) and hydrogen, because it has the lowest ionisation energies and the biggest atoms: \(\ce{Ra(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Ra(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}\).

\(\ce{RaSO4}\) should be even less soluble than \(\ce{BaSO4}\), because sulfate solubility decreases down the group. Radium hydroxide should be more soluble than barium hydroxide.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Metals X, Y and Z are Mg, Ca and Ba in some order. X reacts with cold water at a moderate rate, giving a cloudy solution. Y reacts violently with cold water. Z reacts hardly at all with cold water but burns in steam. Identify X, Y and Z, and predict what happens when each is added to dilute sulfuric acid. [4 marks]
Reactivity with water increases down the group: Mg < Ca < Ba. [B1]

Z is magnesium, X is calcium (moderate, and the cloudiness is sparingly soluble \(\ce{Ca(OH)2}\)) and Y is barium. [B1]

With dilute sulfuric acid, magnesium (Z) reacts steadily to give a solution of soluble \(\ce{MgSO4}\) and hydrogen. [B1]

Calcium (X) and barium (Y) start to react, but the reaction slows or stops because insoluble or sparingly soluble \(\ce{CaSO4}\) and \(\ce{BaSO4}\) coat the metal. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) The elements Mg, Ca and Ba react with water at different rates. Use ionisation energies to explain the order of reactivity, and state what the data show about the ions formed. [4 marks]
Reactivity increases from Mg to Ca to Ba. [B1]

The sum of the first and second ionisation energies falls from Mg (2186 kJ mol⁻¹) to Ca (1740) to Ba (1468), so the two outer electrons are lost more easily. [B1]

The atoms get larger and the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded. [B1]

The third ionisation energy is much larger than the second (Mg: 7740 against 1450), so only two electrons are lost and the ions are \(\ce{M^2+}\). [B1]
:::

## Explaining the trends (A Level)

The rest of the chapter is A Level, Paper 4. It explains why the thermal stability goes up and the solubility goes in two directions.

### Polarisation and thermal stability

When a cation sits next to an anion, its positive charge attracts the anion's electrons. The electron cloud of the anion is pulled towards the cation, and this distortion is called **polarisation**.

:::def Polarisation of an ion
The distortion of the electron cloud of an anion by a nearby cation. A cation with a high **charge density** (a small ion with a high charge) has high polarising power. A large anion, whose electrons are far from its nuclei and loosely held, is easily polarised.
:::

All the Group 2 cations have the same charge, 2+. But the radius increases down the group, so the **charge density decreases** (the same charge is spread over a bigger ion). \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) has the highest charge density and \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) the lowest.

:::html

The carbonate ion, \(\ce{CO3^2-}\), is a large anion with a diffuse electron cloud. A small cation distorts the cloud strongly, drawing electron density from the carbonate towards itself. That **weakens a C–O bond** inside the carbonate ion, so the ion breaks apart into \(\ce{O^2-}\), which joins the cation as the oxide, and \(\ce{CO2}\), which escapes as a gas. The less the cation distorts the ion, the more heat is needed to break it up.

The same argument works for the nitrate ion, \(\ce{NO3-}\): a small cation with high charge density distorts the N–O bonds and the nitrate decomposes more easily.

:::analogy
Think of the cation as a magnet and the anion's electron cloud as a loose, soft balloon. A strong magnet close to the balloon pulls it out of shape, and a weak magnet hardly changes it. Mg²⁺ is the strong magnet and Ba²⁺ the weak one.
:::

:::key Why thermal stability increases down Group 2
Down the group the cation radius increases while the charge stays at 2+, so the charge density and the polarising power fall. The carbonate (or nitrate) ion is distorted less, its C–O (or N–O) bonds are weakened less, and a higher temperature is needed to decompose it. So \(\ce{MgCO3}\) decomposes at a lower temperature than \(\ce{BaCO3}\).
:::

(Group 1 carbonates are more stable than Group 2 carbonates because the Group 1 cations have a smaller charge and a larger radius, so a much lower charge density.) Decomposition also becomes more favourable as the temperature rises, because it makes a gas: the entropy increases (see [Lattice energy, entropy and Gibbs free energy](#chem.lattice)).

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why magnesium carbonate decomposes at a lower temperature than barium carbonate. [4 marks]
\(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) have the same charge, but \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) has a smaller ionic radius, so a higher charge density. [B1]

So \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) has a stronger polarising effect on the carbonate ion, distorting its electron cloud more. [B1]

This weakens a C–O bond in the carbonate ion more in magnesium carbonate. [B1]

So less heat energy is needed to break it down into MgO and \(\ce{CO2}\), and it decomposes at a lower temperature than \(\ce{BaCO3}\). [B1]
:::

### Solubility, ΔHsol, ΔHhyd and lattice energy

Whether a compound dissolves depends on an energy balance, summed up in a cycle. The enthalpy change of solution, ΔHsol, is the enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic solid dissolves in enough water to give a very dilute solution. It can be split into two steps: breaking up the lattice into gaseous ions, and hydrating the ions.

:::def Enthalpy change of hydration, ΔHhyd
The enthalpy change when 1 mol of a gaseous ion dissolves in enough water to give a very dilute solution. It is exothermic, because polar water molecules are attracted to the ion.
:::

\[\Delta H_{\mathrm{sol}} = -\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{hyd}}\]

Here \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}}\) is negative (it is the energy released when gaseous ions form the lattice), so \(-\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}}\) is positive, and \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{hyd}}\) is the sum for all the ions in the formula and is negative. The lattice energy gets less negative, and the hydration enthalpy less negative, when the ions are bigger.

- The **lattice energy** depends on the ion charges and the distance between the ion centres, which is the sum of the two ionic radii, \(r_+ + r_-\).
- The **hydration enthalpy** of an ion depends on its charge density: a small, highly charged ion is hydrated more strongly.

Down Group 2 the cations get larger, so both the lattice energy and the hydration enthalpy of the cation become **less exothermic**. What decides the change in solubility is which of the two falls faster.

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why the enthalpy change of hydration of \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is more exothermic than that of \(\ce{Ca^2+}\). [3 marks]
Both ions have a charge of 2+, but \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is smaller, so it has the higher charge density. [B1]

Water molecules, which are polar, are attracted more strongly to the ion with the higher charge density. [B1]

More energy is released when \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is hydrated, so its hydration enthalpy is more exothermic (more negative). [B1]
:::

**Hydroxides (the anion \(\ce{OH-}\) is small).** The cation is a large part of the distance \(r_+ + r_-\), so increasing the cation radius reduces the lattice energy a lot. This fall in the energy needed to break up the lattice is larger than the fall in the hydration enthalpy of the cation. So \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{sol}}\) becomes less endothermic (more exothermic) and the solubility **increases** down the group.

**Sulfates (the anion \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) is large).** The anion is already much bigger than any of the cations, so \(r_+ + r_-\) hardly changes down the group, and the lattice energy changes only a little. But the hydration enthalpy of the cation still falls a lot as the cation gets larger. So \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{sol}}\) becomes more endothermic (less exothermic) and the solubility **decreases** down the group.

:::key Explaining the solubility trends
Hydroxides: the lattice energy falls more than the hydration enthalpy down the group, so ΔHsol becomes less endothermic and the solubility rises. Sulfates: the lattice energy changes little (the large anion dominates the ion distance) but the cation's hydration enthalpy falls a lot, so ΔHsol becomes more endothermic and the solubility falls.
:::

The enthalpy change is only part of the picture, because entropy also affects whether a solid dissolves (see [Lattice energy, entropy and Gibbs free energy](#chem.lattice)), but this enthalpy argument is the one the syllabus asks for.

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why barium sulfate is much less soluble in water than magnesium sulfate. [4 marks]
Dissolving involves breaking up the lattice (which takes energy) and hydrating the ions (which releases energy), so \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{sol}} = -\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{hyd}}\). [B1]

The sulfate ion is large, so changing the cation from \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) to \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) hardly changes the distance between the ions, and the lattice energy changes very little. [B1]

The \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) ion is larger than \(\ce{Mg^2+}\), so it has a lower charge density and its hydration enthalpy is much less exothermic. [B1]

So \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{sol}}\) is much more endothermic (less exothermic) for barium sulfate, and it is less soluble. [B1]
:::

:::exam Writing an A Level explanation
Mark schemes for these questions award marks for named ideas, in order: (1) the cation has the **same charge** but a **larger radius** down the group; (2) so a **lower charge density** and weaker **polarising power**; (3) the anion's **electron cloud is distorted less** and the **C–O (or N–O) bond is weakened less**; (4) so **more energy is needed** to decompose it. For solubility, write the cycle first, then say which of −ΔHlatt and ΔHhyd changes more, for this anion. "The bond gets weaker" or "the ions get bigger" alone scores nothing.
:::

:::eg (A Level) For a salt MX, the lattice energy \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}}\) is −2480 kJ mol⁻¹ and the hydration enthalpies of the ions are −1650 kJ mol⁻¹ for \(\ce{M^2+}\) and −950 kJ mol⁻¹ for \(\ce{X^2-}\) (data for this question). Calculate ΔHsol, and say whether the dissolving is exothermic or endothermic.
\(\Delta H_{\mathrm{sol}} = -\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{hyd}}(\ce{M^2+}) + \Delta H_{\mathrm{hyd}}(\ce{X^2-})\)

\(= +2480 + (-1650) + (-950) = -120\) kJ mol⁻¹.

The value is negative, so the dissolving is exothermic.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: Explain why magnesium carbonate decomposes at a lower temperature than barium carbonate. [3 marks]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: The magnesium ion is smaller than the barium ion, so it has a higher charge density.
Line 2: This means it repels the carbonate ion strongly.
Line 3: So the C–O bonds are weakened and the carbonate decomposes at a lower temperature.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: a cation and an anion attract each other. The cation does not repel the carbonate, it polarises (distorts) the electron cloud of the carbonate ion. The mark for polarisation is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct line 2: the \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) ion polarises the carbonate ion, distorting its electron cloud strongly. [B1]

The student scores 2 out of 3. "Polarise" or "distort the electron cloud" is the word the mark scheme wants.
:::

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: Which Group 2 metal reacts with cold water only very slowly, and what is produced with steam?
A: Magnesium. With steam it gives magnesium oxide and hydrogen: \(\ce{Mg(s) + H2O(g) -> MgO(s) + H2(g)}\).
Q: Write the equation for the reaction of calcium with cold water, and state what you would see.
A: \(\ce{Ca(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Ca(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}\). Steady bubbles of gas form, the calcium dissolves, and the solution turns cloudy and warms.
Q: Write equations for the reaction of magnesium oxide with (a) water and (b) dilute hydrochloric acid.
A: (a) \(\ce{MgO(s) + H2O(l) -> Mg(OH)2(s)}\) (b) \(\ce{MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l)}\).
Q: Write the equation for the thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate and of magnesium nitrate.
A: \(\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}\). \(\ce{2Mg(NO3)2(s) -> 2MgO(s) + 4NO2(g) + O2(g)}\).
Q: What is observed when magnesium nitrate is heated?
A: A white solid is left, brown fumes of nitrogen dioxide form, and the oxygen relights a glowing splint.
Q: State the trend in solubility of the hydroxides and of the sulfates down Group 2.
A: Hydroxides: increases. Sulfates: decreases.
Q: Describe how you would test for sulfate ions in a solution.
A: Add dilute hydrochloric (or nitric) acid, then aqueous barium chloride (or nitrate). A white precipitate of \(\ce{BaSO4}\) shows sulfate ions.
Q: 0.243 g of magnesium reacts with excess dilute hydrochloric acid. Calculate the volume of hydrogen formed at room temperature and pressure.
A: \(n(\ce{Mg}) = 0.243/24.3 = 0.0100\) mol, which gives 0.0100 mol of \(\ce{H2}\). \(V = 0.0100 \times 24.0 = 0.240\) dm³ (240 cm³).

Q: [3 marks] ★ Write an equation for the reaction of barium with water, and state how the reactivity of barium compares with that of calcium.
A: \(\ce{Ba(s) + 2H2O(l) -> Ba(OH)2(aq) + H2(g)}\) [B1] [B1]
Barium is more reactive than calcium. [B1]
Q: [2 marks] ★ State the trend in the solubility of the Group 2 hydroxides down the group, and give one use of magnesium hydroxide.
A: The solubility increases down the group. [B1]
It is used as an antacid (indigestion remedy). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Compare what happens when dilute sulfuric acid is added to magnesium and to barium, and explain the difference.
H: Think about the solubility of the two sulfates.
A: Magnesium reacts and dissolves to give hydrogen and a colourless solution of \(\ce{MgSO4}\). [B1]
Barium reacts only briefly. [B1]
Barium sulfate is insoluble. [B1]
It forms a coating on the metal and stops the acid reaching it, so the reaction stops. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ 4.23 g of strontium nitrate, \(\ce{Sr(NO3)2}\), is heated until it has completely decomposed. Calculate the mass of solid left. \(A_r\): Sr 87.6.
H: The solid left is the oxide, SrO.
A: \(2\ce{Sr(NO3)2} \rightarrow 2\ce{SrO} + 4\ce{NO2} + \ce{O2}\) [B1]
\(M_r(\ce{Sr(NO3)2}) = 87.6 + 2(14.0 + 48.0) = 211.6\), so \(n = 4.23/211.6 = 0.01999\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{SrO}) = 0.01999\) mol, \(M_r(\ce{SrO}) = 87.6 + 16.0 = 103.6\) [B1]
Mass \(= 0.01999 \times 103.6 = 2.07\) g [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) 25.0 cm³ of a saturated solution of strontium hydroxide is titrated with hydrochloric acid of concentration 0.0500 mol dm⁻³. The mean titre is 33.0 cm³. Calculate the solubility of strontium hydroxide in g dm⁻³. \(A_r\): Sr 87.6.
H: Write the equation first: \(\ce{Sr(OH)2 + 2HCl -> SrCl2 + 2H2O}\).
A: \(n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.0500 \times 0.0330 = 1.65 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{Sr(OH)2}) = 8.25 \times 10^{-4}\) mol, so \(c = 8.25 \times 10^{-4}/0.0250 = 0.0330\) mol dm⁻³ [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{Sr(OH)2}) = 87.6 + 2(17.0) = 121.6\) [B1]
Solubility \(= 0.0330 \times 121.6 = 4.01\) g dm⁻³ [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ 3.95 g of the carbonate of a Group 2 metal, M, is heated until it has completely decomposed and 3.07 g of the oxide is left. Calculate the relative atomic mass of M, and identify M.
H: The mass lost is carbon dioxide. Find its moles, then the \(M_r\) of the carbonate.
A: Mass of \(\ce{CO2} = 3.95 - 3.07 = 0.88\) g [B1]
\(n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.88/44.0 = 0.0200\) mol, so \(n(\ce{MCO3}) = 0.0200\) mol [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{MCO3}) = 3.95/0.0200 = 197.5\) [M1]
\(A_r(\text{M}) = 197.5 - 60.0 = 137.5\), so M is barium (137.3) [A1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ Barium ions are poisonous, yet patients drink a suspension of barium sulfate before an X-ray. Explain why this is safe and why barium sulfate is used.
A: Barium sulfate is insoluble in water (and in stomach acid), so almost no \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) ions are released. [B1]
It passes through the body without being absorbed. [B1]
It absorbs X-rays, so the gut shows up in the image. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (A Level) Describe and explain the trend in the thermal stability of the Group 2 carbonates.
H: Which property of the cation changes down the group, and which does not?
H: What does the cation do to the electron cloud of the carbonate ion?
A: Thermal stability increases down the group (a higher temperature is needed to decompose the carbonate). [B1]
The cations all have a 2+ charge but the ionic radius increases down the group. [B1]
So the charge density of the cation decreases and its polarising power falls. [B1]
The electron cloud of the carbonate ion is distorted less by the larger cation. [B1]
So the C–O bonds in the carbonate ion are weakened less. [B1]
So more heat energy is needed to decompose the carbonate into the oxide and \(\ce{CO2}\). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (A Level) Explain why the solubility of the Group 2 hydroxides increases down the group but that of the sulfates decreases.
H: Use ΔHsol = −ΔHlatt + ΔHhyd. Both terms get smaller down the group: which changes more in each case?
A: Both the lattice energy and the hydration enthalpy of the cation become less exothermic down the group, since the cations get larger. [B1]
Hydroxides: the \(\ce{OH-}\) ion is small, so the lattice energy falls substantially as the cation gets bigger. [B1]
It falls by more than the hydration enthalpy, so ΔHsol becomes less endothermic (more negative) and the solubility increases. [B1]
Sulfates: the \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) ion is large and dominates the distance between the ions, so the lattice energy changes very little. [B1]
But the hydration enthalpy of the cation becomes much less exothermic. [B1]
So ΔHsol becomes more endothermic (less negative) and the solubility decreases. [B1]

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ (A Level) Lithium is the first member of Group 1. Lithium carbonate decomposes on heating but sodium and potassium carbonates do not, at Bunsen burner temperatures. Use your knowledge of Group 2 to explain this, and predict whether lithium nitrate or sodium nitrate would be more stable to heat.
H: Compare the charge and radius of \(\ce{Li+}\) with \(\ce{Na+}\), then use charge density.
A: \(\ce{Li+}\) is the smallest Group 1 ion, so it has the highest charge density in Group 1. [B1]
So it has the strongest polarising power and distorts the carbonate ion the most. [B1]
This weakens the C–O bonds so that \(\ce{Li2CO3}\) decomposes into \(\ce{Li2O}\) and \(\ce{CO2}\). [B1]
\(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{K+}\) are larger, with lower charge densities, so they polarise the carbonate less and their carbonates do not decompose on heating with a Bunsen burner. [B1]
The same reasoning applies to the nitrates, so lithium nitrate is less stable to heat than sodium nitrate. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Describe an experiment to compare the thermal stability of calcium carbonate and barium carbonate, and say which result you expect.
H: What must you keep the same, and what do you measure?
A: Heat equal amounts (in moles, so different masses) of each carbonate in the same hard-glass tube with the same Bunsen flame. [B1]
Pass the gas into limewater (or use a gas syringe) and time how long it takes for the limewater to turn cloudy. [B1]
Keep the flame, distance, size of the delivery tube and the volume of limewater the same. [B1]
Calcium carbonate decomposes faster (a shorter time), because barium carbonate is more thermally stable. [B1]

:::recap
- Group 2 metals have two outer electrons and form \(\ce{M^2+}\) ions (oxidation number +2). The sum of the first two ionisation energies falls down the group, so reactivity increases down the group.
- With oxygen: \(\ce{2M + O2 -> 2MO}\). With water: Mg very slowly (fast with steam, giving MgO); Ca, Sr and Ba give \(\ce{M(OH)2}\) and \(\ce{H2}\), faster down the group. With dilute acids they give a salt and hydrogen; the sulfate coating slows Ca, Sr and Ba with \(\ce{H2SO4}\).
- Oxides, hydroxides and carbonates are bases: they react with acids to give a salt and water (and \(\ce{CO2}\) for carbonates). The oxides and hydroxides are more alkaline down the group.
- Thermal decomposition: \(\ce{MCO3 -> MO + CO2}\); \(\ce{2M(NO3)2 -> 2MO + 4NO2 + O2}\). Thermal stability **increases** down the group.
- Solubility: the hydroxides increase down the group, the sulfates decrease. Uses: Mg(OH)₂ antacid; Ca(OH)₂ to treat acidic soil; BaSO₄ barium meal and the test for sulfate (acid, then Ba²⁺: white precipitate).
- (A Level) Down the group the cation is larger, the charge density and polarising power lower, so the anion is polarised less, the C–O or N–O bonds weaken less, and decomposition needs more heat.
- (A Level) ΔHsol = −ΔHlatt + ΔHhyd. Hydroxides (small anion): lattice energy falls more than the cation's ΔHhyd, so solubility rises. Sulfates (large anion): lattice energy changes little but the cation's ΔHhyd falls a lot, so solubility falls.
:::
