:::why
A halogenoalkane is the most useful "switch" in organic chemistry. The carbon–halogen bond is easy to break, so the halogen can be swapped for an OH group, a CN group or an NH₂ group, or removed to make a double bond. Chemists making medicines, dyes and plastics use halogenoalkanes as stepping stones for exactly this reason, and so will you in every synthesis question from now on.
:::

## What is a halogenoalkane?

Take an alkane such as butane, \(\ce{C4H10}\), and replace one hydrogen atom with a bromine atom. You get \(\ce{C4H9Br}\), a **halogenoalkane**. The halogen atom (F, Cl, Br or I, written X in general) is the functional group: it decides how the molecule reacts.

:::def Halogenoalkane
A compound in which one or more hydrogen atoms of an alkane have been replaced by halogen atoms. With one halogen atom the general formula is \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}X}\).
:::

To name one, add the prefix fluoro-, chloro-, bromo- or iodo- to the alkane name, with a number for the carbon that carries the halogen: \(\ce{CH3CHClCH3}\) is 2-chloropropane. Branches are named in the same way and listed in alphabetical order, so \(\ce{(CH3)3CBr}\) is 2-bromo-2-methylpropane.

### Primary, secondary and tertiary

How a halogenoalkane reacts depends on how crowded the carbon holding the halogen is. Count the alkyl groups (carbon groups) joined to that carbon.

:::def Primary, secondary and tertiary halogenoalkanes
In a primary (1°) halogenoalkane the carbon bonded to the halogen is joined to one other carbon atom (or none); in a secondary (2°) to two other carbon atoms; in a tertiary (3°) to three other carbon atoms.
:::

The three isomers of \(\ce{C4H9Br}\) below show one of each type. In skeletal formulas each corner or line end is a carbon atom, with enough hydrogen atoms to make four bonds.

:::warn Branched does not mean tertiary
1-bromo-2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2Br}\), is branched, but it is a **primary** halogenoalkane: the carbon holding the bromine is joined to only one other carbon. Always look at the carbon bonded to the halogen, not at the shape of the whole chain.
:::

### Why the C–X bond reacts

Halogens are more electronegative than carbon, so the C–X bond is polar: the carbon atom carries a partial positive charge, \(\ce{C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}\). A species with a lone pair of electrons is attracted to that δ+ carbon and can form a new bond to it. Such a species is called a **nucleophile** ("nucleus-loving").

:::def Nucleophile
A species that donates a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond: an electron-pair donor. Examples are \(\ce{OH-}\), \(\ce{CN-}\), \(\ce{NH3}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\).
:::

When the nucleophile bonds to the carbon, the halogen leaves as a halide ion, \(\ce{X-}\), taking both electrons of the C–X bond with it. One group has replaced another, so this is a **nucleophilic substitution**. The bond breaks by **heterolytic fission**: one atom keeps both electrons.

## Making halogenoalkanes

You have already met two routes, and a third starts from alcohols. Exam questions ask for the reagent and the conditions, so learn both.

| Starting compound | Reagent and conditions | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|
| alkane | \(\ce{Cl2}\) or \(\ce{Br2}\), ultraviolet light | free-radical substitution |
| alkene | \(\ce{HX(g)}\) or \(\ce{X2}\), room temperature | electrophilic addition |
| alcohol | \(\ce{HX(g)}\); or KCl with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) or \(\ce{H3PO4}\) | substitution |
| alcohol | \(\ce{PCl3}\) and heat; or \(\ce{PCl5}\); or \(\ce{SOCl2}\) | substitution |

The equations for making chloroethane from ethanol show what happens to the other atoms:

\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + PCl5 -> CH3CH2Cl + POCl3 + HCl}\]
\[\ce{3CH3CH2OH + PCl3 ->[\text{heat}] 3CH3CH2Cl + H3PO3}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + SOCl2 -> CH3CH2Cl + SO2 + HCl}\]

With KCl and concentrated sulfuric acid, the acid first makes hydrogen chloride, \(\ce{KCl + H2SO4 -> KHSO4 + HCl}\), and the HCl then reacts with the alcohol.

:::tip Why chemists like SOCl₂
Both by-products of the \(\ce{SOCl2}\) reaction, \(\ce{SO2}\) and \(\ce{HCl}\), are gases. They bubble out of the mixture, leaving the halogenoalkane easier to purify.
:::

Free-radical substitution is a poor way to make one particular halogenoalkane. Further substitution gives a mixture (\(\ce{CH3CH2Cl}\), \(\ce{CH3CHCl2}\), \(\ce{CH2ClCH2Cl}\) and more) that must be separated. Addition to an alkene, or substitution of an alcohol, gives one main product.

:::eg Give the reagents and conditions to make 2-bromopropane (a) from propene and (b) from propan-2-ol.
(a) Propene reacts with hydrogen bromide gas, \(\ce{HBr(g)}\), at room temperature by electrophilic addition: \(\ce{CH3CH=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CHBrCH3}\). The bromine goes mainly onto the middle carbon (Markovnikov addition), because the secondary carbocation formed on the way is more stable than the primary one.

(b) Propan-2-ol reacts with \(\ce{HBr(g)}\) (substitution of the OH group):
\[\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + HBr -> CH3CHBrCH3 + H2O}\]
:::

## Four nucleophilic substitutions to learn

Every one of these reactions has the same pattern: \(\ce{R-X + Nu^- -> R-Nu + X-}\), where Nu is the nucleophile. Only the nucleophile and the conditions change.

:::key Nucleophilic substitution reactions of halogenoalkanes
| Nucleophile | Reagent and conditions | Organic product |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{OH-}\) | NaOH(aq), heat (under reflux) | alcohol |
| \(\ce{CN-}\) | KCN in ethanol, heat (under reflux) | nitrile (one more carbon atom) |
| \(\ce{NH3}\) | excess \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure | primary amine |
| \(\ce{H2O}\) | aqueous \(\ce{AgNO3}\) in ethanol, warm | alcohol (the halide ion is precipitated as AgX) |
:::

With bromoethane as the example:

\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH- -> CH3CH2OH + Br-}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + CN- -> CH3CH2CN + Br-}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br}\]

The first reaction is also called **hydrolysis**, because the C–Br bond is broken by water or hydroxide ions. The product of the second, \(\ce{CH3CH2CN}\), is propanenitrile: the chain has grown from two carbons to three, which is why the cyanide reaction matters so much in synthesis. Ammonia is used in excess so that most of the bromoethane meets an ammonia molecule rather than the amine product, and the second ammonia molecule removes the \(\ce{H+}\) as ammonium bromide.

:::lab Heating under reflux
Halogenoalkanes and ethanol are volatile, and the reactions are slow at room temperature. Heating under reflux lets you boil the mixture for a long time without losing anything: the vapour condenses in the vertical condenser and drips back into the flask. Add anti-bumping granules for smooth boiling, and never close the top of the condenser.
:::

:::warn Aqueous or ethanolic: the solvent changes the product
NaOH dissolved in **water** gives substitution (an alcohol). NaOH dissolved in **ethanol** gives elimination (an alkene; see below). KCN is always used in **ethanol**: in water the cyanide ions would react with water to give hydroxide ions, and you would make some alcohol as well. Write the solvent every time: "NaOH" alone scores nothing for conditions.
:::

:::eg Name the organic product, and write an equation, for the reaction of 2-iodopropane with KCN in ethanol.
The \(\ce{CN-}\) ion replaces the iodine atom on carbon 2:
\[\ce{CH3CHICH3 + KCN -> CH3CH(CN)CH3 + KI}\]
Naming a nitrile counts the carbon of the CN group as part of the chain. The longest chain that includes it has three carbons (\(\ce{CH3-CH-CN}\)) with a methyl branch on carbon 2, so the product is 2-methylpropanenitrile.
:::

## The SN2 mechanism: one step

A **mechanism** shows how the electrons move. Curly arrows show the movement of a pair of electrons, and each arrow starts at a lone pair or a bond and ends where that pair goes. For a primary halogenoalkane such as bromoethane, everything happens in one step.

:::steps
1. The C–Br bond is polar, \(\ce{C^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\).
2. A lone pair on the oxygen of \(\ce{OH-}\) is attracted to the δ+ carbon. The nucleophile approaches from the side opposite the bromine atom.
3. As the new C–O bond forms, the C–Br bond breaks heterolytically. For an instant both bonds are half-made, half-broken: this is the **transition state**, which carries the negative charge spread over the whole group.
4. The bromine leaves as \(\ce{Br-}\) with both electrons of the old bond, and ethanol is formed.
:::

The name describes the mechanism: **S**ubstitution, **N**ucleophilic, **2** molecules (the halogenoalkane and the nucleophile) taking part in the single, rate-determining step. (The rate-determining step is the slowest step of a mechanism; it sets the speed of the whole reaction. See the slow and fast steps in [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons).)

:::analogy
Think of an umbrella turned inside out by the wind. The nucleophile pushes in from one side, the three other groups on the carbon flip over like the umbrella's spokes, and the bromine is pushed out of the other side. The attack has to come from the back, which is why crowded carbons resist SN2.
:::

:::exam Drawing a mechanism for full marks
Examiners look for: the dipole \(\ce{C^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\); a curly arrow from a **lone pair** on the nucleophile's O (or C of \(\ce{CN-}\), or N of \(\ce{NH3}\)) to the δ+ carbon; a curly arrow from the **C–Br bond** to Br; the transition state in square brackets with partial bonds and the overall charge; and the products, including \(\ce{Br-}\). An arrow that starts at the minus sign instead of a lone pair, or at the Br atom instead of the bond, loses the mark.
:::

## The SN1 mechanism: two steps

A tertiary halogenoalkane such as 2-bromo-2-methylpropane reacts differently. Its carbon is surrounded by three bulky methyl groups, so a nucleophile cannot reach the back of it. Instead the C–Br bond breaks first, on its own.

:::def Carbocation
An ion with a positive charge on a carbon atom, such as \(\ce{(CH3)3C+}\). It has only six electrons in the outer shell of that carbon.
:::

Step 1 is slow, because a bond breaks and nothing forms to pay for it. Step 2 is fast: a positive ion and a negative ion attract strongly. Only one molecule, the halogenoalkane, takes part in the slow step, so this is S**N1** (substitution, nucleophilic, unimolecular).

### Why tertiary prefers SN1: the inductive effect

The carbocation in step 1 can only form if it is stable enough. Alkyl groups help. Each alkyl group pushes electron density along its σ bond towards the positive carbon, which spreads out (and so reduces) the positive charge.

:::def Inductive effect (of alkyl groups)
The donation of electron density along σ bonds by alkyl groups towards a neighbouring atom. The more alkyl groups attached to a positive carbon, the more its charge is reduced and the more stable the carbocation.
:::

So a tertiary carbocation, with three alkyl groups, is the most stable, and a primary carbocation, with one, is the least stable. Put this together with crowding around the carbon:

| Halogenoalkane | Carbocation stability | Room for backside attack | Main mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| primary | low (one alkyl group) | plenty | SN2 |
| secondary | medium | some | a mixture of SN1 and SN2 |
| tertiary | high (three alkyl groups) | blocked by three alkyl groups | SN1 |

:::key Which mechanism?
Primary halogenoalkanes tend to react by SN2, tertiary halogenoalkanes by SN1, and secondary halogenoalkanes by a mixture of the two, depending on their structure.
:::

:::eg Explain why 2-bromo-2-methylpropane reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions by the SN1 mechanism and not by SN2.
The carbon bonded to Br is joined to three methyl groups, so it is a tertiary halogenoalkane. [B1]

The three methyl groups crowd the carbon and block a nucleophile from approaching the side opposite the bromine, so SN2 is difficult. [B1]

The three methyl groups donate electron density to the positive carbon (positive inductive effect), so the tertiary carbocation formed when the C–Br bond breaks is stable enough to form. [B1]
:::

## Elimination: making alkenes

Change the solvent from water to ethanol and the hydroxide ion behaves differently. Instead of attacking the carbon, it acts as a **base**: it removes an \(\ce{H+}\) ion from the carbon next to the C–Br carbon, a double bond forms between the two carbons, and \(\ce{Br-}\) leaves. A small molecule (HBr, which ends up as \(\ce{NaBr + H2O}\)) has been removed from the halogenoalkane, so this is an **elimination** reaction.

\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + NaOH ->[\text{ethanol, heat}] CH2=CH2 + NaBr + H2O}\]

:::cols
:::col NaOH in water, heat
\(\ce{OH-}\) acts as a **nucleophile**: substitution gives an alcohol. Bromoethane gives ethanol.
:::
:::col NaOH in ethanol, heat
\(\ce{OH-}\) acts as a **base**: elimination gives an alkene. Bromoethane gives ethene.
:::
:::

When the bromine is in the middle of a chain, the \(\ce{H+}\) can come from the carbon on either side, so more than one alkene can form.

:::eg 2-bromobutane is heated with sodium hydroxide in ethanol. Name the organic products.
The carbon holding Br is carbon 2. The \(\ce{H+}\) can be removed from carbon 1 or from carbon 3.

Removing it from carbon 1 gives a double bond between C1 and C2: but-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}\).

Removing it from carbon 3 gives a double bond between C2 and C3: but-2-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}\). Each carbon of this double bond carries two different groups (H and \(\ce{CH3}\)), so but-2-ene has two geometrical isomers, cis and trans (see [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism)).

So there are three alkene products: but-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene.
:::

## How fast? The C–X bond strength

Which halogenoalkane is hydrolysed fastest? You can find out with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol. Water is the nucleophile; as the halide ion is released, it meets \(\ce{Ag+}\) ions and a precipitate of the silver halide forms. The quicker the precipitate appears, the faster the hydrolysis.

\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + H2O -> CH3CH2CH2CH2OH + H+ + Br-}\]
\[\ce{Ag+(aq) + Br-(aq) -> AgBr(s)}\]

:::lab Comparing rates with silver nitrate
Put equal volumes of ethanol, aqueous silver nitrate and each halogenoalkane in three test tubes, all in a water bath at the same temperature (about 50 °C). Ethanol is needed because halogenoalkanes do not mix with water. Start a clock and note when a precipitate first appears in each tube. Keep the amounts, temperature and chain length the same so that only the halogen changes.
:::

| Halogenoalkane | C–X bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | Precipitate | Rate of hydrolysis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1-chlorobutane | 340 | white, AgCl | slowest |
| 1-bromobutane | 280 | cream, AgBr | medium |
| 1-iodobutane | 240 | pale yellow, AgI | fastest |

Here is the surprise. Chlorine is the most electronegative of the three, so the C–Cl bond is the most polar and its carbon the most δ+. If polarity decided the rate, chloroalkanes would react fastest. They react slowest. The **bond energy** decides: the C–I bond is the weakest, so it breaks most easily, and iodoalkanes are the most reactive.

:::key Reactivity of halogenoalkanes
Rate of hydrolysis: iodoalkane > bromoalkane > chloroalkane, because the C–X bond energy decreases from C–Cl (340 kJ mol⁻¹) to C–I (240 kJ mol⁻¹). Bond strength matters more than bond polarity.
:::

The same test also identifies an unknown halogen: the colour of the precipitate tells you which halide was released. Tertiary halogenoalkanes give a precipitate faster than primary ones with the same halogen, because the SN1 route through a stable carbocation is quick.

:::warn It is the bond, not the ion
A common wrong answer says "iodine is more reactive". Iodine is the *least* reactive halogen element (see [Group 17: the halogens](#chem.group17)). What matters here is that the C–I **bond** is the weakest. Write "the C–I bond is weaker than the C–Br bond, so less energy is needed to break it".
:::

## Halogenoalkanes in synthesis

Because so many groups can replace the halogen, a halogenoalkane is often the middle step of a route. From bromoethane alone you can reach ethanol, ethene, ethylamine and propanenitrile, and from propanenitrile, by hydrolysis, propanoic acid (see [Carboxylic acids, esters and nitriles](#chem.org.acids)).

:::eg (Real context) A chemist needs propanoic acid but only has ethene. Suggest a three-step route, giving reagents and conditions for each step.
The product has one more carbon than ethene, so a cyanide step is needed to add a carbon.

Step 1: ethene with \(\ce{HBr(g)}\) at room temperature gives bromoethane (electrophilic addition). [B1]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CH2Br}\]
Step 2: bromoethane heated with KCN in ethanol gives propanenitrile (nucleophilic substitution). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + KCN -> CH3CH2CN + KBr}\]
Step 3: propanenitrile heated with dilute acid (hydrolysis) gives propanoic acid. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CN + H+ + 2H2O -> CH3CH2COOH + NH4+}\]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 5.00 g of 1-bromobutane is heated under reflux with excess NaOH(aq). After purification, 2.10 g of butan-1-ol is collected. Calculate the percentage yield.
The equation is \(\ce{C4H9Br + OH- -> C4H9OH + Br-}\): 1 mol of 1-bromobutane gives 1 mol of butan-1-ol.

\(M_r(\ce{C4H9Br}) = 4(12.0) + 9(1.0) + 79.9 = 136.9\) and \(M_r(\ce{C4H9OH}) = 4(12.0) + 10(1.0) + 16.0 = 74.0\). [B1]
\[n(\ce{C4H9Br}) = \frac{5.00}{136.9} = 0.03652\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[\text{maximum mass of butan-1-ol} = 0.03652 \times 74.0 = 2.703\,\mathrm{g}\] [M1]
\[\text{percentage yield} = \frac{2.10}{2.703} \times 100 = 77.7\,\%\] [A1]

Losses happen when some product stays in the apparatus during distillation and when a little alkene forms by elimination.

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: Classify each as primary, secondary or tertiary: (a) 2-chlorobutane, (b) 1-iodo-2-methylpropane, (c) 2-bromo-2-methylbutane.
A: (a) secondary: C2 is joined to C1 and C3. (b) primary: the carbon holding I is joined to only one carbon. (c) tertiary: C2 is joined to three carbons (C1, C3 and the methyl branch).
Q: Name the organic product when 1-bromopropane is heated under reflux with NaOH(aq), and write the equation.
A: Propan-1-ol.
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br + OH- -> CH3CH2CH2OH + Br-}\]
Q: Give the reagent and conditions to convert bromoethane into ethylamine.
A: Excess ammonia in ethanol, heated under pressure (in a sealed tube).
Q: Write an equation for the reaction of propan-1-ol with phosphorus(V) chloride.
A: \[\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + PCl5 -> CH3CH2CH2Cl + POCl3 + HCl}\]
Q: Which of 1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane gives a precipitate first with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol? Explain.
A: 1-iodobutane. The C–I bond (240 kJ mol⁻¹) is weaker than C–Br (280) and C–Cl (340), so it breaks fastest and iodide ions are released first, giving pale yellow AgI.
Q: 1-bromopropane is heated with KCN in ethanol. Name the product and state how many carbon atoms it has.
A: Butanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}\). It has four carbon atoms: the three of the chain plus the carbon of the CN group.
Q: State the two curly arrows in the SN2 mechanism for \(\ce{CN-}\) with bromomethane.
A: One from the lone pair on the carbon of \(\ce{CN-}\) to the δ+ carbon of \(\ce{CH3Br}\); one from the C–Br bond to the Br atom.
Q: Suggest why elimination from 2-bromo-2-methylpropane gives only one alkene, while 2-bromobutane gives three (counting cis and trans).
A: In 2-bromo-2-methylpropane the three carbons next to C2 are all identical methyl groups, so whichever one loses \(\ce{H+}\), the product is the same: 2-methylpropene, which has no cis/trans isomers. In 2-bromobutane, C1 and C3 are different, giving but-1-ene and but-2-ene, and but-2-ene exists as cis and trans isomers.

Q: [2 marks] ★ Give the reagent and conditions needed to convert 1-chloropropane into butanenitrile.
A: KCN (or NaCN) [B1] in ethanol, heated under reflux. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★ Describe the SN2 mechanism for the reaction between hydroxide ions and bromoethane. You may use a diagram.
A: Dipole shown on the C–Br bond, \(\ce{C^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\). [B1]
Curly arrow from a lone pair on the O of \(\ce{OH-}\) to the δ+ carbon. [B1]
Curly arrow from the C–Br bond to the Br atom. [B1]
Transition state in brackets with partial C–O and C–Br bonds and a negative charge; products \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) and \(\ce{Br-}\). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ 6.85 g of 2-bromo-2-methylpropane was hydrolysed and gave 2.96 g of 2-methylpropan-2-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)3COH}\). Calculate the percentage yield.
H: Both compounds contain four carbon atoms, and 1 mol of halogenoalkane gives 1 mol of alcohol.
A: \(M_r\) values: \(\ce{C4H9Br}\) 136.9 and \(\ce{C4H9OH}\) 74.0. [B1]
\(n(\ce{C4H9Br}) = 6.85 / 136.9 = 0.05004\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
Maximum mass of alcohol \(= 0.05004 \times 74.0 = 3.703\,\mathrm{g}\) [M1]
Percentage yield \(= 2.96 / 3.703 \times 100 = 79.9\,\%\) [A1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ Primary halogenoalkanes react mainly by SN2 and tertiary ones by SN1. Explain this difference.
A: In a tertiary halogenoalkane, three alkyl groups donate electron density (inductive effect) and stabilise the carbocation, so SN1 is possible. [B1]
The three alkyl groups also block attack from the side opposite the halogen, so SN2 is hindered. [B1]
A primary carbocation has only one alkyl group and is unstable, while the carbon is open to backside attack, so SN2 is preferred. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Propene is converted into a nitrile in two steps. (a) Give the reagent and conditions for each step. (b) Name the major nitrile product and explain why it is the major product.
H: In step 1, which carbon does the bromine join? Think about which carbocation forms.
A: (a) Step 1: \(\ce{HBr(g)}\) at room temperature. [B1] Step 2: KCN in ethanol, heat. [B1]
(b) 2-methylpropanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH(CN)CH3}\). [B1]
In step 1 the secondary carbocation \(\ce{CH3CH+CH3}\) is more stable than the primary one, because two alkyl groups donate electron density to it. [B1]
So the main intermediate is 2-bromopropane, and \(\ce{CN-}\) replaces the Br on carbon 2. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ A student warms three halogenoalkanes, A, B and C, each with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol at 50 °C. A gives a pale yellow precipitate after 1 minute, B a cream precipitate after 5 minutes, and C a white precipitate after 30 minutes. (a) Identify the halogen in each compound. (b) Use the bond energies C–Cl 340, C–Br 280 and C–I 240 kJ mol⁻¹ to explain the order of the times.
A: (a) A contains iodine, B bromine and C chlorine. [B1]
(b) The halide ion is released when the C–X bond breaks. [B1]
Bond energies: C–I 240, C–Br 280, C–Cl 340 kJ mol⁻¹. [B1]
The weaker the bond, the less energy is needed to break it, so the C–I bond breaks fastest and the C–Cl bond slowest. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) 1.37 g of a bromoalkane, \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}Br}\), was completely hydrolysed and the bromide ions were precipitated as 1.88 g of silver bromide. Find the relative molecular mass of the bromoalkane and its molecular formula.
H: One mole of bromoalkane gives one mole of AgBr. \(M_r(\ce{AgBr}) = 107.9 + 79.9\).
A: \(n(\ce{AgBr}) = 1.88 / 187.8 = 0.01001\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
\(M_r = 1.37 / 0.01001 = 137\) [M1 A1]
\(14n + 1 + 79.9 = 136.9\) gives \(n = 4\): the formula is \(\ce{C4H9Br}\). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Compound P, \(\ce{C4H9Br}\), is heated with NaOH in ethanol and gives only one organic product, an alkene with no geometrical isomers. Compound Q, an isomer of P, gives three alkenes under the same conditions. P reacts with aqueous silver nitrate faster than Q. (a) Identify P and Q. (b) Name the mechanism by which P reacts with NaOH(aq) and explain why P gives a precipitate with silver nitrate faster than Q.
H: Which isomer of \(\ce{C4H9Br}\) gives three alkenes? Look back at the worked examples.
H: Only one isomer has three identical groups next to the C–Br carbon.
A: (a) P is 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CBr}\) [B1]: all three neighbouring carbons are identical, so the only alkene is 2-methylpropene. [B1]
Q is 2-bromobutane [B1]: it gives but-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene. [B1]
(b) P reacts by SN1. [B1]
P is tertiary, so it forms a stable tertiary carbocation quickly and releases \(\ce{Br-}\) faster than the secondary isomer Q. [B1]

Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Sodium methoxide, \(\ce{CH3O^-Na+}\), dissolved in methanol reacts with bromoethane by nucleophilic substitution. Suggest the organic product, give the equation, and describe the curly arrows of the mechanism you expect.
H: The methoxide ion has lone pairs on its oxygen, just like the hydroxide ion.
H: Bromoethane is primary. Which mechanism do primary halogenoalkanes prefer?
A: The \(\ce{CH3O-}\) ion is a nucleophile and replaces Br, giving methoxyethane, \(\ce{CH3OCH2CH3}\). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + CH3O- -> CH3CH2OCH3 + Br-}\] [B1]
Bromoethane is primary, so the mechanism is SN2. [B1]
Curly arrows: from a lone pair on the O of \(\ce{CH3O-}\) to the δ+ carbon, and from the C–Br bond to Br, through a transition state with partial C–O and C–Br bonds. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ (A Level: see the last section of [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism)) 3-bromo-3-methylhexane, \(\ce{CH3CH2C(CH3)BrCH2CH2CH3}\), has a chiral centre. A single optical isomer of it is hydrolysed by NaOH(aq). Predict whether the alcohol formed is optically active, and explain using the shape of the intermediate.
H: A racemic mixture has equal amounts of both enantiomers, so it does not rotate plane-polarised light.
H: The compound is tertiary. Which mechanism does it follow, and what is the intermediate?
H: A carbocation's positive carbon has three bonds in a plane. From which sides can \(\ce{OH-}\) attack?
A: The compound is tertiary, so it reacts by SN1 through a carbocation. [B1]
The carbocation is planar (three bonds at 120° around the positive carbon). [B1]
\(\ce{OH-}\) can attack equally from either face of the plane, giving the two optical isomers of 3-methylhexan-3-ol in equal amounts. [B1]
The product is a racemic mixture, so it is not optically active. [B1]

:::recap
- A halogenoalkane has a polar \(\ce{C^{\delta+}-X^{\delta-}}\) bond. Primary, secondary and tertiary depend on how many carbons are joined to the C–X carbon.
- Made from alkanes (X₂, UV), alkenes (HX(g) or X₂, room temperature) or alcohols (HX(g); KCl + concentrated H₂SO₄ or H₃PO₄; PCl₃ and heat; PCl₅; SOCl₂).
- Substitution: NaOH(aq), heat → alcohol; KCN in ethanol, heat → nitrile (+1 carbon); excess NH₃ in ethanol under pressure → amine; AgNO₃(aq) in ethanol → precipitate of AgX.
- Elimination: NaOH in ethanol, heat → alkene. Water as solvent favours substitution, ethanol elimination.
- SN2 (primary): one step, attack from the side opposite X, transition state in brackets. SN1 (tertiary): slow C–X bond breaking to a carbocation, then fast attack. Secondary: both.
- Alkyl groups stabilise carbocations by their inductive effect: tertiary > secondary > primary.
- Rate of hydrolysis: R–I > R–Br > R–Cl, because bond energy C–I 240 < C–Br 280 < C–Cl 340 kJ mol⁻¹. Precipitates: AgCl white, AgBr cream, AgI pale yellow.
:::
