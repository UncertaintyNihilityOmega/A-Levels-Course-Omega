:::why
In a structured paper the chemistry is not enough: the same knowledge written two ways can earn 3 marks or 1. Examiners mark against a scheme of single creditworthy points, so a long answer that never states the point scores nothing, and a short answer that states each point exactly scores everything. This guide shows how to write for the mark scheme: what each command word wants, how marks are given, how to word the standard definitions, and what examiners look for in an equation, a mechanism and an explanation.
:::

## The two papers

| Fact | Paper 2 | Paper 4 |
|---|---|---|
| Name | AS Level Structured Questions | A Level Structured Questions |
| Time | 1 hour 15 minutes | 2 hours |
| Marks | 60 | 100 |
| Content | AS Level, topics 1–22 | A Level, topics 23–37; AS knowledge is required |
| Weighting | 46% of AS Level; 23% of A Level | 38.5% of A Level |
| Assessment objectives | AO1 and AO2 | AO1 and AO2 |

The questions are written in parts, (a), (b), (c) and so on, with the marks for each part in brackets. Two points about the content matter for Paper 4. **AS knowledge is assumed**, so a question on lattice energy may need a Hess cycle from [Chemical energetics](#chem.energetics) and an A Level question on organic synthesis uses the AS reactions from [Organic synthesis (AS)](#chem.org.synthesis). And the pace is slightly different: Paper 2 gives you 1.25 minutes per mark on average (75 ÷ 60); Paper 4 gives 1.2 minutes per mark (120 ÷ 100). A part worth 3 marks deserves about 3 to 4 minutes, and a part worth 1 mark should take well under a minute.

:::tip
Use the marks as a guide to length. One mark is one point: a 2-mark question wants two separate facts, and a 4-mark "explain" wants four linked points. If you have written three lines for a 1-mark question you are spending time you need elsewhere.
:::

## Command words

The first word of a question tells you what kind of answer earns the marks. The syllabus uses these command words:

| Command word | What it needs in chemistry |
|---|---|
| **State** | One fact, with no reason. "State the shape of \(\ce{NH3}\)" wants "pyramidal" |
| **Give** | The answer only: a formula, a name, a number or a reagent |
| **Identify** | Name or select the substance or feature, from information or the context |
| **Define** | The exact definition, word for word (see the table below) |
| **Describe** | What you would see or do, step by step, with reagents, conditions and observations; or what a trend or graph shows |
| **Explain** | A reason linked to the data or the chemistry: because of (the cause), so (the effect) |
| **Calculate** | A numerical answer with working, units and the right significant figures |
| **Determine** | Work out a quantity from data or a graph, showing the method |
| **Deduce** | Reach a conclusion from information given in the question (not from memory alone) |
| **Predict** | Say what you expect to happen, using the pattern or theory you know |
| **Suggest** | Apply your knowledge to a situation you have not met; there may be several acceptable answers |
| **Show (that)** | Reach the given answer with every step shown; a bare answer earns nothing |
| **Sketch** | A simple labelled graph or diagram with the correct shape and key features, not to scale |
| **Compare** | Say what is similar and what is different, using both substances in each point |
| **Contrast** | Say only what is different |
| **Justify** | Support an answer or a choice with evidence |
| **Analyse**, **Examine**, **Consider** | Work through the information in detail and say what it shows |
| **Demonstrate** | Show something is true by working or evidence |
| **Discuss**, **Evaluate** | Give points for and against, or strengths and weaknesses, then reach a conclusion |

:::warn
"Explain" and "state" are not interchangeable. "State why the boiling point of water is high" can earn one mark for a fact; "explain" asks for the cause, such as hydrogen bonding between molecules and the need for energy to break them. Read the command word before you start writing.
:::

## How the marks are given

Mark schemes use three kinds of mark. A **B mark** is independent: it is earned by a correct statement, formula or observation whatever else is on the page. An **M mark** is for a correct method step, in calculations and organic reasoning. An **A mark** is for an accurate answer and depends on the M mark before it: if the method is wrong, the answer cannot score, even if it happens to be right.

Three rules save marks:

- **Error carried forward.** If you make a slip in one step, later steps are marked on the value you used. A wrong Mr in part (a) can still earn the method and answer marks in part (b) if you use it correctly. So always show your working; a bare wrong answer earns nothing, but a wrong answer with the method shown can earn most of the marks.
- **Units and significant figures.** The syllabus says misuse of units or significant figures, units on ratios, or wrong significant figures may be penalised. Give an answer to the same number of significant figures as, or one more than, the least precise data. Write the unit every time.
- **Read the mark scheme's phrases.** Mark schemes say "allow" for an alternative accepted answer, "ignore" for something that does not affect the mark, and "reject" for an answer that loses it. A contradictory extra statement can lose a mark, so do not add a second answer "just in case".

:::exam
Cambridge mark schemes award marks for chemistry words in the right place. "More energy is needed" earns nothing; "more energy is needed to overcome the stronger id-id forces between molecules" earns the mark. Name what is being overcome, and what causes it.
:::

## Definitions: word for word

Definitions are among the easiest marks in the paper, and the ones most often lost by paraphrasing. Learn these exactly. Each links to its chapter.

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| First ionisation energy ([atoms](#chem.atoms)) | The energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions |
| Relative atomic mass, \(A_r\) ([moles](#chem.moles)) | The weighted average mass of an atom of an element, taking account of the abundance of its isotopes, compared with one twelfth of the mass of an atom of carbon-12 |
| Electronegativity ([bonding](#chem.bonding)) | The power of an atom to attract electrons to itself |
| Bond energy ([bonding](#chem.bonding)) | The energy required to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in the gaseous state |
| Standard enthalpy change of formation ([energetics](#chem.energetics)) | The enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states under standard conditions |
| Standard enthalpy change of combustion ([energetics](#chem.energetics)) | The enthalpy change when one mole of a substance is burnt completely in oxygen under standard conditions |
| Standard enthalpy change of neutralisation ([energetics](#chem.energetics)) | The enthalpy change when one mole of water is formed by the reaction of an acid with an alkali under standard conditions |
| Le Chatelier's principle ([equilibria](#chem.equilibria)) | If a change is made to a system at dynamic equilibrium, the position of equilibrium moves to minimise this change |
| Activation energy, \(E_a\) ([kinetics](#chem.kinetics)) | The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for a collision to be effective (to result in a reaction) |
| Lattice energy ([lattice energy](#chem.lattice)) | The enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions under standard conditions |
| First electron affinity ([lattice energy](#chem.lattice)) | The enthalpy change when one electron is added to each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions |
| Standard electrode (reduction) potential ([electrochemistry](#chem.electrochem)) | The voltage measured when a half-cell is connected to a standard hydrogen electrode, under standard conditions: 298 K, ions at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ and gases at 101 kPa |
| Buffer solution ([pH and buffers](#chem.acids2)) | A solution that minimises changes in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added (or when it is diluted) |
| Transition element ([transition elements](#chem.transition)) | A d-block element that forms one or more stable ions with incomplete d orbitals |
| Ligand ([complexes](#chem.complexes)) | A species that contains a lone pair of electrons and uses it to form a dative covalent bond to a central metal atom or ion |
| Stability constant, \(K_{stab}\) ([complexes](#chem.complexes)) | The equilibrium constant for the formation of a complex ion in a solvent from its constituent ions or molecules |

:::tip
Make a flashcard for each row, with the term on the front and the definition on the back, and say it aloud. The hardest ones are those with a unit in the wording ("one mole", "gaseous", "standard conditions"): every one of those words is a mark.
:::

## Equations

An equation question earns marks for the formulae, the balance and the state symbols, in that order of importance.

- **Balanced symbol equations.** Write the correct formulae first and balance only by changing the numbers in front. Check atoms and charge. For a redox equation built from half-equations, make the electrons equal and cancel them.
- **State symbols** only when the question asks for them, or when they are the point (an ionic precipitation, \(\ce{Ag+(aq) + Cl-(aq) -> AgCl(s)}\)). Gases in a combustion equation are written (g), liquid water (l).
- **Ionic equations.** Write the full equation, split the strong electrolytes that are aqueous into ions, cancel the spectator ions and write what is left. The charge must balance as well as the atoms.
- **Organic equations.** Use molecular or structural formulae as the question requires. In an organic redox reaction, [O] and [H] are acceptable to show the oxidising and reducing agent. Give the reagent and condition on the arrow (or beside it) when the question asks "state the reagents and conditions".

:::warn
A reagent and its solvent are both part of the answer: "NaOH in ethanol, heat" (elimination) is not the same as "NaOH(aq), heat" (substitution). Always give the reagent, the solvent where it matters, and the temperature or other conditions.
:::

## Mechanisms: what the arrows earn

A mechanism question is marked in single steps: usually one mark for each curly arrow, dipole, intermediate or transition state, and the products. A curly arrow shows the movement of a pair of electrons; it starts at a lone pair or at the middle of a bond (never at an atom or a charge), and it ends at the atom or bond that receives the pair. Learn the checklist for each type, and practise drawing each from memory.

:::exam Free-radical substitution (methane and chlorine, ultraviolet light)
Marks go for: the condition (ultraviolet light); the **initiation** step, \(\ce{Cl2 -> 2Cl^.}\) (homolytic fission); both **propagation** steps, \(\ce{Cl^. + CH4 -> CH3^. + HCl}\) and \(\ce{CH3^. + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + Cl^.}\), where a radical is used up and another formed; and a **termination** step in which two radicals combine, such as \(\ce{CH3^. + Cl^. -> CH3Cl}\). Show the radical dot on the right atom. Name each step. These are equations, not curly-arrow drawings.
:::

:::exam Electrophilic addition (an alkene with hydrogen bromide or bromine)
Marks go for: the dipole \(\ce{H^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\) (for bromine, the induced dipole in the \(\ce{Br2}\) molecule as it approaches the double bond); a curly arrow from the C=C **bond** to the electrophile (the \(\ce{H}\) or the near \(\ce{Br}\) atom); a curly arrow from the H–Br (or Br–Br) **bond** to the bromine; the **carbocation intermediate** with a + charge on the right carbon; a curly arrow from a **lone pair on \(\ce{Br-}\)** to the positive carbon; and the product. For an unsymmetrical alkene, the major product comes from the more stable carbocation, which has more alkyl groups pushing electrons towards it (the inductive effect).
:::

:::exam Nucleophilic substitution, SN2 and SN1 (halogenoalkanes)
**SN2** (primary): the dipole \(\ce{C^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\); a curly arrow from the **lone pair** on the nucleophile (for example \(\ce{OH-}\)) to the carbon; a curly arrow from the C–Br **bond** to the bromine; a **transition state** in square brackets with dashed partial bonds and the negative charge outside the top-right bracket; and the products. **SN1** (tertiary): step 1, the slow step, an arrow from the C–Br bond to Br giving the carbocation and \(\ce{Br-}\); step 2, an arrow from the lone pair of the nucleophile to the carbocation. Say which step is rate-determining.
:::

:::exam Nucleophilic addition (HCN with an aldehyde or ketone)
Marks go for: the dipole \(\ce{C^{\delta+}=O^{\delta-}}\); a curly arrow from the **lone pair on the carbon** of \(\ce{CN-}\) to the carbonyl carbon; a curly arrow from the C=O **bond** to the oxygen; the intermediate with the negative charge on O; a curly arrow from a lone pair on \(\ce{O-}\) to the H of HCN (with the H–CN bond breaking, which regenerates the \(\ce{CN-}\) catalyst); and the hydroxynitrile product. The reagent is HCN with a little KCN as the catalyst and the nucleophile is \(\ce{CN-}\).
:::

:::exam Electrophilic substitution (A Level: benzene with nitration or bromination)
Marks go for: the equation forming the electrophile (\(\ce{NO2+}\) from concentrated nitric and sulfuric acids, or \(\ce{Br+}\) from \(\ce{Br2}\) and \(\ce{AlBr3}\)); a curly arrow from the **ring** to the electrophile; the intermediate drawn as a partial ring (a horseshoe) with a + charge, with both H and the new group on the same carbon; a curly arrow from the **C–H bond** back into the ring; and the products, \(\ce{H+}\) and the catalyst regenerated. The two usual slips are a full circle in the intermediate and an arrow that starts at the electrophile.
:::

:::exam Addition–elimination (A Level: acyl chloride with a nucleophile)
Marks go for: the dipole \(\ce{C^{\delta+}=O^{\delta-}}\); a curly arrow from a **lone pair** on the nucleophile (O or N) to the carbonyl carbon; a curly arrow from the C=O **bond** to the oxygen; the **tetrahedral intermediate**, with the negative charge on O and a positive charge on the nucleophile's atom; a curly arrow from a lone pair on \(\ce{O-}\) back to form C=O; a curly arrow from the C–Cl **bond** to the chlorine; and the products, including HCl. An arrow that starts at the chlorine atom or at the charge loses the mark.
:::

See [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen) for the pattern of an organic mechanism question with the marks written out.

## Extended explanations

A long "explain" question earns marks for linked points. Use one of two shapes.

**Explaining a trend: name the factor, give its effect, and state the result.**

| Trend | Factors to name |
|---|---|
| First ionisation energy across Period 3 | Nuclear charge increases, shielding is about the same, the radius decreases, so the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is stronger |
| First ionisation energy down a group | The radius and shielding increase, so the attraction is weaker, despite the larger nuclear charge |
| Melting point of an ionic compound | The charges on the ions and their radii: higher charge and smaller radius mean stronger electrostatic attraction |
| Boiling point of a series of similar molecules | The strength of the id-id forces, which depends on the number of electrons (and so the size of the molecule) |
| Boiling point of molecules with N–H or O–H | Hydrogen bonding in addition to the other forces |
| Rate at a higher temperature | A larger fraction of the molecules have at least the activation energy, and the collisions are more frequent |

**Comparing two substances: say what is different about each.** "Magnesium oxide has a higher melting point than sodium chloride" is incomplete; the answer must say that the ions in magnesium oxide have a larger charge and a smaller radius, and so the electrostatic attraction between them is stronger. Both substances must appear in the comparison, using a word such as "whereas" or "but" to join them.

:::tip
For "explain" questions, ask three questions before you write: what structure or bonding does each substance have? What factor makes them different? How does that change the energy needed? The answer is these three points in order.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak answer: explain why the first ionisation energy of sulfur is lower than that of phosphorus. The question gives 1060 kJ mol⁻¹ for phosphorus and 1000 kJ mol⁻¹ for sulfur. [3 marks]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: The fourth 3p electron in sulfur has to pair up with another electron in the same 3p orbital, and the two electrons in that orbital repel each other.
Line 2: This repulsion makes the paired electron easier to remove, so the ionisation energy of sulfur is lower.
Line 3: Sulfur also has a larger atomic radius than phosphorus, so its outer electron is further from the nucleus.

Line 1 is correct: spin-pair repulsion between the two electrons in one orbital [B1]. Line 2 is correct and links the repulsion to the energy needed [B1]. **Line 3 is wrong: atomic radius decreases across a period, so sulfur is slightly smaller than phosphorus, not larger. The third mark is lost [B0].**

Correct line 3: sulfur has a greater nuclear charge and similar shielding, which on its own would make the first ionisation energy higher, but the repulsion between the paired electrons outweighs it. [B1]

The student scores 2 out of 3. A reason that contradicts the periodic trend earns nothing, however confident it sounds, so check every "because" against what you know.
:::

:::eg Model answer, built mark by mark: explain why magnesium oxide has a much higher melting point than sodium chloride. [3 marks]
Step 1: say what the structure is in both substances. Both are giant ionic lattices, held by electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions. [B1]

Step 2: name the factors that differ. The ions in magnesium oxide, \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\), have double the charge of \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\), and they are smaller. [B1]

Step 3: link the factors to the energy. The electrostatic attraction between the ions in magnesium oxide is much stronger, so much more energy is needed to overcome it and melt the solid. [B1]
:::

## Practical-style questions in Papers 2 and 4

Paper 2 and Paper 4 test AO1 and AO2 only (AO3, experimental skills, belongs to Papers 3 and 5), but many of their questions are set around experiments: test results to describe, data to process, a method to comment on. Expect to:

- **describe a test and its observation**, such as the reagent and the colour of a precipitate for a halide ion, or the colour change when an alcohol is oxidised (see [Practical skills and qualitative analysis](#chem.practical)). Give the reagent, the condition and the observation in the syllabus's own words.
- **use data from an experiment**: a titration result, a temperature change, a mass loss. Show the working with units; titre readings are to the nearest 0.05 cm³ and concordant results are within 0.10 cm³.
- **choose apparatus or improve a method**: say why a burette is more precise than a measuring cylinder, and what a practical error such as heat loss does to the result. "Human error" is not an acceptable answer: name the specific error and its effect.
- **plan or evaluate** at A Level: see [Planning, analysis and evaluation](#chem.planning).

## Paper 4 synoptic questions

A Level questions often start from a new substance or a data table and mix topics. An example might begin with a ligand exchange at Topic 28, then ask for a Boltzmann diagram from Topic 8, then a calculation of moles from Topic 2. The AS ideas are not re-taught: they are assumed. Make a short list of AS skills to keep fresh while you revise A Level:

:::steps
1. **Mole calculations**: reacting masses and volumes, concentrations, percentage yield, limiting reagent.
2. **Energetics**: bond-energy and Hess's law calculations, and the sign of ΔH.
3. **Shapes and bonding**: VSEPR shapes, polarity, and which forces act between molecules.
4. **Organic reactions**: reagents, conditions and mechanisms of every AS functional group, since A Level synthesis chains them.
5. **Equilibrium and rate**: Le Chatelier's principle, Kc and Kp, the Boltzmann distribution.
:::

Try each question below on paper first. Then compare your answer with the mark scheme point by point and count the marks you would really have earned.

:::try Write for the mark scheme
Q: [2 marks] (#chem.lattice) Define the first electron affinity of an element.
H: A definition names the quantity, the amount of substance, the state of the particles and the sign of the ion formed.
A: The enthalpy change when one mole of electrons is added to one mole of gaseous atoms [B1] to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions [B1]. Leaving out "gaseous", or "one mole", loses a mark.

Q: [3 marks] (#chem.bonding) Explain why the boiling point of HF is higher than that of HCl.
H: Three linked points: what forces HF has, what HCl has instead, and what that does to the energy needed.
A: HF has hydrogen bonds between its molecules, because H is bonded to the very electronegative F, which has a lone pair. [B1] HCl has only permanent dipole–dipole and instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces between its molecules, because Cl is not small and electronegative enough for hydrogen bonding. [B1] Hydrogen bonds are stronger, so more energy is needed to overcome the forces between HF molecules. [B1] "Hydrogen bonds are broken" is wrong: the covalent H–F bond stays intact when the liquid boils.

Q: [3 marks] (#chem.moles) (a) Calculate the amount, in mol, of NaOH in 25.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide solution. [1] (b) The solution just neutralises sulfuric acid of concentration 0.125 mol dm⁻³, \(\ce{H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O}\). Calculate the volume of acid in cm³. [2] A student writes (a) "25.0 × 0.200 = 5.00 mol" and (b) "5.00 ÷ 2 = 2.50 mol of acid; volume = 2.50 ÷ 0.125 = 20.0 dm³ = 20 000 cm³". Mark the student's answer.
H: Mark part (a) alone first. Then mark part (b) using the student's own answer to (a), not the correct one.
A: Correct answers: (a) \(25.0/1000 \times 0.200 = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [B1]; (b) \(n(\ce{H2SO4}) = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}/2 = 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1], volume \(= 2.50 \times 10^{-3}/0.125 = 0.0200\) dm³ \(= 20.0\) cm³ [A1].
Student: (a) 0 marks, because the volume was not converted to dm³. (b) The student divides by 2 for the 1 : 2 ratio, which earns the method mark. The volume is worked out correctly from the student's own 2.50 mol, and the dm³ to cm³ conversion is correct, which earns the answer mark by **error carried forward** (ecf). The final 20 000 cm³ is the right consequence of the student's own 5.00 mol, so it earns the A mark by ecf; the only mark lost is the B mark in (a). Total 2 of 3 marks.

Q: [4 marks] (#chem.org.halogen) Describe, in words, the SN1 mechanism of the hydrolysis of 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CBr}\), with hydroxide ions.
H: Use the mark scheme words: curly arrow, from where to where, the intermediate, and which step is the slowest.
A: A curly arrow goes from the C–Br bond to the Br atom [B1], so the bond breaks heterolytically and forms a tertiary carbocation, \(\ce{(CH3)3C+}\), and a bromide ion, \(\ce{Br-}\). [B1] This is the slow, rate-determining step, and it involves only the halogenoalkane, so the rate depends only on its concentration. [B1] A curly arrow goes from the lone pair on the \(\ce{OH-}\) ion to the positively charged carbon [B1], forming 2-methylpropan-2-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)3COH}\). A bare "OH⁻ attacks the C–Br carbon" does not earn the arrow mark.
:::

:::quiz Command words
? A question begins "State". What does it want?
- A fact and the reason for it.
+ One fact, with no reason.
- A full definition with an example.
- Several linked points.
> "State" asks for a single fact, such as "pyramidal" for the shape of ammonia. A reason is wasted time here; it belongs to "explain".

? "Explain why the boiling point of HF is higher than that of HCl." Which answer has the structure the question wants?
- HF has a higher boiling point because it has a higher boiling point.
- HF is a smaller molecule than HCl.
+ HF has hydrogen bonds, which are stronger than the forces between HCl molecules, so more energy is needed to separate the molecules.
- Hydrogen bonds between HF molecules are broken when it boils, which is hard.
> "Explain" needs a cause linked to its effect: the type of force, how its strength compares, and the energy needed. The last option is close, but it says the bond is "hard" to break instead of linking strength to energy, and it does not compare with HCl.

? "Deduce the formula of the compound" is followed by data in the question. Where should the answer come from?
- From memory of the compound's formula alone.
- From a guess based on the name.
+ From the information given in the question, with the reasoning shown.
- From any similar compound you know.
> "Deduce" means reach a conclusion from the information provided. A remembered answer without the reasoning from the data does not earn the marks.

? A question asks you to "suggest" why a compound you have never studied has a high melting point. What do you do?
- Leave it blank, because it is not in the syllabus.
- Write everything you know about melting points.
+ Apply the bonding and structure ideas you know to this compound; several answers may be accepted.
- Give the definition of melting point.
> "Suggest" tests whether you can apply known chemistry to a new situation. Use the clues in the question, and write an answer even if the compound is unfamiliar.

? "Compare the boiling points of HF and HCl." Which answer earns the mark?
- HF has hydrogen bonds.
- The boiling points are different.
+ HF has a higher boiling point than HCl.
- HCl has instantaneous dipole–induced dipole forces.
> A comparison uses both substances in the same statement and says how they differ. The other options describe only one substance, or say nothing about which is higher.
:::
