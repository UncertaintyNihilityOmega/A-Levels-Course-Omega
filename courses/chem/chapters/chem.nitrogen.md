## Nitrogen: why is it so unreactive?

Nitrogen gas, \(\ce{N2}\), is the main gas in the air, and you can breathe it in and out without any change. It does not burn, it does not react with water and it does not react with most metals at room temperature. Compare it with oxygen, which makes up about a fifth of the air and which reacts with almost everything. Two facts about the \(\ce{N2}\) molecule explain the difference.

**A very strong bond.** The two nitrogen atoms are joined by a triple bond, \(\ce{N#N}\). The Data booklet gives bond energies of N–N 160, N=N 410 and N≡N 944 kJ mol⁻¹. The triple bond is much more than three times the single bond, and nearly twice the strength of the double bond in oxygen (O=O 496 kJ mol⁻¹). Breaking \(\ce{N2}\) into atoms, which a reaction usually needs to do, takes a huge amount of energy.

**No polarity.** Both atoms are the same, so the shared electrons are equally shared and there is no δ+ or δ− on the molecule. A reagent that is attracted to charge (for example a nucleophile looking for a δ+ atom, or an electrophile looking for electron-rich regions) has nothing to attack. The only attractions between \(\ce{N2}\) molecules are weak id–id forces, which is why nitrogen is a gas down to −196 °C.

:::key Why N₂ is unreactive
(1) The N≡N triple bond is very strong (944 kJ mol⁻¹), so a very large activation energy is needed to break it. (2) \(\ce{N2}\) is non-polar, so it has no δ+ or δ− atoms to attract attacking reagents.
:::

:::warn Not because it is a gas
"Nitrogen is unreactive because it is a gas" or "because it is a noble gas" scores nothing. Chlorine and oxygen are gases too, and they are very reactive. Always give the **bond energy** and the **lack of polarity**.
:::

Nitrogen does react, but only when it is given a great deal of energy: in lightning, in a hot engine and in the Haber process (high temperature and pressure with an iron catalyst). Each of these appears later in this chapter.

:::eg Explain why nitrogen is much less reactive than oxygen.
The N≡N bond is very strong (944 kJ mol⁻¹ compared with 496 kJ mol⁻¹ for O=O), so much more energy is needed to break it. [B1]
\(\ce{N2}\) is a non-polar molecule, so it has no partial charges to attract an attacking reagent. [B1]
:::

## Ammonia and the ammonium ion

Nitrogen forms hydrides, and the most important is **ammonia**, \(\ce{NH3}\). It is a colourless gas with a pungent smell, and it is very soluble in water. The nitrogen atom has five outer electrons: three form bonds with hydrogen atoms and two stay as a **lone pair**. With three bonding pairs and one lone pair, the molecule is a trigonal pyramid with H–N–H angles of about 107° (see [Shapes of molecules and intermolecular forces](#chem.shapes)).

### Ammonia as a base

The lone pair on nitrogen can form a bond to a proton. A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton, so ammonia is a base: it uses its lone pair to accept \(\ce{H+}\).

\[\ce{NH3 + H+ -> NH4+}\]

With an acid, the products are ammonium salts:

\[\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}\]
\[\ce{NH3(aq) + HNO3(aq) -> NH4NO3(aq)}\]
\[\ce{2NH3(aq) + H2SO4(aq) -> (NH4)2SO4(aq)}\]

When a drop of concentrated ammonia solution and a drop of concentrated hydrochloric acid are held side by side, the two gases meet in the air between them and form a white smoke of solid ammonium chloride. In water, ammonia is only a **weak base**: only a small fraction of the molecules take a proton from water, and the solution is alkaline.

\[\ce{NH3(aq) + H2O(l) <=> NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq)}\]

The proton has no electrons of its own, so both electrons of the new N–H bond come from the nitrogen atom. This is a **coordinate (dative covalent) bond**. Once the ion has formed, all four N–H bonds are identical, and you cannot tell which one was the dative one. The ammonium ion \(\ce{NH4+}\) has four bonding pairs and no lone pair around nitrogen, so its shape is **tetrahedral** with H–N–H angles of 109.5°. Nitrogen has no empty orbitals in its second shell, but it does not need any: the proton brings the empty orbital, and nitrogen supplies the pair.

:::eg Write an equation for the reaction of ammonia with hydrogen chloride, name the type of bond formed between the nitrogen atom and the new hydrogen and explain how it forms.
\(\ce{NH3 + HCl -> NH4Cl}\) (the ionic solid, containing \(\ce{NH4+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\)). [B1]
The N–H bond is a coordinate (dative covalent) bond. [B1]
The nitrogen atom donates its lone pair of electrons to the \(\ce{H+}\) (from HCl), so both electrons in the new bond come from nitrogen. [B1]
:::

### Displacing ammonia from an ammonium salt

An ammonium ion can give a proton to a stronger base, and hydroxide ion is a stronger base than ammonia. When an ammonium salt is warmed with an alkali, \(\ce{OH-}\) takes \(\ce{H+}\) from \(\ce{NH4+}\) and ammonia gas is **displaced**:

\[\ce{NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> NH3(g) + H2O(l)}\]

\[\ce{NH4Cl(s) + NaOH(aq) -> NaCl(aq) + NH3(g) + H2O(l)}\]

Ammonia is the only common alkaline gas, so it turns damp red litmus paper blue. This is the standard test for ammonium ions: warm the solid or solution with aqueous sodium hydroxide and hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube. (The tests for cations in [Practical skills and qualitative analysis](#chem.practical) list it with the other ions: no precipitate forms with \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), but ammonia is given off on warming.)

:::lab Testing for ammonium ions
Warm about 2 cm³ of the solution with the same volume of sodium hydroxide solution in a test tube (gloves and eye protection: the alkali is corrosive). Do not boil it, and keep the mouth of the tube pointing away from people. Hold a piece of damp red litmus paper at the mouth. It turns blue if ammonia is given off.
:::

:::eg Solid ammonium sulfate is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Write an ionic equation and describe how you would test for the gas.
\(\ce{NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> NH3(g) + H2O(l)}\) [B1]
Hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube. [B1]
It turns blue, showing that the gas is alkaline ammonia. [B1]
:::

:::warn Write the charge
"NH₄" on its own is not an ion. Write \(\ce{NH4+}\) in every equation, and say that it is "the ammonium ion". The ion \(\ce{NH4+}\) can be written inside a compound as \(\ce{NH4Cl}\) or \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\) (the brackets show that two ammonium ions are present).
:::

## Making ammonia and fertilisers

Ammonia is made industrially by the Haber process, \(\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}\) with ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹, at about 200 atm and 400–450 °C with an iron catalyst. The conditions are explained fully in [Chemical equilibria](#chem.equilibria). A rough estimate of the energy change comes from bond energies. The reaction breaks one N≡N and three H–H bonds and forms six N–H bonds.

:::eg (Exam style) Use bond energies to estimate ΔH for \(\ce{N2 + 3H2 -> 2NH3}\). The bond energies in kJ mol⁻¹ are N≡N 944, H–H 436, N–H 390. The accepted value is −92 kJ mol⁻¹: suggest why your answer is not exactly this.
Bonds broken: \(944 + 3 \times 436 = 2252\) kJ. [M1]
Bonds made: \(6 \times 390 = 2340\) kJ. [M1]
\[\Delta H = 2252 - 2340 = -88\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] [A1]
The N–H bond energy is an average taken over many compounds, so it is not exactly the value for N–H in ammonia. [B1]
:::

Most ammonia is turned into nitrogen fertilisers. Plants take in nitrogen as ammonium and nitrate ions and use it to make proteins, so soil from which crops are harvested needs the nitrogen put back. Two of the most important fertilisers are made by simple neutralisation of ammonia with an acid:

\[\ce{NH3 + HNO3 -> NH4NO3}\]
\[\ce{2NH3 + H2SO4 -> (NH4)2SO4}\]

Ammonium nitrate and ammonium sulfate differ in the amount of nitrogen they supply per kilogram. This is calculated as a percentage by mass of nitrogen.

:::eg (Real context) A farmer compares two fertilisers: ammonium nitrate, \(\ce{NH4NO3}\), and ammonium sulfate, \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\). Calculate the percentage of nitrogen by mass in each, and say which supplies more nitrogen per kilogram.
\(M_r(\ce{NH4NO3}) = 2(14.0) + 4(1.0) + 3(16.0) = 80.0\) [B1]
\[\%\,\mathrm{N} = \frac{2 \times 14.0}{80.0} \times 100 = 35.0\,\%\] [A1]
\(M_r(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}) = 2(14.0 + 4 \times 1.0) + 32.1 + 4(16.0) = 132.1\) [B1]
\[\%\,\mathrm{N} = \frac{2 \times 14.0}{132.1} \times 100 = 21.2\,\%\] [A1]
Ammonium nitrate supplies more nitrogen per kilogram (35.0% against 21.2%). [B1]
:::

:::tip A practical point about ammonium fertilisers
Never mix an ammonium fertiliser with lime or any other alkali. The alkali displaces ammonia gas, which escapes into the air, and the nitrogen is lost. This is the same reaction as the test above.
:::

A fertiliser that is not taken up by crops is washed by rain into rivers and lakes, where the extra nutrients encourage the rapid growth of algae. The algae block the light and, when they die, bacteria that feed on them use up the oxygen in the water, so fish die. For this reason the amount of fertiliser applied has to be matched to what the crop needs.

## Oxides of nitrogen

Nitrogen forms several oxides, and two of them are pollutants. **Nitrogen monoxide**, NO, is a colourless gas. **Nitrogen dioxide**, \(\ce{NO2}\), is a brown, poisonous gas with a sharp smell. Together they are called \(\ce{NO_x}\) ("NO-x"). Their formation starts with the unreactive \(\ce{N2}\) molecule, and so it needs a source of a great deal of energy.

\[\ce{N2(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO(g)}\]

The reaction is endothermic, because the strong N≡N and O=O bonds must be broken. It does not happen at room temperature, but it does at very high temperatures. Once NO has formed and meets more oxygen in the air, it is oxidised further:

\[\ce{2NO(g) + O2(g) -> 2NO2(g)}\]

**Natural source.** Lightning. A bolt of lightning heats the air around it to a very high temperature for a moment, and some \(\ce{N2}\) and \(\ce{O2}\) react to form NO. Rain washes the oxides down to the soil, where they act as a natural fertiliser.

**Man-made source.** The internal combustion engines of cars and lorries. Burning fuel in the cylinder produces very high temperatures, and the nitrogen and oxygen of the air that is drawn in then react to form NO. The nitrogen comes from the air, not from the fuel. Power stations and other furnaces that burn fuel in air at high temperature do the same.

:::def Oxides of nitrogen (NOₓ)
NO and \(\ce{NO2}\), formed when nitrogen and oxygen combine at the high temperatures of lightning or of a combustion engine. NO is colourless; \(\ce{NO2}\) is brown and toxic.
:::

An engine makes three pollutants that you should be able to name. **Carbon monoxide**, CO, forms by incomplete combustion; it is toxic because it binds to haemoglobin in the blood. **Unburnt hydrocarbons** escape from the fuel. **Oxides of nitrogen** form from the nitrogen and oxygen of the air. (Carbon dioxide and, in diesels, soot are also released; see [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons).)

## Catalytic converters

A catalytic converter fitted to the exhaust of a car removes the three pollutants. Inside a steel box, a ceramic honeycomb is coated with a thin layer of the metals **platinum, palladium and rhodium**. The honeycomb gives the hot exhaust gases a very large surface area to flow over.

On the metal surface, the gas molecules stick (they are **adsorbed**), their bonds are weakened, they react and the products leave the surface. A catalyst in a different phase from the reactants is a **heterogeneous catalyst** (see [Reaction kinetics](#chem.kinetics); the A Level treatment is in [Rate equations and catalysis](#chem.rates2)). The main reactions are redox reactions in which carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons are oxidised and nitrogen oxides are reduced:

\[\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}\]
\[\ce{4CO + 2NO2 -> 4CO2 + N2}\]
\[\ce{C3H8 + 10NO -> 3CO2 + 4H2O + 5N2}\]

Carbon monoxide is oxidised (oxidation number of carbon +2 → +4) and nitrogen in NO is reduced (+2 → 0). Unburnt hydrocarbons are oxidised, in the same way, to carbon dioxide and water.

:::eg Write balanced equations for the removal from exhaust gases of (a) NO by CO, (b) \(\ce{NO2}\) by CO, and (c) NO by propane, \(\ce{C3H8}\). Give the changes in oxidation number of nitrogen and carbon in (a).
(a) \(\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}\) [B1]
(b) \(\ce{4CO + 2NO2 -> 4CO2 + N2}\) [B1]
(c) \(\ce{C3H8 + 10NO -> 3CO2 + 4H2O + 5N2}\) [B1]
In (a), nitrogen goes from +2 (in NO) to 0 (in \(\ce{N2}\)), so it is reduced. [B1]
Carbon goes from +2 (in CO) to +4 (in \(\ce{CO2}\)), so it is oxidised. [B1]
:::

Two limits on a converter are worth knowing. It does not work well until it is hot, so it is least effective in the first minutes of a journey. And lead in the fuel would coat the metal surface and poison the catalyst, so cars with converters must use lead-free petrol. A converter also turns each carbon monoxide molecule into carbon dioxide, so it does not reduce the amount of carbon dioxide released, but it replaces the toxic gas with a much less harmful one.

## Photochemical smog and PAN

Hydrocarbons and oxides of nitrogen released by exhausts do not stay unchanged in the air. In strong sunlight they react to form a mixture of new pollutants called **photochemical smog**. One of its components is **peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN)**, formed when NO and \(\ce{NO2}\) react with unburnt hydrocarbons. (You do not need to know its structure.)

:::def Photochemical smog
A haze in the air over some cities, formed when sunlight causes oxides of nitrogen and unburnt hydrocarbons from vehicle exhausts to react together, producing secondary pollutants such as PAN. It irritates the eyes and the lungs.
:::

PAN is a **secondary pollutant**: it is not emitted by anything directly, but forms in the air from **primary pollutants** (NO, \(\ce{NO2}\) and hydrocarbons) in the presence of sunlight. It is worst in hot, sunny weather in cities with heavy traffic and still air. Smog also contains ozone at ground level, which is harmful to breathe, formed from the same primary pollutants. A catalytic converter reduces smog by removing both the \(\ce{NO_x}\) and the hydrocarbons that PAN is made from.

## Acid rain

Rain water is naturally slightly acidic, with a pH of about 5.6, because carbon dioxide dissolves in it to form a weak acid. **Acid rain** is rain that is more acidic than this because strong acids from pollutants have dissolved in it.

:::def Acid rain
Rain with a pH lower than that of unpolluted rain (about 5.6), caused by acidic gases such as sulfur dioxide and oxides of nitrogen dissolving in the water droplets in the air and being oxidised to strong acids (sulfuric and nitric acid).
:::

Sulfur dioxide, \(\ce{SO2}\), is made when sulfur in coal and oil burns, and when sulfide ores are heated in air. In the air it is oxidised to sulfur trioxide, which dissolves in water to form sulfuric acid.

\[\ce{S + O2 -> SO2}\]
\[\ce{SO2 + 1/2 O2 -> SO3}\]
\[\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}\]

On its own the oxidation of \(\ce{SO2}\) by oxygen in the air is slow. **Oxides of nitrogen** make acid rain in two different ways.

**Directly.** Nitrogen dioxide dissolves in water to give a mixture of nitric acid and nitrous acid. It can also be fully oxidised in the presence of oxygen.

\[\ce{2NO2 + H2O -> HNO3 + HNO2}\]
\[\ce{4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4HNO3}\]

**As a catalyst for the oxidation of \(\ce{SO2}\).** Nitrogen dioxide oxidises sulfur dioxide, and is reduced to NO. NO is then oxidised back to \(\ce{NO2}\) by oxygen in the air.

\[\ce{SO2 + NO2 -> SO3 + NO}\]
\[\ce{NO + 1/2 O2 -> NO2}\]

Adding the two equations and cancelling the NO and \(\ce{NO2}\) gives \(\ce{SO2 + 1/2 O2 -> SO3}\), so the \(\ce{NO2}\) is regenerated. It speeds up the oxidation without being used up: it is a catalyst. The sulfur trioxide then dissolves in water droplets to form sulfuric acid.

\(\ce{SO2 + NO2 -> SO3 + NO}\), then \(\ce{NO + 1/2 O2 -> NO2}\) (the \(\ce{NO2}\) is regenerated), then \(\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}\). Overall: \(\ce{SO2 + 1/2 O2 -> SO3}\) with \(\ce{NO2}\) as the catalyst. Because everything is a gas, \(\ce{NO2}\) is a homogeneous catalyst.
:::

:::warn NO₂ is not "used up"
Students often say that \(\ce{NO2}\) is a reactant because it appears in the first equation. It is a catalyst because it is **regenerated** in the second step: the same amount is present at the end. Always write both equations and say that \(\ce{NO2}\) is reformed.
:::

:::eg Show, with equations, that nitrogen dioxide acts as a catalyst in the oxidation of sulfur dioxide in the atmosphere, and state what finally forms in rain.
Step 1: \(\ce{SO2 + NO2 -> SO3 + NO}\) [B1]
Step 2: \(\ce{NO + 1/2 O2 -> NO2}\) [B1]
Adding the steps and cancelling the intermediates gives \(\ce{SO2 + 1/2 O2 -> SO3}\), so \(\ce{NO2}\) is used in step 1 and reformed in step 2: it is a catalyst. [B1]
\(\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}\): sulfuric acid forms in the rain. [B1]
:::

:::eg Nitrogen dioxide is oxidised in air in the presence of water to nitric acid. Write the equation, and give the oxidation numbers of nitrogen in \(\ce{NO2}\), \(\ce{HNO3}\) and \(\ce{HNO2}\).
\(\ce{4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4HNO3}\) [B1]
Nitrogen is +4 in \(\ce{NO2}\), +5 in \(\ce{HNO3}\) and +3 in \(\ce{HNO2}\). [B1]
So in \(\ce{2NO2 + H2O -> HNO3 + HNO2}\) nitrogen is both oxidised (+4 → +5) and reduced (+4 → +3), a disproportionation. [B1]

Acid rain damages the environment in several ways: it dissolves limestone and marble buildings (\(\ce{CaCO3 + H2SO4 -> CaSO4 + H2O + CO2}\)), corrodes metal structures, harms trees and acidifies lakes and streams so that fish and other animals cannot live in them. Cutting the amount of \(\ce{SO2}\) and \(\ce{NO_x}\) released is the way to reduce it, and catalytic converters in cars deal with the \(\ce{NO_x}\).

## Sulfur and the Contact process

The syllabus topic title is "Nitrogen and sulfur", but sulfur appears only in connection with the oxides above, and with the Contact process, which makes the sulfuric acid used for fertilisers (ammonium sulfate), detergents, paints and many other products. It is described with the other equilibria in [Chemical equilibria](#chem.equilibria). In brief, sulfur is burned in air to make sulfur dioxide, which is oxidised to sulfur trioxide in the reversible reaction

\[\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}\]

using a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst at about 400–450 °C and 1–2 atm. The sulfur trioxide is dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid (not water, which would react too violently) to make oleum, \(\ce{H2S2O7}\), and the oleum is then diluted to the required strength of sulfuric acid.

\[\ce{SO3 + H2SO4 -> H2S2O7}\]
\[\ce{H2S2O7 + H2O -> 2H2SO4}\]

:::eg (Exam style) Sulfur is converted into sulfuric acid in a series of steps. Calculate the maximum mass of sulfuric acid that can be made from 100 g of sulfur, assuming that all of the sulfur ends up as sulfuric acid.
Overall, each atom of S becomes one molecule of \(\ce{H2SO4}\), so 1 mol of S gives 1 mol of \(\ce{H2SO4}\). [B1]
\(n(\mathrm{S}) = \dfrac{100}{32.1} = 3.115\) mol [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{H2SO4}) = 2(1.0) + 32.1 + 4(16.0) = 98.1\) [B1]
\[m(\ce{H2SO4}) = 3.115 \times 98.1 = 306\,\mathrm{g}\] [A1]
:::

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [3 marks] ★ Explain why nitrogen is unreactive although oxygen is reactive. Use the bond energies N≡N 944 kJ mol⁻¹ and O=O 496 kJ mol⁻¹.
A: The N≡N triple bond is stronger than the O=O double bond. [B1]
So much more energy is needed to break the bond in \(\ce{N2}\), and the activation energy of its reactions is much higher. [B1]
\(\ce{N2}\) is also non-polar, so there is no partial charge to attract attacking species. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ Explain, with an equation, how ammonia reacts as a base with hydrogen chloride, and name the type of bond that forms.
A: \(\ce{NH3 + HCl -> NH4Cl}\) [B1]
The lone pair on the nitrogen atom of ammonia accepts a proton (it takes \(\ce{H+}\) from HCl), so ammonia is a Brønsted–Lowry base. [B1]
The new N–H bond is a coordinate (dative covalent) bond. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.practical) A white solid, X, dissolves in water. When aqueous sodium hydroxide is added and the mixture is warmed, a gas is given off that turns damp red litmus paper blue. (a) Name the gas. (b) Identify the ion in X that releases it. (c) Write an ionic equation. (d) Explain why this is an acid–base reaction.
A: (a) Ammonia, \(\ce{NH3}\). [B1]
(b) The ammonium ion, \(\ce{NH4+}\). [B1]
(c) \(\ce{NH4+ + OH- -> NH3 + H2O}\) [B1]
(d) The \(\ce{NH4+}\) ion gives a proton to \(\ce{OH-}\), so \(\ce{NH4+}\) is the acid and \(\ce{OH-}\) is the base. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Calculate the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium nitrate, \(\ce{NH4NO3}\), and in ammonium sulfate, \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\), and say which gives more nitrogen per kilogram of fertiliser.
H: Each formula unit of \(\ce{NH4NO3}\) contains two nitrogen atoms, and so does \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\).
A: \(M_r(\ce{NH4NO3}) = 80.0\) [B1] and \(\%\,\mathrm{N} = 28.0/80.0 \times 100 = 35.0\,\%\) [A1]
\(M_r(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}) = 132.1\) [B1] and \(\%\,\mathrm{N} = 28.0/132.1 \times 100 = 21.2\,\%\) [A1]
Ammonium nitrate gives more nitrogen per kilogram. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Explain why oxides of nitrogen form in a petrol engine but not when petrol burns in a cold open dish. Give two equations.
H: Think about the energy needed to break the N≡N bond.
A: In the cylinder the temperature is very high, which gives \(\ce{N2}\) molecules enough energy to break the strong N≡N bond and react with \(\ce{O2}\); a cold flame does not. [B1] [B1]
\(\ce{N2 + O2 -> 2NO}\) [B1]
\(\ce{2NO + O2 -> 2NO2}\) [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ State what a catalytic converter contains, and use equations to show how it removes (a) CO and NO and (b) unburnt hydrocarbons, using \(\ce{C3H8}\) as an example. Name the type of catalysis.
A: A honeycomb coated with platinum, palladium and rhodium. [B1]
\(\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}\) [B1]
\(\ce{C3H8 + 10NO -> 3CO2 + 4H2O + 5N2}\) [B1]
The catalyst is a solid and the reactants are gases, so this is heterogeneous catalysis. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ Explain how nitrogen oxides contribute to acid rain (a) directly and (b) by catalysis of the oxidation of \(\ce{SO2}\), with equations.
H: In (b), write two equations: one in which \(\ce{NO2}\) oxidises \(\ce{SO2}\), then one that remakes \(\ce{NO2}\).
A: (a) \(\ce{4NO2 + O2 + 2H2O -> 4HNO3}\), so nitric acid forms in the rain. [B1]
(b) \(\ce{SO2 + NO2 -> SO3 + NO}\) [B1]
\(\ce{NO + 1/2 O2 -> NO2}\) [B1]
\(\ce{NO2}\) is regenerated, so it is a catalyst. [B1]
\(\ce{SO3 + H2O -> H2SO4}\) forms sulfuric acid in the rain. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (#chem.org.hydrocarbons) Describe how PAN forms in the atmosphere, why the problem is worst in sunny, crowded cities, and how a catalytic converter reduces it.
H: PAN is a secondary pollutant. Name the primary pollutants it forms from.
A: PAN forms when NO and \(\ce{NO2}\) from engines react with unburnt hydrocarbons. [B1]
The reaction needs sunlight (it is a photochemical reaction), so it is most serious in bright weather. [B1]
Heavy traffic gives high concentrations of the primary pollutants, and still air stops them dispersing. [B1]
PAN is a secondary pollutant and forms part of photochemical smog, which irritates the eyes and lungs. [B1]
A converter removes NO by reaction with CO, e.g. \(\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}\). [B1]
It also oxidises unburnt hydrocarbons to \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\), so less of both raw materials of PAN reaches the air. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Use the bond energies N≡N 944, H–H 436 and N–H 390 kJ mol⁻¹ to estimate ΔH for the formation of 1 mol of ammonia, \(\ce{1/2 N2 + 3/2 H2 -> NH3}\). Why does the Haber process still need a high temperature although the reaction is exothermic?
A: Bonds broken: \(\tfrac12(944) + \tfrac32(436) = 472 + 654 = 1126\) kJ. [M1]
Bonds made: \(3 \times 390 = 1170\) kJ. [M1]
\(\Delta H = 1126 - 1170 = -44\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\) [A1]
The strong N≡N bond gives the reaction a high activation energy, so a high temperature is needed for a fast enough rate even though the position of equilibrium favours ammonia at low temperature. [B1]

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ (#chem.org.halogen) Ammonia reacts with hydrogen chloride and also with bromoethane, \(\ce{CH3CH2Br}\). Explain, with the same feature of the ammonia molecule, why it behaves as a base in the first reaction and as a nucleophile in the second. State the organic product of the second reaction when ammonia is in excess.
H: Both reactions use the lone pair, but they give it to different atoms.
A: In the reaction with HCl the lone pair on nitrogen forms a bond to \(\ce{H+}\): ammonia accepts a proton, so it is a base. [B1]
In the reaction with bromoethane the lone pair on nitrogen forms a bond to the δ+ carbon of the C–Br bond: ammonia donates a pair of electrons to carbon, so it is a nucleophile. [B1]
Both are the same event, an electron pair on nitrogen used to make a new bond; they differ in the atom attacked (H or C). [B1]
Excess ammonia in ethanol, heated under pressure, gives ethylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2NH2}\). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ (#chem.moles) 1.70 kg of ammonia reacted with excess sulfuric acid and gave 5.80 kg of ammonium sulfate. Calculate the percentage yield.
H: Write the equation first. How many moles of \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\) does 1 mol of \(\ce{NH3}\) give?
A: \(2\ce{NH3} + \ce{H2SO4} \rightarrow \ce{(NH4)2SO4}\), so 2 mol \(\ce{NH3}\) gives 1 mol \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\). [B1]
\(n(\ce{NH3}) = \dfrac{1700}{17.0} = 100\) mol, so the maximum is 50.0 mol of \(\ce{(NH4)2SO4}\). [M1]
Maximum mass \(= 50.0 \times 132.1 = 6605\) g. [M1]
\[\text{yield} = \frac{5800}{6605} \times 100 = 87.8\,\%\] [A1]

:::recap
- N₂ is unreactive: N≡N 944 kJ mol⁻¹ and non-polar (not "because it is a gas").
- NH₃ is a Brønsted–Lowry base (lone pair accepts H⁺): NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl. NH₄⁺ is tetrahedral, 109.5°, with one dative N–H bond (all four bonds identical).
- Ammonium salt + NaOH, warm: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃ + H₂O. NH₃ turns damp red litmus blue.
- Ammonia is made by the Haber process and neutralised to ammonium nitrate (35.0% N) and ammonium sulfate (21.2% N).
- NO forms in lightning and hot engines: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO; 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂. NO is colourless, NO₂ brown.
- Catalytic converter (Pt, Pd, Rh, heterogeneous): 2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂; CO, NOₓ and hydrocarbons removed.
- NO and NO₂ + unburnt hydrocarbons in sunlight give PAN, a secondary pollutant in photochemical smog.
- Acid rain: SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄; NO₂ forms HNO₃ (4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃; 2NO₂ + H₂O → HNO₃ + HNO₂); NO₂ catalyses SO₂ + ½O₂ → SO₃ (SO₂ + NO₂ → SO₃ + NO; NO + ½O₂ → NO₂).
- Sulfur: Contact process makes H₂SO₄ from SO₂ (V₂O₅, 400–450 °C, 1–2 atm).
:::
