## What is an alcohol?

An alcohol is an organic compound in which a hydrogen atom of a hydrocarbon has been replaced by a **hydroxyl group**, –OH, attached to a carbon atom that has only single bonds.

:::def Alcohol
A compound containing the hydroxyl functional group, –OH, bonded to a saturated carbon atom. With one OH group the general formula is \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}\), written R–OH.
:::

To name an alcohol, find the longest carbon chain that contains the carbon bonded to the OH group, replace the final "-e" of the alkane with "-ol" and add the position of the OH: \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}\) is propan-1-ol and \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}\) is propan-2-ol. Number from the end that gives the OH group the lower number. A molecule with two or more OH groups is named with "-diol" or "-triol", keeping the "e" before the "d" or "t" and numbering every OH: ethane-1,2-diol, \(\ce{CH2(OH)CH2OH}\), is the antifreeze in car radiators, and propane-1,2,3-triol (glycerol) has OH on every carbon.

### Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols

How an alcohol reacts depends on how many carbon atoms are joined to the carbon that carries the OH group.

:::def Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols
In a primary (1°) alcohol the carbon bonded to the OH group is joined to one other carbon atom (or none, as in methanol); in a secondary (2°) alcohol it is joined to two other carbon atoms; in a tertiary (3°) alcohol it is joined to three other carbon atoms.
:::

Three of the isomers of \(\ce{C4H9OH}\) below show one of each type, and a fourth molecule has two OH groups. In skeletal formulas, each corner or line end is a carbon atom.

:::warn Branched does not mean tertiary
2-methylpropan-1-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2OH}\), has a branch, but it is a **primary** alcohol: the carbon bonded to OH is joined to only one other carbon. Always look at the carbon that carries the OH group.
:::

When a molecule has more than one OH group, classify each OH separately. Propane-1,2-diol has a primary OH on carbon 1 and a secondary OH on carbon 2. Propane-1,2,3-triol has two primary OH groups and one secondary.

:::eg Classify every OH group in (a) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3}\), (b) \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH2OH}\) and (c) \(\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3}\). Name each compound.
(a) The OH is on carbon 2 of a four-carbon chain: butan-2-ol. That carbon is joined to carbons 1 and 3, so it is secondary. [B1]

(b) Number from the end that gives the OH groups the lower numbers: \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH2OH}\) is butane-1,3-diol. The OH on carbon 1 sits on a carbon joined to one other carbon: primary. The OH on carbon 3 sits on a carbon joined to two other carbons (carbons 2 and 4): secondary. [B1]

(c) The longest chain through the carbon that holds the OH has four carbons: \(\ce{CH3-C(OH)(CH3)-CH2-CH3}\), butane with the OH and a methyl branch both on carbon 2: 2-methylbutan-2-ol. That carbon is joined to three other carbons (two methyl groups and the ethyl group), so it is tertiary. [B1]
:::

## Hydrogen bonding, boiling points and solubility

The O–H bond is very polar (O 3.5, H 2.1), so the hydrogen atom is strongly \(\delta+\) and the oxygen atom has two lone pairs. An alcohol molecule can therefore form **hydrogen bonds** to other alcohol molecules and to water molecules (see [Shapes of molecules and intermolecular forces](#chem.shapes)).

The effect on boiling points is large. Compare alcohols with alkanes of similar relative molecular mass:

| Compound | Formula | \(M_r\) | Boiling point / °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| ethane | \(\ce{CH3CH3}\) | 30.0 | −89 |
| methanol | \(\ce{CH3OH}\) | 32.0 | 65 |
| propane | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH3}\) | 44.0 | −42 |
| ethanol | \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) | 46.0 | 78 |
| propan-1-ol | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}\) | 60.0 | 97 |
| butan-1-ol | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}\) | 74.0 | 118 |

Propane (44.0) and ethanol (46.0) have almost the same \(M_r\) and number of electrons, so their id–id forces are similar. Ethanol boils 120 °C higher, and the difference is the hydrogen bonding that only the alcohol has. Within the alcohols, the boiling point rises with chain length because the id–id forces between the longer hydrocarbon parts get stronger.

:::exam Explaining boiling points
Name the forces on both sides: "ethanol has hydrogen bonds between its molecules in addition to id–id forces; propane has only id–id forces. Hydrogen bonds are stronger, so more energy is needed to separate ethanol molecules." Saying "ethanol has stronger bonds" without naming the type of force scores nothing.
:::

**Solubility in water.** Methanol, ethanol and the propanols mix with water in all proportions, because their OH groups form hydrogen bonds with water molecules that are as strong as those they break. As the carbon chain gets longer, the non-polar hydrocarbon part gets bigger and cannot form hydrogen bonds, so solubility falls: butan-1-ol is only partly soluble in water and longer alcohols hardly dissolve. Ethanol is a good solvent for the same reason: it dissolves polar and many non-polar substances.

## Making alcohols

There are six routes to learn. The same table also helps you in synthesis questions, because every route starts from a different functional group.

| Route | Starting compound | Reagent and conditions |
|---|---|---|
| (a) hydration | alkene | steam, \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst, high temperature and pressure |
| (b) oxidation | alkene | cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) (gives a diol) |
| (c) substitution | halogenoalkane | NaOH(aq), heat under reflux |
| (d) reduction | aldehyde or ketone | \(\ce{NaBH4}\) (in water or ethanol) or \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) (in dry ether) |
| (e) reduction | carboxylic acid | \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether |
| (f) hydrolysis | ester | dilute acid (or dilute alkali), heat under reflux |

Examples of each:

\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O(g) <=>[H3PO4] CH3CH2OH}\]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + [O] + H2O -> CH2(OH)CH2(OH)}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH- -> CH3CH2OH + Br-}\]
\[\ce{CH3CHO + 2[H] -> CH3CH2OH}\]
\[\ce{CH3COOH + 4[H] -> CH3CH2OH + H2O}\]
\[\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O <=>[H+] CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH}\]

Routes (a) to (c) were met in [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons) and [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen). Routes (d) and (e) are **reduction**, shown with [H] (hydrogen supplied by the reducing agent): an aldehyde gives a primary alcohol and a ketone a secondary one (see [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl)). Sodium tetrahydridoborate, \(\ce{NaBH4}\), is a milder reducing agent that reduces aldehydes and ketones but not carboxylic acids. Lithium tetrahydridoaluminate, \(\ce{LiAlH4}\), is more powerful and reduces carboxylic acids as well; it reacts violently with water, so it is used in dry ether.

Route (f) is the reverse of esterification, covered below. With dilute alkali the other product is the salt of the acid, not the free acid, and the reaction goes to completion:

\[\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3 + NaOH -> CH3COONa + CH3CH2OH}\]

:::eg Ethanol is made (a) from ethene by hydration and (b) by fermentation of glucose, \(\ce{C6H12O6 -> 2C2H5OH + 2CO2}\). Calculate the maximum mass of ethanol from 1.00 kg of glucose, and compare the two methods.
\(M_r(\ce{C6H12O6}) = 6(12.0) + 12(1.0) + 6(16.0) = 180.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 2(12.0) + 6(1.0) + 16.0 = 46.0\). [B1]
\[n(\ce{C6H12O6}) = \frac{1000}{180.0} = 5.556\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[n(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 2 \times 5.556 = 11.11\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[m(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 11.11 \times 46.0 = 511\,\mathrm{g}\] [A1]

Just over half the mass of glucose ends up as ethanol, because the rest leaves as carbon dioxide.

Comparison of the two methods:

| | hydration of ethene | fermentation |
|---|---|---|
| raw material | ethene from crude oil (not renewable) | sugars from plants (renewable) |
| type of process | continuous | batch (one vessel at a time) |
| rate | fast | slow (days) |
| product | pure ethanol, very little waste | dilute ethanol, which must be distilled |
| use | industrial solvent and chemical feedstock | alcoholic drinks and bioethanol fuel |

Hydration is reversible, so the equilibrium position matters (see [Equilibria](#chem.equilibria)): the reaction produces one product from two gases, so a high pressure favours it, and unreacted ethene is recycled.
:::

## Reactions of alcohols

### Combustion

Alcohols burn in plenty of air to give carbon dioxide and water, and give out a lot of energy:

\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + 3O2 -> 2CO2 + 3H2O}\]

Ethanol produced by fermentation can be blended with petrol as a fuel. Burning it releases carbon dioxide, but the carbon was taken from the air by the plants that made the sugar, so it is often called carbon-neutral on a short timescale. (This ignores the energy used to grow, harvest and distil the crop.)

### Substitution: alcohols to halogenoalkanes

The OH group can be replaced by a halogen. You met these reactions in [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen):

:::key Alcohol to halogenoalkane
| Reagent and conditions | Equation (ethanol) |
|---|---|
| \(\ce{HX(g)}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2OH + HBr -> CH3CH2Br + H2O}\) |
| KCl with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) or \(\ce{H3PO4}\) | the acid makes \(\ce{HCl}\), which then reacts as above |
| \(\ce{PCl3}\) and heat | \(\ce{3CH3CH2OH + PCl3 -> 3CH3CH2Cl + H3PO3}\) |
| \(\ce{PCl5}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2OH + PCl5 -> CH3CH2Cl + POCl3 + HCl}\) |
| \(\ce{SOCl2}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2OH + SOCl2 -> CH3CH2Cl + SO2 + HCl}\) |
:::

\(\ce{PCl5}\) is a useful test for an OH group (misty acidic fumes of hydrogen chloride appear), but it also reacts with water, so the sample must be dry.

### Reaction with sodium

Sodium metal reacts with an alcohol, more slowly than with water. Bubbles of hydrogen are given off, the sodium dissolves and a solution of the sodium alkoxide forms. When the solution is evaporated, white solid sodium ethoxide is left.

\[\ce{2CH3CH2OH + 2Na -> 2CH3CH2ONa + H2}\]

The reaction is the same as the reaction of sodium with water, \(\ce{2H2O + 2Na -> 2NaOH + H2}\), with the ethoxide ion \(\ce{CH3CH2O-}\) in place of the hydroxide ion. The O–H bond breaks and the hydrogen leaves as \(\ce{H+}\), which is reduced by sodium to hydrogen gas, so the alcohol is behaving as an acid. The test for hydrogen is a lighted splint, which "pops".

### Oxidation

Alcohols are oxidised by acidified potassium dichromate(VI), \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), or acidified potassium manganate(VII), \(\ce{KMnO4}\). The dichromate ion changes colour as it is reduced:

\[\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\ \text{(orange)} \longrightarrow \ce{Cr^3+}\ \text{(green)}\]

The \(\ce{MnO4-}\) ion goes from purple to colourless (\(\ce{Mn^2+}\) is very pale pink). The result depends on the class of alcohol:

:::key Oxidation of alcohols
| Alcohol | Product with the oxidising agent | What the oxidant does to the alcohol |
|---|---|---|
| primary (1°) | aldehyde, then carboxylic acid | lose H from O–H and from the C–OH carbon; the aldehyde is oxidised further |
| secondary (2°) | ketone | lose H from O–H and from the C–OH carbon; the ketone is not oxidised further |
| tertiary (3°) | no reaction | no H on the C–OH carbon, so the orange colour stays |
:::

Using [O] for the oxygen supplied by the oxidising agent:

\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CHO + H2O}\]
\[\ce{CH3CHO + [O] -> CH3COOH}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] -> CH3COCH3 + H2O}\]

Water is the by-product. Hydrogen gas is **not** a product: the hydrogen atoms are removed by the oxidising agent and form water.

#### Distillation or reflux: how to stop at the aldehyde, or go on to the acid

A primary alcohol can be oxidised in two stages, and you control the stage by how you heat the mixture.

:::cols
:::col To get the aldehyde: distil
Warm the alcohol with the acidified oxidising agent and **distil off the aldehyde as it forms**. The aldehyde cannot form hydrogen bonds between its molecules, so it boils at a much lower temperature than the alcohol (propanal 49 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C). It leaves the mixture before the oxidant can react with it again. Use excess alcohol.
:::
:::col To get the carboxylic acid: reflux
Heat the alcohol under **reflux** with an **excess** of acidified oxidising agent. Any aldehyde that evaporates condenses and drips back into the flask, where it stays until it is oxidised to the acid (propanoic acid, 141 °C). The acid can be distilled off afterwards.
:::
:::

:::eg The alcohol propan-1-ol is warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). (a) Name the product and give the equation if the product is distilled out as it forms. (b) Name the product and the observation if the mixture is heated under reflux with excess dichromate. (c) Write the balanced ionic equation for (a).
(a) The product is propanal, \(\ce{CH3CH2CHO}\). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CH2CHO + H2O}\] [B1]

(b) The aldehyde cannot escape and is oxidised further, so the product is propanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH2COOH}\). [B1] The orange mixture turns green as \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) is reduced to \(\ce{Cr^3+}\). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3CH2COOH + H2O}\]

Balance the organic one like the inorganic ones: C and O are already equal; add \(\ce{2H+}\) to balance hydrogen, then \(\ce{2e-}\) to balance the charge. The second is multiplied by 3 so that both involve 6 electrons. [M1]
\[\ce{3CH3CH2CH2OH + Cr2O7^2- + 8H+ -> 3CH3CH2CHO + 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}\] [A1]

(The \(\ce{H+}\) cancel: \(14 - 6 = 8\) on the left.)
:::

:::eg Acidified potassium manganate(VII) also oxidises ethanol to ethanal. Write the balanced ionic equation, using \(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5e- -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O}\) and \(\ce{CH3CH2OH -> CH3CHO + 2H+ + 2e-}\), and state the colour change.
The half-equations need the same number of electrons, so multiply the manganate half-equation by 2 (10 electrons) and the ethanol half-equation by 5 (10 electrons). [M1]
\[\ce{2MnO4- + 16H+ + 5CH3CH2OH -> 2Mn^2+ + 8H2O + 5CH3CHO + 10H+}\]
Cancel 10 of the 16 \(\ce{H+}\) ions:
\[\ce{2MnO4- + 6H+ + 5CH3CH2OH -> 2Mn^2+ + 8H2O + 5CH3CHO}\] [A1]
The purple solution becomes colourless (very pale pink) as \(\ce{MnO4-}\) is reduced to \(\ce{Mn^2+}\). [B1]

Distillation gives the **aldehyde**; reflux with excess oxidant gives the **acid**. If the question says "collected as it formed" it wants distillation. If it says "excess oxidising agent, heated for some time" it wants reflux and the carboxylic acid. Examiners also look for the condenser water going in at the **bottom** and out at the **top**.
:::

#### Tertiary alcohols are not oxidised

A tertiary alcohol has no hydrogen atom on the carbon that holds the OH group. The oxidising agent normally removes that hydrogen (along with the O–H hydrogen), so under the conditions of the exam nothing happens: the orange dichromate stays orange. Do not write "tertiary alcohols are oxidised slowly"; the answer is "no reaction".

This gives a simple test. Warm the alcohol with acidified dichromate(VI): an orange to green change means 1° or 2°; no change means 3°.

:::tip Telling aldehyde from ketone
Both primary and secondary alcohols turn dichromate green. To tell which you had, test the oxidation product with Tollens' or Fehling's reagent (an aldehyde reacts and a ketone does not; see [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl)).
:::

### Dehydration

Heating an alcohol with concentrated sulfuric acid (or with phosphoric acid), or passing its vapour over heated aluminium oxide, removes the OH group and an H atom from a neighbouring carbon as a molecule of water. A double bond forms: an **elimination** reaction giving an alkene.

\[\ce{CH3CH2OH ->[H2SO4][heat] CH2=CH2 + H2O}\]

For ethanol with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), the temperature is about 170 °C. Tertiary alcohols dehydrate most easily. When the OH carbon has more than one different neighbouring carbon with hydrogen atoms, more than one alkene can form, as with halogenoalkanes (see [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen)).

:::lab Dehydrating ethanol with aluminium oxide
A little ethanol is soaked up on mineral wool at one end of a hard-glass test tube; lumps of aluminium oxide are placed further along the tube. Heat the aluminium oxide strongly and warm the ethanol so its vapour passes over the hot catalyst. The gas formed is collected over water. Always **take the delivery tube out of the water before you stop heating**, or the water will suck back into the hot tube and crack it.
:::

:::eg Butan-2-ol is heated with concentrated sulfuric acid. Name the alkenes that can form.
The OH is on carbon 2. An H atom can be removed from carbon 1 (a \(\ce{CH3}\) group) or from carbon 3 (a \(\ce{CH2}\) group).

Removing H from carbon 1 gives a double bond between carbons 1 and 2: but-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}\). [B1]

Removing H from carbon 3 gives a double bond between carbons 2 and 3: but-2-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}\). [B1]

Each carbon of the double bond in but-2-ene holds two different groups (H and \(\ce{CH3}\)), so it exists as cis and trans isomers (see [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism)). [B1]

There are three alkene products: but-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene.
:::

### Esterification

An alcohol reacts with a carboxylic acid, with concentrated sulfuric acid as a catalyst and heat under reflux, to give an **ester** and water. This is the reaction of ethanol you must know.

\[\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH <=>[H2SO4][heat] CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}\]

The ester is ethyl ethanoate: the name starts with the alkyl group of the **alcohol** (ethyl) and ends with the name of the **acid** (ethanoate). Esters have fruity smells; ethyl ethanoate is used as a solvent in nail-varnish remover and glues.

The reaction is reversible and reaches equilibrium with a mixture of all four substances, so the yield is never 100%. The concentrated sulfuric acid is the catalyst, and it also absorbs water, which pushes the equilibrium towards the ester (Le Chatelier). Using an excess of one reactant also raises the yield. More about esters is in [Carboxylic acids, esters and nitriles](#chem.org.acids).

:::eg (Exam style) 7.20 g of propan-1-ol is heated under reflux with excess ethanoic acid and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid. 9.10 g of propyl ethanoate is collected. Calculate the percentage yield.
\[\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2CH2OH <=> CH3COOCH2CH2CH3 + H2O}\] (1 mol of alcohol gives 1 mol of ester) [B1]

\(M_r(\ce{C3H7OH}) = 3(12.0) + 8(1.0) + 16.0 = 60.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{CH3COOC3H7}) = 5(12.0) + 10(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 102.0\). [B1]
\[n(\text{propan-1-ol}) = \frac{7.20}{60.0} = 0.1200\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[\text{maximum mass of ester} = 0.1200 \times 102.0 = 12.24\,\mathrm{g}\] [M1]
\[\text{percentage yield} = \frac{9.10}{12.24} \times 100 = 74.3\,\%\] [A1]

The yield is below 100% because the reaction is an equilibrium, and because some ester is lost in purification.
:::

:::eg Give the reagents and conditions to convert propan-1-ol into (a) 1-chloropropane, (b) propene, (c) propyl ethanoate, and write the organic product of its reaction with sodium.
(a) \(\ce{PCl5}\) at room temperature (or \(\ce{SOCl2}\), or \(\ce{PCl3}\) with heat, or KCl with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\)): \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + PCl5 -> CH3CH2CH2Cl + POCl3 + HCl}\). [B1]

(b) Concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) and heat, or the vapour passed over heated \(\ce{Al2O3}\): dehydration to \(\ce{CH3CH=CH2}\). [B1]

(c) Ethanoic acid and a few drops of concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), heated under reflux. [B1]

With sodium, the product is sodium propoxide, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2ONa}\), with hydrogen gas. [B1]
:::

## Tests for alcohols

**Classifying by oxidation.** Warm the alcohol with acidified dichromate(VI): 1° and 2° alcohols turn the orange solution green; a 3° alcohol does not. This is how a breathalyser worked in its first form: breath passed over orange dichromate crystals, and ethanol vapour turned them green. Modern breath testers use a fuel cell or infrared absorption instead.

**The tri-iodomethane (iodoform) test.** Some alcohols contain the group \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\), a methyl group and an H atom on the carbon with the OH. Alkaline aqueous iodine oxidises them to a compound with the \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) group, then replaces the three hydrogen atoms of the methyl group by iodine atoms and splits the molecule. The products are a pale **yellow precipitate of tri-iodomethane**, \(\ce{CHI3}\), and the salt of a carboxylic acid, \(\ce{RCO2-}\).

\[\ce{CH3CH(OH)R + 4I2 + 6OH- -> CHI3 + RCOO- + 5I- + 5H2O}\]

:::lab The tri-iodomethane test
Add a few drops of the alcohol to a test tube with aqueous iodine, then add sodium hydroxide solution drop by drop until the brown colour of the iodine fades to pale yellow. Warm gently in a water bath. A pale yellow precipitate of \(\ce{CHI3}\), which has a medicinal ("antiseptic") smell, is a positive result. Do not smell the tube directly; waft the vapour towards you.
:::

Which alcohols give a positive result? Only those with \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\), and so only **ethanol** (the one primary alcohol, where R is H) and **secondary alcohols with a methyl group on the OH carbon**, such as propan-2-ol and butan-2-ol:

| Alcohol | Has \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\)? | Yellow precipitate? |
|---|---|---|
| ethanol, \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) | yes (R = H) | yes |
| propan-1-ol | no | no |
| propan-2-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}\) | yes | yes |
| butan-2-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}\) | yes | yes |
| 2-methylpropan-2-ol | no (no H on the C–OH carbon) | no |

For ethanol, the equation is \(\ce{CH3CH2OH + 4I2 + 6OH- -> CHI3 + HCOO- + 5I- + 5H2O}\), with methanoate as the \(\ce{RCOO-}\). The same test also detects the \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) group in aldehydes and ketones (ethanal and propanone give it; see [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl)).

:::warn Not every secondary alcohol gives the test
Pentan-3-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH3}\), is a secondary alcohol, but there is no methyl group next to the C–OH carbon, so it gives no yellow precipitate. The group \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\) must be present.
:::

:::eg Four isomers have the formula \(\ce{C4H9OH}\). (a) Draw (describe) all four and classify them. (b) Which gives a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine?
(a) Butan-1-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}\), primary; butan-2-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}\), secondary; 2-methylpropan-1-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2OH}\), primary; 2-methylpropan-2-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)3COH}\), tertiary. [B1]

(b) Look for \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\). Only butan-2-ol has it: the OH carbon carries a methyl group, a hydrogen and an ethyl group. [B1] The primary alcohols and the tertiary alcohol give no precipitate. [B1]

Butan-2-ol gives \(\ce{CHI3}\) and propanoate: \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3 + 4I2 + 6OH- -> CHI3 + CH3CH2COO- + 5I- + 5H2O}\).
:::

:::eg Three isomeric alcohols, X, Y and Z, are butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol, in an unknown order. Describe tests that identify each, with the observations.
Test 1: warm a sample of each with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). [B1] X gives no colour change (the solution stays orange), so X is 2-methylpropan-2-ol (tertiary). [B1] Y and Z both turn green. [B1]

Test 2: add alkaline aqueous iodine and warm each of the other two. Y gives a pale yellow precipitate of \(\ce{CHI3}\), so Y is butan-2-ol, and Z gives no precipitate, so Z is butan-1-ol. [B1]
:::

## Why alcohols are weaker acids than water

Alcohols react with sodium like water does, so they can lose \(\ce{H+}\). They are not acidic enough to react with sodium hydroxide solution or to turn litmus red, and they are **weaker acids than water**: sodium reacts with an alcohol more slowly than with water.

Whether an \(\ce{O-H}\) group gives up \(\ce{H+}\) depends on how stable the anion left behind is. Water leaves \(\ce{OH-}\); ethanol leaves the ethoxide ion \(\ce{CH3CH2O-}\). In the ethoxide ion the alkyl group is attached to the oxygen that carries the negative charge. The alkyl group pushes electron density towards the oxygen (the inductive effect you met with carbocations), which **increases** the negative charge on oxygen and makes the ion less stable. An ion that is less stable is harder to make, so the alcohol loses \(\ce{H+}\) less readily than water does.

:::key Acidity of alcohols compared with water
Alcohols are weaker acids than water. The electron-donating alkyl group makes the alkoxide ion, \(\ce{RO-}\), less stable than \(\ce{OH-}\) (the negative charge on oxygen is intensified), so the alcohol loses \(\ce{H+}\) less easily and sodium reacts less vigorously with it.
:::

Phenol, in which the OH group is on a benzene ring, behaves very differently: it is a stronger acid than water (see [Phenol and the acidity of organic compounds](#chem.org.phenol)). A carboxylic acid is stronger still.

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why ethanol is a weaker acid than water. [3 marks]
When ethanol acts as an acid it loses \(\ce{H+}\) and forms the ethoxide ion, \(\ce{CH3CH2O-}\). [B1]
The ethyl group is electron-donating (positive inductive effect) and pushes electron density towards the oxygen atom. [B1]
This increases the charge density on the oxygen, which makes the ethoxide ion less stable than \(\ce{OH-}\), so ethanol loses \(\ce{H+}\) less readily than water. [B1]
:::

## Putting it together: a route and a mistake to avoid

:::eg (Real context) Vinegar is a dilute solution of ethanoic acid. A chemist has only ethene and wants ethanoic acid. Give a two-step route, with reagents and conditions, and an equation for each step.
Step 1: hydrate the ethene, using steam with a \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst at high temperature and pressure, to make ethanol. [B1]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O(g) <=> CH3CH2OH}\]
Step 2: oxidise the ethanol by heating under reflux with an excess of acidified potassium dichromate(VI). [B1] Reflux, not distillation, is needed so that the ethanal formed first is oxidised on to ethanoic acid. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3COOH + H2O}\]
:::

:::eg Spot the error: describe how to prepare a sample of ethanal from ethanol, and state the observation. [3 marks]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Reagent: acidified potassium dichromate(VI).
Line 2: Method: heat under reflux with excess acidified dichromate.
Line 3: Observation: the orange solution turns green.

Line 1 is correct [B1]. **Line 2 is wrong: reflux with an excess of oxidising agent oxidises the ethanal that forms on to ethanoic acid, so the aldehyde is never collected. The method mark is lost [B0].** Line 3 is correct [B1].

Correct line 2: warm the ethanol with the oxidising agent and distil off the ethanal as it forms (it boils at a much lower temperature than ethanol), using excess ethanol. [B1]

The student scores 2 out of 3. The choice of distillation or reflux decides whether you collect the aldehyde or the acid.
:::

Q: Name and classify (a) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}\), (b) \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}\), (c) \(\ce{(CH3)3COH}\).
A: (a) butan-1-ol, primary. (b) butan-2-ol, secondary. (c) 2-methylpropan-2-ol, tertiary.
Q: Why is ethanol (b.p. 78 °C) a liquid at room temperature but propane (b.p. −42 °C), which has a similar \(M_r\), a gas?
A: Ethanol molecules form hydrogen bonds between the O–H groups as well as id–id forces. Propane has only id–id forces, so far less energy is needed to separate its molecules.
Q: Write the equation for the reaction of ethanol with sodium and say what you would see.
A: \(\ce{2CH3CH2OH + 2Na -> 2CH3CH2ONa + H2}\). The sodium fizzes (hydrogen is given off) and dissolves, more slowly than in water.
Q: Give the reagent and conditions to convert propan-2-ol into propanone, and the observation.
A: Warm with acidified potassium dichromate(VI); orange to green.
Q: What do you obtain if propan-1-ol is (a) distilled and (b) refluxed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI)? Why do they differ?
A: (a) propanal. (b) propanoic acid. In distillation the aldehyde leaves the mixture as it forms and cannot be oxidised again; in reflux it is returned to the flask and oxidised to the acid.
Q: Write the equation for the reaction between ethanol and ethanoic acid. Name the ester and the catalyst.
A: \(\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH <=> CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}\). Ethyl ethanoate. Concentrated sulfuric acid.
Q: Which of propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol gives a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine? Explain.
A: Propan-2-ol. It contains the group \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\). Propan-1-ol does not have a methyl group on the carbon with the OH.
Q: Give the reagent that converts (a) ethanal into ethanol and (b) ethanoic acid into ethanol, and state why one of them needs a stronger reagent.
A: (a) \(\ce{NaBH4}\) or \(\ce{LiAlH4}\). (b) \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether. A carboxylic acid is harder to reduce than an aldehyde, and \(\ce{NaBH4}\) is not strong enough.

Q: [3 marks] ★ Butan-1-ol can be converted into butanal or into butanoic acid with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). State how the conditions differ for each.

A: \(M_r(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 46.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C5H10O2}) = 102.0\) [B1]

Q: [5 marks] ★★ Pentan-2-ol, pentan-3-ol and 2-methylbutan-2-ol are three isomeric alcohols. Describe two tests that together distinguish all three, and give the observations.
A: Test 1: warm each with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). [B1]
2-methylbutan-2-ol (tertiary) gives no change: the solution stays orange. [B1]
Pentan-2-ol and pentan-3-ol (both secondary) turn the solution green. [B1]
Test 2: warm each of the other two with alkaline aqueous iodine. [B1]
Pentan-2-ol, which contains \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\), gives a pale yellow precipitate of \(\ce{CHI3}\); pentan-3-ol gives no precipitate. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ 2-methylbutan-2-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3}\), is heated with concentrated sulfuric acid. Name the type of reaction, name the two alkenes formed and say whether either shows geometrical isomerism.
H: The OH carbon has neighbouring carbons that are a methyl group (two of them) and a \(\ce{CH2}\) group.
A: Dehydration (elimination of water). [B1]
Removing H from a methyl group gives 2-methylbut-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3}\). [B1]
Removing H from the \(\ce{CH2}\) group gives 2-methylbut-2-ene, \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH3}\). [B1]
Neither has geometrical isomers: in the first one carbon has two H atoms, and in the second one carbon has two methyl groups, so each has two identical groups on one end of the double bond. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ Write the equation for the reaction between methanol and sodium. Name the organic product and describe what you would see.
A: \(\ce{2CH3OH + 2Na -> 2CH3ONa + H2}\) [B1]
sodium methoxide [B1]
Bubbles of gas (hydrogen) are given off and the sodium dissolves. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) Ethanol in a 25.0 cm³ sample of a dilute solution is oxidised completely to ethanoic acid by 20.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ acidified potassium dichromate(VI), according to \(\ce{3C2H5OH + 2Cr2O7^2- + 16H+ -> 3CH3COOH + 4Cr^3+ + 11H2O}\). Calculate the mass of ethanol in the sample.
H: Moles of dichromate = concentration × volume in dm³. Then use the 3 : 2 ratio.
A: \(n(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}) = 0.0200 \times 0.0500 = 1.000 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 1.000 \times 10^{-3} \times 3/2 = 1.500 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 46.0\) [B1]
Mass \(= 1.500 \times 10^{-3} \times 46.0 = 0.0690\) g [A1]

(b) Name the yellow precipitate. (c) Name the three alkenes. (d) State why P cannot be a tertiary alcohol.
H: Which type of alcohol is oxidised to a compound that cannot be oxidised again?

A: (a) Q is not oxidised further, so it is a ketone, which means P is a secondary alcohol. [B1]
The iodine test shows the group \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\); of the C₄ alcohols only butan-2-ol has it, so P is butan-2-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3}\). [B1] [B1]
Q is butanone, \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH3}\). [B1]
(b) tri-iodomethane, \(\ce{CHI3}\) [B1]
(c) but-1-ene, cis-but-2-ene and trans-but-2-ene [B1] [B1]
(d) A tertiary alcohol has no H on the carbon bonded to OH, so it is not oxidised by dichromate. [B1]

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ Alcohol R, \(\ce{C5H12O}\), gives a pale yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine. When R is dehydrated it gives exactly two alkenes, and neither shows geometrical isomerism. Identify R and the two alkenes. Explain why pentan-2-ol is not R.
H: R contains \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\). There are two such alcohols with five carbons: pentan-2-ol and one branched one.
H: Draw the alkenes each could give. Remember that cis and trans isomers count as different alkenes.
A: R contains \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\) (the yellow precipitate), so it is pentan-2-ol or 3-methylbutan-2-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH(CH3)2}\). [B1]
Pentan-2-ol gives pent-1-ene and pent-2-ene, and pent-2-ene has cis and trans isomers, so it would give three alkenes. [B1]
3-methylbutan-2-ol gives, by removing H from the \(\ce{CH3}\), 3-methylbut-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH(CH3)2}\); [B1]
and, by removing H from the CH on the other side, 2-methylbut-2-ene, \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH3}\). [B1]
Neither alkene has geometrical isomers (one carbon of each double bond carries two identical groups), so R is 3-methylbutan-2-ol. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ 2,2,2-trichloroethanol, \(\ce{CCl3CH2OH}\), is a stronger acid than ethanol. Use the idea of inductive effects to explain why.
H: Alkyl groups push electron density towards the oxygen of the alkoxide ion and weaken ethanol as an acid. What would a group with three chlorine atoms do?
H: Chlorine is more electronegative than carbon.
A: The loss of \(\ce{H+}\) from \(\ce{CCl3CH2OH}\) leaves the ion \(\ce{CCl3CH2O-}\). [B1]
The three electronegative chlorine atoms pull electron density along the σ bonds, away from the oxygen (an electron-withdrawing inductive effect). [B1]
This spreads out the negative charge on oxygen, so the ion is more stable than \(\ce{CH3CH2O-}\), where the alkyl group pushes electron density onto the oxygen. [B1]
A more stable anion is formed more easily, so \(\ce{CCl3CH2OH}\) loses \(\ce{H+}\) more readily: it is the stronger acid. [B1]

:::recap
- An alcohol has an OH group on a saturated carbon, \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}\). Primary: the C–OH carbon has one other carbon attached; secondary: two; tertiary: three. Classify each OH in a molecule separately.
- O–H gives hydrogen bonding: high boiling points (ethanol 78 °C, propane −42 °C) and small alcohols mix with water. Solubility falls as the chain gets longer.
- Made by: steam + alkene (\(\ce{H3PO4}\)); cold dilute acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) on an alkene (diol); NaOH(aq) + halogenoalkane, heat; \(\ce{NaBH4}\) or \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) on an aldehyde or ketone; \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) on a carboxylic acid; hydrolysis of an ester with dilute acid or alkali, heat.
- With Na: \(\ce{2C2H5OH + 2Na -> 2C2H5ONa + H2}\), less vigorous than water. With HX, KCl + concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) or \(\ce{H3PO4}\), \(\ce{PCl3}\) (heat), \(\ce{PCl5}\) or \(\ce{SOCl2}\): halogenoalkane.
- Oxidation (acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), orange → green; or \(\ce{KMnO4}\), purple → colourless): 1° → aldehyde (distil off as formed) → carboxylic acid (excess oxidant, reflux); 2° → ketone; 3° no reaction.
- Dehydration: heated \(\ce{Al2O3}\) or concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) → alkene (elimination). Butan-2-ol gives but-1-ene, cis- and trans-but-2-ene.
- Esterification: alcohol + carboxylic acid, concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, heat under reflux, reversible; ester named alkyl alkanoate (ethyl ethanoate).
- Alkaline aqueous iodine gives yellow \(\ce{CHI3}\) with \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\) (ethanol, propan-2-ol, butan-2-ol) but not propan-1-ol or tertiary alcohols.
- Alcohols are weaker acids than water: the alkyl group donates electrons and destabilises \(\ce{RO-}\).
:::
