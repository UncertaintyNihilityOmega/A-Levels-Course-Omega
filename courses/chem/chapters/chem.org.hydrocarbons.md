## Alkanes: saturated hydrocarbons

A **hydrocarbon** contains carbon and hydrogen only. The simplest hydrocarbons, the alkanes, have no functional group at all: every carbon atom is joined to four other atoms by single bonds.

:::def Alkane
A saturated hydrocarbon: a compound of carbon and hydrogen only, with single bonds only. The general formula is \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}\).
:::

"Saturated" means that each carbon atom holds as many other atoms as it possibly can (four), so no more atoms can be added to the molecule. The first six alkanes are methane \(\ce{CH4}\), ethane \(\ce{C2H6}\), propane \(\ce{C3H8}\), butane \(\ce{C4H10}\), pentane \(\ce{C5H12}\) and hexane \(\ce{C6H14}\).

### Boiling points

Alkane molecules are non-polar, so the only attraction between them is the weakest force of all, the id–id (induced dipole–induced dipole) force (see [Shapes of molecules and intermolecular forces](#chem.shapes)). The strength of this force depends on how many electrons a molecule has and how closely its molecules can touch.

| Alkane | Formula | Boiling point / °C | State at room temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| methane | \(\ce{CH4}\) | −162 | gas |
| ethane | \(\ce{C2H6}\) | −89 | gas |
| propane | \(\ce{C3H8}\) | −42 | gas |
| butane | \(\ce{C4H10}\) | −0.5 | gas |
| pentane | \(\ce{C5H12}\) | 36 | liquid |
| hexane | \(\ce{C6H14}\) | 69 | liquid |

**Longer chain, higher boiling point.** Each extra \(\ce{CH2}\) adds electrons, so the id–id forces between molecules are stronger and more energy is needed to separate them.

**More branching, lower boiling point.** Three isomers of \(\ce{C5H12}\) have the same number of electrons, yet they boil at 36 °C (pentane), 28 °C (2-methylbutane) and 9.5 °C (2,2-dimethylpropane). A branched molecule is more compact, so its molecules touch each other over a smaller surface and the id–id forces are weaker.

This is why crude oil can be separated by fractional distillation. Short-chain alkanes boil at low temperatures and rise to the top of the column; long-chain alkanes stay lower down.

### Why alkanes are unreactive

Alkanes are used as fuels and lubricants because they hardly react with anything at room temperature. They do not react with acids, alkalis, oxidising agents such as acidified potassium manganate(VII), or reducing agents. There are two reasons.

:::key Why alkanes are unreactive
1. **Strong bonds.** The C–H bond (410 kJ mol⁻¹) and the C–C bond (350 kJ mol⁻¹) are strong, so a lot of energy is needed to break them.
2. **Almost no polarity.** The electronegativities of carbon (2.5) and hydrogen (2.1) are close, so the bonds are almost non-polar. There is no δ+ atom for a nucleophile to attack and no δ− atom for an electrophile to attack, and there are no lone pairs or π bonds to start a reaction.
:::

Alkanes react only when something provides enough energy or a very reactive species: a flame (combustion) or ultraviolet light and a halogen (free-radical substitution).

### Making alkanes

You will meet two ways to make an alkane, and both start from alkenes or from bigger alkanes.

| Method | Reagent and conditions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| hydrogenation of an alkene | \(\ce{H2}\)(g), Pt or Ni catalyst, heat | \(\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2 -> CH3CH3}\) |
| cracking of a longer alkane | heat with \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | \(\ce{C10H22 -> C8H18 + C2H4}\) |

Hydrogenation is covered under alkenes below. Cracking has its own section.

## Burning alkanes

All alkanes burn in air. Burning is an oxidation reaction that gives out a large amount of energy, which is why they make good fuels.

**Complete combustion** happens when there is plenty of oxygen. All the carbon becomes carbon dioxide and all the hydrogen becomes water.

\[\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}\]
\[\ce{C3H8 + 5O2 -> 3CO2 + 4H2O}\]

**Incomplete combustion** happens when the supply of oxygen is limited (a blocked chimney, a car engine running rich). Some carbon ends up as carbon monoxide, CO, or as carbon (soot), C, and less energy is released.

\[\ce{2CH4 + 3O2 -> 2CO + 4H2O}\]
\[\ce{CH4 + O2 -> C + 2H2O}\]

:::warn Carbon monoxide is a silent killer
Carbon monoxide is colourless and has no smell. It is toxic because it binds to haemoglobin in the blood much more strongly than oxygen does, so the blood can no longer carry oxygen round the body. Gas fires, boilers and engines must have a good air supply and a way for the exhaust gases to escape.
:::

To balance a combustion equation, balance the carbon first (one \(\ce{CO2}\) for each C), then the hydrogen (one \(\ce{H2O}\) for every two H), and then count the oxygen atoms on the right. Halve the number to get the \(\ce{O2}\) molecules needed. If it is a fraction, double every coefficient.

:::eg (Exam style) 20.0 cm³ of butane, \(\ce{C4H10}\), is mixed with 200 cm³ of oxygen (an excess) and burnt. After the mixture cools to room temperature, the water has condensed. All volumes are measured at room temperature and pressure. Calculate the volume and the composition of the gas that remains.
The equation is \(\ce{C4H10 + 13/2 O2 -> 4CO2 + 5H2O}\). For gases at the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes contain equal numbers of molecules, so the coefficients give the ratio of volumes (see [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles)). [B1]

Oxygen used \(= 20.0 \times 6.5 = 130\) cm³ [M1]. Oxygen left over \(= 200 - 130 = 70\) cm³.

Carbon dioxide formed \(= 20.0 \times 4 = 80.0\) cm³ [M1].

Gas remaining \(= 70 + 80.0 = 150\) cm³, made of 80.0 cm³ of \(\ce{CO2}\) and 70 cm³ of \(\ce{O2}\). [A1]

(Shaking the gas with sodium hydroxide solution would absorb the \(\ce{CO2}\), leaving 70 cm³ of oxygen.)
:::

:::eg (Real context) Octane, \(\ce{C8H18}\), is a typical component of petrol. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide released when 1.00 kg of octane burns completely.
\[\ce{C8H18 + 25/2 O2 -> 8CO2 + 9H2O}\] (1 mol of octane gives 8 mol of \(\ce{CO2}\)) [B1]

\(M_r(\ce{C8H18}) = 8(12.0) + 18(1.0) = 114.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{CO2}) = 12.0 + 2(16.0) = 44.0\). [B1]
\[n(\ce{C8H18}) = \frac{1000}{114.0} = 8.772\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[n(\ce{CO2}) = 8 \times 8.772 = 70.18\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[m(\ce{CO2}) = 70.18 \times 44.0 = 3088\,\mathrm{g} = 3.09\,\mathrm{kg}\] [A1]

Every kilogram of petrol burnt releases about three kilograms of carbon dioxide, because the carbon atoms in the fuel combine with oxygen from the air.
:::

## Free-radical substitution

Alkanes do react with chlorine or bromine when ultraviolet (UV) light shines on the mixture. A hydrogen atom is replaced by a halogen atom, so this is a **substitution**. It happens through a chain of steps in which the particles that do the work are free radicals.

:::def Free radical
A species with an unpaired electron. It is formed when a covalent bond breaks by homolytic fission, each atom taking one of the two shared electrons. In formulas the unpaired electron is shown as a dot, as in \(\ce{Cl^.}\).
:::

Free radicals are extremely reactive because the unpaired electron is looking for a partner. Reactions of free radicals follow three stages, which you must be able to name and write for ethane and chlorine. The overall change is

\[\ce{CH3CH3 + Cl2 ->[UV] CH3CH2Cl + HCl}\]

:::def Initiation, propagation and termination
**Initiation** is the step that makes the first free radicals. **Propagation** steps use up one free radical and make another, so the chain keeps going. **Termination** steps use up two free radicals to make a molecule, so the chain stops.
:::

**Initiation.** Ultraviolet light supplies enough energy to break the Cl–Cl bond, the weakest bond in the mixture (242 kJ mol⁻¹, compared with 410 for C–H and 350 for C–C). Each chlorine atom keeps one electron of the bond (homolytic fission):

\[\ce{Cl2 ->[UV] 2Cl^.}\]

**Propagation.** A chlorine atom takes a hydrogen atom from an ethane molecule, making hydrogen chloride and an ethyl radical. The ethyl radical then takes a chlorine atom from a \(\ce{Cl2}\) molecule, making the product and a new chlorine atom, which starts the cycle again.

\[\ce{Cl^. + CH3CH3 -> CH3CH2^. + HCl}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2^. + Cl2 -> CH3CH2Cl + Cl^.}\]

One chlorine atom can set off thousands of cycles before the chain ends, which is why a short flash of light can convert a lot of alkane.

**Termination.** Two free radicals meet and join. There are three possibilities:

\[\ce{Cl^. + Cl^. -> Cl2}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2^. + Cl^. -> CH3CH2Cl}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2^. + CH3CH2^. -> CH3CH2CH2CH3}\]

The third equation makes butane, a by-product that is found in small amounts in the product mixture. It is good evidence that ethyl radicals really are present.

:::warn Mark the dots, and the right arrow
Every free radical must be written with its dot (\(\ce{Cl^.}\), not \(\ce{Cl}\)), or the equation is wrong. In propagation, a radical is on each side of the arrow, so one radical is used and one is made. If two radicals are on the left and none on the right, it is termination. Initiation needs UV light written over the arrow.
:::

### Further substitution

The chloroethane made in propagation still contains C–H bonds, so it can be attacked by another chlorine atom. Further substitution gives \(\ce{CH3CHCl2}\) and \(\ce{CH2ClCH2Cl}\), then trichloro- compounds, and so on:

\[\ce{Cl^. + CH3CH2Cl -> CH3CHCl^. + HCl}\]
\[\ce{CH3CHCl^. + Cl2 -> CH3CHCl2 + Cl^.}\]

A radical does not choose which hydrogen atom it removes, so the product is a **mixture** of many chloroalkanes that must be separated. If the alkane is in large excess, a chlorine atom is more likely to meet an alkane molecule than a chloroalkane, and the mono-substituted product dominates. Free-radical substitution is therefore a poor way to make one particular halogenoalkane in the laboratory. Better routes use an alkene or an alcohol (see [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen)).

:::eg Write the equations for the initiation, the propagation and one termination step in the reaction of methane with bromine in UV light to form bromomethane.
Initiation: \(\ce{Br2 ->[UV] 2Br^.}\) [B1]

Propagation: \(\ce{Br^. + CH4 -> CH3^. + HBr}\) [B1]
\[\ce{CH3^. + Br2 -> CH3Br + Br^.}\] [B1]

Termination, for example \(\ce{CH3^. + CH3^. -> CH3CH3}\) [B1] (also acceptable: \(\ce{Br^. + Br^. -> Br2}\) or \(\ce{CH3^. + Br^. -> CH3Br}\)).

The overall equation is \(\ce{CH4 + Br2 -> CH3Br + HBr}\). A trace of ethane in the products shows that the chain really goes through methyl radicals.
:::

### Why both propagation steps run

A chain reaction only keeps going if both propagation steps are possible. You can test each with bond energies: bonds broken (energy in) minus bonds formed (energy out).

:::eg Use bond energies to show that both propagation steps in the chlorination of methane are exothermic, and compare the first step with the first step in bromination. Bond energies / kJ mol⁻¹: C–H 410, H–Cl 431, Cl–Cl 242, C–Cl 340, H–Br 366.
Step 1, \(\ce{Cl^. + CH4 -> CH3^. + HCl}\): break one C–H (410), make one H–Cl (431). [B1]
\[\Delta H = 410 - 431 = -21\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] [M1]

Step 2, \(\ce{CH3^. + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + Cl^.}\): break one Cl–Cl (242), make one C–Cl (340).
\[\Delta H = 242 - 340 = -98\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] [M1]

Both steps give out energy, so the chain runs.

For bromine, step 1 is \(\ce{Br^. + CH4 -> CH3^. + HBr}\): \(\Delta H = 410 - 366 = +44\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\). [A1]

This step takes in energy, so a bromine atom is less eager to remove a hydrogen atom than a chlorine atom. This is why bromine reacts with alkanes more slowly than chlorine does.
:::

## Cracking

Crude oil contains more long-chain alkanes than the market needs, and too few short ones. Petrol (short chains) and the alkenes used to make plastics are in much greater demand than the thick fuel oils.

:::def Cracking
The breaking of C–C bonds in long-chain alkane molecules by heat, to form shorter-chain alkanes and alkenes.
:::

For the 9701 course, the conditions are: **heat with an aluminium oxide catalyst, \(\ce{Al2O3}\)**. A long-chain alkane splits into a shorter alkane and an alkene, or into several alkenes with hydrogen:

\[\ce{C10H22 -> C8H18 + C2H4}\]
\[\ce{C12H26 -> C8H18 + 2C2H4}\]

Cracking has two uses. It turns surplus heavy fractions into shorter alkanes of lower \(M_r\), which are more useful as petrol and burn more easily. It also makes alkenes, which are far more reactive than alkanes and are the starting point for polymers and other products.

:::eg A molecule of decane, \(\ce{C10H22}\), is cracked. (a) One of the products is ethene, \(\ce{C2H4}\). Write an equation if the other product is an alkane. (b) On another cracking, propene \(\ce{C3H6}\) is formed with one other product. Give its formula and say what type of compound it is.
(a) Atoms are conserved: carbon \(10 - 2 = 8\), hydrogen \(22 - 4 = 18\), so the other product is \(\ce{C8H18}\), an alkane (octane) because it fits \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}\). [B1]
\[\ce{C10H22 -> C8H18 + C2H4}\] [B1]

(b) Carbon \(10 - 3 = 7\), hydrogen \(22 - 6 = 16\): \(\ce{C7H16}\), an alkane (heptane). [B1]
\[\ce{C10H22 -> C7H16 + C3H6}\]
:::

## Pollution from engines

A car engine burns petrol in a limited supply of air at a very high temperature. Three pollutants leave the exhaust together with carbon dioxide and water.

| Pollutant | How it forms and the harm it does | In the catalytic converter |
|---|---|---|
| carbon monoxide, CO | incomplete combustion of the fuel; toxic, because it binds to haemoglobin | oxidised to \(\ce{CO2}\) |
| oxides of nitrogen, \(\ce{NO_x}\) | nitrogen and oxygen from the air combine in the very hot engine; cause acid rain and photochemical smog | reduced to \(\ce{N2}\) |
| unburnt hydrocarbons | some fuel leaves the cylinder without burning; contribute to photochemical smog | oxidised to \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) |

A catalytic converter, a honeycomb coated with platinum, palladium and rhodium, removes all three at once. The key reaction is

\[\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}\]

in which carbon monoxide is oxidised and nitric oxide is reduced. The details, and the chemistry of acid rain and smog, are in [Nitrogen and sulfur](#chem.nitrogen).

A converter does not reduce the amount of carbon dioxide produced, which is the main greenhouse gas from burning any hydrocarbon. It simply swaps the toxic CO for the less harmful \(\ce{CO2}\).

## Alkenes: unsaturated hydrocarbons

Alkenes are hydrocarbons with at least one carbon–carbon double bond. Ethene, \(\ce{CH2=CH2}\), is the most important organic chemical made by industry.

:::def Alkene
An unsaturated hydrocarbon containing at least one carbon–carbon double bond. With one double bond the general formula is \(\ce{C_nH_{2n}}\).
:::

"Unsaturated" means the molecule has fewer hydrogen atoms than the alkane with the same number of carbons, so it can still take more atoms on. Do not confuse the words: an alkane is **saturated**, an alkene is **unsaturated**.

### The double bond: a σ bond and a π bond

In ethene each carbon atom forms three σ bonds (two to hydrogen, one to the other carbon). These lie in one flat plane with bond angles of about 120°. The fourth electron on each carbon is in a p orbital that sticks up and down out of the plane. The two p orbitals overlap sideways to form a **π bond**, a cloud of electron density above and below the plane of the molecule.

Two things follow from this picture.

1. The π electrons are **exposed**, above and below the molecule, and far from the nuclei. They are an electron-rich region that attracts any species short of electrons.
2. The π bond is **weaker** than the σ bond. The average C=C bond energy is 610 kJ mol⁻¹ and the C–C bond energy is 350 kJ mol⁻¹, so the π bond contributes only about \(610 - 350 = 260\) kJ mol⁻¹.

:::def Electrophile
A species that accepts a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond: an electron-pair acceptor. Examples are \(\ce{H+}\), the \(\delta+\) end of a polar molecule such as \(\ce{HBr}\), and an induced \(\delta+\) end of \(\ce{Br2}\).
:::

An electrophile is attracted to the π bond. The π electrons are used to make a new σ bond to the electrophile, and the molecule ends up with the double bond turned into a single bond. A reaction where two molecules combine to make one is an **addition**. Alkenes therefore undergo **electrophilic addition**, the typical reaction of alkenes.

:::def Electrophilic addition
A reaction in which an electrophile attacks the electron-rich C=C bond of an alkene and an atom or group is added to each of the two carbon atoms, so the double bond becomes a single bond.
:::

Alkanes cannot do this. They have no π bond, so they have no electron-rich region for an electrophile to find.

### Making alkenes

There are three ways to make an alkene. The first is the elimination reaction of [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen), and the third is cracking, which you have just met.

| Method | Reagent and conditions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| elimination of HX from a halogenoalkane | NaOH in ethanol, heat | \(\ce{CH3CH2Br -> CH2=CH2}\) |
| dehydration (elimination of \(\ce{H2O}\)) of an alcohol | heat with \(\ce{Al2O3}\), or concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) and heat | \(\ce{CH3CH2OH -> CH2=CH2 + H2O}\) |
| cracking of an alkane | heat with \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | \(\ce{C10H22 -> C8H18 + C2H4}\) |

Dehydration of alcohols is explained in [Alcohols](#chem.org.alcohols).

## Addition reactions of alkenes

Every addition reaction replaces the π bond with two new σ bonds. The next table lists what you must learn. Reagents and conditions are marked in the exam, so write both.

:::key Addition reactions of ethene (and other alkenes)
| Reaction and reagent | Conditions | Product (from ethene) |
|---|---|---|
| hydrogenation: \(\ce{H2}\)(g) | Pt or Ni catalyst, heat | ethane, \(\ce{CH3CH3}\) |
| hydration: steam, \(\ce{H2O}\)(g) | \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst, high temperature and pressure | ethanol, \(\ce{CH3CH2OH}\) |
| hydrogen halide: \(\ce{HBr}\)(g) or \(\ce{HCl}\)(g) | room temperature | bromoethane, \(\ce{CH3CH2Br}\) |
| halogen: \(\ce{Br2}\) (or \(\ce{Cl2}\)) | room temperature | 1,2-dibromoethane, \(\ce{CH2BrCH2Br}\) |
| oxidation: cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) | room temperature | ethane-1,2-diol, \(\ce{CH2(OH)CH2(OH)}\) |
:::

Written as equations:

\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2 ->[Ni][heat] CH3CH3}\]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O(g) <=>[H3PO4] CH3CH2OH}\]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CH2Br}\]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> CH2BrCH2Br}\]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + [O] + H2O -> CH2(OH)CH2(OH)}\]

Hydration is reversible (see [Equilibria](#chem.equilibria)). Industry uses high pressure to push the position of equilibrium towards the single product, ethanol, and recycles unreacted ethene.

:::eg Explain why addition of hydrogen to ethene is exothermic, and calculate \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2 -> CH3CH3}\). Bond energies / kJ mol⁻¹: C=C 610, H–H 436, C–C 350, C–H 410.
Bonds broken: one C=C (610) and one H–H (436), a total of 1046 kJ mol⁻¹. [B1]

Bonds formed: one C–C (350) and two new C–H (2 × 410 = 820), a total of 1170 kJ mol⁻¹. [B1]
\[\Delta H = 1046 - 1170 = -124\,\mathrm{kJ\,mol^{-1}}\] [M1] [A1]

It is exothermic because the two new σ bonds that form release more energy than the energy needed to break the weak π bond and the H–H bond.
:::

The same counting works for \(\ce{HBr}\): breaking C=C (610) and H–Br (366), and forming C–C (350), C–H (410) and C–Br (280), gives \(\Delta H = 976 - 1040 = -64\) kJ mol⁻¹. Addition reactions of alkenes are exothermic for the same reason.

### Hydrogenation and margarine

Vegetable oils contain long hydrocarbon chains with several C=C bonds, which keep them liquid. Heating the oil with hydrogen and a nickel catalyst converts some or all of the C=C bonds to C–C bonds. The saturated chains pack together more closely, so the melting point rises and the oil becomes a soft solid: margarine. Manufacturers stop before all the double bonds are used up, so the product spreads easily.

### The test for a double bond

:::key Test for an alkene
Shake the compound with orange **bromine water** (aqueous bromine) at room temperature. An alkene decolourises it quickly, from orange to colourless. An alkane does not.
:::

The bromine adds across the C=C bond and its colour disappears. An alkane does not react with bromine water at room temperature in ordinary light (it needs UV light), so there is no change. (The product in water is partly the bromo-alcohol, because water also adds to the intermediate, but for the test only the loss of colour matters.)

Cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) also decolourises (purple to colourless) with an alkene. It is not a specific test for alkenes, because many other compounds, such as alcohols and aldehydes, are also oxidised by it. Bromine water is the test to quote.

## The mechanism of electrophilic addition

A mechanism shows exactly how the electrons move. You must be able to draw two: bromine with ethene, and hydrogen bromide with propene.

### Bromine and ethene

A bromine molecule is non-polar, so how can it be an electrophile? When a \(\ce{Br2}\) molecule approaches the electron-rich C=C bond, the π electrons repel the electrons in the Br–Br bond. The bromine atom nearer the double bond becomes slightly positive and the far one slightly negative: an **induced dipole**.

:::steps
1. The π electrons of ethene repel the bonding electrons of the nearby \(\ce{Br2}\), giving an induced dipole \(\ce{Br^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\).
2. Step 1 (slow): the pair of electrons of the π bond forms a bond to the \(\delta+\) bromine atom. At the same time the Br–Br bond breaks heterolytically, and the \(\delta-\) bromine leaves as \(\ce{Br-}\) with both electrons. The other carbon atom is left with only six electrons in its outer shell and a positive charge: a **carbocation**.
3. Step 2 (fast): a lone pair on \(\ce{Br-}\) forms a bond to the positive carbon, giving 1,2-dibromoethane.
:::

### Hydrogen bromide and propene

Hydrogen bromide is already polar, \(\ce{H^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\), because bromine is more electronegative than hydrogen. No induced dipole is needed. The mechanism has the same two steps, but now the attacking electrophile is the \(\delta+\) hydrogen atom.

Propene has an unsymmetrical double bond: one carbon holds two H atoms, the other holds an H and a \(\ce{CH3}\) group. The hydrogen can join either carbon, so two carbocations are possible.

| If the H adds to… | The carbocation is… | Alkyl groups on the charged carbon |
|---|---|---|
| the \(\ce{CH}\) carbon (the middle one) | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2+}\), primary (charge on the end carbon) | 1 |
| the \(\ce{CH2}\) carbon (the end one) | \(\ce{CH3CH+CH3}\), secondary (charge on the middle carbon) | 2 |

The two possible products are 2-bromopropane (through the secondary carbocation) and 1-bromopropane (through the primary one). In practice almost all the product is 2-bromopropane. To see why, we need to compare the two carbocations.

### Which carbocation? The inductive effect

An alkyl group such as \(\ce{CH3}\) pushes electron density along its σ bond towards the carbon it is joined to. This donation of electron density is the **inductive effect** of alkyl groups. It matters most next to a positive charge.

:::def Inductive effect (of alkyl groups)
The donation of electron density along σ bonds by alkyl groups towards a neighbouring atom. An alkyl group joined to a positively charged carbon reduces the charge on that carbon and so stabilises the carbocation.
:::

The more alkyl groups on the positive carbon, the more its charge is spread and the more stable the ion.

| Carbocation | Alkyl groups on \(\ce{C+}\) | Stability |
|---|---|---|
| primary, \(\ce{RCH2+}\) | 1 | least stable |
| secondary, \(\ce{R2CH+}\) | 2 | more stable |
| tertiary, \(\ce{R3C+}\) | 3 | most stable |

For propene, the secondary carbocation \(\ce{CH3CH+CH3}\) is more stable than the primary one, so it forms faster and more of it forms. Almost all the HBr adds so that the bromine ends up on the middle carbon. Where an unsymmetrical alkene reacts with HBr, the **major product** comes from the more stable carbocation. The older rule of thumb (Markovnikov's rule) says that the hydrogen joins the carbon that already has more hydrogen atoms; the carbocation argument is the reason it works.

:::warn Where does the positive charge go?
Draw the charge on the carbon that has lost its share of the π bond, not on the carbon that received the hydrogen. In \(\ce{CH3CH+CH3}\) the middle carbon carries the charge; the two end carbons are now \(\ce{CH3}\) groups. A common slip is to put the charge on the carbon that has just gained the H.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) But-1-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH=CH2}\), reacts with hydrogen bromide. Name the major and the minor product, and explain why one is major. [4 marks]
The H adds to the \(\ce{CH2}\) end, giving the secondary carbocation \(\ce{CH3CH2CH+CH3}\) (two alkyl groups on the charged carbon). Br⁻ then bonds to the charged carbon: 2-bromobutane, the major product. [B1]

If the H adds to the \(\ce{CH}\) carbon, the primary carbocation \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2+}\) forms, and the minor product is 1-bromobutane. [B1]

Alkyl groups donate electron density (inductive effect) to the positive carbon. [B1]

The secondary carbocation has two alkyl groups, so its charge is reduced more and it is more stable than the primary, which has only one. It forms faster, so 2-bromobutane is major. [B1]
:::

An alkene with a symmetrical double bond, such as ethene or but-2-ene, gives only one product with HBr, because both carbons are the same. Steam adds in the same way: propene and steam give mainly propan-2-ol.

## Oxidation of alkenes by potassium manganate(VII)

Acidified potassium manganate(VII), \(\ce{KMnO4}\), is a strong oxidising agent, and what it does to a C=C bond depends on the conditions.

### Cold, dilute: a diol

Cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) adds an OH group to each carbon of the double bond, turning the alkene into a **diol** (a compound with two OH groups). The purple solution becomes colourless as the \(\ce{MnO4-}\) ion is reduced to \(\ce{Mn^2+}\).

\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + [O] + H2O -> CH2(OH)CH2(OH)}\]

The product from ethene is ethane-1,2-diol. Propene gives propane-1,2-diol.

### Hot, concentrated: the C=C bond is split

Hot, concentrated, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) is a more powerful oxidising agent and **breaks the C=C bond in two**. Each half of the molecule becomes a new compound. What you get depends on the groups on each carbon of the double bond:

:::key Hot concentrated acidified KMnO₄ splits C=C
| Carbon of the double bond | Product |
|---|---|
| \(\ce{=CH2}\) (two H atoms) | \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) |
| \(\ce{=CHR}\) (one H atom, one alkyl group) | carboxylic acid, \(\ce{RCOOH}\) |
| \(\ce{=CRR'}\) (two alkyl groups) | ketone, \(\ce{RCOR'}\) |
:::

Because each product depends on what was attached to the carbon, you can work backwards from the products to find where the double bond was. For example:

\[\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3 + 4[O] -> 2CH3COOH}\]

but-2-ene gives two molecules of ethanoic acid, because each carbon of the double bond is a \(\ce{=CHCH3}\) group.

:::eg An alkene \(\ce{C6H12}\) is heated with hot concentrated acidified potassium manganate(VII). The organic products are propanone, \(\ce{CH3COCH3}\), and propanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH2COOH}\). Deduce the structure of the alkene.
Propanone is a ketone, \(\ce{RCOR'}\) with R = R′ = \(\ce{CH3}\). It came from a carbon that carried two methyl groups: \(\ce{(CH3)2C=}\). [B1]

Propanoic acid is a carboxylic acid, \(\ce{RCOOH}\) with R = \(\ce{CH3CH2}\). It came from a carbon with one H and one ethyl group: \(\ce{=CHCH2CH3}\). [B1]

Joining the two halves at the double bond gives \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH2CH3}\): five carbons in the longest chain with the double bond at carbon 2 and a methyl on carbon 2, so the alkene is 2-methylpent-2-ene. [B1]

Check: the carbon count is 3 + 3 = 6, and the hydrogen count is 6 + 6 = 12, \(\ce{C6H12}\). ✓
:::

:::eg What are the organic products of heating but-2-ene and 2-methylbut-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3}\), separately, with hot concentrated acidified potassium manganate(VII)?
But-2-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}\): each carbon of the double bond has one H and one \(\ce{CH3}\), so each half gives ethanoic acid. The product is two molecules of \(\ce{CH3COOH}\). [B1]

2-methylbut-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3}\): the \(\ce{=CH2}\) end gives \(\ce{CO2}\) (and \(\ce{H2O}\)) [B1]. The other carbon holds two alkyl groups (a methyl and an ethyl), so it gives a ketone, \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH3}\), butanone. [B1]
:::

## Addition polymerisation

Ethene molecules can add to each other. The π bond of one molecule opens and bonds to the next, and the process repeats thousands of times to form a very long chain.

:::def Addition polymerisation
The joining together of many small unsaturated molecules (monomers) by opening their C=C bonds, to make one very long saturated molecule (a polymer). The polymer is the only product; nothing else is lost.
:::

\[\ce{n CH2=CH2 -> -[CH2-CH2]_n-}\]

The unit in square brackets is the **repeat unit**: the part of the polymer that repeats along the chain. The \(n\) shows that it occurs many times. The double bond has been lost: in the polymer, every carbon is joined by single bonds only.

:::def Repeat unit
The shortest section of a polymer chain that, repeated over and over, makes the whole chain. For an addition polymer it comes from one monomer molecule, with the C=C replaced by C–C and a bond extended from each end carbon.
:::

Poly(ethene) comes from ethene. Poly(chloroethene), also called PVC, comes from chloroethene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCl}\). Poly(propene) comes from propene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH3}\). In each case the two carbon atoms of the double bond become the two carbons of the main chain, and every other group stays attached as a **side group**.

### From a polymer back to a monomer

To find the monomer from a section of an addition polymer:

:::steps
1. Find the repeat unit: look for a pattern of two chain carbons that repeats.
2. Keep the two carbons of the repeat unit and all the groups attached to them.
3. Put a double bond between the two carbons, and remove the bonds that went outwards.
:::

A section of polymer that reads \(\ce{-CH2-CHCl-CH2-CHCl-CH2-CHCl-}\) repeats the unit \(\ce{-CH2-CHCl-}\), so the monomer is \(\ce{CH2=CHCl}\), chloroethene.

:::eg Draw the repeat unit of poly(chloroethene), and give the monomer of the polymer section \(\ce{-CH2-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH(CH3)-}\).
Chloroethene is \(\ce{CH2=CHCl}\). Open the double bond: the chain carbon atoms are joined by one bond, and each carbon keeps its groups. [B1]

The repeat unit is \(\ce{-[CH2-CHCl]-}\), shown in square brackets with a bond through each bracket, and the label \(n\) at the lower right. [B1]

The second polymer repeats \(\ce{-CH2-CH(CH3)-}\). Join the two chain carbons by a double bond and keep the \(\ce{CH3}\) side group: \(\ce{CH2=CHCH3}\), propene. [B1]
:::

### Disposal problems

Addition polymers are very useful: they are light, cheap, strong and unreactive. That last property becomes a problem when they are thrown away.

- **They are not biodegradable.** The chain is made only of strong, non-polar C–C and C–H bonds (and C–Cl in PVC), which microorganisms' enzymes cannot break. Discarded plastic stays in landfill and in the sea for decades.
- **Burning gives harmful products.** Burning in a limited supply of air gives carbon monoxide (toxic) and soot. Burning PVC also gives hydrogen chloride, a toxic and corrosive gas that forms hydrochloric acid with moisture.
- Even complete combustion releases carbon dioxide, a greenhouse gas.

The burning of PVC can be written with the repeat unit:

\[\ce{2C2H3Cl + 5O2 -> 4CO2 + 2HCl + 2H2O}\]

Ways to deal with the problem are recycling (melting and reshaping some plastics), burning the waste to generate electricity with the exhaust gases passed through scrubbers that remove acidic gases like HCl, and reducing the amount used.

:::eg (Real context) Burning PVC releases hydrogen chloride. Calculate the mass of HCl formed when 1.00 kg of PVC, \(\ce{(C2H3Cl)_n}\), burns completely. [4 marks]
The repeat unit is \(\ce{C2H3Cl}\): \(M_r = 2(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 35.5 = 62.5\), and every chlorine atom in the polymer ends up in one HCl. [B1]
\[n(\ce{C2H3Cl}) = \frac{1000}{62.5} = 16.00\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\(n(\ce{HCl}) = 16.00\) mol; \(M_r(\ce{HCl}) = 1.0 + 35.5 = 36.5\) [M1]
\[m(\ce{HCl}) = 16.00 \times 36.5 = 584\,\mathrm{g}\] [A1]
:::

A: The C–H and C–C bonds are strong (410 and 350 kJ mol⁻¹), and the bonds are almost non-polar (C 2.5, H 2.1), so there is no δ+ or δ− atom for a nucleophile or an electrophile to attack.
Q: Write the equations for initiation and the two propagation steps for ethane with chlorine.
A: Initiation: \(\ce{Cl2 ->[UV] 2Cl^.}\) Propagation: \(\ce{Cl^. + CH3CH3 -> CH3CH2^. + HCl}\) and \(\ce{CH3CH2^. + Cl2 -> CH3CH2Cl + Cl^.}\)
Q: Why does free-radical substitution give a mixture of products?
A: Radicals are not selective, so further hydrogen atoms in the product are also replaced, giving di- and tri-substituted compounds.
Q: Give the reagent and conditions to convert ethene into (a) bromoethane, (b) ethanol, (c) ethane.
A: (a) \(\ce{HBr}\)(g) at room temperature. (b) Steam with \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst, high temperature and pressure. (c) \(\ce{H2}\) with a Pt or Ni catalyst, heat.
Q: How can you tell the difference between hexane and hex-1-ene using a simple test?
A: Shake with bromine water. Hex-1-ene decolourises it (orange to colourless); hexane does not.
Q: Give the product of the reaction of propene with hydrogen chloride and explain why it is the major product.
A: 2-chloropropane. The H adds to the end carbon to form the secondary carbocation \(\ce{CH3CH+CH3}\), which is stabilised by two alkyl groups (inductive effect), more than the primary carbocation.
Q: Draw the repeat unit of poly(propene), and name the monomer of the polymer section \(\ce{-CH2-CHF-CH2-CHF-}\).
A: Repeat unit \(\ce{-[CH2-CH(CH3)]-}\) in brackets with bonds through the brackets and \(n\). The section repeats \(\ce{-CH2-CHF-}\), so the monomer is \(\ce{CH2=CHF}\), fluoroethene.
:::

Q: [2 marks] ★ Write the equation for the complete combustion of hexane, \(\ce{C6H14}\).
A: \(\ce{2C6H14 + 19O2 -> 12CO2 + 14H2O}\) [B1] [B1] (B1 for correct formulas of the products, B1 for balancing; accept \(\ce{C6H14 + 19/2 O2 -> 6CO2 + 7H2O}\))
Q: [3 marks] ★ Propane reacts with chlorine in UV light to give 1-chloropropane. Write the two propagation steps, and one termination step that forms hexane.
A: \(\ce{CH3CH2CH3 + Cl^. -> CH3CH2CH2^. + HCl}\) [B1]
\(\ce{CH3CH2CH2^. + Cl2 -> CH3CH2CH2Cl + Cl^.}\) [B1]
\(\ce{2CH3CH2CH2^. -> CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3}\) [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ Use bond energies to calculate \(\Delta H\) for \(\ce{CH2=CH2 + Cl2 -> CH2ClCH2Cl}\). Bond energies / kJ mol⁻¹: C=C 610, Cl–Cl 242, C–C 350, C–Cl 340.
H: Break C=C and Cl–Cl. Make C–C and two C–Cl.
A: Bonds broken: \(610 + 242 = 852\) kJ mol⁻¹. [M1]
Bonds formed: \(350 + 2 \times 340 = 1030\) kJ mol⁻¹. [M1]
\(\Delta H = 852 - 1030 = -178\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ 0.350 g of an alkene reacts with exactly 0.800 g of bromine. Find the \(M_r\) of the alkene and deduce its molecular formula.
H: One mole of an alkene with one C=C reacts with one mole of \(\ce{Br2}\); \(M_r(\ce{Br2}) = 2 \times 79.9\).
A: \(n(\ce{Br2}) = 0.800 / 159.8 = 5.006 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(n(\text{alkene}) = 5.006 \times 10^{-3}\) mol, so \(M_r = 0.350 / 5.006 \times 10^{-3} = 69.9\) [M1] [A1]
\(\ce{C_nH_{2n}}\): \(14n = 70\), so \(n = 5\), \(\ce{C5H10}\). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Describe the mechanism of the reaction between hydrogen bromide and 2-methylpropene, \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CH2}\). Name the major product and explain why it is major.
H: Which carbocation can form: one with a charge on the end carbon, or one with a charge on the middle carbon with three alkyl groups?
A: Curly arrow from the C=C bond to the \(\delta+\) H of \(\ce{H^{\delta+}-Br^{\delta-}}\). [B1]
Curly arrow from the H–Br bond to Br. [B1]
Carbocation intermediate \(\ce{(CH3)3C+}\), a tertiary carbocation with the charge on the middle carbon. [B1]
Curly arrow from a lone pair on \(\ce{Br-}\) to \(\ce{C+}\); product 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CBr}\). [B1]
It is major because the tertiary carbocation has three alkyl groups, which donate electron density by the inductive effect, so it is more stable than the primary carbocation \(\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2+}\). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Alkene X gives carbon dioxide and butanone, \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH3}\), on heating with hot concentrated acidified potassium manganate(VII). Alkene Y, \(\ce{C7H14}\), gives propanone and butanoic acid. Identify X and Y.
H: A carbon with two H atoms gives \(\ce{CO2}\); a carbon with two alkyl groups gives a ketone; a carbon with one H gives an acid.
A: X: the \(\ce{=CH2}\) end gives \(\ce{CO2}\) and the other carbon, bearing a methyl and an ethyl group, gives butanone, so X is 2-methylbut-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3}\). [B1] [B1]
Y: \(\ce{(CH3)2C=}\) gives propanone and \(\ce{=CHCH2CH2CH3}\) gives butanoic acid, so Y is \(\ce{(CH3)2C=CHCH2CH2CH3}\), 2-methylhex-2-ene. [B1] [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) A plastic factory burns 250 kg of PVC waste. Calculate the mass of hydrogen chloride that forms if all the chlorine in the PVC ends up as HCl.
H: The repeat unit is \(\ce{C2H3Cl}\); one chlorine atom gives one HCl molecule.
A: \(M_r(\ce{C2H3Cl}) = 62.5\) and \(M_r(\ce{HCl}) = 36.5\) [B1]
\(n(\ce{C2H3Cl}) = 250 \times 1000 / 62.5 = 4000\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{HCl}) = 4000\) mol [M1]
\(m = 4000 \times 36.5 = 146\,000\) g \(= 146\) kg [A1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ Compound W, \(\ce{C6H12}\), decolourises bromine water and shows geometrical isomerism. W reacts with hot concentrated acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) to give only one organic product, propanoic acid. (a) Identify W and explain why it shows geometrical isomerism. (b) Write an equation for the reaction of W with hydrogen bromide and explain why only one organic product is formed. (c) Give the reagent and conditions to convert W to hexane.
H: A single acid product means the two halves of the molecule are identical.
H: Which alkene of formula \(\ce{C6H12}\) has the same group, \(\ce{CH3CH2}\), on both carbons of the double bond?
A: (a) W is hex-3-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH=CHCH2CH3}\): each half \(\ce{=CHCH2CH3}\) gives propanoic acid. [B1] [B1] Rotation about the C=C bond is restricted, and each carbon of the double bond carries two different groups (H and \(\ce{C2H5}\)), so there are cis and trans isomers. [B1]
(b) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH=CHCH2CH3 + HBr -> CH3CH2CHBrCH2CH2CH3}\) [B1] The double bond is symmetrical, so H and Br add to either carbon and give the same compound, 3-bromohexane. [B1]
(c) \(\ce{H2}\) gas with a nickel (or platinum) catalyst, heat. [B1] [B1]

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ The free-radical bromination of 2-methylpropane, \(\ce{(CH3)3CH}\), gives mainly 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, not 1-bromo-2-methylpropane. The step that decides this is the removal of a hydrogen atom by \(\ce{Br^.}\). Suggest, using the same idea as for carbocations, why the radical \(\ce{(CH3)3C^.}\) forms more easily than \(\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2^.}\).
H: Compare the number of alkyl groups on the carbon that holds the unpaired electron.
H: Alkyl groups donate electron density through σ bonds.
A: Removing the unique H atom (on the middle carbon) leaves a radical with three alkyl groups on the carbon with the unpaired electron: a tertiary radical. [B1]
Removing an H from a methyl group leaves a primary radical, with one alkyl group on that carbon. [B1]
Alkyl groups donate electron density (inductive effect) to the electron-deficient carbon. [B1]
The tertiary radical is more stabilised than the primary, so it is formed faster; more of the tertiary radical means more 2-bromo-2-methylpropane. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Hydrogen bromide adds to 3,3,3-trifluoropropene, \(\ce{CF3CH=CH2}\), to give mainly \(\ce{CF3CH2CH2Br}\), the opposite orientation from propene. The three fluorine atoms pull electrons towards themselves through the σ bonds (they are very electronegative). Explain why this changes the major product.
H: Draw both possible carbocations and decide which is the more destabilised by the CF₃ group.
H: Is \(\ce{CF3}\) an electron donor or an electron acceptor?
A: The two possible carbocations are \(\ce{CF3CH+CH3}\) (H adds to the \(\ce{CH2}\) end) and \(\ce{CF3CH2CH2+}\) (H adds to the middle carbon). [B1]
The \(\ce{CF3}\) group withdraws electron density, so it makes a neighbouring positive charge worse. [B1]
In \(\ce{CF3CH+CH3}\) the positive carbon is directly next to the \(\ce{CF3}\) group, so it is strongly destabilised; in \(\ce{CF3CH2CH2+}\) the charge is one carbon further away. [B1]
So the carbocation with the charge further from the CF₃ group forms more readily, \(\ce{Br-}\) attacks that carbon, and \(\ce{CF3CH2CH2Br}\) is the major product. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which statement explains why alkanes are unreactive towards most reagents?
- They are unsaturated
+ Their C–C and C–H bonds are strong and almost non-polar
- They contain a lone pair on each carbon atom
- They have only ionic bonds
> The strong C–H (410 kJ mol⁻¹) and C–C (350) bonds and the small difference in electronegativity (C 2.5, H 2.1) mean there is nothing for polar reagents to attack. Alkanes are saturated, not unsaturated, and have no lone pairs.
? Ethane reacts with chlorine in UV light. Which equation is a propagation step?
- \(\ce{Cl2 -> 2Cl^.}\)
- \(\ce{2CH3CH2^. -> CH3CH2CH2CH3}\)
+ \(\ce{Cl^. + CH3CH3 -> CH3CH2^. + HCl}\)
- \(\ce{CH3CH2^. + Cl^. -> CH3CH2Cl}\)
> Propagation uses one free radical and makes another. The first option is initiation, and the other two use up two radicals (termination).
? 40 cm³ of methane is burnt in 100 cm³ of oxygen, and the products return to room temperature (water is liquid). What is the total volume of gas left?
- 40 cm³
+ 60 cm³
- 100 cm³
- 140 cm³
> \(\ce{CH4 + 2O2 -> CO2 + 2H2O}\): 40 cm³ of methane uses 80 cm³ of oxygen, leaving 20 cm³, and makes 40 cm³ of \(\ce{CO2}\). The gas left is 40 + 20 = 60 cm³. Counting only the \(\ce{CO2}\) gives 40; counting the water as a gas as well gives 140.
? Which reagent converts propene into propan-2-ol as the major product?
- \(\ce{H2}\) with a nickel catalyst
+ steam with a phosphoric acid catalyst
- aqueous sodium hydroxide
- dry hydrogen bromide
> Steam with an acid catalyst adds across the C=C bond, with OH on the more substituted carbon (through the more stable secondary carbocation). Hydrogen gives propane, HBr gives 2-bromopropane, and NaOH(aq) does not react with an alkene.
? Why does the carbocation \(\ce{(CH3)3C+}\) form more easily than \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2+}\)?
- It has more hydrogen atoms
- Its carbon atom has a lone pair
+ Three alkyl groups donate electron density and reduce the positive charge
- The positive charge is spread across all four carbons equally
> The inductive effect of alkyl groups stabilises the positive carbon. Hydrogen atoms do not donate electron density in the same way, and a carbocation has no lone pair; its carbon has only six electrons.
? Which compound gives only ethanoic acid when heated with hot concentrated acidified potassium manganate(VII)?
- propene
- but-1-ene
+ but-2-ene
- 2-methylpropene
> But-2-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}\), has the same \(\ce{=CHCH3}\) group on both ends, so splitting gives two ethanoic acid molecules. Propene gives ethanoic acid and \(\ce{CO2}\), but-1-ene gives propanoic acid and \(\ce{CO2}\), and 2-methylpropene gives propanone and \(\ce{CO2}\).
? Which of these is the repeat unit of poly(propene)?
- \(\ce{-[CH2-CH2-CH2]-}\)
- \(\ce{-[CH2=CH-CH3]-}\)
+ \(\ce{-[CH2-CH(CH3)]-}\)
- \(\ce{-[CH2-CH2-CH3]-}\)
> The repeat unit keeps the two carbons of the former double bond in the chain, and the \(\ce{CH3}\) hangs off as a side group. The second option still has a C=C, and the other two put the methyl group into the chain or lose a bond.
? Why is poly(chloroethene) a problem when it is burned?
- It burns to form chlorine gas
- It melts into a poisonous liquid
+ It forms hydrogen chloride, which is toxic and corrosive
- It does not burn at all
> Burning PVC releases HCl from the C–Cl bonds. Chlorine gas is not formed; PVC does burn; and it is a gas, not a poisonous liquid, that causes the harm.

:::recap
- Alkanes are saturated (\(\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}\)) and unreactive because C–H (410) and C–C (350 kJ mol⁻¹) bonds are strong and almost non-polar. Boiling point rises with chain length and falls with branching (id–id forces).
- Complete combustion gives \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\); incomplete combustion gives CO (toxic, binds to haemoglobin) or C.
- Free-radical substitution (alkane + \(\ce{Cl2}\) or \(\ce{Br2}\), UV): initiation \(\ce{Cl2 -> 2Cl^.}\), propagation (\(\ce{Cl^. + C2H6 -> C2H5^. + HCl}\); \(\ce{C2H5^. + Cl2 -> C2H5Cl + Cl^.}\)), termination (two radicals combine, e.g. \(\ce{2C2H5^. -> C4H10}\)). Further substitution gives a mixture.
- Cracking: heat with \(\ce{Al2O3}\), long alkane to shorter alkane + alkene.
- Engine pollutants: CO, \(\ce{NO_x}\), unburnt hydrocarbons; a Pt/Pd/Rh catalytic converter turns them into \(\ce{CO2}\), \(\ce{N2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) (\(\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}\)).
- Alkenes (\(\ce{C_nH_{2n}}\)) are unsaturated. The C=C has a σ bond and a weaker π bond (electron-rich), so alkenes undergo electrophilic addition.
- Made by: NaOH in ethanol + heat from a halogenoalkane; heated \(\ce{Al2O3}\) or concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) from an alcohol; cracking.
- Additions: \(\ce{H2}\), Pt/Ni, heat → alkane; steam, \(\ce{H3PO4}\) → alcohol; HX(g) at room temperature → halogenoalkane; \(\ce{Br2}\) at room temperature → dibromoalkane; cold dilute acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) → diol.
- Test for C=C: bromine water, orange → colourless.
- Mechanism: π bond → electrophile (curly arrow from the bond), then carbocation, then \(\ce{Br-}\) lone pair → \(\ce{C+}\). \(\ce{Br2}\) first becomes an induced dipole; HBr is already polar.
- Alkyl groups stabilise carbocations (inductive effect): tertiary > secondary > primary, so HBr + propene gives mainly 2-bromopropane.
- Hot concentrated acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\): \(\ce{=CH2}\) → \(\ce{CO2}\), \(\ce{=CHR}\) → \(\ce{RCOOH}\), \(\ce{=CRR'}\) → ketone.
- Addition polymers: remove the C=C, brackets with bonds through them and n; side groups hang off the two-carbon chain unit. They do not biodegrade and PVC burns to give HCl.
:::
