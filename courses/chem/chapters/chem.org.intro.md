Q: A hydrocarbon contains 92.3 % carbon and 7.7 % hydrogen by mass. What is its empirical formula? (from [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles))

A: Tetrahedral, with bond angles of 109.5°.

## Why carbon?

A carbon atom has four electrons in its outer shell. It completes its octet by forming **four covalent bonds**, and it makes strong bonds to other carbon atoms, to hydrogen, to oxygen, to nitrogen and to the halogens. Because the C–C bond is strong (average bond energy 350 kJ mol⁻¹) and non-polar, carbon atoms can join in long chains, branched chains and rings. Few other elements can do this to the same extent.

Chains, branches and rings give a huge number of different molecules, even from just carbon and hydrogen. Adding a few other atoms gives even more. These molecules are described by their carbon skeleton and by any special group of atoms attached to it.

:::def Straight-chained, branched and cyclic
A **straight-chained** compound has all its carbon atoms in one unbranched chain. A **branched** compound has alkyl groups (carbon chains) joined to the main chain. A **cyclic** compound has its carbon atoms joined in a ring.
:::

"Straight-chained" does not mean drawn as a straight line. A chain of carbon atoms zigzags (each C–C–C angle is about 109.5°), and bonds can turn about single bonds. It only means that there are no branches.

## Hydrocarbons, alkanes and homologous series

The simplest organic compounds contain only carbon and hydrogen.

:::def Hydrocarbon
A compound that contains carbon and hydrogen only.
:::

:::def Alkane
A saturated hydrocarbon with no functional group. The carbon atoms are joined by single bonds only, and the general formula is \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}\).
:::

:::def Saturated and unsaturated
A **saturated** compound has only single bonds between its carbon atoms. An **unsaturated** compound contains at least one double or triple bond between carbon atoms, so it holds fewer hydrogen atoms than the saturated compound with the same number of carbons.
:::

The first six straight-chain alkanes are the stems for naming every other organic compound. Learn the stems in this table.

| Number of carbons | Stem | Alkane | Molecular formula | Condensed structural formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | meth- | methane | \(\ce{CH4}\) | \(\ce{CH4}\) |
| 2 | eth- | ethane | \(\ce{C2H6}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH3}\) |
| 3 | prop- | propane | \(\ce{C3H8}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH3}\) |
| 4 | but- | butane | \(\ce{C4H10}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH3}\) |
| 5 | pent- | pentane | \(\ce{C5H12}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3}\) |
| 6 | hex- | hexane | \(\ce{C6H14}\) | \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3}\) |

Each member differs from the one before by one \(\ce{CH2}\) group. A series like this is called a homologous series.

:::def Homologous series
A family of organic compounds with the same functional group and the same general formula, in which each member differs from the next by a \(\ce{CH2}\) group. The members have similar chemical properties, and their physical properties change gradually along the series.
:::

The alkanes are the series with no functional group, and they are not very reactive: the C–C and C–H bonds are strong and almost non-polar (C 2.5 and H 2.1 on the Pauling scale). Every other homologous series is an alkane with one group of atoms added or changed.

:::def Functional group
An atom or group of atoms in a molecule that is responsible for its characteristic chemical reactions.
:::

The functional group dictates the properties of a compound. Butane (\(\ce{C4H10}\)) and butan-1-ol (\(\ce{C4H9OH}\)) have almost the same carbon skeleton, but the C–O and O–H bonds of the alcohol are polar and can form hydrogen bonds, so the alcohol has a much higher boiling point and reacts with sodium, oxidising agents and acids in ways that butane does not. The functional group decides both the physical properties (through its polarity and intermolecular forces) and the chemical properties (through its bonds). That is why organic chemistry is arranged by functional group.

## Four ways to write a formula

One molecule can be shown in several ways. Use the one the question asks for, and know how to turn one into another. Take propan-1-ol as the example.

:::def Molecular formula
The actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule, such as \(\ce{C3H8O}\).
:::

:::def Empirical formula
The simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms of each element in a compound. For \(\ce{C3H8O}\) it is \(\ce{C3H8O}\) (no common factor); for \(\ce{C4H8}\) it is \(\ce{CH2}\).
:::

:::def General formula
The simplest algebraic formula for all members of a homologous series, for example \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}\) for the alcohols.
:::

:::def Structural, displayed and skeletal formulas
A **structural formula** shows the arrangement of atoms in the molecule in a simplified way, such as \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH}\). A **displayed formula** shows every atom and every bond. A **skeletal formula** shows only the carbon skeleton as a zigzag line, plus the functional groups.
:::

:::key Reading a skeletal formula
- Each **corner** and each **line end** is a carbon atom.
- Each line is a bond. A double bond is two parallel lines, a triple bond is three.
- Hydrogen atoms attached to carbon are **not drawn**. Each carbon makes four bonds in total, so count the bonds shown and add enough hydrogen atoms to make four. (The one common exception: the H of an aldehyde group is often drawn, as –CHO, to make the group clear.)
- Atoms other than carbon and hydrogen are drawn as their symbols (O, N, Cl, Br). A hydrogen atom attached to one of these **is** drawn: write OH and NH₂, not just O and N.
- Draw the zigzag so that bond angles look about 120°. An alkyne or nitrile unit (\(\ce{C#C}\), \(\ce{C#N}\)) is drawn as a straight line with its neighbours.
:::

:::eg Draw the displayed formula of methyl propanoate, \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\).

## The functional groups of the AS course

Nine homologous series besides the alkanes are needed at AS. Each is named after its functional group. In general formulas, **R** stands for an alkyl group (such as \(\ce{CH3}\) or \(\ce{CH3CH2}\)), R′ for a second alkyl group that may be different, and **X** for a halogen atom.

| Compound | Functional group | Ending (suffix) or prefix | General formula | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| alkene | C=C | -ene | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n}}\) | propene |
| halogenoalkane | –X (F, Cl, Br, I) | prefix fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo- | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}X}\) | 1-chloropropane |
| alcohol | –OH (hydroxyl) | -ol | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}OH}\) | propan-1-ol |
| aldehyde | –CHO (carbonyl at the end of a chain) | -al | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n}O}\) | propanal |
| ketone | C=O (carbonyl between two carbons) | -one | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n}O}\) | propanone |
| carboxylic acid | –COOH (carboxyl) | -oic acid | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n}O2}\) | propanoic acid |
| ester | –COO– | alkyl -oate | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n}O2}\) | methyl propanoate |
| amine (primary) | –NH₂ | -amine | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}NH2}\) | propylamine |
| nitrile | –C≡N | -nitrile | \(\ce{C_nH_{2n+1}CN}\) | propanenitrile |

In the general formulas for the amines and nitriles, n counts the carbon atoms of the alkyl group R only. The carbon of the nitrile group is extra.

At AS a primary amine is named from its alkyl group, as in ethylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2NH2}\), and propylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2NH2}\) (propan-1-amine is the systematic name).

Three group names are easily confused, so learn them exactly:

- **Carbonyl**, C=O. In an aldehyde the carbonyl carbon is at the end of the chain and carries one hydrogen (–CHO). In a ketone it sits between two carbon atoms.
- **Hydroxyl**, –OH, in alcohols.
- **Carboxyl**, –COOH, in carboxylic acids: a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on the same carbon.

Notice that an aldehyde and a ketone with the same number of carbons have the same molecular formula (\(\ce{C3H6O}\) for propanal and propanone), and so do a carboxylic acid and an ester (\(\ce{C3H6O2}\) for propanoic acid and methyl ethanoate). Compounds like these are isomers, which are the topic of [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism).

### Primary, secondary and tertiary

The carbon atom that carries the functional group may be bonded to one, two or three other carbon atoms. This changes the reactions of alcohols and halogenoalkanes.

:::def Primary, secondary and tertiary alcohols
In a primary (1°) alcohol the carbon bonded to the –OH group is bonded to one other carbon atom (or none, in methanol). In a secondary (2°) alcohol it is bonded to two other carbon atoms, and in a tertiary (3°) alcohol to three other carbon atoms. Halogenoalkanes are classified in the same way, by the carbon bonded to the halogen.
:::

For example, butan-1-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}\), is primary; butan-2-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3}\), is secondary; and 2-methylpropan-2-ol, \(\ce{(CH3)3COH}\), is tertiary. At AS level only primary amines (\(\ce{R-NH2}\)) are needed.

## Naming organic compounds

Systematic names follow rules so that every compound has one name and every name gives one structure. The name has a stem (the longest chain), a suffix or prefix for the functional group, and prefixes for branches.

:::steps
1. Find the **longest unbranched carbon chain that contains the functional group** (for a C=C, both carbons of the double bond; for a nitrile, the carbon of the C≡N group counts). Its length gives the stem: meth-, eth-, prop-, but-, pent-, hex-. A ring is named with the prefix **cyclo-**, such as cyclohexane.
2. Choose the **ending** from the table: -ene, -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid, -amine, -nitrile; an ester is named alkyl alkanoate. Halogens are shown as prefixes (chloro-, bromo-).
3. **Number the chain** from the end that gives the functional group (or the C=C) the lowest number. The carbon of –CHO, –COOH and –C≡N is always carbon 1, so those need no number.
4. Name each **branch** (methyl, ethyl) and halogen as a prefix with its number. Put the prefixes in **alphabetical order**. For identical groups use di-, tri-, with a number for each (2,2-dimethyl). If a name needs a choice between the two directions, the lowest numbers go to the functional group first, then to the branches.
5. Write the name in one word: numbers are separated by commas, and numbers are separated from letters by hyphens.
:::

Three small points about spelling. The final -e of the alkane stem is dropped before an ending that begins with a vowel (butan-2-ol, butanal, butanone, butanoic acid), but it is kept before an ending that begins with a consonant (butanenitrile). The number of the functional group goes just before its ending: butan-2-ol, not 2-butanol; but-2-ene, not 2-butene. And a position number is not needed when only one position is possible, as in ethanol or propanoic acid.

**Esters** have a two-part name. In \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\) the part with the C=O carbon (three carbons including it) comes from propanoic acid, so it ends in **-oate**: propanoate. The part joined to the single-bonded oxygen comes from the alcohol and is named as an alkyl group: methyl. The name is written alkyl first, alkanoate second: **methyl propanoate**. Reading the formula in the other direction gives a different ester: \(\ce{HCOOCH2CH2CH3}\) is propyl methanoate. At AS only straight chains are needed for esters and nitriles, with up to six carbons in each part of an ester.

:::eg Name (a) \(\ce{CH3C(CH3)=CHCH3}\), (b) \(\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH(OH)CH3}\), (c) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2Cl}\).
(a) The longest chain containing both carbons of the C=C has four carbons: but-ene. Numbering from the left, the double bond is between carbons 2 and 3. Numbering from the right also gives the double bond the locant 2, so the branch decides: from the left the methyl is on carbon 2, from the right on carbon 3. Use the lower number: **2-methylbut-2-ene**.

(b) The longest chain containing the carbon with OH has four carbons. Number from the end nearer the OH: the OH is on carbon 2 and the methyl branch on carbon 3. The name is **3-methylbutan-2-ol**. (Numbered from the other end, the OH would be on carbon 3, so that direction is wrong.)

(c) The longest chain containing the carbon with Cl is four carbons: \(\ce{ClCH2-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH3}\). Chlorine is on carbon 1 and the methyl branch on carbon 2: **1-chloro-2-methylbutane**. (The prefixes are in alphabetical order: chloro before methyl.)
:::

:::eg Name (a) \(\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CH2COOH}\), (b) \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH2CH3}\), (c) \(\ce{CH3CH(CH3)CN}\).
(a) The carbon of –COOH is carbon 1, then \(\ce{CH2}\) is carbon 2, \(\ce{CH(CH3)}\) is carbon 3 and the last \(\ce{CH3}\) is carbon 4. It is a butanoic acid with a methyl on carbon 3: **3-methylbutanoic acid**.

(b) The part with the C=O carbon has three carbons, so propanoate. The group on the single-bonded oxygen is \(\ce{CH2CH3}\), ethyl. The name is **ethyl propanoate**.

(c) The carbon of the CN group is carbon 1 and counts in the chain. The chain C(≡N)–CH–CH₃ has three carbons, with a methyl branch on carbon 2: **2-methylpropanenitrile**. The molecule has four carbon atoms in all, but the stem is propane because the branch is named separately.
:::

:::warn Three naming slips
Numbering from the wrong end (the functional group must get the lowest number); naming an ester backwards (\(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\) is methyl propanoate, not propyl methanoate); and forgetting that the carbon of a nitrile group is part of the chain (\(\ce{CH3CH2CN}\) is propanenitrile, three carbons, not ethanenitrile).
:::

## Bonds break in two ways

Organic reactions happen when bonds break and new ones form. The way a covalent bond breaks sets the kind of species that forms and so the kind of reaction.

:::def Homolytic fission
The breaking of a covalent bond so that **each** atom keeps one of the two shared electrons. It forms two free radicals.
:::

:::def Heterolytic fission
The breaking of a covalent bond so that **one** atom keeps both of the shared electrons. It forms a positive ion and a negative ion.
:::

:::def Free radical
A species with an unpaired electron. It is shown with a dot, such as \(\ce{Cl^.}\) or \(\ce{CH3^.}\).
:::

Free radicals are very reactive. They form when a non-polar bond is broken by ultraviolet light or heat; for chlorine, the equation is:

\[\ce{Cl-Cl ->[UV] 2Cl^.}\]

The reaction of an alkane with chlorine in UV light goes through free radicals in a chain reaction with three stages. Methane is the example here, and the full treatment is in [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons).

- **Initiation**: radicals are made. \(\ce{Cl2 ->[UV] 2Cl^.}\)
- **Propagation**: a radical reacts and makes another radical, so the chain continues. \(\ce{Cl^. + CH4 -> CH3^. + HCl}\), then \(\ce{CH3^. + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + Cl^.}\)
- **Termination**: two radicals combine and the chain stops. \(\ce{CH3^. + Cl^. -> CH3Cl}\)

A polar bond, such as the C–Br bond in bromomethane, usually breaks heterolytically. The more electronegative atom takes both electrons. A **curly arrow** shows this. It is a full-headed arrow that shows the movement of a **pair of electrons**.

:::key Curly arrows
A curly arrow starts at a **bond** or at a **lone pair** of electrons and ends at the atom or bond where that pair goes. It never starts at a charge symbol or at an atom. A radical step (one electron moving) is written as an equation in this course, not drawn with arrows.
:::

### Nucleophiles and electrophiles

Ions and polar molecules attack each other. Two words describe the attackers.

:::def Nucleophile
A species that donates a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond: an electron-pair donor. It is attracted to a δ+ or positive centre. Examples: \(\ce{OH-}\), \(\ce{CN-}\), \(\ce{NH3}\), \(\ce{H2O}\).
:::

:::def Electrophile
A species that accepts a pair of electrons to form a new covalent bond: an electron-pair acceptor. It is attracted to a region of high electron density. Examples: \(\ce{H+}\), \(\ce{CH3+}\), and the δ+ end of a polar molecule such as HBr.
:::

A nucleophile always has a lone pair (or a π bond) and attacks a δ+ carbon, as in a halogenoalkane. An electrophile attacks an electron-rich region, such as the C=C of an alkene. The names of the types of mechanism add the attacker to what happens: a **nucleophilic substitution** is a substitution started by a nucleophile.

## Types of reaction

Organic equations are classified by what happens to the atoms. Learn each term with one example.

| Reaction | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **addition** | two molecules join to form one; a double bond becomes a single bond | \(\ce{CH2=CH2 + Br2 -> CH2BrCH2Br}\) |
| **substitution** | one atom or group is replaced by another | \(\ce{CH4 + Cl2 -> CH3Cl + HCl}\) |
| **elimination** | a small molecule is removed from one molecule, leaving a double bond | \(\ce{CH3CH2Br -> CH2=CH2 + HBr}\) |
| **hydrolysis** | a compound is broken apart by water or by hydroxide ions | \(\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH- -> CH3CH2OH + Br-}\) |
| **condensation** | two molecules join, and a small molecule such as water is lost | \(\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH -> CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}\) |
| **oxidation** | gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen | \(\ce{CH3CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CHO + H2O}\) |
| **reduction** | gain of hydrogen or loss of oxygen | \(\ce{CH3CHO + 2[H] -> CH3CH2OH}\) |

Some reactions fit two labels: \(\ce{CH3CH2Br + OH-}\) is a hydrolysis (water or OH⁻ breaks the molecule) and also a substitution (OH replaces Br). Give the label the question asks for; if it lists both, "substitution" describes what happens to the atoms and "hydrolysis" what does the breaking.

In organic equations, [O] stands for oxygen supplied by an oxidising agent, and [H] for hydrogen supplied by a reducing agent. They let you balance the equation without writing the reagent.

The mechanism of a reaction is the step-by-step account of how the bonds break and form. Four mechanisms are named in this course.

| Mechanism | Attacking species | Typical example | Where it is taught |
|---|---|---|---|
| free-radical substitution | radical | methane + chlorine, UV | [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons) |
| electrophilic addition | electrophile | ethene + hydrogen bromide | [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons) |
| nucleophilic substitution | nucleophile | bromoethane + hydroxide ions | [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen) |
| nucleophilic addition | nucleophile | ethanal + hydrogen cyanide | [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl) |

:::eg Classify each reaction as addition, substitution, elimination, hydrolysis, oxidation or condensation. (a) \(\ce{CH3CH=CH2 + HBr -> CH3CHBrCH3}\) (b) \(\ce{CH3CH2OH + HBr -> CH3CH2Br + H2O}\) (c) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br -> CH3CH=CH2 + HBr}\) (d) \(\ce{CH3COOCH3 + H2O -> CH3COOH + CH3OH}\) (e) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + [O] -> CH3CH2CHO + H2O}\)
(a) **Addition**: HBr joins to the molecule and the C=C becomes a single bond.

(b) **Substitution**: the OH group is replaced by Br.

(c) **Elimination**: HBr is removed and a C=C forms.

(d) **Hydrolysis**: the ester is broken by water into an acid and an alcohol. (The reverse reaction, in which an ester forms with loss of water, is a condensation.)

(e) **Oxidation**: the alcohol loses two hydrogen atoms (to [O], forming water) and gains a C=O.
:::

The shape around a carbon atom depends on how many regions of electron density surround it, just as in [Shapes of molecules and intermolecular forces](#chem.shapes). In organic chemistry the regions are described by the hybrid orbitals the carbon uses, as you saw in [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding). A double bond or a triple bond counts as **one** region.

| sp² | 3 | 3 | 1 | trigonal planar, 120° | the carbons of ethene, C=O in a carbonyl |
| sp | 2 | 2 | 2 | linear, 180° | the carbons of ethyne, the C of \(\ce{C#N}\) |

In a double bond the first bond is a σ bond (end-on overlap) and the second is a π bond (sideways overlap of p orbitals, above and below the line of the nuclei). A triple bond is one σ and two π bonds. The π bond is weaker than the σ bond: the average C=C bond energy (610 kJ mol⁻¹) is less than twice the C–C value (350 kJ mol⁻¹). Because the π electrons are exposed above and below the molecule, alkenes are attacked by electrophiles.

**Ethene is planar.** The two carbons and the four hydrogens all lie in one plane, with H–C–H and H–C=C angles close to 120°. The p orbitals that make the π bond are perpendicular to this plane, and they overlap only if they stay parallel, which is why the double bond cannot rotate. That fact gives cis–trans isomerism in [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism).

A quick check on counting: for a molecule with no ring, the number of σ bonds is always one less than the number of atoms, because each σ bond joins one more atom to the structure. Every additional bond within a double or triple bond is a π bond.

:::eg Count the σ and π bonds in (a) but-1-ene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}\), and (b) propenal, \(\ce{CH2=CHCHO}\).
(a) But-1-ene has 4 carbons and 8 hydrogens, 12 atoms. With no ring there are 12 − 1 = 11 σ bonds: 3 C–C (including the σ of the C=C) and 8 C–H. There is 1 π bond, in the C=C. **11 σ and 1 π.** Check: the C=C counts one σ and one π, and the other two C–C bonds are σ, so 3 σ; the 8 C–H bonds are σ; so 11 σ.

(b) Propenal has 3 carbons, 4 hydrogens and 1 oxygen, 8 atoms, so 8 − 1 = 7 σ bonds. It has two double bonds (C=C and C=O), so 2 π bonds. **7 σ and 2 π.**
:::

:::eg Identify the type of hybridisation, the shape and the bond angle around each carbon atom in \(\ce{CH2=CH-CH2-C#N}\) (but-3-enenitrile).
Count the regions of electron density around each carbon; a multiple bond counts as one region.

Work from left to right. (In the name the nitrile carbon is carbon 1, so the \(\ce{CH2=}\) carbon is carbon 4; the left-to-right order here is only for counting.)

- The \(\ce{CH2=}\) carbon: two H and one double bond is 3 regions: **sp²**, trigonal planar, 120°.
- The \(\ce{=CH}{-}\) carbon: one H, one double bond and one single bond is 3 regions: **sp²**, trigonal planar, 120°.
- The \(\ce{-CH2}{-}\) carbon: two H and two single bonds is 4 regions: **sp³**, tetrahedral, 109.5°.
- The \(\ce{-C#N}\) carbon: one single bond and one triple bond is 2 regions: **sp**, linear, 180° (the C–C≡N unit is a straight line).
:::

:::exam Shapes in an exam answer
"Planar" means all the atoms are in one plane, which is true for ethene (six atoms) but not for ethane, where each carbon is tetrahedral. Always give both the shape and the bond angle, and say how many σ and π bonds a double or triple bond contains.
:::

? Which pair of compounds are in the same homologous series?
- propane and propene
- propanal and propanone
+ ethanol and propan-1-ol
- ethanol and methoxymethane
> Members of a series have the same functional group and differ by \(\ce{CH2}\): ethanol and propan-1-ol are both alcohols. Propane and propene have different groups; propanal and propanone, and ethanol and methoxymethane, are pairs of isomers with the same formula.
? What is formed by the heterolytic fission of a covalent bond?
- two free radicals
- two neutral molecules
+ a positive ion and a negative ion
- two positive ions
> In heterolytic fission one atom keeps both shared electrons, so it becomes negative and the other positive. Two radicals form in homolytic fission.
? Which statement about ethene, \(\ce{CH2=CH2}\), is correct?
- Each carbon is tetrahedral with bond angles of 109.5°.
- The π bond lies in the plane of the six atoms.
+ All six atoms lie in one plane and the bond angles are about 120°.
- The molecule is linear.
> Each carbon of ethene is sp² with three σ bonds at 120° in one plane. The π electrons lie above and below that plane, in p orbitals perpendicular to it.
? How many σ and π bonds are there in propenal, \(\ce{CH2=CHCHO}\)?
- 6 σ and 3 π
+ 7 σ and 2 π
- 7 σ and 1 π
- 8 σ and 1 π
> There are 8 atoms and no ring, so 7 σ bonds; there are two double bonds (C=C and C=O), so 2 π bonds.

:::recap
- A hydrocarbon has C and H only; an alkane is saturated with no functional group (\(\ce{C_nH_{2n+2}}\)). A homologous series shares one functional group and one general formula, each member differing by \(\ce{CH2}\).
- The functional group decides the properties. Learn: alkene C=C; halogenoalkane –X; alcohol –OH; aldehyde –CHO; ketone C=O; carboxylic acid –COOH; ester –COO–; primary amine –NH₂; nitrile –C≡N.
- Formulas: molecular (actual atoms), empirical (simplest ratio), general, structural, displayed (all atoms and bonds), skeletal (carbon zigzag; no H on carbon, but OH and NH₂ are drawn).
- Naming: longest chain containing the functional group; lowest number to the group; branches alphabetical; -e dropped before a vowel but kept in -nitrile; esters are alkyl alkanoate (methyl propanoate = \(\ce{CH3CH2COOCH3}\)); the nitrile carbon is in the chain.
- Homolytic fission gives two free radicals (unpaired electron); heterolytic fission gives a positive and a negative ion. A radical chain has initiation, propagation and termination.
- A nucleophile donates an electron pair; an electrophile accepts one. Curly arrows start at a bond or lone pair.
- Reaction types: addition, substitution, elimination, hydrolysis, condensation, oxidation ([O]) and reduction ([H]).
- sp³: 4 σ, tetrahedral, 109.5°. sp²: 3 σ + 1 π, trigonal planar, 120°. sp: 2 σ + 2 π, linear, 180°. A double bond is 1 σ + 1 π; a triple bond is 1 σ + 2 π. Ethene is planar.
:::
