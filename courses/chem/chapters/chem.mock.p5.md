Q: [14 marks] (#chem.planning) **Question 1: planning.**

The enthalpy change of combustion, \(\Delta_c H\), of a substance is the enthalpy change when one mole of the substance burns completely in oxygen. A student wants to find out how the enthalpy change of combustion of alcohols changes along the series methanol, ethanol, propan-1-ol and butan-1-ol, by heating water in a metal calorimeter with a spirit burner.

You are provided with: the four alcohols, each in a spirit burner with a lid; a copper calorimeter; a thermometer with 1 °C divisions; a balance reading to 0.01 g; a 100 cm³ measuring cylinder; a stand and clamp; a draught shield; a stopwatch; distilled water.

(a) Predict how \(\Delta_c H\) changes from methanol to butan-1-ol, and explain your prediction. (2 marks)

(b) Identify the independent variable and the dependent variable, and state one variable that must be controlled, with how you would control it. (3 marks)

(c) Describe how you would carry out the investigation. Include the quantities you would use. (4 marks)

(d) State one hazard in this investigation and a precaution that deals with it. (2 marks)

(e) Draw a results table with suitable headings, and explain how you would use the results to calculate \(\Delta_c H\) for each alcohol. (3 marks)
A: (a) \(\Delta_c H\) becomes more negative (more exothermic) from methanol to butan-1-ol [B1]
because each extra \(\ce{CH2}\) group means more carbon and hydrogen atoms burnt per mole, so more \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\) are formed and more bonds are made (more energy released) [B1]

(b) independent variable: the alcohol used (the number of carbon atoms in its molecule) [B1]
dependent variable: the temperature rise of the water (or the mass of alcohol burnt for a fixed temperature rise) [B1]
control, any one of: the mass or volume of water (e.g. 100 cm³ measured with a measuring cylinder); the distance between the burner and the calorimeter (fixed with the clamp and stand); the same calorimeter and draught shield; the starting temperature of the water [B1] (the method of controlling it must be stated)

(c) put a fixed volume of water, e.g. 100 cm³ (100 g), into the copper calorimeter in the clamp, at a fixed height above the burner; record the initial temperature [B1]
weigh the spirit burner with its lid on and record the mass [B1]
light the burner, stir the water with the thermometer, and heat until the temperature has risen by about 20 °C; put the lid on to extinguish, and record the highest temperature reached [B1]
reweigh the burner and lid to find the mass of alcohol burnt; repeat with the other three alcohols, using fresh cold water each time, and replicate each (to check consistency) [B1]

(d) hazard: the alcohols are flammable (and methanol is toxic, and the vapours are harmful) [B1]
precaution: keep the lids on the burners and the stock bottles away from the flame; keep the working area clear of other flammables; work in a well-ventilated area [B1]

(e) table with headings with units: alcohol; initial temperature / °C; final temperature / °C; temperature rise, \(\Delta T\) / °C; initial mass of burner / g; final mass of burner / g; mass of alcohol burnt / g [B1]
heat absorbed by the water \(q = mc\Delta T\), with \(m\) = mass of water (100 g) and \(c = 4.18\) J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ (for example \(100 \times 4.18 \times 20.0 = 8360\) J) [B1]
\(n\) = mass burnt / \(M_r\) of the alcohol; \(\Delta_c H = -q/n\) (in kJ mol⁻¹, negative); for example 0.50 g of ethanol (\(M_r\) 46.0, 0.0109 mol) would give about \(-769\) kJ mol⁻¹; the experimental values are less exothermic than the true values because heat is lost to the surroundings and combustion is incomplete [B1]

Q: [16 marks] (#chem.planning) **Question 2: analysis and evaluation.**

Hydrogen peroxide decomposes in the presence of iodide ions as a catalyst:

\[\ce{2H2O2(aq) -> 2H2O(l) + O2(g)}\]

A student investigated how the concentration of hydrogen peroxide affects the rate. She prepared 50.0 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide solution of each concentration, \(c\), by diluting 1.00 mol dm⁻³ hydrogen peroxide with distilled water, using a 10 cm³ measuring cylinder (graduated in 1.0 cm³ divisions) for the stock solution. She added 5.0 cm³ of aqueous potassium iodide of fixed concentration, fitted a bung connected to a gas syringe, and measured the time, \(t\), for 20.0 cm³ of oxygen to be collected. Each experiment was done once.

| \(c\) / mol dm⁻³ | \(t\) / s | \(1/t\) / 10⁻³ s⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.100 | 102.5 | 9.76 |
| 0.200 | 49.6 | 20.2 |
| 0.300 | 34.3 | (a) |
| 0.400 | 25 | 40.0 |
| 0.500 | 27.5 | 36.4 |
| 0.600 | 16.2 | 61.7 |
| 0.700 | 14.6 | 68.5 |
| 0.800 | 12.6 | (a) |
| 0.900 | 11.0 | 90.9 |

(a) Calculate the two missing values of \(1/t\), to three significant figures. (2 marks)

(b) Describe the graph the student should draw to find the relationship between rate and concentration: the axes, a suitable scale, and how to draw the line. (2 marks)

(c) The line of best fit passes through (0.100, 9.80) and (0.900, 90.1), where the \(y\) values are in units of 10⁻³ s⁻¹. Calculate the gradient, with its unit, and the \(y\)-intercept, and state what the intercept shows. (3 marks)

(d) Identify the anomalous result and suggest one reason for it. (2 marks)

(e) State one inconsistency in the way the student recorded the data. (1 mark)

(f) State the conclusion about how rate depends on concentration of hydrogen peroxide, using the graph and the data. (2 marks)

(g) The student did each experiment once. Evaluate how this affects the reliability of the conclusion, and suggest one improvement. (2 marks)

(h) For the 0.200 mol dm⁻³ solution she measured 10.0 cm³ of the stock solution with the measuring cylinder, which can be read to ±0.5 cm³. Calculate the percentage error in this volume, and suggest an apparatus that reduces it, justifying your answer with a calculation. (2 marks)
A: (a) \(1/34.3 = 29.2\) and \(1/12.6 = 79.4\) (×10⁻³ s⁻¹) [B1] [B1]

(b) plot \(1/t\) / s⁻¹ (or ×10⁻³ s⁻¹) on the y-axis against concentration / mol dm⁻³ on the x-axis, both axes labelled with quantity and unit [B1]
scale with the points filling more than half of the grid in both directions, in simple steps (for example 10 ×10⁻³ s⁻¹ and 0.100 mol dm⁻³ per large square, so 1/t from 0 to 100); plot each point with ×, circle the anomaly and ignore it, and draw one straight line of best fit [B1]

(c) gradient \(= (90.1 - 9.80)/(0.900 - 0.100)\) [M1]
\(= 100 \times 10^{-3}\) s⁻¹ per mol dm⁻³, so 0.100 dm³ mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ [A1]
y-intercept \(= 9.80 - 100.4 \times 0.100 = -0.24\), which is approximately 0 (\(-0.24 \times 10^{-3}\) s⁻¹), so the line passes through the origin [B1]

(d) the result at 0.500 mol dm⁻³, \(t = 27.5\) s, is anomalous: its \(1/t\) of 36.4 is well below the line (the line gives about 50, corresponding to about 20 s) [B1]
possible reason: the stopwatch was stopped late, or started too early, or gas leaked before the bung was fitted, or the volume of stock hydrogen peroxide was measured wrongly [B1]

(e) the time at 0.400 mol dm⁻³ was recorded as 25, while the others are recorded to one decimal place; it should be 25.0 s [B1]

(f) the graph of \(1/t\) against \(c\) is a straight line through the origin [B1]
so \(1/t\) (and therefore the rate) is directly proportional to the concentration of hydrogen peroxide: the reaction is first order with respect to \(\ce{H2O2}\) (for example, doubling \(c\) from 0.200 to 0.400 mol dm⁻³ doubles \(1/t\) from 20.2 to 40.0) [B1]

(g) with only one result at each concentration there is no way to know how consistent the results are (the anomaly cannot be checked), so the conclusion is less reliable [B1]
repeat each experiment (replicate) and use the mean of consistent results [B1]

(h) percentage error \(= 0.5/10.0 \times 100 = 5.0\%\) [B1]
use a burette: two readings of ±0.05 cm³ give \(0.10/10.00 \times 100 = 1.0\%\), which is smaller [B1]
