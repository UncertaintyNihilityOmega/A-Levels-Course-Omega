## Inside the atom

An atom is far smaller than anything you can see, but experiments have shown what it is like inside. In the early 1900s, Rutherford's students fired a beam of positively charged particles at a very thin sheet of gold foil. Most of the particles went straight through. A small number were deflected through large angles, and a very few bounced almost straight back.

The only way to explain this is that an atom is **mostly empty space**, with all its positive charge and almost all its mass packed into a tiny, dense **nucleus** at the centre. The nucleus is roughly ten thousand to a hundred thousand times smaller across than the whole atom. If the atom were a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a pea. The nucleus contains **protons** and **neutrons**. The **electrons** move around it in regions called shells, and they take up the space.

| Particle | Relative charge | Relative mass | Where it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | +1 | 1 | in the nucleus |
| neutron | 0 | 1 | in the nucleus |
| electron | −1 | 1/1836 | in shells outside the nucleus |

Relative means "compared with a proton". An electron has about 1/1836 of the mass of a proton, so the electrons add almost nothing to the mass of an atom. Nearly all of the mass is in the nucleus, and all of the positive charge is too. The electrons carry the negative charge, spread over the whole volume of the atom, and in a neutral atom there are equal numbers of protons and electrons, so the charges cancel.

:::def Proton number (atomic number), Z
The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It identifies the element.
:::

:::def Nucleon number (mass number), A
The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom.
:::

Because the number of neutrons is the nucleon number minus the proton number, you can always work out all three particle numbers from \(A\) and \(Z\). An element is written as \({}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}\), with the nucleon number at the top left and the proton number at the bottom left. For an ion, put the charge at the top right.

:::key Counting particles
For \({}^{A}_{Z}\mathrm{X}^{q}\) (charge \(q\), written 3+ or 2−):
- number of protons = \(Z\)
- number of neutrons = \(A - Z\)
- number of electrons = \(Z - q\), where a positive charge means electrons lost and a negative charge means electrons gained.
:::

:::eg How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in \({}^{56}_{26}\mathrm{Fe}^{3+}\)?
Protons = proton number = 26.

Neutrons = nucleon number − proton number = 56 − 26 = 30.

The ion has a 3+ charge, so it has lost three electrons from the neutral atom, which has 26. Electrons = 26 − 3 = 23.

Check: the charge is protons minus electrons = 26 − 23 = +3. ✓
:::

:::eg How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in \({}^{32}_{16}\mathrm{S}^{2-}\)?
Protons = 16. Neutrons = 32 − 16 = 16.

The 2− charge means two electrons gained: electrons = 16 + 2 = 18.

Check: protons minus electrons = 16 − 18 = −2. ✓
:::

### Beams of particles in an electric field

Suppose you could make three separate beams, one of protons, one of neutrons and one of electrons, all moving at the same speed. If each beam passes between a positively charged plate and a negatively charged plate, they behave very differently.

- **Neutrons** have no charge, so the plates do not affect them. The beam goes straight on.
- **Protons** are positive, so they are attracted towards the negative plate and bend that way.
- **Electrons** are negative, so they bend towards the positive plate, the opposite way.

The size of the bend depends on the charge and on the mass. A bigger charge means a bigger force, and a bigger mass means more inertia, so less acceleration from the same force. The deflection is proportional to the charge divided by the mass. A proton has charge +1 and mass 1, so charge ÷ mass = 1. An electron has charge −1 and mass 1/1836, so charge ÷ mass has size 1836. The electron beam therefore bends about 1800 times more than the proton beam, in the opposite direction.

:::warn Electrons are not "in" the nucleus
The nucleus holds only protons and neutrons. Electrons are in shells outside it. Writing that an atom has "electrons in the nucleus" loses the mark, and so does saying that mass is spread evenly through the atom.
:::

## Isotopes

Not all atoms of an element are identical. Chlorine atoms always have 17 protons, but some have 18 neutrons and others have 20. These are two forms of the same element.

:::def Isotopes
Atoms of the same element (the same proton number) with different numbers of neutrons, and therefore different nucleon numbers.
:::

The two isotopes of chlorine are written \({}^{35}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\) and \({}^{37}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}\). Hydrogen has three: \({}^{1}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) (no neutrons), deuterium \({}^{2}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) (one neutron) and tritium \({}^{3}_{1}\mathrm{H}\) (two neutrons).

**Isotopes have the same chemical properties.** Chemical reactions depend on the electrons, mainly the outer ones, and isotopes of an element have the same number of protons and so the same number of electrons and the same electron configuration. Both isotopes of chlorine form \(\ce{Cl-}\) ions, both react with sodium to form sodium chloride, and both form a white precipitate with silver nitrate.

**Isotopes have different physical properties**, because they have different masses. Syllabus questions limit this to **mass** and **density**: an atom of \({}^{37}\mathrm{Cl}\) is heavier than an atom of \({}^{35}\mathrm{Cl}\), and a sample of the heavier isotope packs more mass into the same volume. Heavy water, \(\ce{D2O}\), is made of deuterium and oxygen. It is denser than ordinary water, so an ice cube made from it sinks in ordinary water.

:::eg (Exam style) Naturally occurring magnesium contains \({}^{24}\mathrm{Mg}\), \({}^{25}\mathrm{Mg}\) and \({}^{26}\mathrm{Mg}\). (a) State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in an atom of \({}^{25}\mathrm{Mg}\). (b) Explain why the three isotopes have the same chemical properties but a sample of \({}^{26}\mathrm{Mg}\) is denser than a sample of \({}^{24}\mathrm{Mg}\). [4 marks]
(a) Magnesium has proton number 12. Protons = 12 [B1]. Neutrons = 25 − 12 = 13 and electrons = 12 in a neutral atom [B1].

(b) The isotopes have the same number of electrons, so the same electron configuration, and the electrons decide how an atom reacts. [B1]

\({}^{26}\mathrm{Mg}\) has two more neutrons, so each atom has a greater mass. The same number of atoms fill about the same volume, so the density is greater. [B1]
:::

The masses of isotopes, and how often each one occurs, give the relative atomic mass of an element. That calculation is in [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles), and mass spectra, which measure isotopes directly, are in [Infrared spectroscopy and mass spectrometry](#chem.analysis).

## Shells, sub-shells and orbitals

You already know that electrons sit in shells around the nucleus. This section gives the full picture used at A Level. It has three levels: shells contain sub-shells, and sub-shells contain orbitals.

:::def Shell (principal quantum number, n)
A group of electrons at a similar distance from the nucleus and with similar energy. Shells are numbered \(n = 1, 2, 3, \dots\) from the nucleus outwards. The number \(n\) is the principal quantum number. Higher \(n\) means higher energy and, on average, further from the nucleus.
:::

:::def Sub-shell
A subdivision of a shell, labelled s, p, d or f. Each shell \(n\) contains \(n\) kinds of sub-shell: shell 1 has only 1s; shell 2 has 2s and 2p; shell 3 has 3s, 3p and 3d.
:::

:::def Orbital
A region of space around the nucleus that can hold up to two electrons, which must have opposite spins.
:::

Electrons behave as if they spin, and there are two possible directions of spin. Two electrons can share one orbital only if they spin in opposite directions. In diagrams a spin is drawn as an arrow, ↑ or ↓, so a full orbital is ↑↓.

| Sub-shell | Number of orbitals | Most electrons it holds |
|---|---|---|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |

So shell 1 holds 2 electrons (1s), shell 2 holds 2 + 6 = 8 (2s, 2p), and shell 3 holds 2 + 6 + 10 = 18 (3s, 3p, 3d). The syllabus asks about the elements hydrogen to krypton, so you need 1s to 4p.

### Orbital shapes

An orbital is not a path that an electron follows. It is the region in which the electron is most likely to be found: a "cloud" of negative charge.

- An **s orbital** is a **sphere** around the nucleus. A 2s orbital is a larger sphere than a 1s.
- A **p orbital** is a **dumbbell**: two lobes on opposite sides of the nucleus. There are three p orbitals in each p sub-shell, pointing along the x, y and z axes at right angles to one another, and the nucleus is at the centre of each dumbbell.

### The order of filling

In the ground state, electrons occupy the lowest energy orbitals available.

:::def Ground state
The arrangement of the electrons of an atom (or ion) that has the lowest total energy: every electron is in the lowest-energy orbital available.
:::

The sub-shells fill in order of increasing energy:

\[\text{1s} < \text{2s} < \text{2p} < \text{3s} < \text{3p} < \text{4s} < \text{3d} < \text{4p}\]

The surprise is that 4s is lower in energy than 3d. The energy of an orbital depends on the whole atom, not just on its shell number, and for potassium and calcium the 4s orbital turns out lower than the 3d. So 4s fills first, and then the 3d sub-shell fills.

### Putting electrons into boxes

Three rules decide where the electrons of an atom go:

:::key Filling the orbitals
1. Fill the lowest energy orbital first (the order above).
2. An orbital holds at most two electrons, with opposite spins (↑↓).
3. In a sub-shell with more than one orbital (p or d), put one electron in each orbital, all with the same spin, before pairing any of them.
:::

Rule 3 is explained by repulsion. Electrons are negative, so they repel one another. Two electrons in the same orbital are squeezed into the same region of space, so they repel more than two electrons in different orbitals. The lowest energy arrangement keeps electrons apart by using empty orbitals of the same energy before pairing up. (This idea is known as Hund's rule, but the name is optional in the exam.)

Nitrogen has 7 electrons: 1s² 2s² 2p³. The three 2p electrons go into three separate orbitals, so nitrogen has **three unpaired electrons**. Oxygen has 8 electrons: 1s² 2s² 2p⁴. The fourth 2p electron has to pair up in one of the orbitals, so oxygen has **two** unpaired electrons.

### Writing configurations

The **full configuration** lists every occupied sub-shell with the number of electrons as a superscript. The **shorthand** replaces the inner electrons by the symbol of the noble gas that has the same arrangement, in square brackets. Cambridge writes the 3d sub-shell before the 4s, so iron is:

- full: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s²
- shorthand: [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²

For the elements from scandium to zinc, the 3d sub-shell fills after 4s:

| Element | Z | Shorthand configuration |
|---|---|---|
| Sc | 21 | [Ar] 3d¹ 4s² |
| Ti | 22 | [Ar] 3d² 4s² |
| V | 23 | [Ar] 3d³ 4s² |
| **Cr** | 24 | **[Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹** |
| Mn | 25 | [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² |
| Fe | 26 | [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² |
| Co | 27 | [Ar] 3d⁷ 4s² |
| Ni | 28 | [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s² |
| **Cu** | 29 | **[Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹** |
| Zn | 30 | [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² |

Two elements break the pattern. Chromium is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹, not 3d⁴ 4s², and copper is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, not 3d⁹ 4s². The 3d and 4s sub-shells are very close in energy, so moving one electron from 4s into 3d can lower the total energy. In chromium, it gives six unpaired electrons, each in its own orbital, and so reduces the repulsion between electrons. In copper it gives a completely full 3d sub-shell.

:::warn The Cr and Cu exceptions
If you write Cu as [Ar] 3d⁹ 4s² or Cr as [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s², you lose the mark. Remember "half-filled 3d⁵" and "full 3d¹⁰" as the two special cases. Every other element from Sc to Zn follows the rule 3dˣ 4s².
:::

## Configurations of ions and free radicals

To write the configuration of an **ion**, start from the neutral atom and add or remove electrons.

- **Negative ions** gain electrons, which go into the next empty orbitals as usual. \(\ce{Cl-}\) has 18 electrons: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶, which is the same as argon.
- **Positive ions** lose electrons from the **outermost** shell first. \(\ce{Na+}\) loses its 3s electron and has the configuration of neon, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) loses both its 3s electrons and has the same configuration.
- **Transition elements lose their 4s electrons before their 3d electrons.** The 4s electrons are in the outer shell (\(n = 4\)), further from the nucleus, so they are removed first even though 4s fills first. So \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) is [Ar] 3d⁶ and \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) is [Ar] 3d⁵.

:::def Isoelectronic
Species (atoms or ions) with the same number of electrons and so the same electron configuration, such as \(\ce{N^3-}\), \(\ce{O^2-}\), \(\ce{F-}\), Ne, \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{Al^3+}\), which all have 1s² 2s² 2p⁶.
:::

:::eg Write the full electron configurations of Cu and of \(\ce{Cu^2+}\).
Copper has proton number 29 and is one of the exceptions: the atom is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, shorthand [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

The \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) ion has 29 − 2 = 27 electrons. The first electron comes from the 4s, which empties it. The second comes from the 3d, because 4s is empty and 3d is next. So the 3d sub-shell loses one electron, from 3d¹⁰ to 3d⁹.

\(\ce{Cu^2+}\): 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁹, shorthand [Ar] 3d⁹. It has one unpaired electron. Its partly filled 3d sub-shell is also why copper(II) compounds are coloured, as [Complexes: colour, isomerism and stability](#chem.complexes) explains.
:::

### Free radicals

:::def Free radical
A species with one or more unpaired electrons.
:::

A chlorine atom has the configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵. In the 3p sub-shell, two orbitals hold pairs and the third holds a single electron, so it has one unpaired electron: it is a free radical, written \(\ce{Cl^.}\) The dot represents the unpaired electron. Free radicals are very reactive, and you will meet them as the reactive intermediates in the substitution of alkanes in [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons). An atom of hydrogen, with 1s¹, is also a free radical. So is an oxygen atom, with two unpaired 2p electrons. Transition-metal atoms and ions such as \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) also have unpaired electrons, but in practice chemists do not call them free radicals: the term is used for reactive species such as \(\ce{Cl^.}\) and \(\ce{CH3^.}\).

## Atomic and ionic radius

The **atomic radius** is a measure of the size of an atom. To explain trends, you need two ideas. The first is that the outer electrons are pulled in by the nucleus, and the second is that the inner electrons **shield** the outer ones from the full pull.

:::def Shielding
The repulsion of outer electrons by inner-shell electrons, which reduces the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons.
:::

**Across a period** (Na to Cl, say), the atomic radius **decreases**. Each step adds one proton to the nucleus and one electron to the same outer shell. The new electron is in the same shell, so it is not shielded any better by the extra electron beside it, but the inner shells stay the same. So the nuclear charge increases while the shielding stays about the same. The attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons is greater, and it pulls them closer.

**Down a group** (Li to Cs, say), the atomic radius **increases**. Each atom has one more shell than the one above, so the outer electrons are further from the nucleus, and there are more inner shells shielding them. The nuclear charge also increases, but the extra shell outweighs it.

### Ions compared with their atoms

- A **positive ion is smaller than its atom**. The metal atom usually loses its whole outer shell, so there is one fewer shell. Even when it does not, the protons now pull on fewer electrons and there is less repulsion between electrons.
- A **negative ion is larger than its atom**. The extra electrons repel one another and the outer shell expands, while the nuclear charge is unchanged.

For **isoelectronic ions**, the number of electrons is fixed, so the only thing that changes is the number of protons. The more protons, the stronger the pull on the same ten electrons, and the smaller the ion.

| Ion | \(\ce{N^3-}\) | \(\ce{O^2-}\) | \(\ce{F-}\) | \(\ce{Na+}\) | \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) | \(\ce{Al^3+}\) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protons | 7 | 8 | 9 | 11 | 12 | 13 |
| Electrons | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 | 10 |
| Size | largest | | | | | smallest |

Across period 3, the ionic radius falls from \(\ce{Na+}\) to \(\ce{Al^3+}\), then jumps up for the negative ions \(\ce{P^3-}\), \(\ce{S^2-}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\), because those ions have an extra electron shell (the third shell is full) compared with \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{Al^3+}\), which have lost the third shell. Then it falls again from \(\ce{P^3-}\) to \(\ce{Cl-}\).

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why the atomic radius of chlorine is smaller than that of sodium. [3 marks]
Sodium and chlorine are in the same period: their outer electrons are in the same shell, the third shell. [B1]

Chlorine has 17 protons and sodium has 11, so the nuclear charge is greater in chlorine while the shielding by inner electrons is about the same. [B1]

The attraction between the nucleus and the outer electrons is therefore stronger in chlorine, which pulls them closer, giving a smaller radius. [B1]
:::

:::eg Explain why \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is smaller than \(\ce{Na+}\).
Both ions have the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, so they have the same number of electrons (10) and the same shielding.

\(\ce{Mg^2+}\) has 12 protons and \(\ce{Na+}\) has 11, so \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) has the greater nuclear charge. It pulls the same 10 electrons more strongly, so its radius is smaller.
:::

## Ionisation energy

To remove an electron from an atom you have to supply energy, because the electron is attracted to the nucleus. The energy needed is a direct measure of how strongly the electron is held, so ionisation energies tell you about the structure of the atom.

:::def First ionisation energy
The energy required to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
:::

The equation for the first ionisation energy of sodium is

\[\ce{Na(g) -> Na+(g) + e-}\]

The state symbols matter. The atoms must be **gaseous** so that there are no forces between atoms to complicate the measurement, and the ions are gaseous as well. The energy is given per mole in kJ mol⁻¹, and ionisation is always endothermic, so the value is always positive.

:::def Second ionisation energy
The energy required to remove one electron from each ion in one mole of gaseous 1+ ions to form one mole of gaseous 2+ ions.
:::

\[\ce{Na+(g) -> Na^2+(g) + e-}\]

The third, fourth and later ionisation energies follow the same pattern, each time removing one electron from the ion formed in the step before. The equation for the third ionisation energy of aluminium is \(\ce{Al^2+(g) -> Al^3+(g) + e-}\).

:::warn Write the state symbols, and one electron at a time
"The energy to remove two electrons from sodium" is not the second ionisation energy. An ionisation energy equation removes exactly one electron, shows (g) on every species, and has \(\ce{e-}\) on the right. Missing (g) loses a mark.
:::

### Four factors

How large is an ionisation energy? It depends on the attraction between the nucleus and the electron being removed, which has four causes.

:::key Factors that control ionisation energy
- **Nuclear charge**: more protons, stronger attraction, higher ionisation energy.
- **Atomic radius**: the further the electron is from the nucleus, the weaker the attraction, so the lower the ionisation energy.
- **Shielding**: more inner electrons (in inner shells and sub-shells) repel the outer electron, reducing the attraction, so the ionisation energy is lower.
- **Spin-pair repulsion**: two electrons paired in one orbital repel one another more strongly than two electrons in separate orbitals, so a paired electron is easier to remove.
:::

### Trend down a group

Going down a group, the first ionisation energy **decreases**. For Group 1 the values are Li 519, Na 494, K 418, Rb 403 and Cs 376 kJ mol⁻¹, and for Group 2 Be 900, Mg 736, Ca 590, Sr 548 and Ba 502 kJ mol⁻¹. Going down, the atoms gain a shell, so the outer electron is further from the nucleus and is shielded by more inner shells. These two effects outweigh the increase in nuclear charge, so the attraction to the outer electron falls and less energy is needed to remove it.

Four features need explaining.

1. **A general increase across a period** (Li to Ne, Na to Ar). Each element has one more proton than the one before, while the extra electron goes into the same shell, so shielding hardly changes and the atomic radius gets smaller. The attraction to the outer electron increases, so more energy is needed to remove it.
2. **A sharp drop from each noble gas to the next alkali metal** (Ne 2080 to Na 494, Ar 1520 to K 418). The new outer electron is in a **new shell**, further from the nucleus and shielded by all the electrons of the full shell beneath it. These effects outweigh the extra proton.
3. **A small dip from Be to B and from Mg to Al** (900 to 799, 736 to 577). The outer electron of boron is in a **2p** sub-shell, which has a higher energy than 2s and is slightly further from the nucleus, and it is shielded by the 2s electrons as well as the 1s. The same explanation holds for aluminium, where the electron is removed from 3p instead of 3s.
4. **A small dip from N to O and from P to S** (1400 to 1310, 1060 to 1000). In nitrogen, each 2p electron is alone in its orbital. In oxygen, the fourth 2p electron is paired with another in the same orbital, and **spin-pair repulsion** between the two electrons makes it easier to remove, even though the nuclear charge is greater. Sulfur is the same with 3p⁴.

:::exam Explaining a trend
Examiners reward named causes in the right order: say which **electron** is removed and from which **sub-shell**, compare **nuclear charge**, **distance** and **shielding** (or spin-pair repulsion), then conclude with **attraction** and **energy**. "It is easier to lose" without naming the attraction is not enough.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why the first ionisation energy of magnesium (736 kJ mol⁻¹) is greater than that of aluminium (577 kJ mol⁻¹). [3 marks]
The outer electron of magnesium is in a 3s sub-shell; the outer electron of aluminium is in a 3p sub-shell. [B1]

The 3p sub-shell is higher in energy and further from the nucleus than 3s, and a 3p electron is shielded by the 3s electrons as well as the inner shells. [B1]

So the attraction between the nucleus and the outer electron is weaker in aluminium, and less energy is needed to remove it, despite aluminium's extra proton. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) The first ionisation energy of oxygen (1310 kJ mol⁻¹) is lower than that of nitrogen (1400 kJ mol⁻¹). Explain why. [3 marks]
Nitrogen is 1s² 2s² 2p³, with one electron in each 2p orbital. Oxygen is 1s² 2s² 2p⁴: the electron removed comes from a 2p orbital that contains a pair. [B1]

The two electrons in the same orbital repel one another (spin-pair repulsion). [B1]

This repulsion makes the paired electron easier to remove, so less energy is needed for oxygen than for nitrogen even though oxygen has one more proton. [B1]
:::

### Successive ionisation energies

An atom with several electrons has several ionisation energies. The \(n\)th ionisation energy is for removing the \(n\)th electron, one at a time. Each one is **larger than the last**, because the electron is being pulled from an ion with a higher positive charge: the same number of protons now attracts fewer electrons, there is less repulsion between the remaining electrons, and the ion is smaller.

The increases are not smooth. When the next electron has to come from an **inner shell**, the ionisation energy jumps, because the inner shell is closer to the nucleus and has less shielding. The jump shows how many electrons are in the outer shell. Aluminium has the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p¹: three electrons in the outer shell, which are removed first, and then one from the second shell.

## Using ionisation data in the exam

Two kinds of question appear often.

**Explain a pattern.** Choose from the four factors, naming the sub-shell of the electron removed. Never say "more protons" as a reason for a *decrease*, and never give "a half-full sub-shell is stable" as the reason for the dip at oxygen or sulfur: name the cause, spin-pair repulsion between the two electrons in one orbital.

**Deduce from data.** Look for the biggest jump in a list of successive ionisation energies. The electrons removed before the jump are the outer shell. For a first ionisation energy list, check whether a dip happens at the start of a p sub-shell (B, Al) or at the pairing of electrons in p orbitals (O, S).

:::tip A checklist for ionisation energy answers
1. Name the electron and its sub-shell (3s, 3p, ...).
2. Compare **nuclear charge**, **distance** and **shielding** (or **spin-pair repulsion**).
3. Say what that does to the **attraction** between the nucleus and the electron.
4. Finish with the energy needed.
:::

:::try Start easy, finish at exam standard
Q: How many protons, neutrons and electrons are in \({}^{27}_{13}\mathrm{Al}^{3+}\)?
A: Protons 13; neutrons 27 − 13 = 14; electrons 13 − 3 = 10.
Q: How many neutrons are in \({}^{37}_{17}\mathrm{Cl}^{-}\), and how many electrons?
A: Neutrons 37 − 17 = 20. Electrons 17 + 1 = 18.
Q: Write the full electron configuration of (a) phosphorus (Z = 15), (b) \(\ce{Ca^2+}\), (c) \(\ce{Br-}\) (Z = 35).
A: (a) 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p³. (b) Calcium (Z = 20) is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s²; it loses the 4s² pair: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶. (c) Bromine has 35 electrons and the ion has 36: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶.
Q: Write the shorthand configurations of (a) Mn, (b) \(\ce{Mn^2+}\), (c) \(\ce{Zn^2+}\).
A: (a) [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s². (b) The two 4s electrons go: [Ar] 3d⁵. (c) Zinc is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s², and the 4s pair is lost: [Ar] 3d¹⁰.
Q: How many unpaired electrons are in (a) a carbon atom (1s² 2s² 2p²), (b) a sulfur atom, (c) \(\ce{Fe^3+}\)?
A: (a) The two 2p electrons are in separate orbitals: 2. (b) Sulfur is [Ne] 3s² 3p⁴, so one pair and two single electrons: 2. (c) \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) is [Ar] 3d⁵, with each 3d orbital holding one electron: 5.
Q: Write the equation for the second ionisation energy of calcium, with state symbols.
A: \(\ce{Ca+(g) -> Ca^2+(g) + e-}\)
Q: Put in order of increasing radius: \(\ce{Mg^2+}\), \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Al^3+}\), \(\ce{F-}\).
A: \(\ce{Al^3+}\) < \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) < \(\ce{Na+}\) < \(\ce{F-}\). All four have 10 electrons, so the more protons, the smaller the ion: 13, 12, 11 and 9 protons.
Q: Explain why the first ionisation energy of potassium (418) is lower than that of sodium (494).
A: The outer electron of potassium is in the 4th shell, further from the nucleus than sodium's in the 3rd shell, and it is shielded by an extra full shell. These outweigh potassium's greater nuclear charge, so the attraction is weaker and less energy is needed.
Q: The first four ionisation energies of an element are 590, 1150, 4940 and 6480 kJ mol⁻¹. Which group is it in?
A: The big jump is after the second, so there are two outer electrons: Group 2 (this is calcium).
Q: ★★ Using the sodium sketch as a model, state which electron numbers show the big jumps in the successive ionisation energies of aluminium (Z = 13), and which shell each group of electrons is in.
H: Write the shell populations of aluminium first: 2, 8, 3.
A: Aluminium has 13 electrons, 3 | 8 | 2 from the outer shell inwards, so the big jumps come after electron 3 (electron 4 is the first from the second shell) and after electron 11 (electron 12 is the first from the first shell). Electrons 1 to 3 are in the third shell, 4 to 11 in the second and 12 to 13 in the first.
:::

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [2 marks] ★ State what is meant by the term isotopes.
A: Atoms of the same element (with the same number of protons) [B1] with different numbers of neutrons. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in a \({}^{65}_{30}\mathrm{Zn}^{2+}\) ion.
A: Protons: 30. [B1] Neutrons: 65 − 30 = 35. [B1] Electrons: 30 − 2 = 28. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ Write the full electron configurations of (a) a vanadium atom (Z = 23), (b) a \(\ce{Cu+}\) ion, and (c) a \(\ce{Br-}\) ion.
A: (a) 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d³ 4s² [B1]
(b) Copper is 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ and loses the 4s electron: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰. [B1]
(c) 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁶ [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (a) Write equations, with state symbols, for the first and second ionisation energies of magnesium. (b) Explain why the second ionisation energy is greater than the first.
H: Magnesium has 12 protons. How many electrons does \(\ce{Mg+}\) have, and how does that change the pull on each?
A: (a) \(\ce{Mg(g) -> Mg+(g) + e-}\) [B1] and \(\ce{Mg+(g) -> Mg^2+(g) + e-}\) [B1]
(b) The second electron is removed from a positive ion, which is smaller than the atom. [B1] The same nuclear charge now attracts fewer electrons, with less repulsion between electrons, so the attraction to the outer electron is stronger. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ The first ionisation energy of sulfur (1000 kJ mol⁻¹) is lower than that of phosphorus (1060 kJ mol⁻¹). Explain why.
H: Write the 3p configuration of each atom using boxes.
A: Phosphorus is 3p³ with one electron in each of the three 3p orbitals. [B1] Sulfur is 3p⁴, so one 3p orbital contains a pair of electrons. [B1] There is spin-pair repulsion between the paired electrons. [B1] This makes it easier to remove an electron from sulfur, so less energy is needed, despite its greater nuclear charge. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ The first four ionisation energies of element Y are 590, 1150, 4940 and 6480 kJ mol⁻¹. (a) State the group of Y and explain your answer. (b) Write the equation for the third ionisation energy of Y. (c) Explain why the third ionisation energy is much larger than the second.
H: Look for the biggest jump in the values.
A: (a) Group 2. [B1] There is a very large jump between the second and third ionisation energies, so Y has two outer electrons. [B1]
(b) \(\ce{Y^2+(g) -> Y^3+(g) + e-}\) [B1]
(c) The third electron is removed from an inner shell, which is closer to the nucleus [B1] and is shielded less, so it is attracted much more strongly. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (a) Explain why a sodium ion is smaller than a sodium atom. (b) Explain why a chloride ion is larger than a chlorine atom. (c) Explain why the ionic radii of \(\ce{Na+}\), \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{Al^3+}\) decrease in that order.
H: Count the protons and electrons in each of the species in (c).
A: (a) The sodium atom loses its outer (third) shell when it forms \(\ce{Na+}\), so the ion has fewer shells. [B1] The remaining electrons are also attracted by the same nuclear charge with less repulsion. [B1]
(b) The chloride ion has one more electron than the chlorine atom but the same nuclear charge, so there is more repulsion between electrons in the outer shell and it expands. [B1] The nuclear attraction per electron is less. [B1]
(c) The three ions are isoelectronic (10 electrons). [B1] The number of protons increases (11, 12, 13), so the attraction on the same electrons increases and the radius decreases. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ Chromium has the configuration [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹, not [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s². (a) Draw the electrons-in-boxes diagram for the 3d and 4s sub-shells of chromium. (b) Suggest why this arrangement has the lower energy. (c) Write the shorthand configuration of \(\ce{Cr^3+}\). (d) State the number of unpaired electrons in a chromium atom.
H: Count how many electrons are unpaired in each version.
A: (a) 3d: five boxes each containing a single ↑ [B1]; 4s: one box containing a single ↑ [B1]
(b) The 3d and 4s orbitals are very close in energy, so promoting one 4s electron into the 3d sub-shell has little cost [B1] and gives six electrons in six separate orbitals, which reduces repulsion between electrons so the total energy is lower. [B1]
(c) The 4s electron is lost first, then two 3d electrons: [Ar] 3d³ [B1]
(d) 6 [B1]
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Gallium (Z = 31) is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p¹ and zinc (Z = 30) is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s². The first ionisation energy of gallium is 577 kJ mol⁻¹ and that of zinc is 908 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain why gallium's value is lower although its nuclear charge is greater.
H: Which sub-shell does the electron come from in each atom?
H: The 4p sub-shell is higher in energy than 4s. What shields it?
A: The electron removed from gallium is in a 4p sub-shell, but the one removed from zinc is in a 4s sub-shell. [B1] The 4p sub-shell is higher in energy and slightly further from the nucleus. [B1] A 4p electron is shielded by the 4s electrons as well as by the inner shells, so it is held more weakly. [B1] This outweighs the extra proton in gallium, so less energy is needed to remove it. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★★ The first four ionisation energies of phosphorus are 1060, 1900, 2920 and 4960 kJ mol⁻¹. A student says that phosphorus must be in Group 14 because there is no big jump in the first four values. (a) Explain why the student is wrong to draw this conclusion. (b) Which ionisation energy would show the big jump for phosphorus, and why?
H: A phosphorus atom has the configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p³. How many electrons are in its outer shell?
A: (a) A big jump appears only after all the outer electrons have been removed. [B1] No jump in the first four values shows only that there are at least four outer electrons, not exactly four. [B1]
(b) The sixth ionisation energy: phosphorus has five outer electrons (3s² 3p³), so the first five come from the third shell. [B1] The sixth electron comes from the second shell, which is much closer to the nucleus and less shielded, so the big jump is between the fifth and the sixth. [B1]

:::quiz
? Beams of protons, neutrons and electrons, all at the same velocity, pass through an electric field. Which statement is correct?
- The neutron beam is deflected towards the negative plate.
- The proton beam is deflected more than the electron beam because it is heavier.
+ The electron beam is deflected most, towards the positive plate.
- All three beams are deflected by the same amount.
> The deflection depends on charge ÷ mass. The electron has the same size of charge as the proton but about 1/1836 of the mass, so it is deflected much more, towards the positive plate. Neutrons have no charge and go straight on.

- 13
- 16
+ 10
- 24
> The atom has 13 electrons (equal to the proton number). The 3+ ion has lost 3, leaving 10. 16 adds the charge instead of subtracting it, and 24 subtracts the charge from the nucleon number (27 − 3).

- [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s²
- [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s²
- [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹
+ [Ar] 3d⁶
> Fe is [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². The 4s electrons are lost first, so \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) is [Ar] 3d⁶. [Ar] 3d⁴ 4s² wrongly removes 3d electrons, and [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² is the neutral atom.
? Which species is a free radical?
- \(\ce{Cl-}\)
+ \(\ce{Cl^.}\)
- \(\ce{Cl2}\)
- HCl
> A free radical has one or more unpaired electrons. A chlorine atom, [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵, has one unpaired 3p electron. \(\ce{Cl-}\) has a full outer shell, and \(\ce{Cl2}\) and HCl have all their electrons in pairs.
? Why is the first ionisation energy of potassium lower than that of sodium?

- Potassium has a smaller nuclear charge and atomic radius.
+ The outer electron of potassium is further from the nucleus and more shielded.
- Potassium's 4s electron is paired with another.
> Potassium has 19 protons and sodium has 11, so "fewer protons" is wrong, and the nuclear charge and radius cannot both be smaller. The extra shell puts the 4s electron further away and adds shielding, which outweighs the greater nuclear charge.

- \(\ce{Na+}\)
- \(\ce{Mg^2+}\)
- \(\ce{F-}\)
+ \(\ce{O^2-}\)

- Oxygen has fewer protons than nitrogen.
- Oxygen's outer electron is in a new shell.
+ Spin-pair repulsion between two electrons in one 2p orbital of oxygen.
- Oxygen's outer electron is in a 2s orbital.
> Oxygen has more protons (8) than nitrogen (7), and both outer electrons are in the 2p sub-shell. The fourth 2p electron of oxygen is paired with another, and their repulsion makes it easier to remove.

- It has exactly 3 electrons.
+ It has at least 4 electrons.
> The values increase steadily with no very large jump, so no electron up to the fourth has come from an inner shell. The outer shell has at least four electrons, and the jump could be later.

:::recap
- An atom has a tiny dense nucleus of protons (+1, mass 1) and neutrons (0, mass 1) with electrons (−1, mass 1/1836) in shells around it, and most of the atom is empty space. Proton number is the number of protons; nucleon number is protons plus neutrons.
- Neutrons = A − Z. Electrons = Z − charge. In an electric field neutrons go straight, protons bend towards the negative plate, and electrons bend towards the positive plate by about 1800 times as much (deflection is proportional to charge ÷ mass).
- Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number. Same chemistry (same electron configuration); different mass and density.
- Shell n, sub-shells s, p, d and orbitals: s has 1 orbital (2 electrons), p has 3 (6), d has 5 (10). An orbital holds two electrons with opposite spins. Order of energy: 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p. s orbitals are spheres and p orbitals are dumbbells.
- Fill the lowest energy first, and singly before pairing within p and d (repulsion). Cr is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. Transition elements lose 4s electrons first: Fe²⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁶ and Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵.
- A free radical has one or more unpaired electrons, such as Cl•.
- Atomic radius decreases across a period (more protons, same shell, similar shielding) and increases down a group (extra shell). Cations are smaller than their atoms and anions larger; isoelectronic ions get smaller as protons increase.
- First ionisation energy: the energy to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions. Factors: nuclear charge, radius, shielding, spin-pair repulsion. It falls down a group and generally rises across a period, with dips at B, Al (p sub-shell) and O, S (paired p electrons).
- Successive ionisation energies increase, with a big jump when an electron comes from an inner shell: the number of electrons before the jump is the number in the outer shell (the group).
:::
