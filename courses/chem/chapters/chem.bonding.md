## Why atoms bond

Two atoms bond when the arrangement they make has **lower energy** than the separate atoms. Every bond comes from the same force: **electrostatic attraction** between positive and negative charges. The positive nuclei attract the electrons and the electrons attract the nuclei. This pull is stronger than the repulsion between the two nuclei and between the electrons, so energy is released when a bond forms. The same amount of energy has to be put back in to break the bond.

Atoms of the noble gases have a full outer shell and almost never form bonds. Atoms of other elements can reach a similar full outer shell, usually 8 electrons (an octet) or 2 for hydrogen, in one of three ways:

- by **transferring** electrons from one atom to another, which gives oppositely charged ions that attract (ionic bonding);
- by **sharing** electrons between two atoms (covalent bonding);
- by **pooling** outer electrons in a lattice of positive ions (metallic bonding).

Which way an element chooses depends on how strongly its atoms attract electrons. That property has a name.

## Electronegativity

:::def Electronegativity
The power of an atom to attract electrons to itself.
:::

Electronegativity is measured on the **Pauling scale**. It has no units. Fluorine, the most electronegative element, is 4.0. Metals have low values and non-metals high values.

| Period | Group 1 | Group 2 | Group 13 | Group 14 | Group 15 | Group 16 | Group 17 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | Li 1.0 | Be 1.6 | B 2.0 | C 2.5 | N 3.0 | O 3.5 | F 4.0 |
| 3 | Na 0.9 | Mg 1.3 | Al 1.5 | Si 1.9 | P 2.2 | S 2.6 | Cl 3.0 |

Other values you need: H 2.1, K 0.8, Ca 1.0 and Br 2.6. You are given these values in the Data section of the exam.

### What decides electronegativity

You already know the three factors from [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms), because they are the same ones that decide ionisation energy:

1. **Nuclear charge**: more protons attract the bonding electrons more strongly.
2. **Atomic radius**: the closer the bonding electrons are to the nucleus, the stronger the attraction.
3. **Shielding** by inner shells and sub-shells: more inner electrons weaken the pull of the nucleus on the bonding electrons.

:::key Electronegativity trends
- **Across a period it increases.** The nuclear charge increases, the electrons go into the same shell so shielding is about the same, and the atomic radius decreases. The bonding electrons feel a stronger attraction.
- **Down a group it decreases.** The atomic radius increases and there are more inner shells, so the shielding increases. The bonding electrons are further from the nucleus and held less strongly.
:::

Check the table: along Period 3, Na 0.9, Mg 1.3, Al 1.5, Si 1.9, P 2.2, S 2.6, Cl 3.0 rises steadily. Down Group 17, F 4.0, Cl 3.0, Br 2.6 falls.

:::warn Electronegativity is not reactivity
A very electronegative atom is not always a "reactive" one, and you cannot explain electronegativity by saying "it wants electrons more". Give the three physical causes: nuclear charge, radius, shielding. Also keep it separate from electron affinity and ionisation energy: electronegativity describes an atom *in a bond*.
:::

:::eg Explain why chlorine is more electronegative than sodium, and why fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine. [B1 each]
Chlorine and sodium are in the same period. Chlorine has 17 protons and sodium 11, so chlorine has the larger nuclear charge. [B1]

Both outer shells are the third shell, so shielding is about the same, but chlorine's greater nuclear charge pulls the outer electrons closer (a smaller atomic radius), so the attraction for bonding electrons is stronger. [B1]

Fluorine and chlorine are in the same group. Fluorine's bonding electrons are in the second shell, closer to the nucleus and with fewer inner shells shielding them, than chlorine's in the third shell. [B1]
:::

### Predicting the type of bond

When two atoms with the same electronegativity bond, they share the pair equally. The more their electronegativities differ, the more the shared pair is pulled towards one atom. At the extreme, one atom takes the electrons completely and ions form. So the **difference** in electronegativity, Δχ, is a guide to the bonding:

| Difference in electronegativity | Bonding | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | non-polar covalent (equal sharing) | Cl–Cl |
| small, up to about 1.5 | polar covalent (unequal sharing) | H–Cl, 0.9 |
| large, around 2 or more | ionic (electrons transferred) | Na–Cl, 2.1 |

This is a guide, not a law: the change from covalent to ionic is gradual. In exam questions the values are clear-cut. A bond is **polar** when the shared pair is pulled towards one atom, so that atom carries a partial negative charge, δ−, and the other a partial positive charge, δ+. An example is \(\ce{H^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}}\) in hydrogen chloride; later chapters use the same idea for C–Cl in [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen).

:::eg Use the Pauling values to predict the type of bonding in (a) NaCl, (b) \(\ce{Cl2}\), (c) HCl, (d) CaF₂, (e) C–H bonds in methane.
(a) Na 0.9, Cl 3.0: Δχ = 3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1, which is large, so **ionic**.

(b) Both atoms are chlorine: Δχ = 0, so the pair is shared equally: **non-polar covalent**.

(c) H 2.1, Cl 3.0: Δχ = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, which is small, so **polar covalent**, with \(\ce{H^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}}\).

(d) Ca 1.0, F 4.0: Δχ = 4.0 − 1.0 = 3.0, so **ionic**.

(e) C 2.5, H 2.1: Δχ = 0.4, so a **slightly polar covalent** bond. It is so small that hydrocarbons are treated as non-polar.
:::

## Ionic bonding

:::def Ionic bonding
The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions (cations and anions).
:::

A metal atom with a low electronegativity loses its outer electrons easily. A non-metal atom with a high electronegativity gains them. The metal becomes a positive ion (cation) and the non-metal a negative ion (anion). The attraction between the ions is the ionic bond.

### Dot-and-cross diagrams

A **dot-and-cross diagram** shows only the outer shell of each atom. The electrons of one atom are drawn as dots and the electrons of the other as crosses, so you can see where each electron came from. Electrons are all identical: the dots and crosses are only a bookkeeping trick. For ionic compounds, draw each ion in **square brackets** with its charge outside the top right corner.

Read the sodium chloride row. The sodium atom (2,8,1) has one outer electron. It transfers it to the chlorine atom (2,8,7), which now has 8 outer electrons. The sodium ion, \(\ce{Na+}\), has the configuration of neon, 2,8, so its outer shell is the second shell with 8 electrons. The chloride ion, \(\ce{Cl-}\), has the configuration of argon, 2,8,8. In magnesium oxide the magnesium atom (2,8,2) transfers **two** electrons to the oxygen atom (2,6), giving \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\), both 2,8. In calcium fluoride the calcium atom has two outer electrons but a fluorine atom can accept only one, so **two** fluorine atoms are needed: \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) and two \(\ce{F-}\).

The ionic bond is the attraction between each ion and all the oppositely charged ions around it, so it acts in every direction. The result is a **giant ionic lattice**, a huge regular array of ions, not separate pairs. This is why the formula of an ionic compound is the simplest ratio of ions and why "a molecule of NaCl" is wrong: say "a formula unit of NaCl". The structure and properties of ionic solids, including why MgO has a much higher melting point than NaCl, are explained in [States of matter](#chem.states).

:::warn Check the electron count
The commonest error in a dot-and-cross diagram is the wrong number of electrons. Count first: the number of outer electrons is the group number (Group 1: 1, Group 2: 2, Group 13: 3, ..., Group 17: 7). Every ion you draw in an exam must show a complete outer shell (8 electrons, or 2 for \(\ce{Li+}\)). A metal cation drawn with its outer electron still in place, or an anion with 7, has not been finished.
:::

## Metallic bonding

:::def Metallic bonding
The electrostatic attraction between the positive metal ions and the delocalised electrons.
:::

The atoms of a metal have low electronegativity, so they hold their outer electrons only loosely. In a piece of metal these outer electrons leave their atoms and move freely through the whole structure. They are **delocalised**. What remains are positive ions, in a regular lattice, surrounded by this "sea" of electrons.

The bond is the attraction between every positive ion and the electrons all round it. You will use this in [Periodicity](#chem.periodicity) and [States of matter](#chem.states) to explain melting points, electrical conductivity and why metals can be hammered into shape.

## Covalent bonding

:::def Covalent bonding
The electrostatic attraction between the nuclei of two atoms and a shared pair of electrons.
:::

When two non-metal atoms meet, neither can take an electron from the other (their electronegativities are similar), so they **share**. The shared pair of electrons sits between the two nuclei and both nuclei attract it. Each atom then "counts" the shared pair as part of its own outer shell. A **single bond** is one shared pair, a **double bond** two shared pairs and a **triple bond** three. Outer-shell pairs that are not shared are **lone pairs**.

The diagrams below show the outer shells as circles that overlap. Each circle holds the electrons of one atom: dots for one atom and crosses for the other. A shared pair sits in the overlap, one dot and one cross.

Count the electrons around each atom in the diagram: every atom has a full outer shell when you count the shared pairs for both atoms (2 for each H, 8 for each Cl, O and N). Look at the pattern for the number of bonds an atom forms: H forms 1, O forms 2, N forms 3, C forms 4. It is the number of electrons the atom needs to complete its shell.

:::steps
1. Count the outer (valence) electrons of every atom: the group number for main-group elements. Add one electron for each negative charge on an ion, subtract one for each positive charge. This is the total to draw.
2. Choose the central atom: usually the one that forms the most bonds and is **not** hydrogen. Put a shared pair between each pair of bonded atoms.
3. Give each outer atom a full shell with lone pairs (2 electrons in total for H, 8 for others).
4. Put any electrons that are left on the central atom as lone pairs.
5. If the central atom has fewer than 8 electrons, move a lone pair from an outer atom into a shared pair to make a double (or triple) bond.
6. Check the total number of electrons against step 1.
:::

:::eg Draw dot-and-cross diagrams for (a) water, \(\ce{H2O}\), (b) ammonia, \(\ce{NH3}\), (c) carbon dioxide, \(\ce{CO2}\).
(a) Total outer electrons: 6 (O) + 2 × 1 (H) = 8, which is 4 pairs. O is central with a shared pair to each H, using 2 pairs. The other 2 pairs are lone pairs on O. O has 2 shared + 2 lone = 4 pairs = 8 electrons, and each H has 2.

(b) Total: 5 (N) + 3 × 1 (H) = 8, which is 4 pairs. N is central with a shared pair to each H, using 3 pairs. The fourth pair is a lone pair on N.

(c) Total: 4 (C) + 2 × 6 (O) = 16, which is 8 pairs. Two single C–O bonds use 2 pairs; each O is completed with 3 lone pairs, 6 pairs in all, which uses all 8 pairs. But now C has only 4 electrons (2 pairs). Move one lone pair from each O into a shared pair to make two double bonds. Now each O has 2 shared + 2 lone pairs and C has 4 shared pairs: all have 8 electrons, and the total is still 16.
:::

## Expanded octets

Carbon, nitrogen, oxygen and fluorine never have more than 8 electrons in their outer shell. The second shell has only s and p orbitals, which hold 8 electrons in total. But atoms of elements in **Period 3 and below**, such as phosphorus and sulfur, can have **more than 8** electrons around them. The third shell also has a 3d sub-shell, close in energy to 3s and 3p, so there is room: an electron can be promoted into an empty 3d orbital and used to form an extra bond. This is called an **expanded octet** (or expanded shell).

Phosphorus has 5 outer electrons and forms \(\ce{PCl5}\): five shared pairs, so 10 electrons around P. Sulfur has 6 outer electrons and forms \(\ce{SF6}\): six shared pairs, 12 electrons around S. Sulfur dioxide, \(\ce{SO2}\), has two double bonds and a lone pair on S, which is 10 electrons.

## Coordinate (dative covalent) bonding

In an ordinary covalent bond each atom supplies one electron to the shared pair. In a **coordinate bond** both electrons come from one atom.

:::def Coordinate (dative covalent) bond
A covalent bond in which both electrons of the shared pair are supplied by one atom, the donor, which has a lone pair. The other atom, the acceptor, has an empty orbital to receive it.
:::

Once formed, a coordinate bond is identical to any other covalent bond. The word "dative" only describes where its electrons came from. It is not ionic, even though it can look like a transfer. In a displayed formula it is drawn as an arrow pointing from the donor to the acceptor.

### The ammonium ion

Ammonia gas and hydrogen chloride gas react when they meet to give a white smoke of solid ammonium chloride, \(\ce{NH4Cl}\):

\[\ce{NH3(g) + HCl(g) -> NH4Cl(s)}\]

In ammonia the nitrogen atom has a lone pair. A hydrogen ion, \(\ce{H+}\), has no electron and an empty 1s orbital. The nitrogen lone pair forms a bond to the \(\ce{H+}\): \(\ce{NH3 + H+ -> NH4+}\). All four N–H bonds in \(\ce{NH4+}\) are the same, but one was formed by a coordinate bond.

### Aluminium chloride and the Al₂Cl₆ dimer

Aluminium chloride, \(\ce{AlCl3}\), has an aluminium atom with 3 outer electrons forming three bonds to chlorine, so only 6 electrons surround it. The aluminium atom is **electron-deficient** and has an empty 3p orbital. A chlorine atom of another \(\ce{AlCl3}\) has lone pairs, so it can donate one to the aluminium atom. Two \(\ce{AlCl3}\) molecules join to form a dimer, \(\ce{Al2Cl6}\), found in the vapour near its sublimation temperature.

\[\ce{2AlCl3 <=> Al2Cl6}\]

## Species with an odd number of electrons

Most molecules have an even number of electrons, so all electrons are in pairs. A few have an **odd** number, so one electron is **unpaired**. Such a species is a **free radical** (see [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms)). The methyl radical, \(\ce{CH3}\), has 4 + 3 = 7 outer electrons: three shared pairs with H, and one unpaired electron on carbon (see the methane figure). Radicals are reactive because they pair up the electron.

Nitrogen dioxide, \(\ce{NO2}\), is a stable example. Total outer electrons: 5 + 2 × 6 = 17. The nitrogen atom forms a double bond to one oxygen atom and a coordinate bond to the other, and keeps one unpaired electron. The dative bond is drawn with both electrons as dots from nitrogen.

## σ and π bonds

Covalent bonds form when atomic orbitals overlap. There are two ways they can overlap.

:::def σ bond
A covalent bond formed by the direct (end-on) overlap of atomic orbitals along the line joining the nuclei of the two atoms.
:::

:::def π bond
A covalent bond formed by the sideways overlap of adjacent p orbitals, with the electron density above and below the σ bond (above and below the line joining the nuclei).
:::

A **single bond** is always one σ bond. A **double bond** is one σ and one π. A **triple bond** is one σ and two π, in two planes at right angles. Orbital overlap is better end-on than side-on, so a π bond on its own is **weaker** than a σ bond between the same two atoms. Because the π electrons are above and below the bond, a π bond is also more exposed to attack by electrophiles (species that accept a pair of electrons), which is why alkenes (hydrocarbons with a C=C double bond) are reactive (see [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons)).

|---|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{H2}\) | H–H | 1 | 0 |
| \(\ce{C2H6}\) | 1 C–C, 6 C–H | 7 | 0 |
| \(\ce{C2H4}\) | 1 C=C, 4 C–H | 5 | 1 |
| HCN | 1 C–H, 1 C≡N | 2 | 2 |
| \(\ce{N2}\) | 1 N≡N | 1 | 2 |

:::eg Give the number of σ and π bonds in (a) propene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CH2}\), and (b) ethanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3C#N}\).
(a) Propene: 6 C–H bonds (6 σ), 1 C–C single bond (1 σ), and 1 C=C double bond (1 σ and 1 π). Total: **8 σ and 1 π**.

(b) Ethanenitrile: 3 C–H bonds (3 σ), 1 C–C single bond (1 σ) and 1 C≡N triple bond (1 σ and 2 π). Total: **5 σ and 2 π**.

Check: every bond between two atoms contains exactly one σ bond, so the number of σ bonds equals the number of bonds drawn if you count each double or triple bond as one. Propene has 9 atoms joined in a chain with no rings, so it must have 9 − 1 = 8 σ bonds.
:::

## Hybridisation

Carbon has the configuration 1s² 2s² 2p², with only two unpaired electrons, but it forms four equal bonds in methane at 109.5° to one another. The explanation is **hybridisation**: an atom mixes (hybridises) its s and p orbitals to make new, equivalent **hybrid orbitals**. First one 2s electron is promoted into the empty 2p orbital, giving four unpaired electrons. Then the orbitals mix. The number of hybrid orbitals formed always equals the number of orbitals you mix.

- **sp³**: one s and three p orbitals make four sp³ orbitals, pointing to the corners of a tetrahedron at **109.5°**. Each forms a σ bond. Carbon in methane, \(\ce{CH4}\), and ethane, \(\ce{C2H6}\), is sp³.
- **sp²**: one s and two p orbitals make three sp² orbitals in one plane at **120°**, and one p orbital is left over, at right angles to the plane. The three sp² orbitals form σ bonds and the leftover p orbital forms a π bond with a neighbour. Each carbon in ethene, \(\ce{C2H4}\), is sp².
- **sp**: one s and one p orbital make two sp orbitals pointing in opposite directions at **180°**, and two p orbitals are left over, each forming a π bond. The carbon in HCN (and each carbon in ethyne) is sp; the nitrogen in \(\ce{N2}\) is also sp.

:::key Counting hybridisation
For an atom in a molecule, count the regions of electron density: σ bonds plus lone pairs (a double or triple bond counts as one σ region). 4 regions: sp³ (109.5° family), 3 regions: sp² (120°), 2 regions: sp (180°). The π bonds are made by the p orbitals that are left over.
:::

By this rule the nitrogen in ammonia (3 σ bonds and a lone pair) and the oxygen in water (2 σ bonds and 2 lone pairs) are also sp³. Their bond angles are a little smaller than 109.5° because lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs; see [Shapes of molecules and intermolecular forces](#chem.shapes).

## Bond energy and bond length

:::def Bond energy
The energy required to break one mole of a particular covalent bond in the gaseous state.
:::

:::def Bond length
The internuclear distance of two covalently bonded atoms.
:::

Bond energy is always positive, because energy is **put in** to break a bond. It is measured in kJ mol⁻¹. For a diatomic molecule such as \(\ce{N2}\) there is only one bond, so the value is exact. For a bond that appears in many molecules, such as C–H, the value is an **average** over many compounds.

| Bond | Bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ | Bond | Bond energy / kJ mol⁻¹ |
|---|---|---|---|
| C–C | 350 | H–F | 562 |
| C=C | 610 | H–Cl | 431 |
| C≡C | 840 | H–Br | 366 |
| N≡N | 944 | H–I | 299 |
| O=O | 496 | F–F | 158 |
| Cl–Cl | 242 | I–I | 151 |

Two patterns link bond energy and bond length. As the number of shared pairs increases, the nuclei are held closer together, so the bond gets **shorter** and **stronger**: C≡C is shorter and stronger than C=C, which is shorter and stronger than C–C. Between atoms of the same type, a longer bond is generally weaker, because the shared pair is further from the nuclei and attracted less.

Notice also that the C=C bond energy, 610, is **less than twice** the C–C value, 2 × 350 = 700. This shows that the second bond, the π bond, is weaker than the first, the σ bond.

### Using bond energy to compare reactivity

A reaction that breaks a strong bond needs a lot of energy, so it is usually slow. A reaction that breaks a weak bond is usually fast.

- **\(\ce{N2}\) is unreactive.** The N≡N triple bond has a bond energy of 944 kJ mol⁻¹, among the strongest of any molecule, so breaking it needs a large amount of energy. The molecule is also non-polar, so there is no δ+ atom for a nucleophile (a species that donates a lone pair to a δ+ atom) to attack. This is why nitrogen is used as an inert atmosphere, and why fixing nitrogen into ammonia needs high temperatures and a catalyst (see [Equilibria](#chem.equilibria)).
- **\(\ce{F2}\) is very reactive.** The F–F bond energy, 158 kJ mol⁻¹, is low compared with Cl–Cl, 242. The fluorine atoms are so small that the lone pairs on the two atoms repel each other and weaken the bond. Weak bond, so it breaks easily.
- **Hydrogen halides.** The H–X bond energies fall in the order H–F 562 > H–Cl 431 > H–Br 366 > H–I 299, as the bond lengthens. So HI breaks down on heating most easily, and HF is the most stable.
- **Halogenoalkanes.** The C–X bond energies, C–Cl 340, C–Br 280, C–I 240, explain why iodoalkanes (carbon chains with an iodine atom attached) are hydrolysed (broken down by reaction with water) fastest (see [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen)).

:::eg Explain why nitrogen gas is unreactive at room temperature but fluorine gas is very reactive. [3 marks]
Nitrogen has a triple bond, N≡N, with a very high bond energy (944 kJ mol⁻¹), so a very large amount of energy is needed to break it. [B1] The bond is also non-polar, so there is no δ+ site for another species to attack. [B1]

Fluorine has a single F–F bond with a low bond energy (158 kJ mol⁻¹), so little energy is needed to break it. [B1]
:::

:::eg Use bond energies to estimate the energy of the π bond in C=C. Explain whether your answer fits the description "a π bond is weaker than a σ bond".
Treat C=C as a σ bond plus a π bond. The σ bond is about the same as a C–C single bond, 350 kJ mol⁻¹. So the π bond is about 610 − 350 = 260 kJ mol⁻¹. [M1 A1]

260 is smaller than 350, so the π bond is weaker than the σ bond. This fits the description. [B1]
:::

:::warn A common wrong idea
"The double bond is twice as strong as a single bond" is false: 610 is not 700. And "π bonds are stronger than σ bonds" is also false. Alkenes react more easily than alkanes because the π bond is weak, **and** because its electrons are exposed above and below the molecule. Remember also that bond energy tells you how hard it is to break a bond, not how polar it is: the C–Cl bond is more polar than C–I but harder to break.
:::

:::eg Spot the error: Explain, using electronegativity values, why the bonding in sodium chloride is ionic and the bonding in hydrogen chloride is covalent. [3 marks]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Chlorine (3.0) is more electronegative than sodium (0.9), so the difference is 2.1, which is large.
Line 2: A large difference means that chlorine takes the electron from sodium to form ions, so NaCl is ionic.
Line 3: In HCl the difference is 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, which is small, so the electrons are shared equally and the bond is non-polar covalent.

Line 1 and line 2 are correct [B1] [B1]. **Line 3 has the slip: a difference of 0.9 is small but it is not zero, so the shared pair is pulled towards the more electronegative chlorine atom. The bond is polar covalent, \(\ce{H^{\delta+}-Cl^{\delta-}}\), and "equally" is wrong [B0].**

Correct line 3: the difference is small, so the electron pair is shared (covalent), but unequally, because chlorine is more electronegative: the bond is polar with H δ+ and Cl δ−. [B1]

The student scores 2 out of 3. Only identical atoms (Δχ = 0) share equally.
:::

:::recap
- A bond is electrostatic attraction. Atoms bond to lower their energy, reaching full outer shells by transfer (ionic), sharing (covalent) or pooling (metallic).
- Electronegativity is the power of an atom to attract electrons to itself. It increases across a period and decreases down a group (nuclear charge, radius, shielding). A large difference (around 2 or more, e.g. NaCl 2.1) gives ionic bonding; a small one gives a polar covalent bond; zero gives non-polar.
- Ionic bonding is the attraction between cations and anions in a giant lattice (NaCl, MgO, CaF₂). Never "a molecule of NaCl".
- Metallic bonding is the attraction between positive ions and delocalised electrons.
- A covalent bond is the attraction between two nuclei and a shared pair. Draw dot-and-cross diagrams after counting the outer electrons. Period 3 atoms can expand their octet (PCl₅ 10, SF₆ 12 electrons, SO₂ 10).
- A coordinate (dative) bond has both electrons from one atom (NH₄⁺, Al₂Cl₆ with 2 dative bonds). Radicals such as NO₂ and CH₃ have an unpaired electron.
- σ bond: end-on overlap; π bond: sideways overlap of p orbitals above and below the σ bond. Single = 1σ; double = 1σ + 1π; triple = 1σ + 2π. C₂H₄ has 5σ and 1π; HCN 2σ and 2π; N₂ 1σ and 2π.
- Hybridisation: sp³ 109.5° (4 regions), sp² 120° (3), sp 180° (2).
- Bond energy: energy to break one mole of a bond in the gas phase; bond length: internuclear distance. C–C 350, C=C 610, C≡C 840, N≡N 944, F–F 158. Stronger bonds are shorter and slower to break; a π bond (about 260) is weaker than a σ bond.
:::
