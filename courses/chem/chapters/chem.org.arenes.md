:::try Warm-up
Q: Ethene reacts with bromine at room temperature with no catalyst. Name the type of reaction and say what attacks the C=C bond. (from [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons))
A: Electrophilic addition. The electron-rich C=C bond attacks the δ+ end of the (polarised) bromine molecule, an electrophile.
Q: How many σ and how many π bonds are there in a C=C double bond, and how is the π bond formed? (from [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding))
A: One σ and one π. The π bond forms by sideways overlap of two p orbitals, giving electron density above and below the line joining the nuclei.
Q: What is a nucleophile, and why does it attack the carbon of a C–Br bond? (from [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen))
A: A nucleophile is an electron-pair donor. The carbon of C–Br carries a partial positive charge because bromine is more electronegative, so a lone pair is attracted to it.

## What is an arene?

Benzene has the molecular formula \(\ce{C6H6}\). Its six carbon atoms form a flat ring, each carbon joined to one hydrogen atom. Compounds that contain a benzene ring are called **arenes** (or aromatic compounds, because many of the first ones discovered had pleasant smells). Methylbenzene, \(\ce{C6H5CH3}\), is the simplest arene with a side chain.

:::def Arene
A hydrocarbon (or a compound derived from one) that contains a benzene ring, a ring of six carbon atoms with a delocalised π system.
:::

The formula \(\ce{C6H6}\) is far from saturated. A six-carbon alkane would be \(\ce{C6H14}\), so benzene is missing eight hydrogen atoms, and you might expect it to be as reactive as an alkene. It is not. Working out why takes up the first part of this chapter.

## The structure of benzene

In 1865 Kekulé proposed that benzene is a ring of six carbon atoms with alternating single and double bonds. This **Kekulé model** explains the formula and the 1 : 1 ratio of carbon to hydrogen, but experiments show it is wrong in three ways.

1. **It does not decolourise bromine water.** Cyclohexene, a real C=C compound, turns orange bromine water colourless at once by electrophilic addition. Benzene, which should have three C=C bonds, does not react with bromine water at all.
2. **All the carbon–carbon bonds are the same length.** X-ray diffraction shows a regular hexagon in which every C–C bond is 0.140 nm. A C–C single bond is 0.154 nm and a C=C double bond is 0.134 nm, so benzene's bonds are in between. A Kekulé ring would have three long and three short bonds. The syllabus Data section gives the same message in energy: the C–C bond energy in benzene is 520 kJ mol⁻¹, between C–C (350) and C=C (610).
3. **It is more stable than three double bonds would be.** The hydrogenation of benzene gives off much less heat than three separate C=C bonds would (worked example below).

### Sigma bonds and the delocalised π system

The modern model uses ideas from [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding). Each carbon atom is **sp² hybridised**: it uses three of its four outer orbitals to make three σ bonds (to two carbons and one hydrogen). These σ bonds lie in one plane with bond angles of 120°, so the molecule is **planar**: a flat regular hexagon.

Each carbon has one electron left, in a p orbital at right angles to the plane of the ring. The six p orbitals are side by side, so each overlaps sideways with both neighbours. The result is not three separate π bonds but one continuous ring of π electron density above the plane and another below it. The six electrons are **delocalised**: they belong to the whole ring, not to any one pair of atoms.

:::key The structure of benzene
Benzene is a planar hexagon. Each carbon is sp² hybridised, with σ bonds at 120° to two carbons and one hydrogen. The six p orbitals overlap sideways to give a **delocalised π system** above and below the ring. All six C–C bonds are equal in length (0.140 nm), between a single and a double bond. We draw benzene as a hexagon with a circle.
:::

This explains the three experiments. The delocalised electrons are shared round the whole ring, so the bonds are all equal (point 2). The same delocalisation lowers the energy of the molecule, which makes benzene more stable (point 3), and a stable ring does not give up its π electrons easily, so bromine water has no effect (point 1).

:::eg (Exam style) The enthalpy change of hydrogenation of cyclohexene (one C=C) is −120 kJ mol⁻¹. The enthalpy change of hydrogenation of benzene to cyclohexane is −208 kJ mol⁻¹. Use these data to show that benzene is more stable than the Kekulé structure suggests.
A Kekulé benzene would have three C=C bonds, each hydrogenated like the one in cyclohexene, so the predicted enthalpy change is \(3 \times (-120) = -360\) kJ mol⁻¹. [M1]

The measured value is −208 kJ mol⁻¹, so less energy is released than predicted. [M1]

The difference is \(-208 - (-360) = +152\) kJ mol⁻¹. [A1] Benzene has 152 kJ mol⁻¹ less energy than three localised C=C bonds would give it, so it is more stable: this is the stabilisation from delocalisation. [B1]
:::

## Naming aromatic compounds

The ring is the stem **benzene**. Most groups are named as prefixes: chloro-, bromo-, nitro-, methyl-. Number the ring from the carbon that carries the main group and give the lowest numbers.

A few groups change the stem, and the carbon that holds them is carbon 1:

| –CH₃ | methylbenzene | 2-chloromethylbenzene |

## Why benzene substitutes instead of adding

An alkene is attacked by an electrophile because its C=C bond is a region of high electron density. Benzene also has electron-rich π clouds above and below the ring, so it is attacked by electrophiles too. What it does next is different.

:::def Electrophilic substitution
A reaction in which an electrophile (an electron-pair acceptor) replaces a hydrogen atom of a molecule, here on an aromatic ring.
:::

If benzene took part in electrophilic **addition** like an alkene, the electrophile and a second group would add across the ring and use up part of the delocalised system. The product would lose the stabilisation worth 152 kJ mol⁻¹ that you calculated above. **Substitution** keeps the ring: the hydrogen leaves and the six π electrons end up delocalised again, so the stability is regained. Substitution is the reaction that keeps the delocalised ring, and that is why benzene follows that route.

The ring is also attacked less readily than a C=C bond. Its six π electrons are spread over six carbon atoms, so the electron density at any one place is lower than in the π bond of an alkene, and it cannot polarise a bromine molecule enough to react. Bromine, which attacks cyclohexene at once, does not attack benzene at all unless a catalyst turns it into a much stronger electrophile.

:::def Addition–elimination
A two-stage reaction in which a nucleophile first adds to a C=O carbon atom, and a small molecule or ion is then eliminated. You meet it with acyl chlorides in [Acyl chlorides and A Level carboxylic acids](#chem.org.acyl). Do not confuse it with electrophilic substitution: it is a different type of reaction on a different type of molecule.
:::

## The mechanism of electrophilic substitution

All the substitution reactions of the benzene ring follow one pattern, so learn it once.

:::steps
1. A reagent makes a strong electrophile, \(\ce{E+}\). The electrophile is generated in a separate step before it meets the ring.
2. Two electrons from the delocalised π system of the ring form a new σ bond to \(\ce{E+}\). This step is **slow**, because it breaks the delocalised system. The carbon that has bonded to E now has four σ bonds (it is sp³), so the other five carbons share only four π electrons. The result is a positively charged **intermediate**, drawn with a partial ring (a horseshoe arc) over the five carbons and a + inside.
3. The pair of electrons in the C–H bond of the sp³ carbon moves back into the ring, restoring the six delocalised electrons. \(\ce{H+}\) is lost. This step is **fast**, because the stable ring is regained.
4. The \(\ce{H+}\) combines with the negative ion formed in step 1, which regenerates the catalyst.
:::

### Nitration

Nitration is the substitution of a \(\ce{NO2}\) group. The reagents are **concentrated nitric acid with concentrated sulfuric acid**, at a temperature between 25 °C and 60 °C. Concentrated sulfuric acid is the stronger acid, so it protonates the nitric acid, which then loses water to give the **nitronium ion**, \(\ce{NO2+}\), the electrophile:

\[\ce{HNO3 + 2H2SO4 -> NO2+ + H3O+ + 2HSO4-}\]

The simpler form \(\ce{HNO3 + H2SO4 -> NO2+ + HSO4- + H2O}\) is also accepted.

The \(\ce{H+}\) that leaves reacts with \(\ce{HSO4-}\) to restore the sulfuric acid, so sulfuric acid is a catalyst:

\[\ce{H+ + HSO4- -> H2SO4}\]

\[\ce{C6H6 + HNO3 ->[\ce{H2SO4}][\text{25–60 °C}] C6H5NO2 + H2O}\]

Keep the temperature below 60 °C. A hotter mixture puts a second \(\ce{NO2}\) group on the ring.

### Halogenation

Benzene reacts with chlorine or bromine only in the presence of a **halogen carrier**: anhydrous \(\ce{AlCl3}\) with \(\ce{Cl2}\), or \(\ce{AlBr3}\) with \(\ce{Br2}\), at room temperature. The aluminium atom in \(\ce{AlBr3}\) has only six electrons in its outer shell and accepts a lone pair from a bromine molecule, which polarises it so strongly that it splits into the electrophile:

\[\ce{Br2 + AlBr3 -> Br+ + AlBr4-}\]

The ring attacks \(\ce{Br+}\) exactly as it attacked \(\ce{NO2+}\): the intermediate has H and Br on one carbon, and then the C–H electrons return to the ring. The \(\ce{H+}\) combines with \(\ce{AlBr4-}\) and regenerates the catalyst:

\[\ce{AlBr4- + H+ -> AlBr3 + HBr}\]

\[\ce{C6H6 + Br2 ->[\ce{AlBr3}] C6H5Br + HBr}\]

Chlorination works the same way with \(\ce{Cl2}\) and \(\ce{AlCl3}\), and gives chlorobenzene, \(\ce{C6H5Cl}\).

:::exam Drawing the mechanism of electrophilic substitution
Marks go for: the electrophile generated (with an equation: \(\ce{NO2+}\) from \(\ce{HNO3}\) and \(\ce{H2SO4}\), or \(\ce{Br+}\) from \(\ce{Br2}\) and \(\ce{AlBr3}\)); a curly arrow from the **ring** (the circle) to the electrophile, not the other way round; the intermediate with a partial ring (horseshoe), a + charge, and both H and E on the same carbon; a curly arrow from the **C–H bond** back into the ring; and the products, including \(\ce{H+}\) and the catalyst being regenerated. The most common slips are a full circle in the intermediate and an arrow that starts at the electrophile.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Describe the mechanism for the bromination of benzene, including the formation of the electrophile. [5 marks]

The products are bromobenzene and \(\ce{H+}\); the \(\ce{H+}\) reacts with \(\ce{AlBr4-}\) to regenerate \(\ce{AlBr3}\) and form \(\ce{HBr}\). [B1]
:::

The horseshoe species is an intermediate between the two steps. Do not draw a full circle inside it (the delocalisation is broken at that carbon), do not show a negative ion inside it, and do not forget the + charge.
:::

## The reactions of benzene and methylbenzene to learn

:::key Reactions of arenes
| Reaction | Reagent and conditions | Product from benzene |
|---|---|---|
| Halogenation (ring) | \(\ce{Cl2}\) with \(\ce{AlCl3}\), or \(\ce{Br2}\) with \(\ce{AlBr3}\), room temperature | chlorobenzene, bromobenzene |
| Nitration | concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C | nitrobenzene |
| Friedel–Crafts alkylation | \(\ce{CH3Cl}\) with \(\ce{AlCl3}\), heat | methylbenzene |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | \(\ce{CH3COCl}\) with \(\ce{AlCl3}\), heat | 1-phenylethanone |
| Hydrogenation | \(\ce{H2}\), Pt or Ni catalyst, heat | cyclohexane |
| Side-chain oxidation (methylbenzene) | hot alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then dilute acid | benzoic acid |
:::

### Friedel–Crafts reactions

Friedel–Crafts reactions build a new C–C bond to the ring. The halogenoalkane or acyl chloride is the source of the electrophile, and \(\ce{AlCl3}\) is the catalyst, as in halogenation. The ring attacks a positive carbon, and the mechanism is the same electrophilic substitution:

\[\ce{CH3Cl + AlCl3 -> CH3+ + AlCl4-}\]

\[\ce{CH3COCl + AlCl3 -> CH3CO+ + AlCl4-}\]

\[\ce{C6H6 + CH3Cl ->[\ce{AlCl3}][\text{heat}] C6H5CH3 + HCl}\]

\[\ce{C6H6 + CH3COCl ->[\ce{AlCl3}][\text{heat}] C6H5COCH3 + HCl}\]

The first is **alkylation** (an alkyl group, \(\ce{CH3}\), is added), the second **acylation** (an acyl group, \(\ce{CH3CO}\), is added). The acylation product, 1-phenylethanone, is a ketone with the ring on the C=O carbon.

### Hydrogenation

Under pressure at higher temperature, hydrogen adds to benzene in the presence of a nickel or platinum catalyst. All three formal double bonds are removed and the delocalisation is lost:

\[\ce{C6H6 + 3H2 ->[\text{Ni or Pt}][\text{heat}] C6H12}\]

The product is cyclohexane, \(\ce{C6H12}\). Cyclohexene reacts with hydrogen far more easily than benzene does, in line with benzene's extra stability.

### Oxidation of an alkyl side chain

The ring of methylbenzene survives hot, alkaline potassium manganate(VII) because it is so stable, but the side chain does not. Heating with alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\) oxidises the \(\ce{CH3}\) group to \(\ce{COO-}\), and the mixture is then acidified with dilute acid to turn the benzoate ion into benzoic acid:

\[\ce{C6H5CH3 + 3[O] -> C6H5COOH + H2O}\]

Any alkyl side chain, however long, is shortened to the single carbon of the COOH group.

:::lab Nitrating benzene safely
Benzene is a carcinogen, so schools and industry handle it as little as they can: methylbenzene is used instead where possible as a solvent. Nitration of methylbenzene is done in a fume cupboard with goggles and gloves. Add concentrated sulfuric acid to concentrated nitric acid slowly with cooling, add the arene a little at a time, and keep the mixture in a water bath at about 50 °C or below. Pour the mixture into cold water: the nitro compound separates as a pale yellow oil.
:::

:::eg Write an equation, with reagents and conditions, for each: (a) benzene to bromobenzene; (b) benzene to nitrobenzene; (c) benzene to cyclohexane.
(a) \(\ce{C6H6 + Br2 -> C6H5Br + HBr}\) with \(\ce{Br2}\) and an \(\ce{AlBr3}\) catalyst, room temperature. [B1]

(b) \(\ce{C6H6 + HNO3 -> C6H5NO2 + H2O}\) with concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) and concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), at 25–60 °C. [B1]

(c) \(\ce{C6H6 + 3H2 -> C6H12}\) with \(\ce{H2}\) and a nickel or platinum catalyst, heated. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 15.6 g of benzene is nitrated and 19.1 g of nitrobenzene is obtained. Calculate the percentage yield.
\(M_r(\ce{C6H6}) = 6(12.0) + 6(1.0) = 78.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C6H5NO2}) = 6(12.0) + 5(1.0) + 14.0 + 2(16.0) = 123.0\). [B1]

One mole of benzene gives one mole of nitrobenzene. \(n(\ce{C6H6}) = 15.6 / 78.0 = 0.200\) mol. [M1]

Maximum mass of nitrobenzene \(= 0.200 \times 123.0 = 24.6\) g. [M1]

Percentage yield \(= 19.1 / 24.6 \times 100 = 77.6\,\%\). [A1]
:::

## Methylbenzene: the ring or the side chain?

Methylbenzene has two places where chlorine or bromine can substitute: the ring, and the \(\ce{CH3}\) group. The conditions decide which.

:::cols
:::col Cl₂ with UV light, no catalyst
Free-radical substitution in the **side chain**, as with an alkane. Chlorine atoms form in the light and replace a hydrogen of the \(\ce{CH3}\) group:

\(\ce{C6H5CH3 + Cl2 -> C6H5CH2Cl + HCl}\)

The product is (chloromethyl)benzene. Further substitution can give \(\ce{C6H5CHCl2}\) and \(\ce{C6H5CCl3}\).
:::
:::col Cl₂ with AlCl₃, room temperature, no UV light
Electrophilic substitution in the **ring**. The \(\ce{CH3}\) group directs the incoming group to the 2- and 4-positions:

\(\ce{C6H5CH3 + Cl2 -> ClC6H4CH3 + HCl}\)

The products are 2-chloromethylbenzene and 4-chloromethylbenzene, formed together.
:::
:::

The same split applies to bromine. To remember it: **light, side chain; catalyst, ring**.

:::eg Methylbenzene reacts with chlorine under two sets of conditions. Give the reagents, the condition and the type of reaction in each case, and name the organic products. (a) Chlorine, ultraviolet light. (b) Chlorine, aluminium chloride, no ultraviolet light.
(a) Ultraviolet light breaks the Cl–Cl bond into chlorine atoms, and free-radical substitution takes place in the \(\ce{CH3}\) side chain. The main product is (chloromethyl)benzene, \(\ce{C6H5CH2Cl}\). [B1]

(b) With \(\ce{AlCl3}\) and no UV light the \(\ce{Cl+}\) electrophile attacks the ring (electrophilic substitution). The \(\ce{CH3}\) group directs to the 2- and 4-positions, so the products are 2-chloromethylbenzene and 4-chloromethylbenzene. [B1] [B1]
:::

## Directing effects: where does the next group go?

Once a ring carries one substituent, a second one does not go in at random. The first group directs the new group to particular positions, and also changes how fast the ring reacts.

:::key Directing effects of groups on the ring
| Group already on the ring | New group goes to | Effect on rate |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{-NH2}\), \(\ce{-OH}\), alkyl (\(\ce{-R}\), such as \(\ce{-CH3}\)) | the 2- and 4-positions (and 6) | activates: the ring reacts faster |
| \(\ce{-NO2}\), \(\ce{-COOH}\), \(\ce{-COR}\) (such as \(\ce{-COCH3}\)) | the 3-position (and 5) | deactivates: the ring reacts more slowly |
:::

The reason is the electron density of the ring. A group that **donates** electrons to the ring (\(\ce{-NH2}\) and \(\ce{-OH}\) through lone pairs on N and O, alkyl groups by the inductive effect) makes the π system richer in electrons, particularly at the 2-, 4- and 6-positions. A richer ring is attacked more readily by an electrophile, so the group is **activating**. A group that **withdraws** electrons (\(\ce{-NO2}\), \(\ce{-COOH}\) and \(\ce{-COR}\) have electronegative atoms attached to the ring carbon) makes the ring poorer in electrons and harder to attack. It is **deactivating**, and the new group goes where the ring has been least depleted, which is the 3-position.

Two examples with the same reagents:

\[\ce{C6H5CH3 ->[\ce{HNO3}, \ce{H2SO4}] CH3C6H4NO2}\quad\text{(2- and 4-nitromethylbenzene)}\]

\[\ce{C6H5NO2 ->[\ce{HNO3}, \ce{H2SO4}] C6H4(NO2)2}\quad\text{(1,3-dinitrobenzene)}\]

Methylbenzene nitrates more easily than benzene, at a lower temperature. Nitrobenzene needs harsher conditions (a higher temperature) than benzene and gives 1,3-dinitrobenzene, with the second \(\ce{NO2}\) group in the 3-position relative to the first.

:::eg Predict the organic product(s) of mononitration (a) of benzoic acid; (b) of methylbenzene. Name them and give the reason.
(a) The COOH group withdraws electrons and directs to the 3-position, so the product is 3-nitrobenzoic acid. [B1]

(b) The \(\ce{CH3}\) group donates electrons and directs to the 2- and 4-positions. The products are 2-nitromethylbenzene and 4-nitromethylbenzene. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Real context) TNT, the explosive, is 2,4,6-trinitromethylbenzene, made by nitrating methylbenzene three times. Explain (a) why the three \(\ce{NO2}\) groups end up in the 2-, 4- and 6-positions, and (b) why each nitration needs harsher conditions than the one before.
(a) The \(\ce{CH3}\) group directs to the 2-, 4- and 6-positions. Each \(\ce{NO2}\) group already on the ring directs to the 3- and 5-positions relative to itself, and after the first nitro group goes in at position 2 these are positions 4 and 6, the same ones. [B1] So both kinds of group agree, and the product is 2,4,6-trinitromethylbenzene. [B1]

(b) Each \(\ce{NO2}\) group withdraws electron density and deactivates the ring, so the ring is less attractive to the \(\ce{NO2+}\) electrophile and a higher temperature or stronger mixture is needed. [B1]
:::

## Planning a synthesis: the order of the steps

Directing effects mean that the **order** of the steps decides the product.

:::eg Suggest a two-step synthesis of 3-nitrobenzoic acid from methylbenzene. Explain why the steps must be done in this order.
Step 1: heat methylbenzene with alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then add dilute acid, to give benzoic acid (oxidation of the side chain). [B1]

Step 2: warm benzoic acid with concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) and concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) at 25–60 °C to give 3-nitrobenzoic acid. [B1]

The COOH group directs to the 3-position, so nitrating after oxidation puts the \(\ce{NO2}\) group where it is wanted. [B1] If methylbenzene were nitrated first, the \(\ce{CH3}\) group would direct to the 2- and 4-positions, and oxidation would then give 2- and 4-nitrobenzoic acid instead. [B1]
:::

## Halogenoarenes: why chlorobenzene is so unreactive

Chlorobenzene is made by chlorinating benzene with \(\ce{Cl2}\) and \(\ce{AlCl3}\). Chloroethane is hydrolysed when heated under reflux with NaOH(aq), and gives a precipitate with aqueous silver nitrate. Chlorobenzene does neither under those conditions. Two things stop a nucleophile reacting with it:

- A lone pair of electrons on the chlorine atom overlaps sideways with the π system of the ring and is drawn into it. This gives the C–Cl bond **partial double-bond character**, so it is stronger and shorter than a C–Cl bond in a halogenoalkane and harder to break.
- The ring has a high density of electrons above and below the plane. An incoming nucleophile has a lone pair and is repelled by this electron-rich ring.

:::key Halogenoarene reactivity
Chlorobenzene is much less reactive than chloroethane towards nucleophiles, because the chlorine lone pair delocalises into the ring (partial double-bond character, a stronger C–Cl bond), and the electron-rich ring repels nucleophiles.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Explain why chlorobenzene does not react with NaOH(aq) under the conditions in which chloroethane is hydrolysed to ethanol. [3 marks]
A lone pair on the chlorine atom overlaps with the delocalised π system of the ring. [B1]

This gives the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character, so the bond is stronger and harder to break than in chloroethane. [B1]

The electron-rich ring also repels the hydroxide ion, which is a nucleophile. [B1]
:::

:::recap
- Benzene \(\ce{C6H6}\): planar, sp² carbons, σ bonds at 120°, six p orbitals overlap into a delocalised π system above and below the ring. All C–C bonds are 0.140 nm. It is more stable than the Kekulé structure (hydrogenation −208 kJ mol⁻¹ against −360 predicted).
- Benzene undergoes electrophilic substitution, which keeps the delocalised ring; it does not decolourise bromine water.
- Mechanism: electrophile made; ring π electrons attack it (slow); horseshoe intermediate with + charge; C–H electrons return to the ring, \(\ce{H+}\) lost; catalyst regenerated.
- Nitration: concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) + concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C (\(\ce{NO2+}\)). Halogenation: \(\ce{Cl2}\)/\(\ce{AlCl3}\) or \(\ce{Br2}\)/\(\ce{AlBr3}\) (\(\ce{Cl+}\), \(\ce{Br+}\)).
- Friedel–Crafts: \(\ce{CH3Cl}\) + \(\ce{AlCl3}\), heat (alkylation); \(\ce{CH3COCl}\) + \(\ce{AlCl3}\), heat (acylation). Hydrogenation: \(\ce{H2}\), Pt or Ni, heat. Side-chain oxidation: hot alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then dilute acid, gives benzoic acid.
- Methylbenzene: UV light and \(\ce{Cl2}\) gives the side chain (free radical); \(\ce{AlCl3}\) and no UV gives 2- and 4-chloromethylbenzene (ring).
- Directing: \(\ce{-NH2}\), \(\ce{-OH}\), \(\ce{-R}\) give 2-/4- and activate; \(\ce{-NO2}\), \(\ce{-COOH}\), \(\ce{-COR}\) give 3- and deactivate. The order of steps matters in a synthesis.
- Chlorobenzene resists nucleophiles: the chlorine lone pair delocalises into the ring (partial double bond), and the electron-rich ring repels nucleophiles.
:::
