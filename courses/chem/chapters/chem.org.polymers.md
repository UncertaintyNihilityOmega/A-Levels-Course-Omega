@summary Polymers are long chains built from small molecules. You will recap addition polymerisation, then learn condensation polymerisation: how polyesters and polyamides (nylon, Kevlar, proteins) form with loss of water or HCl, how to draw a repeat unit and find the monomers of a polymer section, how to tell the two types apart, and why condensation polymers can be broken down by hydrolysis while poly(alkenes) cannot.

:::why
Plastics, clothing fibres, bottles, ropes, medical stitches and the proteins in your own body are all polymers. Two simple ideas explain almost all of them: some chains are built by opening C=C bonds, and others by joining two reactive groups with loss of a small molecule. This chapter shows how to draw each kind, how to work backwards from a polymer to its monomers, and why the way a polymer is joined together decides what happens to it when it is thrown away.
:::

**Before you start**, you should know esters from [Carboxylic acids, esters and nitriles](#chem.org.acids), acyl chlorides from [Acyl chlorides and A Level carboxylic acids](#chem.org.acyl), and amines and amides from [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen). Addition polymers were introduced in [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons) and are recapped below.

:::try Warm-up
Q: Write an equation for ethanoic acid reacting with ethanol, and name the ester and the small molecule formed. (from [Carboxylic acids, esters and nitriles](#chem.org.acids))
A: \(\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH <=> CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}\). The ester is ethyl ethanoate and the small molecule is water.
Q: What are the products when ethanoyl chloride reacts with ethanol at room temperature? (from [Acyl chlorides and A Level carboxylic acids](#chem.org.acyl))
A: Ethyl ethanoate and hydrogen chloride: \(\ce{CH3COCl + CH3CH2OH -> CH3COOCH2CH3 + HCl}\).
Q: Write an equation for ethanoyl chloride reacting with ethylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2NH2}\), and name the type of link in the organic product. (from [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen))
A: \(\ce{CH3COCl + CH3CH2NH2 -> CH3CONHCH2CH3 + HCl}\). The product contains an amide link, \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\).
:::

## Addition polymers: a quick recap

An **addition polymer** is made from monomers that contain a C=C double bond. The π bond of each monomer opens and the molecules join end to end. Nothing is lost, so the polymer is the only product.

\[n\,\ce{CH2=CHCl} \longrightarrow \ce{-[CH2-CHCl]_n}{-}\]

Three facts from [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons) carry through this whole chapter.

- The repeat unit has the same atoms as the monomer. The C=C becomes a single C–C bond and a bond goes out from each end carbon.
- The chain (the **backbone**) is made only of carbon atoms. Other groups (H, Cl, CH₃) hang off it as side groups.
- Poly(ethene), poly(propene) and poly(chloroethene) (PVC) are examples.

:::eg (a) Draw the repeat unit of the polymer made from propenenitrile, \(\ce{CH2=CHCN}\). (b) Name the monomer of this section of polymer: \(\ce{-CH2-CH(CN)-CH2-CH(CN)}{-}\)
(a) Open the double bond. The two carbons of the chain keep their groups, and a bond goes out of each end: \(\ce{-[CH2-CH(CN)]_n}{-}\). The CN group is a side group, not part of the chain. [B1]

(b) In the section the unit \(\ce{-CH2-CH(CN)}{-}\) repeats. Put a double bond between its two chain carbons and remove the outgoing bonds: \(\ce{CH2=CHCN}\), propenenitrile. [B1]
:::

Condensation polymers are different, because they form by a different kind of reaction.

## Condensation polymerisation: the idea

You already know condensation reactions on small molecules. An alcohol and a carboxylic acid join to make an ester and water. An amine and an acyl chloride join to make an amide and hydrogen chloride. In each case two molecules join and a small molecule is lost.

:::def Condensation polymerisation
The joining together of many monomer molecules, each with two reactive functional groups, to form a polymer and a small molecule such as \(\ce{H2O}\) or \(\ce{HCl}\), which is lost at every new link.
:::

Make a polymer by doing this with molecules that have **two** functional groups, one at each end. Then every molecule can join to a neighbour at both ends, and the chain keeps growing.

:::analogy
Imagine people holding hands to make a chain. Each person needs two hands, one for the person on the left and one for the person on the right. A person with only one hand free ends the chain. In the same way a monomer with two reactive groups can extend a chain at both ends, while a molecule with only one such group stops the growth. Every handshake gives away a little something, the small molecule \(\ce{H2O}\) or \(\ce{HCl}\).
:::

There are two ways to arrange the groups. Call the two kinds of reactive group A and B, where A can join to B.

:::cols
:::col Two different monomers
A–A and B–B alternate: –A–B–A–B–. Each monomer has the same group at both ends, and the two monomers have groups that react with each other. Example: a diol and a dicarboxylic acid.
:::
:::col One monomer
A–B has both groups in the same molecule, and the chain is –A–B–A–B–. Example: a hydroxycarboxylic acid, or an amino acid.
:::
:::

Four reactions are enough for this whole topic.

| Groups that react | Link formed | Small molecule lost |
|---|---|---|
| –OH and –COOH | ester, –COO– | \(\ce{H2O}\) |
| –OH and –COCl | ester, –COO– | \(\ce{HCl}\) |
| –NH₂ and –COOH | amide, –CONH– | \(\ce{H2O}\) |
| –NH₂ and –COCl | amide, –CONH– | \(\ce{HCl}\) |

:::key Addition and condensation polymers
| | Addition | Condensation |
|---|---|---|
| Monomer | a C=C bond (usually one kind) | two reactive groups per molecule (e.g. –OH, –COOH, –NH₂, –COCl) |
| Products | the polymer only | the polymer and a small molecule (\(\ce{H2O}\) or \(\ce{HCl}\)) |
| Backbone | all carbon atoms | contains ester or amide links |
| Examples | poly(ethene), PVC | polyesters, polyamides, proteins |
:::

The polyester and polyamide families differ only in which link joins the units. Each family comes with two variants, depending on whether the acid end is a carboxylic acid (water lost) or an acyl chloride (HCl lost). The acyl chloride is more reactive, so the reaction is faster and does not run backwards.

## Polyesters

A **polyester** is a polymer in which the units are joined by ester links.

:::def Polyester
A condensation polymer whose monomer units are joined by ester links, \(\ce{-COO-}\). It is made from a diol and a dicarboxylic acid (or dioyl chloride), or from a hydroxycarboxylic acid. (A dioyl chloride is the acyl chloride of the diacid: each COOH is replaced by COCl. The name is explained in the tip "Where the acid end came from" below.)
:::

### A diol and a dicarboxylic acid: PET

The commonest polyester is poly(ethylene terephthalate), **PET**. It is made from ethane-1,2-diol and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid. As a fibre it is called Terylene, and as a plastic it is the PET of drinks bottles. The diol has an OH group at each end and the acid has a COOH group at each end.

Each OH reacts with a COOH to form an ester link and a molecule of water:

\[n\,\ce{HOCH2CH2OH} + n\,\ce{HOOC-C6H4-COOH} \longrightarrow \ce{-[OCH2CH2O-CO-C6H4-CO]_n}{-} + 2n\,\ce{H2O}\]

The equation shows the groups of the ring (\(\ce{C6H4}\) is a benzene ring with two substituents opposite each other). Joining \(n\) of each monomer makes \(2n - 1\) links; for a chain of thousands of units \(2n\) is the same to within a tiny fraction, so the simpler form is normally written.

To read the repeat unit, follow the chain: an oxygen, two CH₂ groups, an oxygen, a C=O group, the ring and another C=O. The first half came from the diol, the second half from the acid. A repeat unit of a polymer made from **two** monomers always contains a piece of **each**.

React the same diol with a **dioyl chloride**, benzene-1,4-dioyl dichloride, and the reaction is the same except that HCl is lost:

\[n\,\ce{HOCH2CH2OH} + n\,\ce{ClOC-C6H4-COCl} \longrightarrow \ce{-[OCH2CH2O-CO-C6H4-CO]_n}{-} + 2n\,\ce{HCl}\]

The repeat unit is identical. Only the small molecule and the speed differ.

:::tip Where the acid end came from
A dicarboxylic acid is named with the ending "-dioic acid" (hexanedioic acid, \(\ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH}\)) and the matching acyl chloride with "-dioyl dichloride" (hexanedioyl dichloride, \(\ce{ClOC(CH2)4COCl}\)). The number of carbons includes both carboxyl carbons. Benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid has an older name, terephthalic acid, which explains the "T" in PET.
:::

### A hydroxycarboxylic acid: poly(lactic acid)

A molecule with an OH group at one end and a COOH group at the other can join to itself. **Poly(lactic acid)**, PLA, is made from 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH}\).

\[n\,\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH} \longrightarrow \ce{-[O-CH(CH3)-CO]_n}{-} + n\,\ce{H2O}\]

Here \(n\) monomers make \(n - 1\) links, so again \(n\) is accepted for a long chain. The side group \(\ce{CH3}\) hangs off the chain exactly as in poly(propene).

PLA is made from sugars or starch from plants such as maize. Fermentation makes lactic acid, and the acid is then polymerised. It is used for compostable packaging and for stitches that dissolve in the body, because the ester links slowly hydrolyse (see the last section of this chapter).

:::eg Draw the repeat unit of the polyester made from propane-1,3-diol and hexanedioyl dichloride, and name the small molecule lost. [3 marks]
Propane-1,3-diol is \(\ce{HOCH2CH2CH2OH}\) and hexanedioyl dichloride is \(\ce{ClOC(CH2)4COCl}\).

Take the H away from each OH of the diol, leaving \(\ce{-O(CH2)3O}{-}\). Take the Cl away from each COCl, leaving \(\ce{-OC(CH2)4CO}{-}\). [M1]

Join the two pieces by ester links, and bracket one piece of each, with the bonds going out through the brackets and \(n\) at the lower right: \(\ce{-[O(CH2)3O-CO(CH2)4CO]_n}{-}\). [B1]

The small molecule is hydrogen chloride, two molecules for each repeat unit. [B1]

\[n\,\ce{HO(CH2)3OH} + n\,\ce{ClOC(CH2)4COCl} \longrightarrow \ce{-[O(CH2)3O-CO(CH2)4CO]_n}{-} + 2n\,\ce{HCl}\]
:::

:::eg The polyester made from 6-hydroxyhexanoic acid. Give the repeat unit and the equation. [3 marks]
6-Hydroxyhexanoic acid is \(\ce{HO(CH2)5COOH}\): one OH at one end and one COOH at the other, so a single monomer is enough. [B1]

Remove H from the OH and OH from the COOH, and join the molecules head to tail: the repeat unit is \(\ce{-[O(CH2)5CO]}{-}\). [B1]

\[n\,\ce{HO(CH2)5COOH} \longrightarrow \ce{-[O(CH2)5CO]_n}{-} + n\,\ce{H2O}\] [B1]

The repeat unit is the whole monomer minus one \(\ce{H2O}\). It holds one piece of the monomer, not two pieces as for a diol and a diacid.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) A sample of PET has an average relative molecular mass of 38 400. Calculate the average number of repeat units in a chain. [4 marks]
The repeat unit is \(\ce{-[OCH2CH2O-CO-C6H4-CO]}{-}\), formula \(\ce{C10H8O4}\).

\(M_r = 10(12.0) + 8(1.0) + 4(16.0) = 192.0\). [B1]

Check from the monomers: \(M_r(\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}) = 62.0\), \(M_r(\ce{HOOC-C6H4-COOH}) = 166.0\), and two molecules of water (2 × 18.0) are lost: \(62.0 + 166.0 - 36.0 = 192.0\). [M1]

\[\text{number of repeat units} = \frac{38\,400}{192.0}\] [M1]

\[= 200\] [A1]
:::

## Polyamides

A **polyamide** is a polymer in which the units are joined by amide links, \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\). Take care over how the link is written: the nitrogen has one hydrogen left, so it is \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\), never \(\ce{-CO-NH2}{-}\).

:::def Polyamide
A condensation polymer whose monomer units are joined by amide links, \(\ce{-CONH}{-}\). It is made from a diamine and a dicarboxylic acid (or dioyl chloride), or from an aminocarboxylic acid.
:::

### A diamine and a dicarboxylic acid: nylon-6,6

**Nylon-6,6** is made from 1,6-diaminohexane, \(\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2}\), and hexanedioic acid, \(\ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH}\). The name counts the carbon atoms in each monomer: six in the diamine, six in the diacid. Each NH₂ reacts with a COOH to make an amide link and a molecule of water.

\[n\,\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2} + n\,\ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH} \longrightarrow \ce{-[NH(CH2)6NH-CO(CH2)4CO]_n}{-} + 2n\,\ce{H2O}\]

With the dioyl chloride, hexanedioyl dichloride, the polymer is the same and HCl is lost instead:

\[n\,\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2} + n\,\ce{ClOC(CH2)4COCl} \longrightarrow \ce{-[NH(CH2)6NH-CO(CH2)4CO]_n}{-} + 2n\,\ce{HCl}\]

Notice the repeat unit alternates a diamine piece and a diacid piece. Reading it from the other end gives \(\ce{-[CO(CH2)4CO-NH(CH2)6NH]_n}{-}\), which is the same polymer, so either way of writing the repeat unit is correct provided it contains one of each piece and has the bonds drawn through the brackets.

Nylon was first made in the 1930s and went into stockings, ropes and parachutes. Strands of nylon are strong because the amide links form hydrogen bonds between chains: the \(\ce{N-H}\) of one chain bonds to the \(\ce{C=O}\) of its neighbour, and the chains line up like threads in a rope.

:::lab Nylon at the boundary of two liquids (teacher demonstration)
Pour a solution of hexanedioyl dichloride in an organic solvent over an aqueous solution of 1,6-diaminohexane. The two liquids do not mix, and a film of nylon forms at the boundary, where the monomers meet. Lift the film out with forceps and wind it onto a rod as a continuous thread, as new film forms underneath. The dioyl chloride is corrosive and gives off HCl, so the demonstration is done in a fume cupboard with goggles and gloves.
:::

### An aminocarboxylic acid: one monomer

A molecule with an NH₂ group at one end and a COOH group at the other can join to itself, like the hydroxycarboxylic acid in PLA. 6-Aminohexanoic acid, \(\ce{H2N(CH2)5COOH}\), gives nylon-6 (six carbons in the monomer):

\[n\,\ce{H2N(CH2)5COOH} \longrightarrow \ce{-[NH(CH2)5CO]_n}{-} + n\,\ce{H2O}\]

### Kevlar: a polyamide with benzene rings

Kevlar is an aromatic polyamide used in body armour, tyres and ropes. It has the same amide link, but both the diamine and the diacid are benzene rings with the two groups opposite each other (the 1,4-positions). It is made from benzene-1,4-diamine and benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (or its dioyl chloride, losing HCl):

### Amino acids and proteins

**Proteins** are polyamides made by living cells. The monomers are amino acids, which have an amine group and a carboxylic acid group on the same carbon atom. The general formula is \(\ce{H2N-CHR-COOH}\), where R is a side group that differs from one amino acid to the next (R is H in glycine and \(\ce{CH3}\) in alanine).

:::def Peptide link
The amide link, \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\), that joins two amino acids when the COOH group of one reacts with the NH₂ group of the next, with loss of a molecule of water. The product of two amino acids is a dipeptide, of three a tripeptide, and many make a polypeptide or protein.
:::

\[\ce{H2N-CH2-COOH} + \ce{H2N-CH(CH3)-COOH} \longrightarrow \ce{H2N-CH2-CO-NH-CH(CH3)-COOH} + \ce{H2O}\]

This dipeptide has an NH₂ end and a COOH end, so it can add more amino acids at either end. A protein is a very long chain of this kind, \(\ce{-[NH-CHR-CO]}{-}\), with the group R changing from unit to unit. That is the one difference from nylon: the repeat is not the same all the way along. The acid and base behaviour of amino acids, zwitterions and the way peptides behave in an electric field are in [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen).

:::eg Name the monomers in this section of protein. [3 marks]
\[\ce{-NH-CH2-CO-NH-CH(CH3)-CO-NH-CH2-CO}{-}\]
The chain contains three \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\) amide (peptide) links, so it is a condensation polymer of amino acids. [B1]

Cut each C–N bond. Add H to each N and OH to each C=O. The pieces between the cuts are \(\ce{H2N-CH2-COOH}\), \(\ce{H2N-CH(CH3)-COOH}\) and \(\ce{H2N-CH2-COOH}\). [M1]

The monomers are glycine (2-aminoethanoic acid), twice, and alanine (2-aminopropanoic acid). [A1]
:::

## Properties: what the link does to the chain

The kind of link decides which forces act between neighbouring chains, and those forces decide how strong the material is.

- **Poly(ethene)** has non-polar chains, so only id-id forces act between them.
- **Polyesters** have polar ester links. The \(\delta+\) carbon of one chain attracts the \(\delta-\) oxygen of the next (pd-pd forces), as well as the id-id forces along the chain.
- **Polyamides** have the amide group \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\), which has an N–H and a C=O. The hydrogen on nitrogen is strongly \(\delta+\) and the oxygen has lone pairs, so chains form **hydrogen bonds** to each other, N–H···O=C. Hydrogen bonds are stronger than pd-pd or id-id forces, so more energy is needed to pull the chains apart. Nylon is a strong fibre for this reason.
- **Kevlar** adds a second effect. The rings make the chain stiff and straight, so chains lie side by side and every amide link can hydrogen-bond to the next chain. The result is a very strong, hardly stretchy fibre.
- **Proteins** use the same N–H···O=C hydrogen bonds to hold a chain in its folded shape.

:::eg Explain why nylon-6,6 fibre is stronger than a fibre of poly(ethene). [3 marks]
Poly(ethene) chains are non-polar, so only weak id-id forces act between neighbouring chains. [B1]

Nylon-6,6 has amide links with N–H and C=O groups, so hydrogen bonds form between the N–H of one chain and the C=O of another. [B1]

Hydrogen bonds are stronger than id-id forces, so more energy is needed to separate the chains and the fibre is stronger. [B1]
:::

:::note Why the amounts of the monomers must be equal
In a polymer made from a diol and a diacid (or a diamine and a diacid), the chain only grows long if the two monomers are present in equal amounts. If there is a little too much diol, the chain ends are all OH groups once the diacid has run out, and nothing is left to join them. The chains stop at a short length. Manufacturers therefore control the ratio of the monomers closely. A single monomer such as 6-aminohexanoic acid carries both groups, so it is always balanced.
:::

## Drawing a repeat unit

For a condensation polymer with two monomers, the repeat unit is a piece of each monomer joined by the new link. Work through the same steps every time.

:::steps
1. Write the two monomers with their functional groups at the ends: \(\ce{HO-X-OH}\) and \(\ce{HOOC-Y-COOH}\) (or \(\ce{ClOC-Y-COCl}\)), or \(\ce{H2N-X-NH2}\) for a polyamide.
2. Take the small molecule out of the ends that react: the H from each –OH or –NH₂, and the OH (or the Cl) from each –COOH (or –COCl). What is left of the diol is \(\ce{-O-X-O}{-}\), and of the acid \(\ce{-OC-Y-CO}{-}\).
3. Join one piece of each: \(\ce{-O-X-O-CO-Y-CO}{-}\) for a polyester, or \(\ce{-NH-X-NH-CO-Y-CO}{-}\) for a polyamide. The new link, \(\ce{-COO-}\) or \(\ce{-CONH}{-}\), must be drawn in full.
4. Put square brackets round the unit so that the bond at each end goes through a bracket, and write \(n\) at the lower right. The bonds through the brackets show the chain continuing.
:::

For a hydroxycarboxylic acid or an amino acid there is only one monomer, and the repeat unit is the whole monomer minus the small molecule: \(\ce{-[O-CH(CH3)-CO]}{-}\) for lactic acid.

:::warn The mistakes that lose marks
1. **Leftover end groups.** A repeat unit never has a free \(\ce{-OH}\), \(\ce{-COOH}\), \(\ce{-NH2}\) or \(\ce{-Cl}\) at its ends. Those atoms went into the small molecule. If your unit still shows them, you have stopped halfway.
2. **Half a unit.** For two monomers the repeat unit contains a piece of the diol (or diamine) **and** a piece of the diacid. \(\ce{-[O(CH2)2O]}{-}\) on its own is not a repeat unit.
3. **A double bond kept.** Condensation polymers have no C=C from the monomers' reactions: the link forms by loss of a small molecule, not by opening a double bond.
4. **A wrong amide link.** It is \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\), with one H on the nitrogen. \(\ce{-CO-NH2}{-}\) has the wrong number of bonds to nitrogen.
5. **No brackets or \(n\).** The brackets with bonds through them show that this is a repeating unit, not a molecule.
:::

## Finding the monomers from a section of polymer

Work backwards: **break each link and put back what the reaction took away**.

:::steps
1. Look at the chain between the carbon atoms. If there are no ester or amide links, it is an addition polymer: use the method in [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons). Otherwise go on.
2. Find every ester link \(\ce{-CO-O}{-}\) or amide link \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\). Break the bond between the C of the C=O and the O (or N).
3. Add \(\ce{OH}\) to the C=O end, giving a carboxylic acid group (or Cl, giving a dioyl chloride; both are acceptable answers). Add \(\ce{H}\) to the O or N end, giving an alcohol or an amine.
4. Name the monomers. If the pieces are the same on every repeat, there is only one monomer type. If the pieces alternate, there are two.
:::

This is exactly the reverse of the hydrolysis of an ester or an amide. That is why the same logic gives the products of breaking the polymer by acid or alkali, as you will see in the section on degradation.

:::eg The section below is from a polyamide. Identify the monomers. [4 marks]
\[\ce{-CO-(CH2)2-CO-NH-(CH2)4-NH-CO-(CH2)2-CO-NH-(CH2)4-NH}{-}\]
The links are \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\), so it is a polyamide formed by condensation polymerisation. [B1]

Cut each C–N bond. The piece \(\ce{-CO-(CH2)2-CO}{-}\) becomes \(\ce{HOOC-(CH2)2-COOH}\) when OH is added to each C=O: butanedioic acid (or butanedioyl dichloride if Cl is added). [B1]

The piece \(\ce{-NH-(CH2)4-NH}{-}\) becomes \(\ce{H2N-(CH2)4-NH2}\) when H is added to each N: butane-1,4-diamine. [B1]

The two pieces alternate, so there are two monomers: butanedioic acid and butane-1,4-diamine. [B1]
:::

:::eg Deduce the monomers of the polymer section below. [3 marks]
\[\ce{-O-CH2-CH2-O-CO-(CH2)3-CO-O-CH2-CH2-O-CO-(CH2)3-CO}{-}\]
The links are \(\ce{-CO-O}{-}\) (ester links), so it is a polyester. [B1]

Break each ester link at the C–O bond and put H on each O and OH on each C=O. [M1]

The monomers are ethane-1,2-diol, \(\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}\), and pentanedioic acid, \(\ce{HOOC(CH2)3COOH}\) (the chain has three CH₂ plus the two carboxyl carbons, five carbons in all). [A1]
:::

## Addition or condensation? Predicting the type

**From the monomers.** A monomer with a C=C double bond and no other reactive group makes an addition polymer. Monomers with two functional groups that react with each other (OH and COOH, NH₂ and COOH, NH₂ and COCl, ...), with no C=C involved, make a condensation polymer.

**From a section of polymer.** Look at the backbone:

- If the backbone is **only carbon atoms** joined by single bonds, it is an addition polymer.
- If the backbone contains **ester or amide links** (O or N in the chain), it is a condensation polymer.

:::eg Decide whether each polymer is formed by addition or condensation, and give the monomer or monomers. [6 marks]
(a)
\[\ce{-CH2-CH(CH3)-CH2-CH(CH3)}{-}\]
(b)
\[\ce{-O-CH2-CH2-O-CO-(CH2)2-CO-O-CH2-CH2-O-CO-(CH2)2-CO}{-}\]
(c)
\[\ce{-CH2-CH(COOCH3)-CH2-CH(COOCH3)}{-}\]

(a) The backbone is only carbon: addition. [B1] The monomer is propene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH3}\). [B1]

(b) The chain has ester links in the backbone: condensation. [B1] The monomers are ethane-1,2-diol and butanedioic acid. [B1]

(c) The backbone is only carbon, and the ester groups are side groups hanging off it, so this is an addition polymer. [B1] The monomer is methyl propenoate, \(\ce{CH2=CHCOOCH3}\). [B1]
:::

:::eg Predict the type of polymerisation for the monomer or monomers in each case, and give a reason. [4 marks]
(a) \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH3}\) (b) \(\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}\) with \(\ce{HOOC(CH2)6COOH}\) (c) \(\ce{H2NCH2CH2COOH}\) (d) \(\ce{CH2=CHCl}\)

(a) Addition: the monomer, but-1-ene, has a C=C and no other reactive group. [B1]

(b) Condensation: each monomer has two functional groups (OH, COOH) that react with each other, with loss of water. [B1]

(c) Condensation: one monomer with an NH₂ group at one end and a COOH group at the other (3-aminopropanoic acid), losing water. [B1]

(d) Addition: chloroethene has a C=C. [B1]
:::

:::warn An ester group is not always a condensation link
In (c) above, the \(\ce{-COOCH3}\) groups sit on the side of the chain. A condensation polyester has the ester link **in the backbone**: break it, and the chain falls apart. Break a side-group ester, and the carbon chain is still in one piece. Always look at what the chain itself is made of.
:::

## Disposal and degradation

Addition polymers and condensation polymers meet very different fates when they are thrown away.

### Poly(alkenes) are inert

A poly(alkene) such as poly(ethene) has a backbone of non-polar C–C bonds and side groups of C–H bonds. Water, acids, alkalis and the enzymes of microorganisms have nothing polar to attack. So poly(alkenes) are **chemically inert** and are not **biodegradable**. They are useful for just that reason (bags, pipes, bottles that do not rot), but a discarded poly(alkene) stays in landfill or in the sea for a very long time.

:::exam Disposing of poly(alkenes)
Name the problem and its cause. **Landfill:** the polymer does not biodegrade, so it takes up space for decades. **Burning (incineration):** can release heat to generate electricity, but gives carbon dioxide (a greenhouse gas), and if the air supply is limited, toxic carbon monoxide and soot; burning poly(chloroethene) also gives toxic, corrosive hydrogen chloride, which must be removed from the exhaust gases. **Recycling:** the plastic is sorted by type, melted and reshaped, which saves raw materials but needs careful sorting.
:::

:::eg (Real context) Poly(ethene) is burned in an incinerator that generates electricity. Calculate the mass of carbon dioxide produced by the complete combustion of 1.00 kg of poly(ethene), \(\ce{(CH2)_n}\). [4 marks]
The repeat unit is \(\ce{CH2}\), so every carbon atom of the polymer ends up as one \(\ce{CO2}\) molecule: \(\ce{CH2 + 1.5O2 -> CO2 + H2O}\) for each repeat unit. \(M_r(\ce{CH2}) = 12.0 + 2(1.0) = 14.0\). [B1]

\[n(\ce{CH2}) = \frac{1000}{14.0} = 71.43\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]

\(n(\ce{CO2}) = 71.43\) mol and \(M_r(\ce{CO2}) = 12.0 + 2(16.0) = 44.0\). [M1]

\[m(\ce{CO2}) = 71.43 \times 44.0 = 3143\,\mathrm{g} = 3.14\,\mathrm{kg}\] [A1]
:::

### Photodegradable polymers

Some polymers are made to break down in sunlight. Their chains contain bonds or groups that absorb ultraviolet light from the Sun, and the energy absorbed breaks bonds in the chain. The long chains fragment into shorter ones that are easier for the environment to deal with. This works only where light can reach, so a photodegradable bag buried deep in landfill, in the dark, will not break down in this way.

### Polyesters and polyamides can be hydrolysed

Condensation polymers contain **polar** links. In an ester link or an amide link the carbon of the C=O is δ+, because it is bonded to two electronegative atoms (O and O, or O and N). Water, hydroxide ions, acid or enzymes can attack that carbon and break the link. This is the reverse of the reaction that made the polymer, and it is **hydrolysis**.

\[\ce{-CO-O}{-} + \ce{H2O -> -COOH} + \ce{HO}{-}\qquad\ce{-CO-NH}{-} + \ce{H2O -> -COOH} + \ce{H2N}{-}\]

The dash after HO and H2N is the bond to the rest of the chain, not a charge: the product ends are an alcohol group, \(\ce{-OH}\), and an amine group, \(\ce{-NH2}\).

Because of this, polyesters and polyamides are **biodegradable** (they can be broken down by acidic or alkaline hydrolysis, and by microorganisms). The conditions decide the products, exactly as for the hydrolysis of esters and amides.

:::key Hydrolysis of condensation polymers
| Polymer | With dilute acid, heat | With aqueous alkali, heat |
|---|---|---|
| polyester | dicarboxylic acid and diol | salt of the dicarboxylic acid (carboxylate ions) and diol |
| polyamide | dicarboxylic acid and a diammonium salt (the amine is protonated) | salt of the dicarboxylic acid and the free diamine |
:::

For nylon-6,6 the repeat unit and the products are:

\[\ce{-[NH(CH2)6NH-CO(CH2)4CO]}{-} + 2\ce{H2O} + 2\ce{H+} \longrightarrow \ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH} + \ce{H3N+(CH2)6NH3+}\]

\[\ce{-[NH(CH2)6NH-CO(CH2)4CO]}{-} + 2\ce{OH-} \longrightarrow {}^{-}\ce{OOC(CH2)4COO-} + \ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2}\]

Chemists use this to recycle some condensation polymers: hydrolysis returns the monomers, which can be purified and polymerised again. Some polyesters, such as PLA, are designed to hydrolyse in compost or in the body. Even so, hydrolysis of many everyday polyesters is slow at ordinary temperatures.

:::eg Explain why poly(chloroethene) is not biodegradable but a polyester can be hydrolysed. [4 marks]
The backbone of poly(chloroethene) is a chain of C–C bonds, with C–H and C–Cl bonds in the side groups. There is no group that water or enzymes can attack, so the chain is inert. [B1]

A polyester contains ester links, in which the carbon of C=O is δ+ because it is bonded to two electronegative oxygen atoms. [B1]

Water, hydroxide ions, acid or enzymes attack the δ+ carbon, breaking the ester link. [B1]

Hydrolysis gives a diol and a dicarboxylic acid (or its salt in alkali), small molecules that microorganisms can use, so the polyester can break down. [B1]
:::

:::eg What are the products of heating nylon-6,6 with (a) dilute hydrochloric acid and (b) aqueous sodium hydroxide? [4 marks]
(a) The conditions are dilute acid and heat. [B1] The amide links are hydrolysed to hexanedioic acid and the amine groups are protonated, giving the 1,6-diammoniumhexane ion \(\ce{H3N+(CH2)6NH3+}\) (present as the chloride). [B1]

(b) The conditions are aqueous alkali and heat. [B1] The links are hydrolysed to sodium hexanedioate (the acid group becomes \(\ce{COO-}\) in alkali) and the free amine, 1,6-diaminohexane, \(\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2}\). [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 10.0 g of hexanedioic acid is reacted with an excess of 1,6-diaminohexane, and 12.1 g of nylon-6,6 is obtained. The repeat unit is \(\ce{C12H22N2O2}\). Calculate the percentage yield, assuming every acid molecule ends up in a repeat unit. [5 marks]
\(M_r(\ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH}) = 6(12.0) + 10(1.0) + 4(16.0) = 146.0\). \(M_r(\text{repeat unit}) = 12(12.0) + 22(1.0) + 2(14.0) + 2(16.0) = 226.0\). [B1]

One mole of acid forms one mole of repeat units. \(n(\text{acid}) = 10.0 / 146.0 = 0.06849\) mol. [M1]

Maximum mass of polymer \(= 0.06849 \times 226.0 = 15.48\) g. [M1]

\[\text{percentage yield} = \frac{12.1}{15.48} \times 100\] [M1]

\[= 78.2\,\%\] [A1]
:::

Q: [2 marks] ★ State the type of polymerisation, and the small molecule lost, when ethane-1,2-diol reacts with benzene-1,4-dioyl dichloride.
A: Condensation polymerisation. [B1] Hydrogen chloride, HCl. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★ Draw the repeat unit of the polyamide made from butane-1,4-diamine and butanedioyl dichloride.
A: The diamine piece is \(\ce{-NH(CH2)4NH}{-}\) and the diacid piece is \(\ce{-CO(CH2)2CO}{-}\). [B1]
They are joined by amide links: \(\ce{-[NH(CH2)4NH-CO(CH2)2CO]}{-}\). [B1]
Square brackets with the bond through each bracket, and \(n\) at the lower right. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Deduce the monomers of the polymer with the section below, and name the type of polymerisation.
\[\ce{-O-(CH2)2-O-CO-(CH2)3-CO-O-(CH2)2-O-CO-(CH2)3-CO}{-}\]
A: The ester links in the chain show a polyester, made by condensation polymerisation. [B1]
Break each ester link: add H to the O and OH to the C=O. [M1]
The monomers are ethane-1,2-diol, \(\ce{HOCH2CH2OH}\) [A1], and pentanedioic acid, \(\ce{HOOC(CH2)3COOH}\) (or pentanedioyl dichloride). [A1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ Explain why poly(ethene) is not biodegradable but PET can be hydrolysed.
H: Think about which bonds are polar, and whether water or an enzyme has a point of attack.
A: Poly(ethene) has only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so water and enzymes have no site to attack. [B1]
PET has ester links in which the carbon of C=O is δ+. [B1]
These are attacked by water, hydroxide ions or acid, breaking the links to give the diol and the acid (or its salt). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ Poly(lactic acid), \(\ce{-[O-CH(CH3)-CO]_n}{-}\), is heated under reflux with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and then with dilute sulfuric acid. (a) Name the type of reaction. (b) Name the organic product after the alkali, and give its formula. (c) Name the organic product after acidification.
A: (a) Hydrolysis of the ester links. [B1]
(b) Sodium 2-hydroxypropanoate [B1], \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)COO^-Na+}\). [B1]
(c) 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid) [B1], \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH}\), because the acid protonates the carboxylate ion. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) 5.00 g of benzene-1,4-dioyl dichloride, \(\ce{ClOC-C6H4-COCl}\), reacts completely with ethane-1,2-diol to make a polyester. Calculate the mass of hydrogen chloride given off.
H: Each molecule of the dioyl chloride loses two chlorine atoms, each leaving as one HCl.
A: \[M_r(\ce{C8H4Cl2O2}) = 8(12.0) + 4(1.0) + 2(35.5) + 2(16.0) = 203.0\] [B1]
\(n = 5.00 / 203.0 = 0.02463\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{HCl}) = 2 \times 0.02463 = 0.04926\) mol; \(M_r(\ce{HCl}) = 36.5\) [M1]
\(m(\ce{HCl}) = 0.04926 \times 36.5 = 1.80\) g [A1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ A polymer has the section below.
\[\ce{-NH-(CH2)5-CO-NH-(CH2)5-CO-NH-(CH2)5-CO}{-}\]
(a) Name the type of link in the chain. (b) Give the formula and name of the monomer. (c) Explain why one monomer is enough. (d) Poly(propene) is made from a different kind of monomer: say how the bonding in the monomer differs.
H: How many functional groups does the monomer need, and where are they?
A: (a) Amide link. [B1]
(b) \(\ce{H2N(CH2)5COOH}\) [B1], 6-aminohexanoic acid. [B1]
(c) It has an NH₂ group at one end and a COOH group at the other, so each molecule can bond to one neighbour through each end. [B1]
(d) The monomer of poly(propene) has a C=C double bond [B1] that opens so that the molecules add to each other, and no small molecule is lost. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ A tripeptide in a protein has the structure below.
\[\ce{H2N-CH2-CO-NH-CH(CH3)-CO-NH-CH2-COOH}\]
(a) Give the names of the amino acids that form it on complete hydrolysis. (b) State the reagent and conditions used, and the form in which the amino acids appear. (c) State how many water molecules were lost in forming the tripeptide.
A: (a) Glycine (2-aminoethanoic acid) and alanine (2-aminopropanoic acid), with glycine twice. [B1][B1]
(b) Heat with dilute hydrochloric acid (or aqueous alkali). [B1] In acid, the amino acids appear as their ammonium salts, \(\ce{H3N+-CHR-COOH}\) (in alkali they appear as the carboxylate ions). [B1]
(c) Two (a tripeptide has two peptide links). [B1]

Q: [4 marks] ★★★ Poly(glycolic acid), \(\ce{-[O-CH2-CO]_n}{-}\), is made from 2-hydroxyethanoic acid, \(\ce{HOCH2COOH}\), and is used for stitches that dissolve in the body. (a) Explain why the stitches dissolve. (b) A 2.00 g piece of poly(glycolic acid) is hydrolysed completely by warm aqueous sodium hydroxide. Calculate the mass of sodium 2-hydroxyethanoate, \(\ce{HOCH2COO^-Na+}\), formed.
H: Each repeat unit gives one carboxylate ion. Work in repeat units, \(\ce{C2H2O2}\).
A: (a) The ester links are hydrolysed by water in the body, giving small, water-soluble molecules. [B1]
(b) \(M_r(\text{repeat unit}, \ce{C2H2O2}) = 2(12.0) + 2(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 58.0\); \(M_r(\ce{HOCH2COONa}) = 98.0\) (2(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 3(16.0) + 23.0). [B1]
\(n = 2.00 / 58.0 = 0.03448\) mol of repeat units, giving 0.03448 mol of the salt. [M1]
Mass \(= 0.03448 \times 98.0 = 3.38\) g. [A1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★★ Lactic acid has a chiral centre. (a) Identify the chiral carbon and the four different groups on it. (b) A manufacturer makes poly(lactic acid) from a single optical isomer of lactic acid. Explain whether each repeat unit of the polymer still has a chiral centre.
H: See [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism). A chiral carbon has four different groups, and the polymer link replaces H on the O and OH on the C=O.
A: (a) Carbon 2 is bonded to H, \(\ce{CH3}\), OH and COOH, four different groups. [B1]
(b) In the repeat unit the groups are H, \(\ce{CH3}\), O (of the ester link) and C=O (of the ester link). [B1] These are still four different groups, so every repeat unit keeps its chiral centre, and a polymer made from one optical isomer is optically active. [B1]

? Which of these polymers is formed by condensation polymerisation?
- poly(propene)
- poly(chloroethene)
+ nylon-6,6
- poly(ethene)
> Nylon-6,6 has amide links in its backbone and forms with loss of water (or HCl). The other three are addition polymers made from C=C monomers.
? A diamine reacts with a dioyl chloride to make a polyamide. Which small molecule is lost?
- \(\ce{H2O}\)
+ HCl
- \(\ce{NH3}\)
- \(\ce{CO2}\)
> Each COCl loses Cl and each NH₂ loses H, so one HCl is lost for every amide link. Water is lost only when the acid end is a carboxylic acid, COOH.
? What is the repeat unit of the polymer made from 2-hydroxypropanoic acid?
+ \(\ce{-[O-CH(CH3)-CO]}{-}\)
- \(\ce{-[CH(CH3)-CO]}{-}\)
- \(\ce{-[O-CH(CH3)-COOH]}{-}\)
- \(\ce{-[CH2-CH(COOH)]}{-}\)
> The unit is the monomer minus water: the H of the OH and the OH of the COOH are gone. The second option has lost the O; the third still has an OH; the fourth is an addition polymer.
? The repeat unit of PET is \(\ce{C10H8O4}\). The diol has \(M_r\) 62.0 and the diacid 166.0. What is the \(M_r\) of the repeat unit?
- 228.0
+ 192.0
- 210.0
- 174.0
> Two molecules of water (2 × 18.0) are lost: 62.0 + 166.0 − 36.0 = 192.0. 228.0 forgets the water; 210.0 takes away only one water.
? Why can a polyester be hydrolysed while poly(ethene) cannot?
- Polyesters are lighter than poly(ethene).
+ The ester link has a δ+ carbon that water or hydroxide ions can attack, but poly(ethene) has only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds.
- Poly(ethene) is soluble in water.
- Polyesters have double bonds that water adds to.
> A polar link gives the nucleophile a site to attack. Poly(ethene) has no polar group and is inert; it is not soluble in water, and a polyester has no C=C.
? A polymer has a chain of carbon atoms only, with \(\ce{-COOCH3}\) groups hanging off it. Which type is it?
- a polyester, because it contains the ester group
+ an addition polymer, because the backbone is only carbon atoms
- a polyamide
- a condensation polymer, because it contains oxygen
> The ester groups are side groups. The chain itself is only carbon, formed from the C=C of methyl propenoate.
? Which pair is the correct hydrolysis product of nylon-6,6 with hot aqueous sodium hydroxide?
+ sodium hexanedioate and 1,6-diaminohexane
- hexanedioic acid and 1,6-diaminohexane
- hexanedioic acid and 1,6-diammoniumhexane ions
- sodium hexanedioate and 1,6-diammoniumhexane ions
> In alkali the acid is deprotonated to its sodium salt and the amine stays as a free base. In acid it is the other way round: free diacid and protonated diammonium ions.
? How many water molecules are lost, in total, when three different amino acid molecules join to make a tripeptide?
- 1
+ 2
- 3
- 0
> A tripeptide has two peptide links, and each link forms with loss of one \(\ce{H2O}\).

:::recap
- Addition polymer: monomers with C=C open their double bonds; the polymer is the only product; all-carbon backbone.
- Condensation polymer: each monomer has two reactive groups; a small molecule (\(\ce{H2O}\) or \(\ce{HCl}\)) is lost at every link; the backbone contains ester or amide links.
- Polyester: diol + dicarboxylic acid (H₂O lost) or dioyl chloride (HCl lost), or a hydroxycarboxylic acid. PET: ethane-1,2-diol + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid. PLA: 2-hydroxypropanoic acid.
- Polyamide: diamine + dicarboxylic acid (H₂O) or dioyl chloride (HCl), or an aminocarboxylic acid. Nylon-6,6: 1,6-diaminohexane + hexanedioic acid. Proteins: amino acids joined by peptide links \(\ce{-CO-NH}{-}\).
- Repeat unit: a piece of each monomer joined by the link; no leftover end groups; square brackets with the bonds through them and \(n\) at the lower right.
- Monomers from a section: break each ester or amide link between C=O and O (or N); add OH to the C=O end (or Cl) and H to the O or N end.
- Type from a section: all-carbon backbone is addition; ester or amide links in the backbone is condensation (ester side groups do not count).
- Disposal: poly(alkenes) are inert and not biodegradable; landfill and burning (CO₂, CO, HCl from PVC) are problems; some polymers photodegrade.
- Polyesters and polyamides hydrolyse because the link has a δ+ carbon: acid gives the diacid and diol (or diammonium salt); alkali gives the diacid salt and diol (or free diamine).
:::
