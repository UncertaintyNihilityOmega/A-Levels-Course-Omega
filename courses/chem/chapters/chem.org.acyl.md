## What is an acyl chloride?

Take a carboxylic acid, \(\ce{RCOOH}\), and replace the OH group by a chlorine atom. You get an **acyl chloride**, \(\ce{RCOCl}\). The group \(\ce{R-C=O}\) that is left is called an **acyl group**, so the compound is the chloride of that acyl group.

:::def Acyl chloride
A compound with the functional group \(\ce{-COCl}\), in which a carbonyl group (C=O) and a chlorine atom are bonded to the same carbon atom. The general formula is \(\ce{RCOCl}\), where R is an alkyl or aryl group (or H).
:::

To name one, take the name of the carboxylic acid and change "-oic acid" to "-oyl chloride": ethanoic acid gives ethanoyl chloride, propanoic acid gives propanoyl chloride, and benzoic acid gives benzoyl chloride. The carbon of the \(\ce{COCl}\) group is always carbon 1.

### Why they are so reactive

Look at the carbonyl carbon. It is bonded to two atoms that are more electronegative than carbon: oxygen (3.5) and chlorine (3.0), against carbon (2.5). Both pull electrons away from it. The carbon carries a large partial positive charge, written \(\ce{C^{\delta+}}\), and is very attractive to any species with a lone pair. In a carboxylic acid the carbon is also bonded to two oxygen atoms, but the OH oxygen donates a lone pair back towards the carbon, which reduces its δ+ charge. In an acyl chloride, the chlorine atom is a poor electron donor, so the carbon stays highly δ+.

The chloride ion is also a good **leaving group**: it leaves easily with both bonding electrons. A very δ+ carbon plus a good leaving group makes acyl chlorides react quickly with every nucleophile in the next sections, at room temperature and without a catalyst.

:::warn They react with water in the air
Ethanoyl chloride fumes in moist air, because it reacts with water vapour and makes tiny droplets of hydrochloric acid. Bottles must be kept tightly closed, and all apparatus must be dry when you want the acyl chloride to react with something other than water.
:::

## Making acyl chlorides

Three reagents replace the OH of a carboxylic acid by Cl. You met them in [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen) for alcohols: here they do the same job on a carboxylic acid.

| Reagent | Conditions | Other products |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{PCl5}\) (phosphorus(V) chloride) | room temperature | \(\ce{POCl3}\) and \(\ce{HCl}\) |
| \(\ce{PCl3}\) (phosphorus(III) chloride) | heat | \(\ce{H3PO3}\) |
| \(\ce{SOCl2}\) (thionyl chloride) | heat | \(\ce{SO2}\) and \(\ce{HCl}\) |

The equations for ethanoic acid are:

\[\ce{CH3COOH + PCl5 -> CH3COCl + POCl3 + HCl}\]
\[\ce{3CH3COOH + PCl3 -> 3CH3COCl + H3PO3}\]
\[\ce{CH3COOH + SOCl2 -> CH3COCl + SO2 + HCl}\]

Misty fumes of hydrogen chloride show that the first reaction has happened. Thionyl chloride is the neatest, because both by-products are gases. They escape, and the acyl chloride is left behind; it is then purified by distillation. All three reactions must be done with dry reagents and dry apparatus, in a fume cupboard, because water would turn the product straight back into the acid.

:::eg Write an equation for the formation of benzoyl chloride from benzoic acid and thionyl chloride, and state two observations or hazards.
Benzoic acid is \(\ce{C6H5COOH}\), so the OH is replaced by Cl:
\[\ce{C6H5COOH + SOCl2 -> C6H5COCl + SO2 + HCl}\]
Both by-products are gases: steamy fumes of \(\ce{HCl}\) and the choking gas \(\ce{SO2}\), so the reaction is done in a fume cupboard. Moisture must be kept out, because water would convert the benzoyl chloride back to benzoic acid.
:::

## Making benzoic acid from methylbenzene

Benzoyl chloride is made from benzoic acid, so the next question is where benzoic acid comes from. The answer is a reaction you saw coming in [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes): the ring resists oxidation, but a side chain on it does not.

An alkyl side chain on a benzene ring is oxidised to a carboxylic acid group by **hot alkaline potassium manganate(VII)**, whatever its length, and the ring stays intact. Methylbenzene gives benzoic acid. The alkali is needed to keep the manganate(VII) working in a controlled way, and a final addition of dilute acid releases the acid from its salt.

:::steps
1. Heat methylbenzene under reflux with \(\ce{KMnO4}\) and \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\). The purple colour fades and a brown precipitate of manganese(IV) oxide, \(\ce{MnO2}\), appears. The organic product is the benzoate ion.
2. Cool, filter off the \(\ce{MnO2}\), and add dilute acid (for example \(\ce{HCl}\)). Benzoic acid is only sparingly soluble in cold water, so it appears as a white precipitate.
3. Filter off the benzoic acid and recrystallise it.
:::

\[\ce{C6H5CH3 + 2MnO4- -> C6H5COO- + 2MnO2 + OH- + H2O}\]
\[\ce{C6H5COO- + H+ -> C6H5COOH}\]

You may write the whole change with oxygen shown as [O], as in the oxidation of alcohols:

\[\ce{C6H5CH3 + 3[O] -> C6H5COOH + H2O}\]

The \(\ce{CH3}\) group has changed from three C–H bonds into a COOH group, so the carbon has been oxidised from oxidation state −3 to +3. Ethylbenzene gives the same product, benzoic acid, because everything beyond the carbon bonded to the ring is lost as carbon dioxide.

:::lab Benzoic acid in the lab
Reflux 1 g of methylbenzene with a solution of \(\ce{KMnO4}\) in dilute \(\ce{NaOH}\) for about 30 minutes, using the apparatus in [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen). Cool, filter off the brown \(\ce{MnO2}\), acidify the filtrate with dilute acid until no more white solid forms, filter with suction and wash with a little cold water. The melting point of the dried solid tells you how pure it is. Wear eye protection: potassium manganate(VII) stains and is a strong oxidising agent.
:::

## The reactions of acyl chlorides

All the reactions in this section have the same pattern: a nucleophile with an N–H or O–H bond replaces the Cl, and hydrogen chloride is made. They happen at **room temperature**, without a catalyst. Acylation is the name for this type of change: the acyl group \(\ce{RCO}{-}\) is transferred to the nucleophile.

Two terms from the next chapter appear in the table. An **amide** has a C=O group bonded directly to a nitrogen atom, \(\ce{-CONH2}\). A **primary amine**, \(\ce{RNH2}\), has one carbon group on the nitrogen and a **secondary amine**, \(\ce{R2NH}\), has two (see [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen)).

| Reactant | Product | Equation (ethanoyl chloride) |
|---|---|---|
| water | carboxylic acid | \(\ce{CH3COCl + H2O -> CH3COOH + HCl}\) |
| alcohol | ester | \(\ce{CH3COCl + C2H5OH -> CH3COOC2H5 + HCl}\) |
| phenol | ester | \(\ce{CH3COCl + C6H5OH -> CH3COOC6H5 + HCl}\) |
| ammonia | primary amide | \(\ce{CH3COCl + NH3 -> CH3CONH2 + HCl}\) |
| primary amine | N-substituted amide | \(\ce{CH3COCl + CH3NH2 -> CH3CONHCH3 + HCl}\) |
| secondary amine | N,N-disubstituted amide | \(\ce{CH3COCl + (CH3)2NH -> CH3CON(CH3)2 + HCl}\) |

### Water, alcohols and phenols: oxygen nucleophiles

Ethanoyl chloride reacts violently with cold water: the liquid fizzes and gives off **steamy fumes of hydrogen chloride**, and the solution that remains is ethanoic acid and hydrochloric acid. With an alcohol such as ethanol it reacts vigorously to give an ester and the same fumes. With phenol it gives a phenyl ester: benzoyl chloride and phenol give **phenyl benzoate**.

\[\ce{C6H5COCl + C6H5OH -> C6H5COOC6H5 + HCl}\]

The last reaction needs the acyl chloride, because a carboxylic acid and phenol hardly react at all. The oxygen of phenol has a lone pair that is partly drawn into the ring (the same delocalisation that makes phenol acidic, see [Phenol and the acidity of organic compounds](#chem.org.phenol)), so phenol is a much weaker nucleophile than ethanol. The very reactive acyl chloride can still be attacked by it.

### Ammonia and amines: nitrogen nucleophiles

Nitrogen has a lone pair too, and is an even better nucleophile than oxygen. Ammonia reacts at once with ethanoyl chloride to give the primary amide ethanamide. The hydrogen chloride made would react with ammonia, so a **second** molecule of ammonia is used up as it forms ammonium chloride, and the overall equation uses two:

\[\ce{CH3COCl + 2NH3 -> CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl}\]

A white smoke of ammonium chloride is seen. In the same way a primary amine such as methylamine gives an N-substituted amide, **N-methylethanamide**, in which the N shows that the methyl group is on the nitrogen atom:

\[\ce{CH3COCl + 2CH3NH2 -> CH3CONHCH3 + CH3NH3+Cl-}\]

A secondary amine such as dimethylamine gives a fully substituted amide, N,N-dimethylethanamide, \(\ce{CH3CON(CH3)2}\). A tertiary amine cannot form a neutral amide, because it has no N–H bond to lose. Amides themselves, and what can be done with them, are in [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen).

:::warn Count the hydrogen atoms on nitrogen
In an amide made from ammonia, \(\ce{CH3CONH2}\), nitrogen has **two** hydrogen atoms. From a primary amine the nitrogen has **one** (\(\ce{CH3CONHCH3}\)), and from a secondary amine **none** (\(\ce{CH3CON(CH3)2}\)). Each carbon–nitrogen bond to a group replaces one N–H. Always check that each nitrogen has three bonds, and never leave out the HCl (or the \(\ce{NH4Cl}\)).
:::

## The mechanism: addition–elimination

A carbonyl carbon is δ+, so a nucleophile attacks it. In an aldehyde (see [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl)) the reaction stops after the addition, because nothing can leave. An acyl chloride has a chlorine atom that can leave, so the reaction goes on: the nucleophile adds, and then Cl⁻ is eliminated. The overall result is substitution of Cl, but the path is in two steps.

:::def Addition–elimination
A reaction in two stages. In the first, a nucleophile adds to the δ+ carbon of a C=O group, giving a tetrahedral intermediate. In the second, the C=O bond reforms and a leaving group (here Cl⁻) is eliminated.
:::

:::steps
1. The δ+ carbon of C=O is attacked by a lone pair on the nucleophile (here the oxygen of \(\ce{H2O}\)). The π electrons of the C=O bond move onto the oxygen, which becomes \(\ce{O^-}\). The carbon now has four bonds: this is the **tetrahedral intermediate**.
2. A lone pair on the \(\ce{O^-}\) moves back down to reform the C=O double bond, and the electrons of the C–Cl bond leave with the chlorine as \(\ce{Cl^-}\).
3. The oxygen that came from water now carries a positive charge and loses \(\ce{H+}\), which joins \(\ce{Cl^-}\) to make \(\ce{HCl}\). The product is a carboxylic acid.
:::

The mechanism is the same for every nucleophile in the table. For an alcohol, replace \(\ce{H2O}\) by \(\ce{ROH}\); for ammonia, by \(\ce{NH3}\), with a lone pair on the nitrogen attacking the carbon and the positive charge ending up on \(\ce{N}\) (\(\ce{-NH3^+}\) in the intermediate, then \(\ce{-NH2}\) after \(\ce{H+}\) is lost). Compare this with the SN2 mechanism in [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen): there the nucleophile attacks a saturated carbon and the leaving group goes in a single step. Here the carbon has a C=O double bond, so a stable tetrahedral intermediate can form first.

An arrow that starts at the chlorine atom, or at the minus charge itself, loses the mark.
:::

## Why acyl chlorides are better than acids

You can make ethyl ethanoate from ethanoic acid or from ethanoyl chloride. Compare the two routes.

| | from ethanoic acid | from ethanoyl chloride |
|---|---|---|
| equation | \(\ce{CH3COOH + C2H5OH <=> CH3COOC2H5 + H2O}\) | \(\ce{CH3COCl + C2H5OH -> CH3COOC2H5 + HCl}\) |
| conditions | heat, concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst | room temperature, no catalyst |
| speed | slow | very fast |
| yield | limited, because the reaction is reversible | high: the HCl escapes, so it goes to completion |
| works with phenol? | no (almost no reaction) | yes |

The price is that the acyl chloride has to be made first (an extra step with a corrosive reagent), it reacts with moisture, and it gives off HCl fumes. Chemists use it when the acid is too unreactive, when the yield matters, or when the alcohol is a phenol.

:::board AQA
AQA also uses acid anhydrides, such as ethanoic anhydride, as acylating agents. They react in the same way as acyl chlorides but give a carboxylic acid as the by-product instead of HCl, and AQA links them to the manufacture of aspirin. Cambridge 9701 uses only acyl chlorides.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 7.85 g of ethanoyl chloride is added to excess ethanol. 6.16 g of ethyl ethanoate is obtained after purification. Calculate the percentage yield.
The equation is \(\ce{CH3COCl + C2H5OH -> CH3COOC2H5 + HCl}\): 1 mol of acyl chloride gives 1 mol of ester.

\(M_r(\ce{CH3COCl}) = 2(12.0) + 3(1.0) + 16.0 + 35.5 = 78.5\) and \(M_r(\ce{CH3COOC2H5}) = 4(12.0) + 8(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 88.0\). [B1]
\[n(\ce{CH3COCl}) = \frac{7.85}{78.5} = 0.100\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[\text{maximum mass of ester} = 0.100 \times 88.0 = 8.80\,\mathrm{g}\] [M1]
\[\text{percentage yield} = \frac{6.16}{8.80} \times 100 = 70.0\,\%\] [A1]

The rest is lost by reaction with moisture, evaporation of the volatile ester and transfers between vessels.
:::

## Esters from phenol and the route from methylbenzene

Putting the last three sections together lets you plan a short synthesis. Methylbenzene is oxidised to benzoic acid, benzoic acid is converted to benzoyl chloride, and benzoyl chloride reacts with phenol (or an alcohol) to give an ester.

:::eg Starting from methylbenzene, give a route to phenyl benzoate. State the reagents and conditions of each step and the intermediate formed. [5 marks]
Step 1: methylbenzene is heated under reflux with \(\ce{KMnO4}\) and \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), then acidified with dilute acid, giving benzoic acid. [B1] [B1]
\[\ce{C6H5CH3 + 3[O] -> C6H5COOH + H2O}\]
Step 2: benzoic acid reacts with \(\ce{SOCl2}\) (or \(\ce{PCl5}\), or \(\ce{PCl3}\) with heat), giving benzoyl chloride. [B1]
\[\ce{C6H5COOH + SOCl2 -> C6H5COCl + SO2 + HCl}\]
Step 3: benzoyl chloride is added to phenol at room temperature, giving phenyl benzoate and \(\ce{HCl}\). [B1] [B1]
\[\ce{C6H5COCl + C6H5OH -> C6H5COOC6H5 + HCl}\]
Phenol is used as a reagent here: it can itself be made from phenylamine by the route in [Phenol and the acidity of organic compounds](#chem.org.phenol).
:::

:::eg (Real context) Write equations for the reaction of ethanoyl chloride with each of: (a) water, (b) methanol, (c) ethylamine, and name the organic product of each.
(a) \(\ce{CH3COCl + H2O -> CH3COOH + HCl}\): ethanoic acid.

(b) \(\ce{CH3COCl + CH3OH -> CH3COOCH3 + HCl}\): methyl ethanoate, a sweet-smelling solvent and the same ester that forms when the acid is heated with methanol.

(c) \(\ce{CH3COCl + 2C2H5NH2 -> CH3CONHC2H5 + C2H5NH3+Cl-}\): N-ethylethanamide. A second molecule of ethylamine takes up the \(\ce{HCl}\).
:::

## Hydrolysis: acyl chloride, chloroalkane, chloroarene

You can now compare three chlorine compounds towards water or hydroxide ions.

- **Ethanoyl chloride** reacts at once with cold water, so a silver nitrate solution gives a white precipitate of \(\ce{AgCl}\) immediately at room temperature.
- **Chloroethane** needs to be heated with NaOH(aq) (or with aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol, warm) and gives its precipitate slowly.
- **Chlorobenzene** does not react under these conditions at all: no precipitate.

:::lab Comparing the three chlorides
Add 1 cm³ of aqueous silver nitrate to each of three test tubes. Using a dropper, add 2 drops of ethanoyl chloride to the first, 2 drops of chloroethane (in 1 cm³ of ethanol) to the second and 2 drops of chlorobenzene (in 1 cm³ of ethanol) to the third; put the second and third in a warm water bath at about 50 °C. In the first tube a white precipitate forms at once. In the second a white precipitate forms slowly. In the third nothing forms. Do the ethanoyl chloride tube in a fume cupboard, with eye protection, because the acyl chloride fumes and reacts violently with water.
:::

| Compound | Reaction with water or OH⁻ | Result with \(\ce{AgNO3(aq)}\) |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{CH3COCl}\) | immediate, room temperature | white precipitate at once |
| \(\ce{CH3CH2Cl}\) | needs heat | slow white precipitate |
| \(\ce{C6H5Cl}\) | none | no precipitate |

The order of the rates is **acyl chloride > chloroalkane > chloroarene**, and the explanations are different for each end of the series.

- In the acyl chloride, the carbonyl carbon is attached to two electronegative atoms, O and Cl, so it is highly δ+ and very open to attack by a nucleophile such as water. The C=O group also lets a stable tetrahedral intermediate form.
- In the chloroalkane, only the Cl atom makes the carbon δ+, so the carbon is less attractive to the nucleophile, and the reaction has to be driven by heat.
- In chlorobenzene, a lone pair on Cl overlaps with the ring (partial double-bond character, a stronger C–Cl bond) and the electron-rich ring repels the nucleophile, so no reaction occurs.

:::key Hydrolysis of chlorine compounds
Rate: \(\ce{RCOCl}\) (fast, cold water) > \(\ce{RCl}\) (slow, heat with NaOH(aq)) > \(\ce{C6H5Cl}\) (no reaction under these conditions). The acyl chloride carbon is very δ+ because it is bonded to both O and Cl; chlorobenzene's C–Cl bond is strengthened by delocalisation of a chlorine lone pair into the ring.
:::

:::eg Explain why ethanoyl chloride is hydrolysed much faster than chloroethane. [3 marks]
The carbon of C=O in ethanoyl chloride is bonded to two electronegative atoms, O and Cl, so it is very δ+. [B1]

It is therefore very strongly attacked by the lone pair on the oxygen of water. [B1]

In chloroethane the carbon is bonded to only one electronegative atom, Cl, and is much less δ+, so the attack is slower and needs heating. [B1]
:::

## Methanoic acid: a carboxylic acid that is also an aldehyde

Methanoic acid, \(\ce{HCOOH}\), is the first carboxylic acid, and it is unlike the rest. Draw its full structure: the carbon is bonded to O (double), OH and **H**.

\[\ce{H-C(=O)-OH}\]

The H–C=O part is exactly the group that makes an aldehyde easy to oxidise (see [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl)). So methanoic acid is itself a **reducing agent** and is oxidised, to carbonic acid, which breaks down to carbon dioxide and water. Other carboxylic acids have an alkyl group on the carbon, no H, and are not oxidised by these reagents.

\[\ce{HCOOH + [O] -> CO2 + H2O}\]

| Reagent | Observation with methanoic acid | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Tollens' reagent, warm | silver mirror | \(\ce{HCOOH + 2Ag+ -> CO2 + 2Ag + 2H+}\) |
| Fehling's solution, warm | orange-red precipitate of \(\ce{Cu2O}\) | (copper(II) reduced to copper(I)) |
| acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) | purple to colourless | \(\ce{5HCOOH + 2MnO4- + 6H+ -> 5CO2 + 2Mn^2+ + 8H2O}\) |
| acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) | orange to green | \(\ce{3HCOOH + Cr2O7^2- + 8H+ -> 3CO2 + 2Cr^3+ + 7H2O}\) |

:::tip Telling methanoic and ethanoic acid apart
Ethanoic acid gives no change with Tollens' reagent, Fehling's solution, acidified manganate(VII) or acidified dichromate(VI). Methanoic acid gives a positive result with all four, which is how you tell the first two acids apart in the laboratory. A warning: in Tollens' and Fehling's solutions the acid is neutralised to the methanoate ion first, and it is the methanoate that is oxidised.
:::

:::eg Explain why methanoic acid gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent but ethanoic acid does not. [3 marks]
Methanoic acid, \(\ce{HCOOH}\), has a hydrogen atom bonded directly to the carbonyl carbon, so it contains an aldehyde-like H–C=O group. [B1]

This group is easily oxidised, to \(\ce{CO2}\) and \(\ce{H2O}\), so the silver ions of Tollens' reagent are reduced to silver metal, the mirror. [B1]

Ethanoic acid has a methyl group, not H, on the carbonyl carbon, so it cannot be oxidised under these conditions and no silver forms. [B1]
:::

## Ethanedioic acid

Ethanedioic acid, \(\ce{HOOC-COOH}\) (also written \(\ce{H2C2O4}\) and sometimes called oxalic acid), is the simplest dicarboxylic acid: two carboxyl groups joined directly. It is a reducing agent, and **warm acidified potassium manganate(VII)** oxidises it to carbon dioxide:

\[\ce{2MnO4- + 5H2C2O4 + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 10CO2 + 8H2O}\]

The purple manganate(VII) is decolourised as it is reduced to the colourless \(\ce{Mn^2+}\) ion. The reaction needs warming to about 60 °C, and the first few drops decolourise slowly because \(\ce{Mn^2+}\) catalyses the reaction (autocatalysis, see [Transition elements](#chem.transition)). Because it is a stable, pure solid that reacts in a known 2 : 5 ratio, hydrated ethanedioic acid, \(\ce{H2C2O4.2H2O}\), is a useful way to find the concentration of a manganate(VII) solution.

:::eg A student dissolves 0.9450 g of hydrated ethanedioic acid, \(\ce{H2C2O4.2H2O}\), in water and makes the solution up to 250.0 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion is warmed with excess dilute sulfuric acid and titrated with 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ \(\ce{KMnO4}\). Calculate the titre.
\(M_r(\ce{H2C2O4.2H2O}) = 2(1.0) + 2(12.0) + 4(16.0) + 2(18.0) = 126.0\). [B1]
\[n(\text{acid in 250 cm}^3) = \frac{0.9450}{126.0} = 0.00750\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[n(\text{acid in 25.0 cm}^3) = 0.000750\,\mathrm{mol}\]
From the equation, 5 mol of acid reacts with 2 mol of \(\ce{MnO4-}\):
\[n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.000750 \times \tfrac{2}{5} = 3.00 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[V = \frac{3.00 \times 10^{-4}}{0.0200} = 0.0150\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 15.0\,\mathrm{cm^3}\] [A1]
:::

:::warn Do not mix up the two acids
Methanoic acid is one carbon, \(\ce{HCOOH}\); ethanedioic acid has two carbons joined, \(\ce{HOOC-COOH}\). With [O], methanoic acid gives one \(\ce{CO2}\) per molecule and ethanedioic acid gives two: \(\ce{HCOOH + [O] -> CO2 + H2O}\) and \(\ce{H2C2O4 + [O] -> 2CO2 + H2O}\). Each molecule gives up two electrons, so both react with manganate(VII) in the same 5 : 2 ratio. Only methanoic acid has the H–C=O group, so only methanoic acid reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents.
:::

Q: Name (a) \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COCl}\) and (b) \(\ce{C6H5COCl}\), and state the other product when each is hydrolysed.
A: (a) butanoyl chloride; (b) benzoyl chloride. Each gives the carboxylic acid (butanoic acid, benzoic acid) and hydrogen chloride.
Q: Write an equation for the reaction of propanoic acid with phosphorus(V) chloride.
A: \[\ce{CH3CH2COOH + PCl5 -> CH3CH2COCl + POCl3 + HCl}\]
Q: Write an equation for the reaction of propanoyl chloride with (a) ethanol and (b) ammonia (excess).
A: (a) \(\ce{CH3CH2COCl + C2H5OH -> CH3CH2COOC2H5 + HCl}\), ethyl propanoate. (b) \(\ce{CH3CH2COCl + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2CONH2 + NH4Cl}\), propanamide.
Q: Give the reagents and conditions needed to convert methylbenzene into benzoic acid.
A: Heat under reflux with \(\ce{KMnO4}\) and \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), then add dilute acid (for example dilute \(\ce{HCl}\)).
Q: Draw the structure of the amide made when ethanoyl chloride reacts with dimethylamine, and name it.
A: \(\ce{CH3CON(CH3)2}\), N,N-dimethylethanamide. Dimethylamine has one N–H bond, and the acyl group takes the place of that hydrogen, so the nitrogen ends up with two methyl groups and the acyl group and no N–H bond:
\[\ce{CH3COCl + (CH3)2NH -> CH3CON(CH3)2 + HCl}\]
Q: State what you would see when ethanoyl chloride is added to (a) water and (b) aqueous silver nitrate.
A: (a) steamy fumes of \(\ce{HCl}\) and a vigorous reaction; (b) the same reaction with a white precipitate of \(\ce{AgCl}\) at once, because the chloride ions released react with \(\ce{Ag+}\).
Q: Explain why phenol reacts with benzoyl chloride but hardly at all with benzoic acid.
A: A carboxylic acid is not electrophilic enough to be attacked by phenol, whose oxygen lone pair is partly drawn into the ring so that phenol is a weak nucleophile. The carbon of benzoyl chloride is very δ+, so even a weak nucleophile can attack it.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [3 marks] ★★★ Chloroethanoyl chloride, \(\ce{ClCH2COCl}\), reacts even faster with water than ethanoyl chloride. Suggest why.
H: Think about how an extra chlorine atom on the neighbouring carbon changes the electron density at the carbonyl carbon.
A: The extra Cl atom on the \(\ce{CH2}\) carbon is electronegative and withdraws electron density through the σ bond (negative inductive effect). [B1]
This increases the δ+ charge on the carbonyl carbon. [B1]
The carbon is therefore attacked more rapidly by the lone pair of water. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ 4-aminophenol, \(\ce{HOC6H4NH2}\), reacts with one mole of ethanoyl chloride at room temperature to give a single organic product that is the active ingredient of a common painkiller (paracetamol). (a) Suggest its structure and name the functional group formed. (b) Explain why the acyl group joins to the nitrogen and not to the oxygen.
H: Compare the lone pair on the nitrogen of a phenylamine with the lone pair on the oxygen of a phenol, using what you know about the ring.
H: Both the OH and the NH₂ group are attached to the same ring.
A: (a) The product is \(\ce{HOC6H4NHCOCH3}\), N-(4-hydroxyphenyl)ethanamide: a ring with OH on one carbon and the NHCOCH₃ group on the carbon opposite. [B1] The new functional group is an amide. [B1]
(b) The nitrogen lone pair of the NH₂ group is a better nucleophile than the oxygen lone pair of the phenol OH, since oxygen is more electronegative and holds its lone pair more tightly. [B1]
The nitrogen therefore attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon first [B1] by the addition–elimination mechanism, and \(\ce{HCl}\) is lost. [B1]

:::recap
- Acyl chlorides, RCOCl, are named "-oyl chloride". The carbonyl carbon is very δ+ because it is bonded to O and Cl, and Cl⁻ is a good leaving group.
- Made from carboxylic acids with PCl₅ (room temperature), PCl₃ and heat, or SOCl₂: the by-products are POCl₃ + HCl, H₃PO₃, or SO₂ + HCl.
- Benzoic acid: methylbenzene heated with KMnO₄ and NaOH(aq), then dilute acid. The whole alkyl side chain becomes COOH.
- Reactions at room temperature: water gives the carboxylic acid; alcohols and phenol give esters; ammonia gives a primary amide; primary and secondary amines give substituted amides. HCl is also formed (with NH₃ or an amine it makes the ammonium chloride).
- Mechanism: addition–elimination. A lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon, C=O electrons go onto O (tetrahedral intermediate), then C=O reforms and Cl⁻ is lost.
- Rate of hydrolysis: acyl chloride (fast, cold water) > chloroalkane (heat with NaOH(aq)) > chlorobenzene (no reaction).
- Methanoic acid, HCOOH, is oxidised by Tollens', Fehling's, acidified KMnO₄ and K₂Cr₂O₇ to CO₂ + H₂O, because it contains H–C=O. Ethanedioic acid is oxidised by warm acidified KMnO₄ to CO₂ (2 MnO₄⁻ : 5 H₂C₂O₄).
:::
