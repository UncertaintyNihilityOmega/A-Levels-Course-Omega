Q: 25.0 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution is neutralised by 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. Calculate the concentration of the alkali. (from [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles))
A: \(n(\ce{HCl}) = 0.100 \times 0.0200 = 2.00 \times 10^{-3}\) mol, and the ratio is 1 : 1, so \(c = 2.00 \times 10^{-3} / 0.0250 = 0.0800\) mol dm⁻³.
Q: 50.0 g of solution rises in temperature by 6.0 °C. Calculate the energy transferred, using c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹. (from [Chemical energetics](#chem.energetics))
A: \(q = mc\Delta T = 50.0 \times 4.18 \times 6.0 = 1254\) J (1.3 kJ to 2 s.f.).
Q: A reaction obscures a cross in 20 s at one concentration and 40 s at half that concentration. How do the rates compare? (from [Reaction kinetics](#chem.kinetics))
A: The rate is proportional to 1/time, so the rate at the lower concentration is half the rate at the higher: 1/40 against 1/20.

## What Paper 3 is

Paper 3 is the **Advanced Practical Skills** paper: a 2 hour laboratory exam worth 40 marks, in which you carry out experiments and answer questions about them. It tests only experimental skills (assessment objective AO3). The mark grid has these minimum allocations, and the rest vary from paper to paper.

This chapter teaches the techniques and the written skills. You need real practice in a laboratory too: your teacher will run the required experiments, and the past papers and mark schemes on the Cambridge website, with the topic questions at the end of this chapter, show you what examiners expect.

## Measuring accurately

Each piece of apparatus has a limit to how well it measures. Know the limit, and use the right tool for the job.

| Apparatus | Typical use | Read to the nearest |
|---|---|---|
| burette | titrations; delivers a variable volume | 0.05 cm³ (readings end in .00 or .05 and so on) |
| pipette | one fixed volume, such as 25.0 cm³, very accurately | the volume is marked on it |
| volumetric flask | making up a solution to exactly 250 cm³ | the volume is marked on it |
| measuring cylinder | rough volumes, for example of reagents in excess | 0.5 cm³ (if marked every 1.0 cm³) |
| thermometer | temperature | 0.5 °C (if marked every 1 °C) |
| balance | masses | the last digit shown (0.01 g or better) |

Use a pipette or burette when the volume goes into a calculation, and a measuring cylinder when the amount does not matter, such as adding excess acid. Always read a liquid level with your eye level with the meniscus, and read the bottom of the meniscus of a colourless liquid. Potassium manganate(VII) solution is too dark to see through, so read the top of its meniscus.

## Titration

A titration finds the concentration of one solution by adding another, of known concentration, until the reaction is exactly complete. The point where it is complete is the **end point**, seen as a colour change. The solution in the burette is the **titrant**; the solution in the flask, measured by pipette, is the **analyte**.

### The method

:::steps
1. **Rinse and fill.** Rinse the burette with a little of the solution it will hold, then fill it above the zero mark. Run solution through the tap until the jet is full of liquid with no air bubble. Remove the funnel, which could drip. Read the starting level.
2. **Pipette.** Rinse the pipette with the solution it will measure. Fill it above the line, adjust the bottom of the meniscus to the line, and let it drain into a clean conical flask. Do not blow out the last drop.
3. **Indicator.** Add a few drops of the indicator (or none, if the reaction is self-indicating). Stand the flask on a white tile.
4. **Rough titration.** Run the titrant in quickly, swirling, to find roughly where the end point is. This titre is not used for the mean.
5. **Accurate titrations.** Repeat. Add the titrant quickly to about 2 cm³ before the rough value, then **drop by drop**, swirling all the time, until one drop gives the permanent change. Wash the inside of the flask with distilled water if titrant has splashed. Read the burette to 0.05 cm³.
6. **Repeat** until you have two **concordant** titres.
:::

:::def Concordant titres
Titres that agree to within 0.10 cm³ of each other.
:::

The **titre** is the final burette reading minus the initial reading. Record both, to two decimal places and ending in 0 or 5, in one table, with units in the headings. The mean is found only from concordant accurate titres: leave out the rough titre and any result that is not within 0.10 cm³ of another.

:::eg A student titrates 25.0 cm³ portions of an acid with sodium hydroxide solution. The burette readings are shown. Calculate the mean titre to use in the calculation.
| | rough | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| final reading / cm³ | 24.30 | 23.90 | 23.65 | 23.50 |
| initial reading / cm³ | 0.00 | 0.50 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| titre / cm³ | 24.30 | 23.40 | 23.65 | 23.50 |

The rough titre is not used. [B1]
Titres 1 and 3 differ by \(23.50 - 23.40 = 0.10\) cm³, so they are concordant. Titre 2 differs from titre 3 by 0.15 cm³, which is more than 0.10, so it is left out. [M1]
\[\text{mean titre} = \frac{23.40 + 23.50}{2} = 23.45\,\mathrm{cm^3}\] [A1]
A mean of two readings that end in 0 or 5 can end in 5 at the second decimal place, so 23.45 is a sensible way to quote it.
:::

:::warn Which titres go into the mean
Averaging all the readings, or including the rough titre, loses marks. If three of four titres are within 0.10 of each other you may use all three. Always say which titres you used.
:::

### Which indicator?

For an acid–alkali titration the indicators on the Cambridge list are methyl orange (pH 2.9–4.6), bromophenol blue (3.0–4.5), thymol blue (8.0–9.6) and thymolphthalein (9.3–10.5). The indicator must change colour in the steep part of the titration curve. For which acid–alkali pairs each works, see [Acids and bases](#chem.acids) (its indicator section lists them).

### Making a standard solution

A **standard solution** has an accurately known concentration. Make one by dissolving an accurately weighed mass of a pure solid in a volumetric flask.

:::steps
1. Weigh a clean, dry weighing bottle with the solid in it. Record the mass to 0.01 g.
2. Tip the solid into a beaker, and weigh the bottle again. The difference is the mass transferred. This is **weighing by difference**, and it means no solid is lost on the paper.
3. Add distilled water to the beaker and stir until the solid has dissolved. Rinse the bottle into the beaker.
4. Pour the solution through a funnel into a 250 cm³ volumetric flask, and rinse the beaker and the funnel into the flask.
5. Add distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus is on the line, adding the last of the water by dropper. Put in the stopper and invert the flask many times to mix.
:::

:::eg A student wants 250.0 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ anhydrous sodium carbonate, \(\ce{Na2CO3}\). (a) What mass is needed? (b) The student actually weighs a bottle at 25.62 g, tips the solid in, and reweighs the empty bottle at 24.28 g. What is the real concentration of the solution?
(a) \(M_r(\ce{Na2CO3}) = 2(23.0) + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 106.0\). [B1]
\(n = 0.0500 \times 0.2500 = 0.01250\) mol, so mass \(= 0.01250 \times 106.0 = 1.325\) g. [M1] [A1]
(b) The mass transferred is \(25.62 - 24.28 = 1.34\) g, so \(n = 1.34 / 106.0 = 0.01264\) mol and
\[c = \frac{0.01264}{0.2500} = 0.0506\,\mathrm{mol\,dm^{-3}}\] [M1] [A1]
It is not necessary to weigh exactly 1.325 g. What matters is that the actual mass is known, so the actual concentration can be calculated.
:::

### Three families of titration

The Cambridge syllabus uses three kinds of titration. They differ in what the end point looks like.

| Family | Titrant → analyte | End point |
|---|---|---|
| acid–alkali | acid or alkali in the burette; an indicator in the flask | the indicator's colour change |
| manganate(VII) | \(\ce{KMnO4}\) in the burette; \(\ce{H2O2}\), \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) or ethanedioate in the flask, acidified | first permanent pale pink: self-indicating |
| thiosulfate–iodine | \(\ce{Na2S2O3}\) in the burette; iodine in the flask | starch added near the end point; blue-black to colourless |

**Manganate(VII).** The purple \(\ce{MnO4-}\) ion is reduced to almost colourless \(\ce{Mn^2+}\), so the reagent is its own indicator. The end point is the first **permanent pale pink** in the flask, which appears when all the reducing agent has reacted and one drop of excess \(\ce{MnO4-}\) is left. The solution must be acidified with dilute sulfuric acid (not hydrochloric acid, whose chloride ions would be oxidised by manganate(VII) and use up some titrant). The equations (see [Transition elements](#chem.transition) for the ethanedioate case):

\[\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5Fe^2+ -> Mn^2+ + 4H2O + 5Fe^3+}\]
\[\ce{2MnO4- + 5H2O2 + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 8H2O + 5O2}\]
\[\ce{2MnO4- + 5C2O4^2- + 16H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 10CO2 + 8H2O}\]

The ethanedioate reaction is slow when cold, so the flask is warmed first (to about 60 °C), and the first drops decolourise slowly until some \(\ce{Mn^2+}\) has formed to catalyse it.

**Thiosulfate–iodine.** Iodine is made in the flask (for example from \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) and excess iodide) and titrated with sodium thiosulfate:

\[\ce{I2 + 2S2O3^2- -> 2I- + S4O6^2-}\]

Titrate until the brown iodine fades to a pale yellow. Then add a few drops of **starch**, which turns blue-black with iodine, and carry on drop by drop until the blue-black colour just disappears. Added too early, the starch holds on to the iodine and gives a false end point.

:::eg (Exam style) A sample of 6.50 g of impure hydrated iron(II) sulfate, \(\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}\), is dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and made up to 250.0 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions are titrated with 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ potassium manganate(VII). The burette readings give titres of 22.30 (rough), 21.10, 21.00 and 21.05 cm³. Calculate the percentage by mass of \(\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}\) in the sample.
The three accurate titres are all within 0.10 cm³ of each other, so the mean is \((21.10 + 21.00 + 21.05)/3 = 21.05\) cm³. [B1]
\[n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.0200 \times \frac{21.05}{1000} = 4.210 \times 10^{-4}\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
From \(\ce{MnO4- + 8H+ + 5Fe^2+ -> ...}\), \(n(\ce{Fe^2+}) = 5 \times 4.210 \times 10^{-4} = 2.105 \times 10^{-3}\) mol in 25.0 cm³. [M1]
In the whole 250.0 cm³: \(2.105 \times 10^{-3} \times 10 = 0.02105\) mol. [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{FeSO4.7H2O}) = 55.8 + 32.1 + 4(16.0) + 7(18.0) = 277.9\), so the mass is \(0.02105 \times 277.9 = 5.850\) g. [B1]
\[\text{percentage} = \frac{5.850}{6.50} \times 100 = 90.0\,\%\] [A1]
:::

## Other quantitative experiments

### Rates: the thiosulfate and acid "cross" experiment

Sodium thiosulfate reacts with dilute acid to make a pale yellow precipitate of sulfur, which makes the solution cloudy:

\[\ce{S2O3^2-(aq) + 2H+(aq) -> S(s) + SO2(g) + H2O(l)}\]

Put the flask on a cross drawn on paper, add the acid, start the clock and stop it when the cross can no longer be seen. The same amount of sulfur is needed each time, so the rate is proportional to 1/time. To change the thiosulfate concentration, vary the volume of thiosulfate solution and make the total volume constant with water, so that the concentration is proportional to the volume. See [Reaction kinetics](#chem.kinetics) for the cross experiment and the Boltzmann distribution. Sulfur dioxide is toxic and the reaction is done in a well-ventilated room, with small volumes.

### Gravimetric: water of crystallisation

Heat a known mass of a hydrated salt in a crucible, standing on a pipeclay triangle, so that the water leaves as steam. Let it cool in a desiccator or on a heatproof mat with the lid on, then weigh. **Heat to constant mass**: heat, cool and weigh again until two successive masses agree (within about 0.01 g), which shows that all the water has gone. Hold the hot crucible with tongs, heat gently at first because some salts spit, and keep the lid ajar so steam can escape.

:::eg A crucible with its lid has a mass of 14.52 g. With 2.44 g of hydrated barium chloride, \(\ce{BaCl2.xH2O}\), added it weighs 16.96 g. After heating, cooling and weighing three times, the masses are 16.62 g, 16.60 g and 16.60 g. Find x.
The masses of the last two weighings agree, so the mass is constant at 16.60 g. [B1]
Mass of anhydrous \(\ce{BaCl2}\) \(= 16.60 - 14.52 = 2.08\) g; mass of water lost \(= 16.96 - 16.60 = 0.36\) g. [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{BaCl2}) = 137.3 + 2(35.5) = 208.3\), so \(n(\ce{BaCl2}) = 2.08 / 208.3 = 0.009986\) mol, and \(n(\ce{H2O}) = 0.36 / 18.0 = 0.02000\) mol. [M1]
\[x = \frac{0.02000}{0.009986} = 2.003 \approx 2\] [A1]
The formula is \(\ce{BaCl2.2H2O}\).
:::

### Thermometric: enthalpy change from a temperature rise

Measure the temperature of a solution for a few minutes, add the second reagent, and record the temperature at regular times. The reaction is not instantaneous and the solution loses heat to its surroundings, so the highest temperature actually reached is lower than the temperature rise that the reaction would give if there were no heat loss. The fix is to plot temperature against time, draw a line of best fit through the cooling points, and **extrapolate it back** to the time of mixing. The difference between this value and the starting temperature is \(\Delta T\).

\(\Delta T = 46.5 - 21.0 = 25.5\) °C. [B1]
\(q = mc\Delta T = 50.0 \times 4.18 \times 25.5 = 5330\) J (5.33 kJ). [M1]

### Gas collection

When a carbonate reacts with acid, carbon dioxide is released. Collect it in a **gas syringe** (or by displacement of water in an upturned measuring cylinder, remembering that some \(\ce{CO2}\) dissolves). Read the volume of gas when the reaction has stopped. At room temperature and pressure one mole of gas has a volume of 24.0 dm³.

:::eg 0.500 g of impure calcium carbonate is added to excess dilute hydrochloric acid, and 84.0 cm³ of carbon dioxide is collected at room conditions. Calculate the percentage of \(\ce{CaCO3}\) in the sample, assuming that the impurities do not give gas.
\[\ce{CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2}\]
\(n(\ce{CO2}) = 84.0 / 24000 = 0.00350\) mol, and the ratio is 1 : 1, so \(n(\ce{CaCO3}) = 0.00350\) mol. [M1] [M1]
\(M_r(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40.1 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 100.1\), so the mass is \(0.00350 \times 100.1 = 0.35035\) g. [B1]
\[\text{percentage} = \frac{0.35035}{0.500} \times 100 = 70.1\,\%\] [A1]
:::

## Uncertainty and error

### Uncertainty in measurements

Every measurement has an **uncertainty**. For a scale, the maximum uncertainty of one reading is **half the difference between the closest calibrations**: a thermometer marked every 1 °C is ±0.5 °C, and a burette marked every 0.1 cm³ is ±0.05 cm³.

:::formula Percentage uncertainty
\[\text{percentage uncertainty} = \frac{\text{uncertainty in the measurement}}{\text{measured value}} \times 100\]
A quantity found from two readings (a titre from two burette readings, a temperature change from two thermometer readings) has **twice** the uncertainty of one reading.
:::

For example, a temperature rise of 14.0 °C found with a thermometer read to ±0.5 °C has an uncertainty of \(2 \times 0.5 = 1.0\) °C, so the percentage uncertainty is \(\dfrac{1.0}{14.0} \times 100 = 7.14\,\%\). A titre of 23.45 cm³ has an uncertainty of \(2 \times 0.05 = 0.10\) cm³, which is \(\dfrac{0.10}{23.45} \times 100 = 0.43\,\%\). A balance showing 0.01 g has ±0.005 g on each reading, so ±0.01 g on a mass found by difference: for 1.34 g that is \(\dfrac{0.01}{1.34} \times 100 = 0.75\,\%\).

The larger the quantity you measure, the smaller the percentage uncertainty. To reduce it, **use a larger quantity** (a bigger mass, a larger temperature rise), or **use apparatus with finer divisions**.

### Systematic and random errors

:::def Systematic error
An error that makes every result too high or too low by a similar amount, for example a balance with a zero error, or heat lost in every calorimetry experiment. Repeating the experiment does not remove it.
:::

:::def Random error
An error that changes unpredictably from one measurement to the next, for example a variation in judging the colour change of an indicator, or in the room temperature. Repeating the measurement and taking a mean reduces it.
:::

"Human error" is **not accepted** as an answer, and neither is "the apparatus was dirty" or "mistakes". Name the specific cause, say whether it makes results too high or too low, and propose a specific remedy.

| Error | Effect | Improvement |
|---|---|---|
| heat lost to the surroundings in calorimetry | \(\Delta T\) too small | insulate the cup and use a lid; extrapolate the cooling line |
| not heating the hydrate to constant mass | water lost too small, x too low | heat, cool and weigh again until constant |
| balance reads 0.00 with the pan empty only after drifting | a systematic error in every mass | zero the balance and weigh by difference |
| reading a burette from above | random and systematic reading error | eye level with the meniscus |
| judging the cross disappearing by eye | random error in t | repeat, and use the same observer and the same cross |

:::eg (Exam style) In a calorimetry experiment the value of \(\Delta H\) the student calculated is smaller in size than expected. Give the main source of error and one improvement.
Heat is lost to the surroundings (the air and the cup) during the reaction, so the measured temperature rise is smaller than it should be. [B1]
Use a polystyrene cup with a lid, to reduce heat loss. [B1]
Plot temperature against time and extrapolate the cooling line back to the time of mixing, to allow for the heat lost while the reaction was happening. [B1]
:::

## Tables and graphs

### Recording results

Draw one table **before** the experiment, and fill it in as you measure.

- Heading: the quantity and its unit, written `volume / cm³` (or `volume (cm³)` or `volume in cm³`). No units in the body of the table.
- Record **every** raw measurement. Do not record only the difference.
- Give all the readings of one kind to the **same number of decimal places** (a burette to 0.05 cm³ gives two decimal places; a thermometer read to 0.5 °C gives one).
- Calculated values go to the same number of significant figures as, or one more than, the least precise data used. A titre of 23.45 cm³ (4 s.f.) may give a result to 3 or 4 s.f.; a concentration of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ allows 3 or 4 s.f.

### Drawing graphs

:::steps
1. Put the quantity you **chose** (the independent variable) on the x-axis and the quantity you **measured** on the y-axis. Label each axis with the quantity and the unit.
2. Choose scales of 1, 2 or 5 units (or tenths or tens of them) to each 20 mm square. Never use 3, 7 or 6. The plotted points should fill **more than half** of the grid in both directions. Start at zero only if the points or the extrapolation need it.
3. Plot each point as a small neat cross (×) or a dot in a circle (⊙), not a blob.
4. Draw one smooth, thin **line of best fit**, with roughly equal numbers of points above and below it. A ruler for a line, a smooth freehand curve for a curve. Do not join the points to make a zigzag.
5. Circle a point that is clearly off the trend as an **anomaly**, and do not use it for the line.
6. For a gradient, draw a large triangle on the line, using two points on the line (not data points unless they lie on it) **more than half the length of the line** apart. Show the triangle and the values read from it. The gradient is \(\Delta y / \Delta x\), with units.
:::

## Qualitative analysis

Qualitative analysis identifies the ions in an unknown by their reactions. The key is a short list of tests, a table of results and logical reasoning. The tests below are those in the Cambridge Qualitative Analysis notes. Learn the observations in these exact words.

### Cations

Add a few drops of aqueous sodium hydroxide, then excess. In a second tube add a few drops of aqueous ammonia, then excess. Note the colour of each precipitate and whether it dissolves.

| Cation | NaOH(aq): a few drops, then excess | NH₃(aq): a few drops, then excess |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{Al^3+}\) | white ppt., soluble in excess | white ppt., insoluble in excess |
| \(\ce{NH4+}\) | no ppt.; ammonia produced on warming | no test listed |
| \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) | faint white ppt. unless \([\ce{Ba^2+}]\) is very low | no ppt. |
| \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) | white ppt. unless \([\ce{Ca^2+}]\) is very low | no ppt. |
| \(\ce{Cr^3+}\) | grey-green ppt., soluble in excess giving a dark green solution | grey-green ppt., insoluble in excess |
| \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) | pale blue ppt., insoluble in excess | pale blue ppt., soluble in excess giving a dark blue solution |
| \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) | green ppt., turning brown on contact with air, insoluble in excess | the same as with NaOH(aq) |
| \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) | red-brown ppt., insoluble in excess | the same as with NaOH(aq) |
| \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) | white ppt., insoluble in excess | the same as with NaOH(aq) |
| \(\ce{Mn^2+}\) | off-white ppt., rapidly turning brown on contact with air, insoluble in excess | the same as with NaOH(aq) |
| \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) | white ppt., soluble in excess | white ppt., soluble in excess |

Look for pairs that the first test cannot tell apart and use the second test. \(\ce{Al^3+}\) and \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) both give a white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH(aq), but only the zinc precipitate dissolves in excess NH₃(aq). \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) both give a white precipitate with NaOH(aq), but only magnesium gives one with NH₃(aq). For why precipitates dissolve in excess (complex ions) see [Transition elements](#chem.transition).

### Anions and gases

| Anion | Test and result |
|---|---|
| \(\ce{CO3^2-}\) | \(\ce{CO2}\) liberated by dilute acids |
| \(\ce{Cl-}\) | white ppt. with \(\ce{Ag+}\)(aq), soluble in NH₃(aq) |
| \(\ce{Br-}\) | cream or off-white ppt. with \(\ce{Ag+}\)(aq), partially soluble in NH₃(aq) |
| \(\ce{I-}\) | pale yellow ppt. with \(\ce{Ag+}\)(aq), insoluble in NH₃(aq) |
| \(\ce{NO3-}\) | NH₃ liberated on heating with \(\ce{OH-}\)(aq) and aluminium foil |
| \(\ce{NO2-}\) | NH₃ liberated in the same way, and decolourises acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\)(aq) |
| \(\ce{SO4^2-}\) | white ppt. with \(\ce{Ba^2+}\)(aq), insoluble in excess dilute strong acids; white ppt. with a high concentration of \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) |
| \(\ce{SO3^2-}\) | white ppt. with \(\ce{Ba^2+}\)(aq), soluble in excess dilute strong acids; decolourises acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\)(aq) |
| \(\ce{S2O3^2-}\) | off-white or pale yellow ppt. forms slowly with \(\ce{H+}\) |

Acidify the unknown with dilute nitric acid before adding silver nitrate, so that carbonate does not give a precipitate. For the halide tests in more detail see [Group 17: the halogens](#chem.group17).

| Gas | Test and result |
|---|---|
| \(\ce{NH3}\) | turns damp red litmus paper blue |
| \(\ce{CO2}\) | gives a white precipitate with limewater |
| \(\ce{H2}\) | "pops" with a lighted splint |
| \(\ce{O2}\) | relights a glowing splint |

Iodine, \(\ce{I2}\), gives a **blue-black** colour with starch.

### Organic tests

| Test | Result | What it shows |
|---|---|---|
| Fehling's solution, warm | orange or red precipitate | an aldehyde |
| Tollens' reagent, warm | silver mirror or black precipitate | an aldehyde |
| alkaline aqueous iodine | yellow precipitate | \(\ce{CH3CO-}\) or \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)-}\) |
| acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\)(aq), warm | purple to colourless | a compound that can be oxidised (such as an alcohol or aldehyde) |

These are explained in [Aldehydes and ketones](#chem.org.carbonyl) and [Alcohols](#chem.org.alcohols).

### A method for an unknown

:::steps
1. Note the appearance of the solid or solution. A coloured solution already narrows the cations (a pale blue solution is probably \(\ce{Cu^2+}\)).
2. Dissolve in water (or dilute acid). Test separate portions with NaOH(aq), a few drops then excess, and with NH₃(aq), a few drops then excess. Write the observations at each stage.
3. Warm the sodium hydroxide test to find \(\ce{NH4+}\).
4. For the anion, test separate portions with dilute acid (and test any gas), then \(\ce{Ag+}\)(aq) followed by NH₃(aq), then \(\ce{Ba^2+}\)(aq) followed by dilute acid.
5. Compare each observation with the tables. If two ions are left, choose a test that differs for them.
6. Confirm your answer with a second test, and state it.
:::

### Recording what you see

State **both** what you observe and what happens next. "A white precipitate forms, which dissolves in excess sodium hydroxide to give a colourless solution" earns the mark; "it dissolved" does not say what dissolved or in what. Name the reagent each time ("in excess NaOH(aq)"). Write the colour, state (precipitate, solution, gas) and any change on standing. "**No change**" or "no precipitate" is a valid observation: write it rather than leaving a gap.

:::tip Gas tests
Always say what you did to see the gas (for example "warm with NaOH(aq) and hold damp red litmus paper at the mouth of the tube"), and the result: "turns blue". The conclusion ("ammonia") is a separate line.
:::

## Evaluating an experiment

At the end of a Paper 3 experiment you are asked what could go wrong, and how to improve it. A good answer has four parts: the **source** of the error, the **direction** of its effect, a **specific improvement**, and where possible a **quantity**.

:::eg A student heats 2.44 g of hydrated barium chloride for one minute and weighs it once, getting 16.70 g with the crucible (the true constant mass is 16.60 g, with the crucible at 14.52 g). Evaluate the result and suggest an improvement.
Anhydrous salt would be calculated as \(16.70 - 14.52 = 2.18\) g and water as \(16.96 - 16.70 = 0.26\) g. [M1]
\(n(\ce{BaCl2}) = 2.18 / 208.3 = 0.01047\) mol and \(n(\ce{H2O}) = 0.26 / 18.0 = 0.01444\) mol, so \(x = 1.38\). [M1]
This is too low because the salt was not heated long enough, and some water remained in the crucible. [B1]
Improvement: heat, cool and weigh again until two successive masses agree to within 0.01 g (constant mass). [B1]
:::

:::eg Spot the error: a student's titration results are rough 24.30, 23.40, 23.65 and 23.50 cm³. The student writes "mean titre = (24.30 + 23.40 + 23.65 + 23.50) / 4 = 23.71 cm³" and records the burette readings as 24.3, 23.4 and so on. [3 marks]
The method marks are: B1 for the burette readings to two decimal places ending in 0 or 5, M1 for choosing the concordant titres, A1 for the correct mean.

**The student loses the first mark: the readings were recorded to 0.1 cm³ although the burette is read to the nearest 0.05 cm³, so the precision is wrong [B0].** The student loses the second mark too, because the rough titre and a titre that is not concordant have been included [M0], and so the mean is wrong [A0].

Correct answer: readings 24.30, 23.40, 23.65 and 23.50; the rough titre is excluded; 23.40 and 23.50 are within 0.10 cm³; mean \(= 23.45\) cm³. [B1] [M1] [A1]

The student scores 0 out of 3, which shows how many marks hang on the recording alone.
:::

Q: A burette is read as 3.40 cm³ at the start and 26.85 cm³ at the end. Calculate the titre and state the uncertainty in it.
A: Titre \(= 26.85 - 3.40 = 23.45\) cm³. Each reading is ±0.05 cm³, so the titre is ±0.10 cm³ (0.43 %).
Q: Four accurate titres are 18.15, 17.70, 17.80 and 17.95 cm³ (the rough titre was 18.60). Which are used in the mean, and what is the mean?
A: 17.70 and 17.80 are within 0.10 of each other; 17.95 differs from 17.80 by 0.15, and 18.15 is further away. The mean of the concordant titres is \((17.70 + 17.80)/2 = 17.75\) cm³.
Q: Why must your eye be level with the meniscus when you read a burette?
A: Reading from above or below makes the level appear higher or lower than it is (parallax error), adding a reading error.
Q: A thermometer is marked every 1 °C and a temperature rise of 8.0 °C is measured. Calculate the percentage uncertainty in the rise.
A: Two readings with ±0.5 °C each give ±1.0 °C. \(1.0 / 8.0 \times 100 = 12.5\,\%\).
Q: What is the end point colour change for (a) potassium manganate(VII) with iron(II), (b) sodium thiosulfate with iodine, using starch?
A: (a) colourless to the first permanent pale pink. (b) blue-black to colourless; the starch is added when the iodine colour has faded to pale yellow.
Q: Which test, and what observation, distinguishes \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) from \(\ce{Ca^2+}\)?
A: Add NH₃(aq): \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) gives a white precipitate (insoluble in excess); \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) gives no precipitate.
Q: A solid gives off a gas that turns damp red litmus blue when warmed with NaOH(aq). Which ion is present?
A: \(\ce{NH4+}\) (ammonium). The gas is ammonia.
Q: Why must hydrochloric acid not be used to acidify a titration with potassium manganate(VII)?
A: Chloride ions would be oxidised to chlorine by the manganate(VII), using up some of it, so the titre would be too large.

Q: [4 marks] ★★★ A student heats the crucible with hydrated barium chloride, as in the worked example, for one minute only and gets x = 1.38 instead of 2. (a) Explain, using the masses, why x is too low. (b) What is the effect on x if the student instead lets the crucible take in water vapour from the air while cooling uncovered?
H: Which mass changes in each case, the mass of anhydrous salt or the mass of water?
A: (a) Some water remains in the solid, so the mass of anhydrous salt is overestimated and the mass of water lost is underestimated. [B1] The ratio of water to salt, and so x, is too low. [B1]
(b) The final mass is too high because of absorbed water, so the same effect arises: the anhydrous mass is too high and the water lost too low, giving x too low. [B1] Cooling in a desiccator, or with the lid on, prevents this. [B1]
:::

:::quiz
? A student calculates the mean titre from the rough titre and three accurate titres. What is wrong?
- Nothing: more readings give a better mean.
+ The rough titre should not be included in the mean.
- The titre should be the maximum reading.
- The mean should be of the two highest readings.
> The rough titre is only a guide to the end point, and is usually too high because the titrant is added fast. Only concordant accurate titres go into the mean.
? A thermometer is marked every 1 °C and used for a rise of 10.0 °C. What is the percentage uncertainty in the rise?
- 5.0 %
+ 10 %
- 0.5 %
- 20 %
> Each reading is ±0.5 °C, so the rise is ±1.0 °C, and 1.0 / 10.0 × 100 = 10 %. The value 5.0 % comes from forgetting that a rise needs two readings.
? Why is no indicator added in a titration of iron(II) with potassium manganate(VII)?
- Iron(II) is colourless, so no indicator is possible.
+ Manganate(VII) is purple and turns almost colourless, so it is its own indicator.
- The reaction has no end point.
- Hydrochloric acid acts as the indicator.
> The end point is the first permanent pale pink, from one drop of excess \(\ce{MnO4-}\). Hydrochloric acid must not be used, as chloride ions are oxidised.
? A white precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH(aq) and also dissolves in excess NH₃(aq). Which ion is present?
- \(\ce{Al^3+}\)
- \(\ce{Mg^2+}\)
+ \(\ce{Zn^2+}\)
- \(\ce{Ca^2+}\)
> Only zinc dissolves in both. Aluminium dissolves in excess NaOH(aq) but not in NH₃(aq), and magnesium dissolves in neither.
? A student heats a hydrated salt once and records the mass. Which change to the method makes the value of x more reliable?
- Heating more strongly with the lid off.
- Weighing the crucible while it is still hot.
+ Heating to constant mass.
- Using a larger crucible.
> Constant mass shows that all the water has gone. A hot crucible gives a false mass because of air currents, and heating strongly can decompose the salt.
? A titre of 22.40 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium thiosulfate reacts with the iodine from 25.0 cm³ of copper(II) solution, where 2 mol of copper(II) ions make 1 mol of iodine, which reacts with 2 mol of thiosulfate ions. What is the concentration of the copper(II) solution?
- 0.0448 mol dm⁻³
+ 0.0896 mol dm⁻³
- 0.179 mol dm⁻³
- 0.112 mol dm⁻³
> \(n(\ce{S2O3^2-}) = 2.240 \times 10^{-3}\) mol, equal to \(n(\ce{Cu^2+})\), and \(2.240 \times 10^{-3} / 0.0250 = 0.0896\). Halving or doubling comes from using the wrong mole ratio.

:::recap
- Paper 3: 2 hours, 40 marks, AO3 only. Minimum grid: manipulation, measurement and observation 12; presentation of data 6; analysis, conclusions and evaluation 10; 12 vary.
- Burette readings to 0.05 cm³; thermometer ±0.5 °C; titres are concordant within 0.10 cm³. Rough titre first, then accurate titres; the mean uses concordant titres only.
- Titrations: acid–alkali (indicator), manganate(VII) (first permanent pale pink, acidify with sulfuric acid), thiosulfate–iodine (starch near the end point; blue-black to colourless).
- Other experiments: rates (thiosulfate and acid, rate ∝ 1/t), gravimetric (heat to constant mass), thermometric (extrapolate to find ΔT; heat loss is the main error), gas collection (24.0 dm³ per mole).
- Percentage uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ value × 100; a difference of two readings has twice the uncertainty of one. Systematic errors shift every result; random errors scatter results. "Human error" is not accepted.
- Tables: units in headings, raw data to the same precision. Graphs: scales of 1, 2 or 5 per 20 mm square, points fill more than half the grid, line of best fit, gradient from a large triangle.
- Cations: NaOH(aq) and NH₃(aq), a few drops then excess. Zn²⁺ dissolves in excess of both; Al³⁺ only in NaOH(aq). Observations must say what is formed and whether it dissolves in excess.
- Anions: Ag⁺(aq) then NH₃(aq) for halides, Ba²⁺(aq) then acid for sulfate and sulfite, acid for carbonate, Al and OH⁻ for nitrate and nitrite. Gases: NH₃ blue litmus; CO₂ limewater; H₂ pops; O₂ relights. I₂ is blue-black with starch.
:::
