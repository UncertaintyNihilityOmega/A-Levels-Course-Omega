:::why
In an exam the question never says which chapter to use. You have to decide that the problem is a titration, a bond-energy cycle or a mass spectrum before you can start. Topic chapters practise the method once you know the topic. This page practises the earlier step: recognising the topic and pulling the method out of memory.
:::

## Why mixed practice works

Doing ten questions on one topic in a row feels productive, because the method is still fresh from the last question. Mixing topics feels harder and slower, and that is the point. Each time you switch topic, you have to recall the method from memory instead of copying it from the question before. Research on learning keeps finding that this effortful recall is what makes knowledge last, and that the same revision spread across several days beats the same revision packed into one evening.

Each set below mixes at least five topics, with the easier questions (★) first and the longer ones (★★ and ★★★) later. The stars mean what they mean in the chapters: ★ is one idea, ★★ two steps, ★★★ several parts or a link between topics.

## How to use this page

| Set | Theme | Main topics |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Foundations | atoms, moles, bonding, shapes, states of matter |
| 2 | Energy and equilibria | enthalpy changes, bond energies, Hess cycles, Kc, Kp, the Contact process, catalysts |
| 3 | Kinetics, acids and redox | oxidation numbers, redox titrations, strong and weak acids, titration curves, rates |
| 4 | Inorganic | Period 3, Group 2, Group 17, nitrogen and pollution |
| 5 | Organic reactions and mechanisms | naming, isomers, mechanisms, alcohols, carbonyls, esters |
| 6 | Synthesis, analysis and synoptic problems | routes, mass spectra, IR, gas volumes, combustion analysis |

1. Do **one set per sitting**, about 50 minutes, without notes and with the Periodic Table and the data values in front of you (the same values the real Data booklet gives).
2. Write your answer on paper before you open the hints or the answer. Each hint opens one at a time: use them only after a real attempt.
3. Mark yourself honestly with the answer open, one mark token ([B1], [M1], [A1]) at a time. A mark you give yourself for "the idea was roughly right" is a mark the examiner will not give.
4. For every question that lost marks, open the **Revise** link under the answer, fix the gap, and write the correction in one line.
5. Return to a set after a week and redo only the questions that lost marks.

:::tip
Use the Revise link as a diagnosis. If you lose marks on a question linked to a chapter you have not finished, go and finish it. If you lose them on a chapter you know well, the mistake is probably about the question (a missing unit, "mol dm⁻³" for a mass, a state symbol) and the fix is a habit, not more reading.
:::

## Set 1: foundations

:::try Set 1: atoms, moles, bonding and states of matter
Q: [4 marks] ★ (#chem.atoms) (a) State the number of protons, neutrons and electrons in one chloride ion, \(\ce{^{37}_{17}Cl-}\). (b) Explain why \(\ce{^{35}Cl}\) and \(\ce{^{37}Cl}\) have the same chemical properties.
A: (a) 17 protons [B1], 20 neutrons [B1] and 18 electrons (17 plus the one gained) [B1].
(b) The two isotopes have the same number of electrons and the same electron configuration, and chemical reactions depend on the electrons. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.atoms) The mass spectrum of an element X shows three peaks: m/z 24 with relative abundance 78.99%, m/z 25 with 10.00% and m/z 26 with 11.01%. Calculate the relative atomic mass of X to 3 significant figures and identify X.
A: Sum of (mass × abundance):
\[24 \times 78.99 + 25 \times 10.00 + 26 \times 11.01 = 2432.02\] [M1]
Divide by the total abundance, 100: 24.32 [M1]
\(A_r = 24.3\) [A1]
X is magnesium. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#chem.atoms) The first four ionisation energies of element Y, in kJ mol⁻¹, are 577, 1820, 2740 and 11600. Y is in Period 3. (a) Write an equation, with state symbols, for the second ionisation energy of Y. (b) Deduce the group of Y and explain your answer. (c) The first ionisation energy of magnesium is 736 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain why that of Y is lower.
A: (a) \(\ce{Y+(g) -> Y^2+(g) + e-}\): correct species [B1] and state symbols [B1]
(b) Group 13. [B1] There is a very large jump between the third and fourth ionisation energies, so the fourth electron comes from an inner shell: Y has three outer electrons. [B1]
(c) Y's outer electron is in a 3p sub-shell, which has a higher energy than the 3s sub-shell of magnesium. [B1] The 3p electron is also shielded by the 3s electrons, so less energy is needed to remove it. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) 2.79 g of iron combines with 1.20 g of oxygen to form an oxide. Find the empirical formula of the oxide and the oxidation number of iron in it.
A: \(n(\ce{Fe}) = 2.79/55.8 = 0.0500\) mol and \(n(\ce{O}) = 1.20/16.0 = 0.0750\) mol [M1]
Ratio Fe : O = 1 : 1.5 = 2 : 3 [M1]
Empirical formula \(\ce{Fe2O3}\) [A1]
Oxidation number of iron is +3 (iron(III) oxide). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) 25.0 cm³ of aqueous potassium hydroxide is neutralised by 18.40 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid. (a) Write the equation. (b) Calculate the concentration of the potassium hydroxide in mol dm⁻³. (c) Calculate its concentration in g dm⁻³.
A: (a) \(\ce{2KOH + H2SO4 -> K2SO4 + 2H2O}\) [B1]
(b) \(n(\ce{H2SO4}) = 0.0500 \times 18.40/1000 = 9.20 \times 10^{-4}\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{KOH}) = 2 \times 9.20 \times 10^{-4} = 1.84 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(c = 1.84 \times 10^{-3}/0.0250 = 0.0736\) mol dm⁻³ [A1]
(c) \(M_r(\ce{KOH}) = 39.1 + 16.0 + 1.0 = 56.1\), so \(0.0736 \times 56.1 = 4.13\) g dm⁻³ [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.bonding) Boron trifluoride, \(\ce{BF3}\), reacts with ammonia to form \(\ce{H3N->BF3}\), a compound with a coordinate bond. (a) Explain how the coordinate bond forms. (b) State the shape and bond angle around boron in \(\ce{BF3}\) and in the product.
A: (a) The nitrogen atom of \(\ce{NH3}\) has a lone pair. [B1] Boron in \(\ce{BF3}\) has only six electrons in its outer shell (an empty orbital), so nitrogen donates both electrons of the bond. [B1]
(b) \(\ce{BF3}\) is trigonal planar with bond angles of 120°. [B1]
In the product, boron has four bonding pairs and no lone pair. [B1] So it is tetrahedral with bond angles of 109.5°. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.states) 0.229 g of a gaseous hydrocarbon occupies 100 cm³ at 101 kPa and 25 °C. The hydrocarbon is an alkene with the general formula \(\ce{C_nH_{2n}}\). Calculate its relative molecular mass and deduce its molecular formula.
A: Convert to SI units: \(p = 101\,000\) Pa, \(V = 1.00 \times 10^{-4}\) m³, \(T = 298\) K [B1]
\[n = \frac{pV}{RT} = \frac{101\,000 \times 1.00 \times 10^{-4}}{8.31 \times 298} = 4.08 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{mol}\] [M1]
\(M_r = 0.229/4.08 \times 10^{-3}\) [M1] \(= 56.1\) [A1]
\(14n = 56\), so \(n = 4\) and the formula is \(\ce{C4H8}\). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (#chem.states) Three solids A, B and C are tested. A has a high melting point, conducts electricity when solid and when molten, and is insoluble in water. B has a very high melting point, does not conduct when solid or molten, and is insoluble in water and in organic solvents. C has a low melting point, does not conduct, and dissolves in hexane. (a) State the structure and bonding in each of A, B and C. (b) Explain why magnesium oxide has a much higher melting point than sodium chloride.
H: Ask of each solid: are there ions, free electrons, or separate molecules? Conducting and melting point are the clues.
H: Both compounds in (b) have the same type of lattice, so compare the charges and the sizes of the ions.
A: (a) A is a giant metallic lattice (positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons). [B1] B is a giant covalent (macromolecular) structure. [B1] C is a simple molecular structure, with weak id–id forces between the molecules. [B1]
(b) Both are giant ionic lattices, but \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) and \(\ce{O^2-}\) carry higher charges and are smaller than \(\ce{Na+}\) and \(\ce{Cl-}\). [B1] The electrostatic attraction between the ions is therefore stronger [B1] and more energy is needed to separate the ions when it melts. [B1]
:::

## Set 2: energy and equilibria

:::try Set 2: enthalpy changes, equilibria and catalysts
Q: [3 marks] ★ (#chem.energetics) Define the standard enthalpy change of combustion of a substance, and write the equation, with state symbols, that represents it for ethanol.
A: The enthalpy change when one mole of the substance is burnt completely in oxygen [B1] under standard conditions (298 K, 101 kPa). [B1]
\(\ce{C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) -> 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l)}\) [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#chem.energetics) 0.460 g of ethanol was burned and the flame heated 200 g of water, whose temperature rose by 13.9 K. (a) Calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol in kJ mol⁻¹, with its sign. (b) The Data booklet value is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹. Give two reasons why the experimental value is less exothermic.
A: (a) \(M_r(\ce{C2H5OH}) = 2(12.0) + 6(1.0) + 16.0 = 46.0\) and \(n = 0.460/46.0 = 0.0100\) mol [B1]
\(q = mc\Delta T = 200 \times 4.18 \times 13.9 = 11\,620\) J [M1]
\(\Delta H = -11.62/0.0100\) [M1]
\(= -1160\) kJ mol⁻¹ (negative because the temperature rose) [A1]
(b) Heat is lost to the surroundings and to the container, not all of it reaching the water. [B1] The combustion may be incomplete (some carbon or carbon monoxide forms), or some ethanol evaporates unburnt. [B1]
Q: [3 marks] ★★ (#chem.energetics) The standard enthalpy changes of combustion of carbon, hydrogen and propane are −394, −286 and −2220 kJ mol⁻¹. Calculate the standard enthalpy change of formation of propane, \(\ce{C3H8(g)}\).
A: \(\ce{3C(s) + 4H2(g) -> C3H8(g)}\), so
\[\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}} = \sum\Delta H_{\mathrm{c}}(\text{reactants}) - \sum\Delta H_{\mathrm{c}}(\text{products})\] [M1]
\(= [3(-394) + 4(-286)] - (-2220)\) [M1]
\(= -106\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.energetics) Use the bond energies N≡N 944, H–H 436 and N–H 390 kJ mol⁻¹ to calculate the enthalpy change for \(\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) -> 2NH3(g)}\), and state whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic.
A: Bonds broken: \(944 + 3(436) = 2252\) kJ [M1]
Bonds made: \(6(390) = 2340\) kJ [M1]
\(\Delta H = 2252 - 2340 = -88\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]
Exothermic, because more energy is released making bonds than is needed to break them. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.equilibria) 0.800 mol of \(\ce{N2O4}\) is placed in a 4.00 dm³ flask and the equilibrium \(\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}\) is set up. At equilibrium the flask contains 0.560 mol of \(\ce{N2O4}\). Write the expression for \(K_c\), calculate its value and give its units.
A: \(K_c = \dfrac{[\ce{NO2}]^2}{[\ce{N2O4}]}\) [B1]
\(n(\ce{NO2}) = 2 \times (0.800 - 0.560) = 0.480\) mol [M1]
\([\ce{N2O4}] = 0.560/4.00 = 0.140\) and \([\ce{NO2}] = 0.480/4.00 = 0.120\) mol dm⁻³ [M1]
\(K_c = 0.120^2/0.140 = 0.103\) [A1]
Units: mol dm⁻³ [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.equilibria) The Contact process is \(\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}\), and the forward reaction is exothermic. (a) State and explain the effect of raising the temperature on the equilibrium yield of \(\ce{SO3}\). (b) State the effect of raising the pressure on the yield, and why the process is run at only 1 to 2 atmospheres. (c) State the effect of the catalyst on the equilibrium yield.
A: (a) The yield falls. [B1] The forward reaction is exothermic, so the position moves to the left (the endothermic direction) to oppose the rise in temperature. [B1]
(b) A higher pressure moves the position to the right, because 3 mol of gas become 2 mol. [B1] At 1 to 2 atmospheres the yield is already high, so the cost of a higher pressure is not worth the small gain. [B1]
(c) A catalyst has no effect on the yield: it speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally, so equilibrium is reached sooner. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.kinetics) (a) Define activation energy. (b) Explain, with reference to the Boltzmann distribution, why a catalyst increases the rate of a reaction.
A: (a) The minimum energy that colliding particles must have for the collision to be effective (to cause a reaction). [B1]
(b) A catalyst provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy. [B1] On the Boltzmann curve the activation energy marker moves to the left, so a larger proportion of molecules (a bigger area under the curve) has at least that energy. [B1] More collisions per second are therefore effective, and the rate rises. [B1]
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#chem.equilibria) At equilibrium a vessel contains 0.40 mol \(\ce{N2}\), 1.20 mol \(\ce{H2}\) and 0.80 mol \(\ce{NH3}\) at a total pressure of 2400 kPa. (a) Write the expression for \(K_p\) for \(\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}\). (b) Calculate the partial pressure of each gas. (c) Calculate \(K_p\) and give its units. (d) The forward reaction is exothermic. State the effect on \(K_p\) of raising the temperature and of adding a catalyst.
H: Mole fraction = moles of the gas / total moles. Partial pressure = mole fraction × total pressure.
H: In \(K_p\), the partial pressure of each gas is raised to the power of its coefficient in the equation.
A: (a) \(K_p = \dfrac{p_{\ce{NH3}}^2}{p_{\ce{N2}} \times p_{\ce{H2}}^3}\) [B1]
(b) Total moles \(= 0.40 + 1.20 + 0.80 = 2.40\) mol [M1]
Mole fractions: \(\ce{N2}\) 1/6, \(\ce{H2}\) 1/2, \(\ce{NH3}\) 1/3 [M1]
Partial pressures: \(\ce{N2}\) 400 kPa, \(\ce{H2}\) 1200 kPa, \(\ce{NH3}\) 800 kPa [M1]
(c) \(K_p = 800^2/(400 \times 1200^3) = 9.26 \times 10^{-7}\) [A1] kPa⁻² [B1]
(d) \(K_p\) decreases when the temperature is raised, because the equilibrium moves to the left. [B1] A catalyst has no effect on \(K_p\). [B1]
:::

## Set 3: kinetics, acids and redox

:::try Set 3: redox, acids and rates
Q: [4 marks] ★ (#chem.redox) State the oxidation number of (a) manganese in \(\ce{MnO4-}\), (b) chromium in \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\), (c) sulfur in \(\ce{SO3^2-}\), (d) nitrogen in \(\ce{NH4+}\).
A: (a) +7 [B1] (b) +6 [B1] (c) +4 [B1] (d) −3 [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.redox) Ethanedioic acid is oxidised by manganate(VII) ions: \(\ce{2MnO4- + 5H2C2O4 + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 10CO2 + 8H2O}\). 25.0 cm³ of aqueous ethanedioic acid needed 21.80 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ potassium manganate(VII) for complete reaction. (a) State the change in oxidation number of manganese. (b) Calculate the concentration of the ethanedioic acid. (c) How is the end-point recognised?
A: (a) +7 to +2 [B1]
(b) \(n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.0200 \times 21.80/1000 = 4.36 \times 10^{-4}\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{H2C2O4}) = 4.36 \times 10^{-4} \times 5/2 = 1.09 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(c = 1.09 \times 10^{-3}/0.0250 = 0.0436\) mol dm⁻³ [A1]
(c) The first permanent pink colour, from one drop of excess \(\ce{MnO4-}\). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★ (#chem.acids) (a) Define a strong acid and a weak acid. (b) State two differences you would observe between 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid and 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid.
A: (a) A strong acid is fully dissociated in aqueous solution; [B1] a weak acid is only partially dissociated. [B1]
(b) Any two of: hydrochloric acid has the lower pH; [B1] it reacts faster with magnesium (or with a carbonate); it conducts electricity better. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.acids) 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ aqueous ammonia from a burette. (a) State the volume of ammonia at the equivalence point. (b) State the approximate pH range of the steep section of the curve and whether the equivalence point is above or below pH 7. (c) Choose between methyl orange (pH 2.9–4.6) and thymolphthalein (pH 9.3–10.5), and justify your choice.
A: (a) 25.0 cm³ [B1]
(b) The steep section runs from about pH 3 to pH 7.5; [B1] the equivalence point is below 7. [B1]
(c) Methyl orange. [B1] Its range lies within the steep section of the curve, but thymolphthalein changes colour only above pH 9, long after the steep section has ended. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.kinetics) Magnesium ribbon was added to excess dilute hydrochloric acid and the volume of hydrogen collected was read every 30 s.

| time / s | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| volume of \(\ce{H2}\) / cm³ | 0 | 24 | 40 | 50 | 56 | 58 |

(a) Calculate the average rate of reaction over the first 30 s and between 60 s and 90 s. (b) Explain why the rate falls as the reaction proceeds.
A: (a) First 30 s: \(24/30 = 0.80\) cm³ s⁻¹ [M1] [A1]
60 s to 90 s: \((50 - 40)/30 = 0.33\) cm³ s⁻¹ [A1]
(b) The concentration of \(\ce{H+}\) falls as the acid is used up (the magnesium surface also shrinks). [B1] There are fewer collisions per second between the reacting particles, so fewer effective collisions per second. [B1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ (#chem.redox) Hydrogen peroxide is oxidised by manganate(VII) ions: \(\ce{2MnO4- + 5H2O2 + 6H+ -> 2Mn^2+ + 5O2 + 8H2O}\). 10.0 cm³ of aqueous hydrogen peroxide was diluted to 250 cm³ in a volumetric flask. 25.0 cm³ portions of the diluted solution needed 19.30 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ \(\ce{KMnO4}\). (a) State the oxidation number of oxygen in \(\ce{H2O2}\) and in \(\ce{O2}\), and the role of hydrogen peroxide in this reaction. (b) Calculate the concentration of the original hydrogen peroxide in mol dm⁻³. (c) Calculate its concentration in g dm⁻³.
H: Work out the moles of \(\ce{H2O2}\) in the 25.0 cm³ portion first, then scale up by the dilution.
H: The 250 cm³ flask contained all the hydrogen peroxide from the original 10.0 cm³.
A: (a) Oxygen is −1 in \(\ce{H2O2}\) and 0 in \(\ce{O2}\). [B1] Its oxidation number increases, so hydrogen peroxide is the reducing agent. [B1]
(b) \(n(\ce{MnO4-}) = 0.0200 \times 19.30/1000 = 3.86 \times 10^{-4}\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{H2O2})\) in 25.0 cm³ \(= 3.86 \times 10^{-4} \times 5/2 = 9.65 \times 10^{-4}\) mol [M1]
In 250 cm³ (the whole of the original 10.0 cm³): \(9.65 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(c = 9.65 \times 10^{-3}/0.0100 = 0.965\) mol dm⁻³ [A1]
(c) \(M_r(\ce{H2O2}) = 34.0\), so \(0.965 \times 34.0 = 32.8\) g dm⁻³ [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.group17) Chlorine reacts with cold dilute aqueous sodium hydroxide: \(\ce{Cl2 + 2NaOH -> NaCl + NaClO + H2O}\). (a) Show, using oxidation numbers, that chlorine is disproportionated. (b) With hot concentrated sodium hydroxide, a different chlorine-containing compound forms besides sodium chloride. Name it and write the equation.
A: (a) Chlorine goes from 0 in \(\ce{Cl2}\) to −1 in NaCl (reduction). [B1] It also goes from 0 to +1 in NaClO (oxidation). [B1] The same element is oxidised and reduced in the same reaction, which is disproportionation. [B1]
(b) Sodium chlorate(V), \(\ce{NaClO3}\) [B1], from \(\ce{3Cl2 + 6NaOH -> 5NaCl + NaClO3 + 3H2O}\) [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (#chem.kinetics) In an experiment a cross under a flask of sodium thiosulfate and acid disappeared after 84 s at 20 °C and after 41 s at 30 °C, with the same concentrations. (a) The rate is proportional to 1/time. Calculate how many times faster the reaction is at 30 °C. (b) Explain, using the Boltzmann distribution, why a 10 K rise has such a large effect.
H: Rate is proportional to 1/t, so the ratio of the rates is \(t_{20}/t_{30}\).
A: (a) rate at 30 °C / rate at 20 °C \(= 84/41\) [M1] \(= 2.0\) times (2 s.f.) [A1]
(b) The Boltzmann distribution curve flattens and its peak moves to the right at the higher temperature. [B1] A much larger proportion of molecules has energy at least equal to the activation energy. [B1] Molecules also move faster, so collisions are more frequent. [B1] The frequency of effective collisions rises, mainly because of the larger proportion above the activation energy. [B1]
:::

## Set 4: inorganic chemistry

:::try Set 4: Period 3, Groups 2 and 17, and nitrogen
Q: [4 marks] ★ (#chem.periodicity) Write an equation for the reaction of each oxide with water and state the approximate pH of the resulting solution: (a) sodium oxide, (b) sulfur trioxide.
A: (a) \(\ce{Na2O(s) + H2O(l) -> 2NaOH(aq)}\) [B1], pH about 13 to 14 [B1]
(b) \(\ce{SO3(g) + H2O(l) -> H2SO4(aq)}\) [B1], pH about 1 [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.periodicity) Predict the approximate pH of the solution formed when each of sodium chloride, aluminium chloride and phosphorus(V) chloride is added to water. Write an equation for the reaction of \(\ce{PCl5}\) with an excess of water and state one observation.
A: NaCl pH 7 [B1]; \(\ce{AlCl3}\) about 3 [B1]; \(\ce{PCl5}\) about 1 to 2 [B1]
\(\ce{PCl5 + 4H2O -> H3PO4 + 5HCl}\) [B1]
Steamy (misty) fumes of hydrogen chloride [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.states) Explain, in terms of structure and bonding, why (a) silicon has a much higher melting point than sulfur, (b) magnesium has a higher melting point than sodium.
A: (a) Silicon is a giant covalent structure, so many strong covalent bonds must be broken. [B1] Sulfur is simple molecular (\(\ce{S8}\)), with only weak id–id forces between the molecules. [B1] Far more energy is needed to melt silicon. [B1]
(b) A magnesium atom gives two delocalised electrons and forms a 2+ ion, whereas sodium gives one delocalised electron and forms a 1+ ion. [B1] The attraction between ions and electrons is stronger in magnesium, so more energy is needed to overcome it. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#chem.group2) 3.70 g of magnesium nitrate is heated strongly until it has all decomposed: \(\ce{2Mg(NO3)2(s) -> 2MgO(s) + 4NO2(g) + O2(g)}\). Calculate (a) the total volume of gas formed at room conditions, (b) the mass of solid residue.
A: \(M_r(\ce{Mg(NO3)2}) = 24.3 + 2(14.0 + 48.0) = 148.3\) [B1]
\(n = 3.70/148.3 = 0.02495\) mol [M1]
(a) 2 mol of salt gives 5 mol of gas, so \(n(\text{gas}) = 0.02495 \times 5/2 = 0.06237\) mol [M1]
\(V = 0.06237 \times 24.0 = 1.50\) dm³ [A1]
(b) \(m(\ce{MgO}) = 0.02495 \times 40.3\) [M1] \(= 1.01\) g [A1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.group2) (a) Describe how the thermal stability of the Group 2 carbonates changes down the group and explain it. (b) State how the solubility of the hydroxides and of the sulfates changes down the group.
A: (a) The carbonates become more stable to heat down the group (they decompose at higher temperatures). [B1] The cations get larger, so their charge density falls and they polarise the carbonate ion less. [B1] The C–O bond in the carbonate ion is weakened less, so more energy is needed to decompose it. [B1]
(b) The hydroxides become more soluble down the group. [B1] The sulfates become less soluble down the group. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#chem.group17) (a) Chlorine water is added to aqueous potassium iodide. State the observation and write the ionic equation. (b) Explain this result in terms of oxidising ability. (c) State two observations when concentrated sulfuric acid is added to solid sodium iodide. (d) Explain why concentrated sulfuric acid is not reduced by sodium chloride.
A: (a) The colourless solution turns brown [B1] and \(\ce{Cl2 + 2I- -> 2Cl- + I2}\). [B1]
(b) Chlorine is a stronger oxidising agent than iodine, so it oxidises iodide ions to iodine. [B1]
(c) Any two of: steamy fumes; purple fumes (or a black solid); a yellow solid; a smell of bad eggs. [B1] [B1]
(d) The chloride ion is a weaker reducing agent than the iodide ion and cannot reduce sulfuric acid, so only hydrogen chloride forms. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.nitrogen) Ammonium chloride is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide. (a) Write the ionic equation with state symbols. (b) State how the gas is identified. (c) Ammonia reacts with hydrogen chloride gas to form ammonium chloride. Explain how the ammonium ion is formed, and state the type of bond involved.
A: (a) \(\ce{NH4+(aq) + OH-(aq) -> NH3(g) + H2O(l)}\) [B1]
(b) It turns damp red litmus paper blue. [B1]
(c) The lone pair on the nitrogen of \(\ce{NH3}\) is donated to a hydrogen ion (proton) from the hydrogen chloride. [B1] The bond is a coordinate (dative covalent) bond, [B1] so ammonia is acting as a Brønsted–Lowry base (proton acceptor). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★★ (#chem.nitrogen) (a) Explain how nitrogen monoxide is formed in a petrol engine and give an equation. (b) Write an equation for the removal of NO from exhaust gas by carbon monoxide, and name the type of catalyst used. (c) Describe two ways in which oxides of nitrogen contribute to acid rain.
H: In (c) one way forms an acid directly; the other involves sulfur dioxide.
A: (a) Nitrogen and oxygen from the air react at the very high temperature in the engine (or in the spark). [B1] \(\ce{N2 + O2 -> 2NO}\) [B1]
(b) \(\ce{2CO + 2NO -> 2CO2 + N2}\) [B1], using a catalytic converter with platinum, palladium or rhodium [B1]
(c) \(\ce{NO2}\) dissolves in rainwater (with oxygen) to form nitric acid. [B1] Oxides of nitrogen act as catalysts: \(\ce{NO2}\) oxidises \(\ce{SO2}\) to \(\ce{SO3}\) (and is re-formed from NO and oxygen), so sulfuric acid forms faster. [B1]
:::

## Set 5: organic reactions and mechanisms

:::try Set 5: naming, isomers, mechanisms and functional groups
Q: [4 marks] ★ (#chem.org.intro) The compound \(\ce{(CH3)2CHCH2CH2OH}\) is an alcohol. (a) Name it. (b) Give its molecular formula. (c) Classify it as primary, secondary or tertiary. (d) Name the type of structural isomerism shown by this compound and the ether \(\ce{CH3OCH2CH2CH2CH3}\).
A: (a) 3-methylbutan-1-ol [B1]
(b) \(\ce{C5H12O}\) [B1]
(c) Primary, because the carbon bonded to the OH group is attached to only one other carbon. [B1]
(d) Functional group isomerism (the same molecular formula, different functional groups). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.org.isomerism) (a) Explain why but-2-ene shows cis-trans isomerism but but-1-ene does not. (b) Explain why 2-bromobutane has optical isomers. (c) State how the two optical isomers of a compound differ in their effect on light.
A: (a) Rotation about the C=C double bond is restricted by the π bond. [B1] Each carbon of the double bond in but-2-ene carries two different groups (H and \(\ce{CH3}\)); in but-1-ene one carbon carries two hydrogen atoms. [B1]
(b) Carbon 2 is a chiral centre, bonded to four different groups (H, Br, \(\ce{CH3}\), \(\ce{C2H5}\)). [B1] The two molecules are non-superimposable mirror images. [B1]
(c) They rotate the plane of plane-polarised light in opposite directions. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.org.hydrocarbons) Ethane reacts with chlorine in the presence of UV light to give chloroethane. Describe the free-radical substitution mechanism by giving an equation for each step, naming each step. State why a mixture of products is formed.
A: Initiation: \(\ce{Cl2 -> 2Cl^.}\) (UV light provides the energy to break the Cl–Cl bond) [B1]
Propagation: \(\ce{Cl^. + C2H6 -> C2H5^. + HCl}\) [B1]
Propagation: \(\ce{C2H5^. + Cl2 -> C2H5Cl + Cl^.}\) [B1]
Termination: \(\ce{2C2H5^. -> C4H10}\) (or \(\ce{C2H5^. + Cl^. -> C2H5Cl}\)) [B1]
Chloroethane can react further with chlorine, giving dichloroethanes and other products. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.org.hydrocarbons) Describe the mechanism of the reaction between propene and hydrogen bromide. Name the mechanism, describe the curly arrows, and explain which product is the major one.
A: Electrophilic addition. [B1]
A curly arrow from the C=C double bond to the δ+ hydrogen of H–Br [B1], and a curly arrow from the H–Br bond to the bromine atom. [B1]
This forms the secondary carbocation \(\ce{CH3CH^+CH3}\) (and \(\ce{Br-}\)), which is attacked by a lone pair on \(\ce{Br-}\). [B1]
The major product is 2-bromopropane, because the secondary carbocation is more stable than the primary one: two alkyl groups push electron density towards the positive carbon (the inductive effect). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.org.halogen) Give the reagent and conditions needed to convert 1-bromopropane into (a) propan-1-ol, (b) butanenitrile, (c) propylamine, (d) propene.
A: (a) NaOH(aq), heat (under reflux) [B1]
(b) KCN in ethanol, heat [B1]
(c) excess ammonia in ethanol, heated under pressure (sealed tube) [B1]
(d) NaOH in ethanol, heat [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#chem.org.alcohols) Butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol are each heated with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). (a) Which alcohol does not react? (b) Name the product of butan-2-ol and state the colour change. (c) Name the two possible products of butan-1-ol and the conditions that give each. (d) Explain why the aldehyde must be distilled off as it forms to obtain it.
A: (a) 2-methylpropan-2-ol (a tertiary alcohol) [B1]
(b) Butanone [B1]; the orange solution turns green. [B1]
(c) Butanal if the product is distilled out as it forms; [B1] butanoic acid if the mixture is heated under reflux with excess oxidising agent. [B1]
(d) The aldehyde has a lower boiling point than the alcohol (no hydrogen bonding between its molecules), so it distils out before it is oxidised further. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ (#chem.org.carbonyl) Propanone reacts with hydrogen cyanide in the presence of a little potassium cyanide. (a) Write the equation and name the product. (b) State the role of the potassium cyanide. (c) Describe the mechanism by its curly arrows.
A: (a) \(\ce{CH3COCH3 + HCN -> (CH3)2C(OH)CN}\), 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile [B1]
(b) It supplies cyanide ions, \(\ce{CN-}\), the nucleophile (HCN is a weak acid and gives few). [B1]
(c) A curly arrow from the lone pair on the carbon of \(\ce{CN-}\) to the δ+ carbon of the C=O. [B1]
A curly arrow from the C=O double bond to the oxygen, giving an intermediate with a negative oxygen. [B1]
A curly arrow from a lone pair on that oxygen to the hydrogen of HCN, forming the product and regenerating \(\ce{CN-}\). [B1]
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#chem.org.acids) Ethanoic acid reacts with propan-1-ol to form the ester propyl ethanoate. (a) Write the equation and state the catalyst. (b) 6.00 g of ethanoic acid reacted with excess propan-1-ol and gave 7.14 g of ester. Calculate the percentage yield. (c) Give the products of heating propyl ethanoate with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and state how ethanoic acid can be obtained from one of them.
H: Find the moles of ethanoic acid first. One mole gives at most one mole of ester.
H: The ester is \(\ce{C5H10O2}\). Work out its \(M_r\) from the formula.
A: (a) \(\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2CH2OH <=> CH3COOCH2CH2CH3 + H2O}\) [B1]; concentrated sulfuric acid is the catalyst. [B1]
(b) \(M_r(\ce{C5H10O2}) = 5(12.0) + 10(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 102.0\) [B1]
Theoretical yield: \(n = 6.00/60.0 = 0.100\) mol, so \(0.100 \times 102.0 = 10.2\) g [M1]
Percentage yield \(= 7.14/10.2 \times 100 = 70.0\%\) [A1]
(c) Sodium ethanoate [B1] and propan-1-ol. [B1]
Acidify the sodium ethanoate with dilute hydrochloric (or sulfuric) acid. [B1]
:::

## Set 6: synthesis, analysis and synoptic problems

:::try Set 6: routes, spectra and problems that cross topics
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#chem.org.synthesis) Devise a three-step synthesis of butanoic acid from propan-1-ol. For each step give the reagent and conditions and the organic product.
A: Step 1: \(\ce{PCl5}\) (or \(\ce{SOCl2}\), or \(\ce{PCl3}\) and heat) [B1], giving 1-chloropropane [B1]
Step 2: KCN in ethanol, heat [B1], giving butanenitrile [B1]
Step 3: heat under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid (or with dilute NaOH(aq), then acidify) [B1], giving butanoic acid [B1]
Q: [11 marks] ★★★ (#chem.analysis) Compound Z contains only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen: 54.5% C, 9.1% H and 36.4% O by mass. In its mass spectrum the molecular ion peak is at m/z 88, the peak at m/z 89 is 4.4% of the height of the molecular ion peak, and the base peak is at m/z 43. Its infrared spectrum has strong absorptions at 1740 and 1240 cm⁻¹, and no absorption between 2500 and 3600 cm⁻¹. (a) Calculate the empirical formula. (b) Deduce the molecular formula. (c) Show that the m/z 89 peak agrees with your molecular formula. (d) Use the infrared data to name the functional group present and to rule out others. (e) The m/z 43 peak is the ion \(\ce{CH3CO+}\). Suggest the structure of Z.
H: For (a) divide each percentage by the relative atomic mass, then divide by the smallest answer.
H: For (c) the number of carbon atoms is 100 × abundance of M+1 / (1.1 × abundance of M⁺).
H: Look at the Data booklet ranges for C=O, C–O and O–H.
A: (a) C: 54.5/12.0 = 4.54; H: 9.1/1.0 = 9.1; O: 36.4/16.0 = 2.275 [M1]
Ratio C : H : O = 2 : 4 : 1 [M1]
Empirical formula \(\ce{C2H4O}\) [A1]
(b) Its mass is 44.0 and the molecular mass is 88, so the molecular formula is \(\ce{C4H8O2}\). [B1]
(c) Number of carbon atoms \(= 100 \times 4.4/(1.1 \times 100)\) [M1] \(= 4\), as in \(\ce{C4H8O2}\) [A1]
(d) 1740 cm⁻¹ is a C=O in the ester range (1710–1750). [B1] 1240 cm⁻¹ is a C–O bond (1040–1300). [B1] There is no broad O–H absorption, so it is not a carboxylic acid or an alcohol. [B1]
(e) An ester containing the \(\ce{CH3CO}\) group, [B1] so Z is ethyl ethanoate, \(\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}\). [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ (#chem.group2) 2.00 g of impure limestone (mainly \(\ce{CaCO3}\)) was heated strongly until it stopped losing mass. It gave 0.390 dm³ of carbon dioxide at room conditions. The impurities do not give off gas. (a) Write the equation. (b) Calculate the percentage by mass of \(\ce{CaCO3}\) in the limestone. (c) The solid residue is added to water. Write an equation and state whether the solution is acidic or alkaline.
A: (a) \(\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}\) [B1]
(b) \(n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.390/24.0 = 0.01625\) mol, which is also \(n(\ce{CaCO3})\) [M1]
\(m(\ce{CaCO3}) = 0.01625 \times 100.1 = 1.63\) g [M1]
Percentage \(= 1.63/2.00 \times 100 = 81.3\%\) [A1]
(c) \(\ce{CaO + H2O -> Ca(OH)2}\) [B1]; the solution is alkaline. [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★★ (#chem.org.hydrocarbons) A section of an addition polymer is –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–. (a) Draw or write the repeat unit and give the name and structure of the monomer. (b) Explain why this is addition polymerisation. (c) State two problems with the disposal of poly(chloroethene) (PVC).
A: (a) Repeat unit –CH₂–CH(CH₃)– [B1]; the monomer is propene, \(\ce{CH2=CHCH3}\). [B1]
(b) The C=C double bonds of the monomers open and join up, and no other molecule is formed. [B1]
(c) It is not biodegradable, so it stays in landfill. [B1] When burnt it gives toxic, acidic hydrogen chloride gas. [B1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ (#chem.org.carbonyl) Compound X has the molecular formula \(\ce{C3H6O}\). It gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine, no change with Fehling's solution, and a yellow precipitate when warmed with alkaline aqueous iodine. (a) Identify X and explain each test. (b) X is reduced by \(\ce{NaBH4}\). Write the equation, using [H], and name the product. (c) State the reagent that converts this product into an alkene.
H: The 2,4-DNPH test says "carbonyl"; Fehling's tells aldehyde from ketone.
A: (a) The orange precipitate shows a carbonyl group (C=O). [B1] No reaction with Fehling's solution shows it is not an aldehyde. [B1] The yellow precipitate (tri-iodomethane) shows a \(\ce{CH3CO}\) group. [B1] X is propanone. [B1]
(b) \(\ce{CH3COCH3 + 2[H] -> CH3CH(OH)CH3}\) [B1]; the product is propan-2-ol. [B1]
(c) Concentrated sulfuric acid (or heated aluminium oxide) dehydrates it to propene. [B1]
Q: [9 marks] ★★★ (#chem.analysis) Compound Y contains carbon, hydrogen and one other element. Its mass spectrum has molecular ion peaks at m/z 122 and 124 of equal height. Heating Y with aqueous sodium hydroxide gives W. When W is heated with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) it forms Z, which does not react with Fehling's solution but gives a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine. (a) Identify the other element in Y and explain the two peaks. (b) Show that Y is \(\ce{C3H7Br}\). (c) Identify Y, W and Z, and state the conditions for the first step.
H: Use 79 for the mass of the lighter bromine isotope.
H: Z is a ketone. Which alcohol is oxidised to a ketone?
A: (a) Bromine. [B1] The two peaks, two mass units apart, come from \(\ce{^{79}Br}\) and \(\ce{^{81}Br}\), which occur in about equal amounts. [B1]
(b) \(122 - 79 = 43\) [M1], which is \(\ce{C3H7}\) (\(3 \times 12 + 7 = 43\)) [A1]
(c) Z is propanone: it is a ketone (no Fehling's reaction) with a \(\ce{CH3CO}\) group (yellow precipitate). [B1] [B1]
W is propan-2-ol, the secondary alcohol that oxidises to propanone. [B1]
Y is 2-bromopropane. [B1]
Step 1: NaOH(aq), heat (under reflux). [B1]
Q: [9 marks] ★★★ (#chem.energetics) Ethanol is made from ethene and steam: \(\ce{C2H4(g) + H2O(g) <=> C2H5OH(g)}\). (a) Use bond energies (C=C 610, O–H 460, C–C 350, C–H 410, C–O 360 kJ mol⁻¹) to calculate the enthalpy change. (b) Explain the effect of raising the temperature on the equilibrium yield and on the rate. (c) Explain the effect of raising the pressure, and why a very high pressure is not used.
H: Count only the bonds that change. The O–H bond that breaks in water is one of two.
A: (a) Bonds broken: C=C and one O–H: \(610 + 460 = 1070\) kJ [M1]
Bonds made: C–C, C–H and C–O: \(350 + 410 + 360 = 1120\) kJ [M1]
\(\Delta H = 1070 - 1120 = -50\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]
(b) The forward reaction is exothermic, so the equilibrium moves left and the yield falls at a higher temperature. [B1] The rate rises, because more molecules have at least the activation energy. [B1] A compromise temperature is therefore used with a catalyst. [B1]
(c) Two moles of gas become one, so a higher pressure moves the equilibrium to the right and raises the yield. [B1] It also raises the rate (higher concentration, more collisions per second). [B1] Very high pressure needs strong, expensive equipment and more energy to compress the gases. [B1]
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ (#chem.moles) 0.560 g of a hydrocarbon P burned completely and gave 1.76 g of carbon dioxide and 0.720 g of water. P decolourises bromine water, and its mass spectrum has its molecular ion peak at m/z 56. (a) Calculate the empirical formula of P. (b) Deduce the molecular formula. (c) State what the bromine water test shows. (d) P has two geometric isomers. Give the structural formula and the name of P and explain why it has two.
H: Moles of C = moles of \(\ce{CO2}\). Moles of H = 2 × moles of \(\ce{H2O}\).
A: (a) \(M_r(\ce{CO2}) = 44.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{H2O}) = 18.0\) [B1]
\(n(\ce{C}) = 1.76/44.0 = 0.0400\) mol [M1]
\(n(\ce{H}) = 2 \times 0.720/18.0 = 0.0800\) mol [M1]
C : H = 1 : 2, so the empirical formula is \(\ce{CH2}\) [A1]
(b) \(56/14.0 = 4\), so the molecular formula is \(\ce{C4H8}\). [B1]
(c) P is unsaturated: it has a C=C double bond (an alkene). [B1]
(d) P is but-2-ene, \(\ce{CH3CH=CHCH3}\). [B1] There is no rotation about the C=C bond and each carbon of it has two different groups, giving cis and trans forms. [B1]
:::

## Ten multiple-choice questions

:::quiz Paper 1 style: mixed topics
? How many neutrons are in one ion of \(\ce{^{27}_{13}Al^{3+}}\)?
- 10
- 13
+ 14
- 27
> The neutron number is the nucleon number minus the proton number: 27 − 13 = 14. The 10 is the number of electrons in the ion, and 13 is the number of protons.
? 20.0 cm³ of 0.0250 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide is neutralised by 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ sulfuric acid. What volume of the acid is needed?
- 12.5 cm³
+ 25.0 cm³
- 40.0 cm³
- 50.0 cm³
> \(n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.0250 \times 0.0200 = 5.00 \times 10^{-4}\) mol. Each mole of \(\ce{H2SO4}\) neutralises 2 mol of NaOH, so \(n(\ce{H2SO4}) = 2.50 \times 10^{-4}\) mol and \(V = 2.50 \times 10^{-4}/0.0100 = 0.0250\) dm³. 50.0 cm³ ignores the 1 : 2 ratio.
? The burning of 0.0200 mol of a fuel raises the temperature of 100 g of water by 6.0 K. What is the enthalpy change of combustion, in kJ mol⁻¹?
- −2.51
+ −125
- +125
- −25.1
> \(q = 100 \times 4.18 \times 6.0 = 2508\) J = 2.508 kJ. Dividing by 0.0200 mol gives 125 kJ mol⁻¹, and the sign is negative because the water got hotter. −2.51 forgets to divide by the moles.
? The forward reaction in an equilibrium is exothermic. Which change alters the value of \(K_c\)?
- adding a catalyst
- increasing the pressure
+ increasing the temperature
- increasing the concentration of a reactant
> Only a change of temperature alters the equilibrium constant. The other changes can move the position of equilibrium, or the rate, but \(K_c\) stays the same.
? In the reaction \(\ce{Cl2 + 2NaOH -> NaCl + NaClO + H2O}\), what happens to the oxidation number of chlorine?
- it falls from 0 to −1 only
- it rises from 0 to +1 only
+ it falls from 0 to −1 and rises from 0 to +1
- it rises from −1 to +1
> Chlorine is both reduced (to chloride) and oxidised (to chlorate(I)), which is disproportionation.
? Which statement about Group 2 compounds is correct, going down the group from magnesium to barium?
- the sulfates become more soluble
- the carbonates decompose at lower temperatures
+ the hydroxides become more soluble
- the nitrates decompose at lower temperatures
> Hydroxides become more soluble down the group, and sulfates less soluble. The thermal stability of carbonates and nitrates increases, because the larger cations polarise the anion less.
? Which change increases the rate of a reaction mainly by increasing the proportion of molecules with energy at least equal to the activation energy?
- increasing the concentration of a reactant
- increasing the surface area of a solid reactant
+ increasing the temperature
- increasing the pressure of a gaseous reactant
> A rise in temperature moves the Boltzmann distribution so that more molecules exceed the activation energy. The other changes only increase the frequency of collisions.
? Which compound gives a yellow precipitate when warmed with alkaline aqueous iodine?
- propanal
- propan-1-ol
- methanol
+ propan-2-ol
> The test needs a \(\ce{CH3CO}\) group or a \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}\) group. Propan-2-ol has the second and is oxidised by the iodine to the first. Propanal and propan-1-ol have neither.
? The molecular ion peak of a compound is at m/z 72 and the peak at m/z 73 is 3.3% of its height. How many carbon atoms does a molecule contain?
- 2
+ 3
- 4
- 5
> Number of carbon atoms \(= 100 \times 3.3/(1.1 \times 100) = 3\). Each carbon atom adds about 1.1% to the m/z 73 peak.
? An infrared spectrum shows a very broad absorption from 2500 to 3000 cm⁻¹ and a strong absorption at 1720 cm⁻¹. Which functional group is present?
- an alcohol
- an ester
- an aldehyde
+ a carboxylic acid
> The broad absorption between 2500 and 3000 cm⁻¹ is the O–H of a carboxylic acid, and 1720 cm⁻¹ is its C=O. An alcohol O–H is at 3200 to 3600 cm⁻¹ and an ester shows no broad O–H band.

:::recap
The five commonest slips across these sets:
- **Units and significant figures.** A concentration needs mol dm⁻³, an enthalpy change kJ mol⁻¹ with a sign, \(K_c\) and \(K_p\) their own units (or none, when they cancel). Give 3 s.f. unless the data say otherwise.
- **Forgetting the mole ratio.** Write the balanced equation first and read the ratio from it (1 : 2 in the sulfuric acid titration, 2 : 5 in the manganate(VII) titrations).
- **Vague explanations.** Name the cause: "the C–I bond is weaker", "the cation has a higher charge density and polarises the anion more", "a larger proportion of molecules has at least the activation energy".
- **Incomplete reagents and conditions.** Give the reagent and the solvent and the conditions (NaOH in ethanol, heat; acidified dichromate, distil or reflux). Each is a separate mark.
- **Curly arrows from the wrong place.** An arrow starts at a lone pair or a bond, never at an atom or a charge, and ends at the atom or bond that receives the electrons.
