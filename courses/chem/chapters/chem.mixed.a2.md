:::why
A topic chapter tells you which topic you are in. Paper 4 does not. One question jumps from a lattice-energy cycle to a rate equation, and the next asks for the colour of a complex and the pH of a buffer. The skill that wins marks is **recognising the topic** from the wording and then recalling the right method. Mixing topics on purpose is the best way to train that, because the brain has to choose a method instead of repeating the one it used for the last ten questions.
:::

## How to use the six sets

Each set has eight questions from at least five different topics, from ★ (one method) to ★★★ (several steps or synoptic). Every question names the chapter to revise, and the answers show the mark scheme line by line.

:::tip How to get the most from a set
1. **Do the set in one sitting**, with a Periodic Table and the Data booklet values open, and a stopwatch: about 1 minute per mark.
2. **Write every answer in full** before you open the hints or the answer. Look at a hint only when you have been stuck for two minutes.
3. **Mark yourself like an examiner.** Give a mark only for a creditworthy point that is written down. Copy the lost marks into a list of "my usual slips": units, significant figures, missing state symbols, explanations without a cause.
4. **Redo the questions you lost marks on** after two days, without the answer. Then go to the revise link under the answer.
5. A ★★★ question is meant to feel hard. If you can do the ★ and ★★ questions in a set, your marks are safe; the ★★★ questions decide the top grades.
:::

The sets follow the order of the course, but each one includes questions from other chapters, so no set is "only" its theme.

## Set 1: Energetics, with moles, Group 2, phenol and bond energies

:::try Set 1: Energetics and friends
Q: [7 marks] (#chem.lattice) ★★ This question is about calcium fluoride, \(\ce{CaF2}\).

(a) Define the term *lattice energy*, and write an equation, with state symbols, for the lattice energy of calcium fluoride. (3 marks)

(b) Calculate the lattice energy of calcium fluoride from these data (data for this question), in kJ mol⁻¹: enthalpy change of formation of \(\ce{CaF2}\) = −1220; \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{at}}\)(Ca) = +178; first ionisation energy of Ca = +590; second ionisation energy of Ca = +1150; first electron affinity of F = −328. The F–F bond energy is 158 kJ mol⁻¹. (4 marks)
A: (a) the enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions [B1]
\(\ce{Ca^2+(g) + 2F-(g) -> CaF2(s)}\): correct species and balancing [B1], correct state symbols [B1]

(b) two moles of F atoms are needed: \(2 \times \tfrac{1}{2}(158) = +158\) kJ [B1]
Hess's law, with \(2 \times (-328)\) for the two electron affinities:
\[\Delta H_{\mathrm{f}} = \Delta H_{\mathrm{at}}(\mathrm{Ca}) + \mathrm{IE}_1 + \mathrm{IE}_2 + 2\Delta H_{\mathrm{at}}(\mathrm F) + 2\,\mathrm{EA}_1 + \Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}}\] [M1]
\[\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}} = -1220 - (178 + 590 + 1150 + 158 - 656) = -1220 - 1420\] [M1]
\(= -2640\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]
:::

Q: [5 marks] (#chem.org.phenol) ★★ Phenol, \(\ce{C6H5OH}\), reacts with bromine water at room temperature to give 2,4,6-tribromophenol.

(a) Write an equation for the reaction. (1 mark)

(b) A student reacted 1.88 g of phenol and obtained 5.00 g of 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Calculate the percentage yield. (4 marks)
A: (a) \(\ce{C6H5OH + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr}\) [B1]

(b) \(M_r\) of phenol = 94.0 and \(M_r\) of \(\ce{C6H2Br3OH}\) = 330.7 [B1]
\(n(\text{phenol}) = 1.88 / 94.0 = 0.0200\) mol [M1]
theoretical mass = \(0.0200 \times 330.7 = 6.614\) g (one mole of product per mole of phenol) [M1]
percentage yield = \(5.00 / 6.614 \times 100 = 75.6\%\) [A1]
:::

Q: [5 marks] (#chem.lattice) ★★ Magnesium chloride dissolves in water. Use these data (data for this question), in kJ mol⁻¹: lattice energy of \(\ce{MgCl2}\) = −2526; enthalpy change of hydration of \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) = −1920; enthalpy change of hydration of \(\ce{Cl-}\) = −364.

(a) Define the term *enthalpy change of hydration*. (1 mark)

(b) Calculate the enthalpy change of solution of magnesium chloride. (3 marks)

(c) The enthalpy change of hydration of \(\ce{Ca^2+}\) is −1650 kJ mol⁻¹. Explain why that of \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) is more exothermic. (1 mark)
A: (a) the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water to form one mole of aqueous ions (to infinite dilution) [B1]

(b) from the energy cycle:
\[\Delta H_{\mathrm{sol}} = -\Delta H_{\mathrm{latt}} + \Delta H_{\mathrm{hyd}}(\ce{Mg^2+}) + 2\Delta H_{\mathrm{hyd}}(\ce{Cl-})\] [M1]
\(= +2526 - 1920 + 2(-364)\) (two chloride ions) [M1]
\(= -122\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]

(c) \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) has the smaller ionic radius (same charge), so a higher charge density and a stronger attraction to the water molecules [B1]
:::

:::try
Q: [3 marks] (#chem.group2) ★ Magnesium carbonate decomposes on heating at a lower temperature than barium carbonate. Explain this difference.
A: the \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) ion is smaller than \(\ce{Ba^2+}\) (same charge), so it has a higher charge density [B1]
it polarises (distorts the electron cloud of) the carbonate ion more strongly [B1]
which weakens the C–O bond in the carbonate ion, so \(\ce{CO2}\) is lost at a lower temperature [B1]
:::

Q: [4 marks] (#chem.lattice) ★★ The thermal decomposition of calcium carbonate is \(\ce{CaCO3(s) -> CaO(s) + CO2(g)}\), with \(\Delta H^{\ominus} = +178\) kJ mol⁻¹ and \(\Delta S^{\ominus} = +161\) J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

(a) Calculate \(\Delta G^{\ominus}\) at 298 K. (2 marks)

(b) Calculate the minimum temperature at which the decomposition is feasible. (2 marks)
A: (a) \(\Delta G^{\ominus} = \Delta H^{\ominus} - T\Delta S^{\ominus} = 178 - 298 \times 0.161\) (entropy converted to kJ) [M1]
\(= +130\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]

(b) feasible when \(\Delta G \le 0\), so \(T = \Delta H / \Delta S = 178\,000 / 161\) [M1]
\(= 1.11 \times 10^{3}\) K [A1]
:::

Q: [4 marks] (#chem.moles) ★★ A 1.25 g sample of impure calcium carbonate was heated strongly until it stopped losing mass. The carbon dioxide given off had a volume of 0.232 dm³, measured at room temperature and pressure (24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹). Calculate the percentage by mass of calcium carbonate in the sample, assuming the impurities do not release a gas.
A: \(M_r(\ce{CaCO3}) = 40.1 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 100.1\) [B1]
\(n(\ce{CO2}) = 0.232 / 24.0 = 9.667 \times 10^{-3}\) mol, and \(n(\ce{CaCO3})\) is the same (1 : 1) [M1]
mass of \(\ce{CaCO3} = 9.667 \times 10^{-3} \times 100.1 = 0.9677\) g [M1]
percentage = \(0.9677 / 1.25 \times 100 = 77.4\%\) [A1]
:::

:::try
Q: [8 marks] (#chem.lattice) ★★★ The Haber process is \(\ce{N2(g) + 3H2(g) <=> 2NH3(g)}\), with \(\Delta H^{\ominus} = -92\) kJ mol⁻¹. Standard entropies in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: \(\ce{N2(g)}\) 192, \(\ce{H2(g)}\) 131, \(\ce{NH3(g)}\) 193.

(a) Calculate \(\Delta S^{\ominus}\) for the reaction. (2 marks)

(b) Explain the sign of \(\Delta S^{\ominus}\). (1 mark)

(c) Calculate \(\Delta G^{\ominus}\) at 298 K. (2 marks)

(d) Calculate the temperature above which the reaction is no longer feasible. (2 marks)

(e) The industrial process is run at a much higher temperature than this. Suggest why. (1 mark)
H: \(\Delta S = \Sigma S(\text{products}) - \Sigma S(\text{reactants})\): multiply each entropy by its coefficient in the equation.
H: Convert \(\Delta S\) to kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ before using \(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\).
H: At the changeover temperature \(\Delta G = 0\), so \(T = \Delta H / \Delta S\); both are negative here.
A: (a) \(\Delta S^{\ominus} = 2(193) - [192 + 3(131)]\) [M1]
\(= 386 - 585 = -199\) J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ [A1]

(b) four moles of gas become two moles of gas, so there are fewer ways of arranging the particles and the entropy falls [B1]

(c) \(\Delta G^{\ominus} = -92 - 298 \times (-0.199)\) [M1]
\(= -32.7\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]

(d) \(\Delta G = 0\) at \(T = \Delta H / \Delta S = (-92) / (-0.199)\) [M1]
\(= 462\) K; above this temperature \(-T\Delta S\) outweighs \(\Delta H\) and \(\Delta G\) becomes positive [A1]

(e) at lower temperatures the rate is far too slow (the activation energy is high), so a higher temperature is used to reach equilibrium in a useful time, with a catalyst [B1]
:::

:::try
Q: [4 marks] (#chem.energetics) ★★ Use bond energies from the Data booklet to calculate the enthalpy change of combustion of methane, \(\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}\), taking \(\ce{C=O}\) in \(\ce{CO2}\) as 805 kJ mol⁻¹. Then suggest why the experimental value, −802 kJ mol⁻¹, is slightly different.
A: bonds broken: \(4(410) + 2(496) = 2632\) kJ [M1]
bonds formed: \(2(805) + 4(460) = 3450\) kJ [M1]
\(\Delta H = 2632 - 3450 = -818\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]
bond energies are averages taken over many compounds, so they do not match the actual bonds in methane and water exactly [B1]
:::

## Set 2: Electrochemistry and redox titrations, with equilibria and electrolysis

:::try Set 2: Electrochemistry and redox titrations
Q: [3 marks] (#chem.electrochem) ★ A current of 0.750 A is passed for 40.0 minutes through aqueous copper(II) sulfate using copper electrodes. Calculate the mass of copper deposited at the cathode. \(F = 9.65 \times 10^{4}\) C mol⁻¹.
A: \(Q = It = 0.750 \times 40.0 \times 60 = 1800\) C [M1]
\(n(\ce{Cu}) = Q / (2F) = 1800 / (2 \times 9.65 \times 10^{4}) = 9.326 \times 10^{-3}\) mol (two electrons per \(\ce{Cu^2+}\)) [M1]
mass \(= 9.326 \times 10^{-3} \times 63.5 = 0.592\) g [A1]
:::

:::try
Q: [6 marks] (#chem.electrochem) ★★ A cell is made of a platinum electrode in a solution containing \(\ce{Fe^3+}\) and \(\ce{Fe^2+}\) (each 1.00 mol dm⁻³), joined by a salt bridge to a platinum electrode in a solution containing \(\ce{Sn^4+}\) and \(\ce{Sn^2+}\) (each 1.00 mol dm⁻³). \(E^{\ominus}(\ce{Fe^3+}/\ce{Fe^2+}) = +0.77\) V and \(E^{\ominus}(\ce{Sn^4+}/\ce{Sn^2+}) = +0.15\) V.

(a) Write the equation for the spontaneous cell reaction. (1 mark)

(b) Calculate \(E^{\ominus}_{\mathrm{cell}}\). (2 marks)

(c) State the direction of electron flow in the external circuit. (1 mark)

(d) Calculate \(\Delta G^{\ominus}\) for the reaction in (a), in kJ mol⁻¹. (2 marks)
A: (a) \(\ce{2Fe^3+ + Sn^2+ -> 2Fe^2+ + Sn^4+}\) [B1]

(b) \(E^{\ominus}_{\mathrm{cell}} = 0.77 - 0.15\) (more positive minus less positive) [M1]
\(= +0.62\) V [A1]

(c) from the \(\ce{Sn^4+}/\ce{Sn^2+}\) half-cell (where \(\ce{Sn^2+}\) is oxidised) through the wire to the \(\ce{Fe^3+}/\ce{Fe^2+}\) half-cell [B1]

(d) \(\Delta G^{\ominus} = -nFE^{\ominus}_{\mathrm{cell}} = -2 \times 9.65 \times 10^{4} \times 0.62\) [M1]
\(= -1.20 \times 10^{5}\) J mol⁻¹ \(= -120\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]
:::

:::try
Q: [4 marks] (#chem.electrochem) ★★ A half-cell consists of a copper electrode in 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ copper(II) sulfate. \(E^{\ominus}(\ce{Cu^2+}/\ce{Cu}) = +0.34\) V and \(E^{\ominus}(\ce{Zn^2+}/\ce{Zn}) = -0.76\) V. Calculate the electrode potential of this half-cell, and the cell potential when it is connected to a standard zinc half-cell.
A: \(E = E^{\ominus} + \dfrac{0.059}{z}\log[\ce{Cu^2+}] = 0.34 + \dfrac{0.059}{2}\log(0.0100)\) [M1]
\(E = 0.34 - 0.059 = +0.28\) V (0.281 V) [A1]
\(E_{\mathrm{cell}} = 0.281 - (-0.76)\) [M1]
\(= 1.04\) V [A1]
:::

Q: [5 marks] (#chem.transition) ★★ Iron tablets contain iron(II) sulfate, \(\ce{FeSO4}\), and an inert binder. One 2.50 g tablet was dissolved in dilute sulfuric acid and the solution was made up to 250 cm³. A 25.0 cm³ portion needed 14.20 cm³ of 0.0200 mol dm⁻³ potassium manganate(VII) for complete reaction. \(\ce{MnO4- + 5Fe^2+ + 8H+ -> Mn^2+ + 5Fe^3+ + 4H2O}\). Calculate the percentage by mass of \(\ce{FeSO4}\) in the tablet.

mass of \(\ce{FeSO4} = 1.42 \times 10^{-2} \times 151.9 = 2.157\) g (\(M_r\) = 55.8 + 32.1 + 64.0) [M1]

The equations are \(\ce{2Cu^2+ + 4I- -> 2CuI + I2}\) and \(\ce{I2 + 2S2O3^2- -> 2I- + S4O6^2-}\). Calculate the percentage by mass of copper in the brass.

Q: [4 marks] (#chem.electrochem) ★★ Aqueous copper(II) sulfate is electrolysed using graphite electrodes. Name the product at each electrode, write the half-equation for each, and explain why that product forms at the cathode.
A: cathode: copper forms, because \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) (\(E^{\ominus} = +0.34\) V) is much more easily reduced than \(\ce{H+}\) or water (\(E^{\ominus} = 0.00\) V and −0.83 V) [B1]
\(\ce{Cu^2+ + 2e- -> Cu}\) [B1]
anode: oxygen forms, because water is oxidised in preference to the sulfate ion [B1]
\(\ce{2H2O -> O2 + 4H+ + 4e-}\) [B1]

Q: [6 marks] (#chem.electrochem) ★★★ The copper(I) ion is unstable in water and disproportionates: \(\ce{2Cu+(aq) -> Cu^2+(aq) + Cu(s)}\). \(E^{\ominus}(\ce{Cu+}/\ce{Cu}) = +0.52\) V and \(E^{\ominus}(\ce{Cu^2+}/\ce{Cu+}) = +0.15\) V.

(a) Use oxidation numbers to show that this is a disproportionation. (2 marks)

(b) Calculate \(E^{\ominus}_{\mathrm{cell}}\) for the reaction and state what it shows. (2 marks)

(c) Calculate \(\Delta G^{\ominus}\) for the reaction as written. (2 marks)
H: In the equation, one \(\ce{Cu+}\) is reduced and another is oxidised: which half-reaction is each?
H: The reduction half-reaction is \(\ce{Cu+ + e- -> Cu}\) (+0.52 V); the oxidation is the reverse of \(\ce{Cu^2+ + e- -> Cu+}\).
H: One electron is transferred, so \(n = 1\).
A: (a) \(\ce{Cu+ -> Cu^2+}\): oxidation number +1 to +2, so oxidation [B1]
\(\ce{Cu+ -> Cu}\): +1 to 0, so reduction; the same species is both oxidised and reduced [B1]

(b) \(E^{\ominus}_{\mathrm{cell}} = +0.52 - (+0.15)\) [M1]
\(= +0.37\) V; positive, so the reaction is feasible [A1]

(c) \(\Delta G^{\ominus} = -nFE^{\ominus} = -1 \times 9.65 \times 10^{4} \times 0.37\) [M1]
\(= -3.57 \times 10^{4}\) J mol⁻¹ \(= -35.7\) kJ mol⁻¹ [A1]

Q: [4 marks] (#chem.equilibria) ★★ At equilibrium, 2.00 mol of dinitrogen tetroxide, \(\ce{N2O4}\), partly dissociated: \(\ce{N2O4(g) <=> 2NO2(g)}\). The equilibrium mixture contained 1.50 mol of \(\ce{N2O4}\) and 1.00 mol of \(\ce{NO2}\) at a total pressure of \(2.00 \times 10^{5}\) Pa. ## Set 3: Acids, buffers, solubility and partition, with amines, gases and phenol

:::try Set 3: Acids, buffers, Ksp and partition
Q: [3 marks] (#chem.org.nitrogen) ★ Put ammonia, ethylamine and phenylamine in order of increasing basicity, and explain the order.
A: phenylamine < ammonia < ethylamine [B1]
ethylamine: the ethyl group donates electron density (positive inductive effect) to nitrogen, so its lone pair is more available to accept \(\ce{H+}\) [B1]
phenylamine: the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available to accept \(\ce{H+}\) [B1]
:::

:::try
Q: [3 marks] (#chem.states) ★★ A sample of a volatile liquid of mass 0.150 g was vaporised and the vapour occupied 62.5 cm³ at 373 K and 101 kPa. Use \(pV = nRT\) to calculate the relative molecular mass of the liquid. \(R = 8.31\) J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
A: \(p = 1.01 \times 10^{5}\) Pa and \(V = 6.25 \times 10^{-5}\) m³, so
\[n = \frac{pV}{RT} = \frac{1.01 \times 10^{5} \times 6.25 \times 10^{-5}}{8.31 \times 373}\] [M1]
\(n = 2.037 \times 10^{-3}\) mol [M1]
\(M_r = 0.150 / 2.037 \times 10^{-3} = 73.7\) [A1]
:::

:::try
Q: [8 marks] (#chem.acids2) ★★★ A buffer solution of volume 500 cm³ contains 0.250 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid and 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium ethanoate. \(K_a\) for ethanoic acid = \(1.74 \times 10^{-5}\) mol dm⁻³.

(a) Calculate the pH of the buffer. (3 marks)

(b) Explain, with an equation, how the buffer keeps its pH almost constant when a small amount of hydrochloric acid is added. (2 marks)

(c) Calculate the pH after 0.0100 mol of hydrogen chloride is added to the buffer. Assume the volume does not change. (3 marks)
H: In a buffer, \([\ce{H+}] = K_a \times [\text{acid}] / [\text{salt}]\); the acid and its salt can both be used as their concentrations.
H: In (c), the added \(\ce{H+}\) reacts completely with the ethanoate ion, so work in moles: acid goes up and ethanoate goes down by 0.0100 mol.
A: (a) \([\ce{H+}] = K_a [\ce{CH3COOH}] / [\ce{CH3COO-}] = 1.74 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.250 / 0.100 = 4.35 \times 10^{-5}\) mol dm⁻³ [M1]
\(\mathrm{pH} = -\log(4.35 \times 10^{-5})\) [M1]
\(= 4.36\) [A1]

(b) \(\ce{CH3COO- + H+ -> CH3COOH}\): the added acid is removed by the ethanoate ion [B1]
the buffer holds a large reserve of both ethanoate and ethanoic acid, so the ratio \([\ce{CH3COOH}]/[\ce{CH3COO-}]\) and hence \([\ce{H+}]\) change very little [B1]

(c) before: \(n(\text{acid}) = 0.125\) mol and \(n(\text{ethanoate}) = 0.0500\) mol; after: 0.135 mol and 0.0400 mol [M1]
\([\ce{H+}] = 1.74 \times 10^{-5} \times 0.135 / 0.0400 = 5.87 \times 10^{-5}\) mol dm⁻³ [M1]
pH \(= 4.23\) (a fall of only 0.13) [A1]
:::

:::try
Q: [6 marks] (#chem.acids2) ★★ The solubility of magnesium hydroxide, \(\ce{Mg(OH)2}\), in water at 298 K is \(1.20 \times 10^{-4}\) mol dm⁻³.

(a) Write the expression for \(K_{sp}\) and calculate its value, with units. (3 marks)

(b) Calculate the solubility of magnesium hydroxide in 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ magnesium chloride solution. (2 marks)

(c) Explain why the solubility is lower in magnesium chloride solution. (1 mark)
A: (a) \(K_{sp} = [\ce{Mg^2+}][\ce{OH-}]^2\) [B1]
\([\ce{Mg^2+}] = s\) and \([\ce{OH-}] = 2s\), so \(K_{sp} = 4s^3 = 4(1.20 \times 10^{-4})^3\) [M1]
\(= 6.91 \times 10^{-12}\) mol³ dm⁻⁹ [A1]

(b) \([\ce{OH-}]^2 = K_{sp} / [\ce{Mg^2+}] = 6.91 \times 10^{-12} / 0.0100\) (the \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) from the dissolved solid is negligible) [M1]
\([\ce{OH-}] = 2.63 \times 10^{-5}\) mol dm⁻³, so solubility \(= \tfrac12[\ce{OH-}] = 1.31 \times 10^{-5}\) mol dm⁻³ [A1]

(c) common ion effect: the extra \(\ce{Mg^2+}\) shifts \(\ce{Mg(OH)2(s) <=> Mg^2+(aq) + 2OH-(aq)}\) to the left, so less dissolves [B1]
:::

:::try
Q: [6 marks] (#chem.acids2) ★★ An organic acid HX is shaken with water and ether. The partition coefficient \(K_{pc} = [\mathrm{HX}]_{\text{ether}} / [\mathrm{HX}]_{\text{water}} = 12.0\). A solution of 0.500 g of HX in 100 cm³ of water is extracted with ether.

(a) Calculate the mass of HX extracted by one 50.0 cm³ portion of ether. (3 marks)

(b) Calculate the total mass extracted by two successive 25.0 cm³ portions, and say which method is better. (3 marks)
A: (a) let \(m\) g be extracted: \(\dfrac{m/50.0}{(0.500 - m)/100} = 12.0\) [M1]
\(2m = 12.0(0.500 - m)\), so \(14m = 6.00\) [M1]
\(m = 0.429\) g [A1]

(b) first extraction: \(\dfrac{x/25.0}{(0.500 - x)/100} = 12.0\) gives \(x = 0.375\) g, leaving 0.125 g [M1]
second extraction of the 0.125 g: \(y = 0.09375\) g [M1]
total \(= 0.469\) g, which is more than 0.429 g, so two small portions are better than one large one [A1]
:::

:::try
Q: [4 marks] (#chem.org.phenol) ★★ Ethanol, water and phenol are all very weak acids.

(a) Put them in order of increasing acidity and explain why phenol is the most acidic. (3 marks)

(b) Write an equation for the reaction of phenol with aqueous sodium hydroxide. Ethanol does not react. (1 mark)
A: (a) ethanol < water < phenol [B1]
the phenoxide ion is stabilised because a lone pair on oxygen overlaps with the delocalised π system, spreading the negative charge into the ring [B1]
the ethoxide ion is destabilised by the electron-donating ethyl group and its charge is not delocalised [B1]

(b) \(\ce{C6H5OH + NaOH -> C6H5ONa + H2O}\) (or with \(\ce{C6H5O- + H2O}\)) [B1]
:::

:::try
Q: [6 marks] (#chem.acids2) ★★ A solution of a weak acid HA of unknown concentration has a pH of 2.95. A 25.0 cm³ portion needed 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ sodium hydroxide for neutralisation.

(a) Calculate the concentration of HA. (2 marks)

(b) Calculate \(K_a\) for HA, stating an assumption you make. (3 marks)

(c) Calculate \(\mathrm{p}K_a\). (1 mark)

- 673 K
> \(T = \Delta H / \Delta S = 40\,000 / 100 = 400\) K. 0.400 K forgets to convert kJ to J; 127 K and 673 K wrongly subtract or add 273 to a temperature that is already in kelvin.

? In a half-cell, \([\ce{Fe^3+}] = 0.100\) mol dm⁻³ and \([\ce{Fe^2+}] = 0.0100\) mol dm⁻³. \(E^{\ominus}(\ce{Fe^3+}/\ce{Fe^2+}) = +0.77\) V. What is \(E\)?
- +0.71 V
- +0.77 V
+ +0.83 V
- +0.89 V
> \(E = 0.77 + 0.059 \log(0.100/0.0100) = 0.77 + 0.059 = +0.83\) V (z = 1, the oxidised form is on top). +0.71 V inverts the ratio; +0.89 V doubles the correction.

? A buffer contains equal amounts of a weak acid HA (\(\mathrm{p}K_a = 4.20\)) and its sodium salt. What is its pH?
- 2.10
+ 4.20
- 7.00
- 9.80
> With \([\mathrm{A^-}] = [\mathrm{HA}]\), \(\log 1 = 0\) and \(\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a\). 9.80 is \(14 - 4.20\), which is not the relation.

? The \(K_{sp}\) of \(\ce{Ag2CrO4}\) is \(4.0 \times 10^{-12}\) mol³ dm⁻⁹. What is its solubility in water, in mol dm⁻³?
- \(2.0 \times 10^{-6}\)
- \(1.6 \times 10^{-4}\)
+ \(1.0 \times 10^{-4}\)
- \(1.0 \times 10^{-12}\)
> \(K_{sp} = (2s)^2 s = 4s^3\), so \(s = (4.0 \times 10^{-12}/4)^{1/3} = 1.0 \times 10^{-4}\). \(2.0 \times 10^{-6}\) takes a square root, \(1.6 \times 10^{-4}\) forgets to divide by 4, and \(1.0 \times 10^{-12}\) forgets the cube root.

? Doubling [A] multiplies the initial rate by 4, and doubling [B] has no effect. What is the rate equation?
- rate = \(k[\mathrm{A}][\mathrm{B}]\)
- rate = \(k[\mathrm{A}]^2[\mathrm{B}]\)
+ rate = \(k[\mathrm{A}]^2\)
- rate = \(k[\mathrm{A}]^4\)
> A factor of 4 for doubling means second order (\(2^2\)); no effect means zero order in B, so B is left out of the equation.

? Why is an aqueous solution of \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) colourless?
- The \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) ion has no d electrons.
- Water ligands cannot split the d orbitals of \(\ce{Zn^2+}\).
- The \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) ion has no unpaired electrons.
+ The \(\ce{Zn^2+}\) ion has a full 3d sub-shell, so no electron can be promoted between d orbitals.
> Zn²⁺ is 3d¹⁰. Colour comes from d–d promotion, which needs a partly filled d sub-shell. Unpaired electrons are not the cause: some coloured ions have none.

? In a ¹H NMR spectrum a signal is a quartet. How many equivalent hydrogens are on the adjacent carbon?
- 4
+ 3
- 2
- 1
> The n + 1 rule: a quartet has 4 lines, so n = 3. A quartet next to a triplet is the ethyl group.

? Which pair of monomers gives a polyamide?
+ \(\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2}\) and \(\ce{ClCO(CH2)4COCl}\)
- \(\ce{HO(CH2)2OH}\) and \(\ce{HOOC(CH2)4COOH}\)
- \(\ce{H2N(CH2)6NH2}\) and \(\ce{HO(CH2)2OH}\)
- \(\ce{CH2=CHCl}\) alone
> A diamine and a diacyl chloride form amide links and lose \(\ce{HCl}\). The second pair gives a polyester, the third has no group that reacts with either, and the last is an addition polymer.

:::recap Common mistakes in A Level questions
- **Lattice energy and hydration**: lattice energy is gaseous ions to solid, so it is exothermic. Take care with two ions in the formula (\(2 \times\) the electron affinity, \(2 \times\) the atomisation) and with the second electron affinity of oxygen, which is endothermic.
- **\(\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S\)**: convert \(\Delta S\) from J to kJ, and give the temperature in kelvin. Entropy changes need a reason (more gas moles, more particles).
- **Electrode potentials**: subtract the less positive from the more positive. Standard conditions are 298 K, 1 mol dm⁻³ and 101 kPa for a gas; use \(n\) from the half-equations in \(\Delta G = -nFE\).
- **Buffers and \(K_{sp}\)**: use moles after the reaction for a buffer with added acid or alkali; for \(K_{sp}\), write the expression first and square or cube the ion concentrations as the formula says.
- **Rate equations**: orders come from data, never from the equation. State units of \(k\). A mechanism must have a rate-determining step that fits the rate equation.
- **Transition elements**: colour needs a partly filled d sub-shell and split d orbitals; write "pale blue precipitate" or "dark blue solution" with the state.
- **Organic marks**: reagent and conditions are separate marks, so give both (for example "concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) and concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C"). Mechanism arrows start at a lone pair or a bond.
- **NMR**: say what each signal tells you (environment, splitting, area) and name the group it comes from.
:::
