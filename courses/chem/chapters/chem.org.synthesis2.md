Q: Which reaction of a halogenoalkane increases the length of the carbon chain, and what are its reagent and conditions? (from [Organic synthesis (AS)](#chem.org.synthesis))
A: Reaction with cyanide ions: KCN in ethanol, heated under reflux. The carbon of the \(\ce{CN-}\) ion becomes part of the chain, giving a nitrile with one more carbon atom.
Q: Name the organic product of the mononitration of benzoic acid and explain where the \(\ce{NO2}\) group goes. (from [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes))
A: 3-nitrobenzoic acid. The COOH group withdraws electrons from the ring and directs the new group to the 3-position.
Q: What conditions are needed to turn phenylamine into a diazonium salt, and why must the temperature be controlled? (from [Amines, amides and amino acids](#chem.org.nitrogen))
A: Sodium nitrite with dilute hydrochloric acid (which makes nitrous acid), below 10 °C. Above that temperature the diazonium ion breaks down, losing nitrogen.

## The skill: from one molecule to another

:::def Synthetic route
A sequence of reactions that converts a starting compound into a target compound, in which the product of each step is the reactant of the next.
:::

The skills in the syllabus are three: to identify the functional groups in a molecule from their reactions and predict what it will do; to devise a route of several steps; and to analyse a route you are given, saying what type of reaction each step is, what reagent it needs and what by-products it makes. The first two are the two directions of the same job, reading a molecule and writing one.

:::steps
1. **Compare the target with the start.** Count the carbon atoms. Is there a ring? Which functional groups must appear, disappear or change?
2. **Work backwards** from the target: which reaction makes this group? What would the compound before it be?
3. **Check the carbon skeleton.** If the target has more carbon atoms than the start, one step must add them (a cyanide step, or a Friedel–Crafts step on a ring).
4. **Put the steps in order.** On a ring, the groups already present decide where a new group goes. Choose the order that puts each group in the right place.
5. **Write the reagent and conditions for every step** (solvent, temperature, heat under reflux or not), and check that no other group in the molecule reacts in the same step.
:::

The first part of this chapter collects the A Level reactions in one place. Each later section then shows a feature of planning that the map alone does not give you.

## Reducing agents compared

Four reducing agents appear in the syllabus. Each reduces some groups and leaves others alone, so choosing the right one is part of planning a route.

:::key Reducing agents in 9701
| Reducing agent | Conditions | Reduces | Does not reduce |
|---|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{NaBH4}\) | in water or ethanol | aldehydes → primary alcohols, ketones → secondary alcohols | C=C, carboxylic acids, esters, amides, nitriles |
| \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) | dry ether (reacts violently with water) | aldehydes, ketones, carboxylic acids (→ primary alcohols), amides (→ amines), nitriles (→ amines) | C=C, the benzene ring |
| \(\ce{H2}\) with Pt or Ni | heat | C=C (→ C–C), the benzene ring (→ cyclohexane), nitriles (→ amines) | acids, amides (not used for these in the syllabus) |
| hot Sn with concentrated HCl, then NaOH(aq) | heat under reflux | a nitro group on a ring (→ \(\ce{NH2}\)) | carboxylic acids (COOH is left alone) |
:::

Two reasons explain why \(\ce{NaBH4}\) is the gentler one. Its reducing power is lower, so it does not reduce carboxylic acids or amides, and it can be used in water or ethanol. \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) is a much stronger source of hydride ions and reacts violently with water, so it is used only in dry ether, and the mixture is treated with water or acid afterwards.

Use **\(\ce{NaBH4}\) when you want to reduce a C=O of an aldehyde or ketone and nothing else**. Use \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) for acids, amides and nitriles. Use \(\ce{H2}\)/Ni to reduce C=C (and the ring if you want a cyclohexane). In a molecule with two reducible groups, the choice of reagent decides which one reacts.

:::eg Give the reagents for each conversion, and a reason for your choice. [6 marks]
(a) \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CHO}\) to \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CH2OH}\) (b) \(\ce{CH2=CHCH2CHO}\) to \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2OH}\) (c) 4-nitrobenzoic acid to 4-aminobenzoic acid

(a) \(\ce{NaBH4}\) in water or ethanol. [B1] It reduces the aldehyde C=O to an alcohol but does not reduce C=C. [B1]

(b) Two reductions are needed: \(\ce{NaBH4}\) for the C=O as in (a), then \(\ce{H2}\) with Ni (or Pt) catalyst, heat, for the C=C. [B1] (Both groups can be reduced to give the saturated alcohol, and the C=C needs the hydrogen and the metal catalyst because \(\ce{NaBH4}\) leaves it alone.) [B1]

(c) Hot Sn with concentrated HCl, then NaOH(aq). [B1] \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) would also reduce the COOH group; Sn/HCl reduces the nitro group and leaves the COOH group alone. [B1]
:::

## Aromatic routes and the order of the steps

:::def Directing effect
The way a group already on a benzene ring sends a new substituent mainly to particular positions (the 2-, 4- and 6-positions, or the 3- and 5-positions), and makes the ring more or less reactive than benzene.
:::

Each group already on the ring sends the next one to a particular position, so the **order** of the steps is part of the answer. You met the directing effects in [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes).

:::key Directing effects: which group goes where
| Group already on the ring | New group goes to | Effect on the ring |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{-NH2}\), \(\ce{-OH}\), alkyl (\(\ce{-R}\), e.g. \(\ce{-CH3}\)) | the 2- and 4-positions (and 6) | more reactive (activating) |
| \(\ce{-NO2}\), \(\ce{-COOH}\), \(\ce{-COR}\) | the 3-position (and 5) | less reactive (deactivating) |
:::

The rule of thumb for a two-group target: find the relationship between the two groups (2-, 3- or 4-) and decide which group, put on first, would direct the second there. If the groups are at 4, the first group must be a 2,4-director. If they are at 3, the first group must be a 3-director.

:::eg Suggest syntheses of (a) 4-nitrobenzoic acid and (b) 3-nitrobenzoic acid from methylbenzene, and explain why the steps are in a different order. [7 marks]
(a) Step 1: concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), at 25–60 °C. [B1] The \(\ce{CH3}\) group directs to the 2- and 4-positions, so a mixture of 2- and 4-nitromethylbenzene forms, and the 4-isomer is separated. [B1]

Step 2: heat with alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then add dilute acid. [B1] This oxidises the \(\ce{CH3}\) group to COOH and gives 4-nitrobenzoic acid. [B1] The nitro group is not affected.

(b) Step 1: heat with alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then add dilute acid, giving benzoic acid. [B1]

Step 2: concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C. [B1] The COOH group directs the new group to the 3-position, so 3-nitrobenzoic acid forms. [B1]

(The order is reversed because \(\ce{CH3}\) is a 2,4-director and COOH is a 3-director. The group that is on the ring when the nitro group arrives controls its position.)
:::

Reducing a nitro group changes its directing effect. The \(\ce{NO2}\) group is a 3-director that makes the ring less reactive; the \(\ce{NH2}\) group it becomes is a 2,4-director that makes the ring far more reactive. So a ring with an \(\ce{NH2}\) group is very reactive: phenylamine reacts with \(\ce{Br2}\)(aq) at room temperature with no catalyst and replaces all three of the 2-, 4- and 6-hydrogens at once. If a synthesis needs a nitro group and then an amino group, check the order: reduce **after** any step that needs the nitro group to direct.

:::tip The ring or the side chain
Methylbenzene has two sites for chlorine or bromine. **Ultraviolet light, no catalyst:** free-radical substitution in the side chain, giving (chloromethyl)benzene. **\(\ce{AlCl3}\) (or \(\ce{AlBr3}\)), no UV light:** electrophilic substitution in the ring, to the 2- and 4-positions. The conditions decide, so always write them.
:::

:::eg Benzene is converted into a yellow azo dye in four steps. Suggest the reagents and conditions, and name the type of reaction, at each step. The dye is 4-hydroxyazobenzene, \(\ce{HOC6H4N=NC6H5}\). [8 marks]
\[\ce{C6H6 -> C6H5NO2 -> C6H5NH2 -> C6H5N2+Cl- -> HOC6H4N=NC6H5}\]

Step 1: concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C; electrophilic substitution (nitration), giving nitrobenzene. [B1] [B1]

Step 2: hot Sn with concentrated HCl, then NaOH(aq); reduction, giving phenylamine. [B1] [B1]

Step 3: \(\ce{NaNO2}\) with dilute HCl (nitrous acid), below 10 °C; diazotisation, giving benzenediazonium chloride. [B1] [B1]

Step 4: phenol in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C; coupling, giving the azo compound. [B1] [B1]

Why below 10 °C in steps 3 and 4: above that temperature the diazonium ion reacts with water and loses nitrogen, giving phenol instead of the dye. NaOH is needed in step 4 because it turns phenol into the phenoxide ion, whose ring is reactive enough to attack the weak diazonium electrophile.
:::

## Adding and removing carbon atoms

Most steps keep the number of carbon atoms. A few change it, and a question that asks you to make a longer molecule is asking for one of these.

:::key Changing the number of carbon atoms
| Step | Effect | Reagent and conditions |
|---|---|---|
| halogenoalkane → nitrile | +1 C | KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux |
| aldehyde or ketone → hydroxynitrile | +1 C | HCN with KCN catalyst, heat |
| arene → alkylarene | +1 C or more (the carbons of the alkyl group) | \(\ce{CH3Cl}\) (or another halogenoalkane) with \(\ce{AlCl3}\), heat |
| arene → aryl ketone | +2 C (for \(\ce{CH3COCl}\)) | \(\ce{CH3COCl}\) with \(\ce{AlCl3}\), heat |
| alkylbenzene → benzoic acid | the side chain is cut to one C (the COOH carbon) | hot alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then dilute acid |
| alkene with C=C in the chain → two shorter molecules | fewer C | hot, concentrated, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) |
:::

A nitrile is the most useful new carbon, because it can become three groups. Hydrolysis gives a carboxylic acid, and reduction gives a primary amine, both keeping the extra carbon:

\[\ce{CH3CH2Br ->[\text{KCN in ethanol}] CH3CH2CN}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CN + 4[H] ->[\ce{LiAlH4}] CH3CH2CH2NH2}\]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CN + 2H2O + H+ -> CH3CH2COOH + NH4+}\]

:::eg Suggest a route from bromoethane to propylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2NH2}\), and explain why the route is not a single step with ammonia. [5 marks]
The product has three carbons and bromoethane has two, so one step must add a carbon. [B1]

Step 1: bromoethane with KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux, gives propanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CN}\). [B1]

Step 2: reduce with \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether (or \(\ce{H2}\) with Ni catalyst) to give propylamine. [B1]

Ammonia would replace the Br and give ethylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2NH2}\), with two carbons only. [B1]

Ethylamine is itself a nucleophile, so it also reacts with bromoethane and gives secondary and tertiary amines as well, so the product is a mixture. [B1]
:::

## Making amines: the choice of route

You now have four ways to make an amine. Choosing the right one depends on what carbon skeleton you need and how pure the product must be.

| Route | Gives | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| halogenoalkane + excess \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure | primary amine | the same number of carbons as the halogenoalkane; a mixture, because the amine reacts further |
| nitrile + \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether (or \(\ce{H2}\)/Ni) | primary amine | one more carbon than the halogenoalkane that made the nitrile; cleaner |
| amide + \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether | primary, secondary (from a substituted amide) | the same number of carbons as the amide |
| nitrobenzene + hot Sn with concentrated HCl, then NaOH(aq) | phenylamine | the aromatic amine |
| halogenoalkane + a primary amine in ethanol, sealed tube | secondary amine | gives a mixture with tertiary amines too |

**Why mixtures form.** An amine has a lone pair on nitrogen, so it is a nucleophile. When ammonia reacts with a halogenoalkane, the primary amine formed is still a nucleophile and can react with another halogenoalkane, giving a secondary amine, then a tertiary amine, then a quaternary ammonium salt. Using a large excess of ammonia helps, because most halogenoalkane molecules then meet an ammonia molecule, but a pure secondary amine is better made another way.

**A clean route to a secondary amine** starts from an acyl chloride. It reacts with a primary amine at room temperature to give a substituted amide, and the amide is then reduced with \(\ce{LiAlH4}\). The amide step stops after one substitution, because an amide is not nucleophilic like an amine, so there is no mixture.

:::eg Suggest a route from ethanoic acid to the secondary amine \(\ce{CH3CH2NHCH3}\), giving reagents and conditions, and name the type of each step. [7 marks]
Step 1: ethanoic acid with \(\ce{SOCl2}\) (or \(\ce{PCl5}\), or \(\ce{PCl3}\) and heat) gives ethanoyl chloride. [B1] Type: substitution. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3COOH + SOCl2 -> CH3COCl + SO2 + HCl}\]
Step 2: ethanoyl chloride with methylamine, \(\ce{CH3NH2}\), at room temperature gives the amide \(\ce{CH3CONHCH3}\). [B1] Type: addition–elimination (condensation). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3COCl + CH3NH2 -> CH3CONHCH3 + HCl}\]
Step 3: \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether reduces the C=O of the amide to \(\ce{CH2}\) and gives \(\ce{CH3CH2NHCH3}\). [B1] Type: reduction. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + 4[H] -> CH3CH2NHCH3 + H2O}\]
The by-products of the three steps are \(\ce{SO2}\) and HCl, then HCl, then water. [B1]
:::

## Chirality in synthesis

Many organic molecules made in a synthesis have a chiral centre (see [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism)). Whether the product is one enantiomer or both depends on how the new bond forms.

:::def Racemic mixture
A mixture of equal amounts of the two enantiomers of a chiral compound. It is not optically active, because the rotations of plane-polarised light by the two enantiomers cancel.
:::

:::key Racemic products from planar groups
When a reagent attacks a **planar** part of a molecule, it can approach from either face with equal probability. If the product has a chiral centre, both enantiomers form in equal amounts: a **racemic mixture**, which does not rotate the plane of plane-polarised light. The planar groups are the trigonal-planar carbon of a C=O group (aldehyde or ketone) and the carbocation of an SN1 reaction.
:::

Examples:

- **HCN adds to the C=O of an aldehyde or ketone**, a planar carbon. The \(\ce{CN-}\) ion can attack from above or below the plane. Propanal gives a racemic mixture of the two enantiomers of 2-hydroxybutanenitrile.
- **\(\ce{NaBH4}\) reduces a ketone** such as butanone to butan-2-ol. The hydride ion attacks the planar C=O carbon from either face, giving a racemic mixture of butan-2-ol.
- **SN1 hydrolysis of a chiral tertiary halogenoalkane** goes through a planar carbocation and also gives a racemic alcohol (see [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen)).

The **drug industry** cares about this, because the two enantiomers of a chiral drug can have different biological effects: one may be active, the other less active, inactive or harmful. Three consequences follow.

- A racemic product from a normal synthesis must be **separated** into its enantiomers if only one is wanted, which is difficult and expensive, because the two have the same physical properties apart from their effect on polarised light.
- Making a 50:50 mixture and discarding half **wastes** material and effort.
- A **chiral catalyst** (including enzymes) can make a reaction produce mainly one enantiomer. This is how many single-isomer drugs are made today, and why chemists are interested in them.

Naproxen, a common painkiller, is sold as a single enantiomer for these reasons.

:::eg Propanal is converted into 2-hydroxybutanoic acid. Give the reagents and conditions, name the mechanism of the first step, and explain why the product is not optically active. [6 marks]
Step 1: propanal with HCN, with a little KCN as catalyst, and heat. [B1] This gives 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CN}\), by nucleophilic addition. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CHO + HCN -> CH3CH2CH(OH)CN}\]
Step 2: heat with dilute acid (hydrolysis of the nitrile) to give 2-hydroxybutanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)COOH}\). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CN + 2H2O + H+ -> CH3CH2CH(OH)COOH + NH4+}\]
The product has a chiral centre at carbon 2 (bonded to H, OH, \(\ce{CH2CH3}\) and COOH). [B1]

The carbon of the C=O in propanal is planar, so the \(\ce{CN-}\) ion attacks from either face with equal probability. [B1]

Both enantiomers form in equal amounts, giving a racemic mixture, which does not rotate plane-polarised light. [B1]
:::

## Predicting the reactions of a molecule with several groups

Real molecules carry several functional groups. Predict their behaviour by taking each group in turn, then check whether two groups compete for the same reagent.

:::steps
1. Name every functional group in the molecule (ring, phenol, amide, carboxylic acid, ester, amine, C=C, C=O, halogen, nitrile).
2. For the reagent in the question, ask which groups it reacts with, using the reaction tables above.
3. If more than one group reacts, give the product of each, unless the reagent and conditions make one react far faster.
4. For an acid-base reagent, rank the strengths: carboxylic acids react with \(\ce{Na2CO3}\) and with NaOH; phenols react with NaOH but not with \(\ce{Na2CO3}\); alcohols with neither; amines react with acids (not alkalis).
:::

:::eg (Real context) Paracetamol, \(\ce{HOC6H4NHCOCH3}\), has a phenol group and a secondary amide group opposite each other on a benzene ring. Predict what happens when it reacts with each reagent. [6 marks]
(a) NaOH(aq), room temperature. The OH of the phenol is weakly acidic and reacts to form the sodium salt, \(\ce{NaOC6H4NHCOCH3}\), and water; the amide is not hydrolysed at room temperature. [B1]
\[\ce{HOC6H4NHCOCH3 + NaOH -> NaOC6H4NHCOCH3 + H2O}\]
(b) \(\ce{Na2CO3}\)(aq). No reaction: phenol is too weak an acid to release \(\ce{CO2}\) from a carbonate. [B1]

(c) \(\ce{Br2}\)(aq), room temperature. The OH group activates the ring, so bromine substitutes in the ring (in the positions next to the OH group) with no catalyst and the orange colour fades. [B1]

(d) Dilute HCl, heated under reflux. The amide link is hydrolysed. [B1] The products are ethanoic acid and 4-hydroxyphenylammonium chloride (the amine is protonated by the acid). [B1]
\[\ce{HOC6H4NHCOCH3 + H2O + HCl -> HOC6H4NH3+Cl- + CH3COOH}\]
(e) \(\ce{CH3COCl}\), room temperature. The phenol OH reacts to give the ester \(\ce{CH3COOC6H4NHCOCH3}\) and HCl; the amide N–H does not react. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Real context) Paracetamol is made industrially from phenol. Suggest a three-step route using syllabus reagents, name the by-products you would expect, and say why the yield is not 100%. [6 marks]
Step 1: phenol with dilute \(\ce{HNO3}\) at room temperature gives 2-nitrophenol and 4-nitrophenol (electrophilic substitution). [B1] The 4-isomer is separated from the mixture. [B1]

Step 2: hot Sn with concentrated HCl, then NaOH(aq), reduces the nitro group, giving 4-aminophenol, \(\ce{HOC6H4NH2}\). [B1] [B1]

Step 3: ethanoyl chloride at room temperature acylates the \(\ce{NH2}\) group, which is the more reactive nucleophile, to give the amide, paracetamol, and HCl. [B1]
\[\ce{HOC6H4NH2 + CH3COCl -> HOC6H4NHCOCH3 + HCl}\]
The yield is below 100% because step 1 also makes 2-nitrophenol (the wrong isomer), because some product is lost in each separation, and because the OH group can also react with the ethanoyl chloride, giving some of the ester. [B1]

(In industry, ethanoic anhydride is used for step 3; it is not in the 9701 syllabus, so the acyl chloride is the reagent to write in an answer.)
:::

## Identifying an unknown compound

Unknown-compound questions combine **tests** (which groups are present) with **spectra** (the formula, the groups and the number of carbon environments). Each gives a piece of the answer.

### Test-tube tests for the A Level groups

The AS tests (2,4-DNPH, Tollens' and Fehling's reagents, alkaline iodine, \(\ce{Na2CO3}\), \(\ce{AgNO3}\) in ethanol, acidified dichromate and permanganate) are in [Organic synthesis (AS)](#chem.org.synthesis). These are the extras:

| Test | Positive result | Shows |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{Br2}\)(aq), room temperature | orange colour fades; white precipitate | phenol (2,4,6-tribromophenol) or phenylamine (2,4,6-tribromophenylamine) |
| \(\ce{Na2CO3}\)(aq) | bubbles of \(\ce{CO2}\) | carboxylic acid; no bubbles with a phenol |
| NaOH(aq), room temperature | the organic compound dissolves | a phenol or a carboxylic acid (not an alcohol) |
| add water | steamy fumes of HCl; then \(\ce{AgNO3}\)(aq) gives a white precipitate | acyl chloride |
| warm with NaOH(aq) | ammonia, turning damp red litmus paper blue | a primary amide (the ammonia comes from hydrolysis) |
| a diazonium salt, in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C | a coloured (yellow, orange or red) solution or precipitate | a phenol (azo coupling) |
| Fehling's or Tollens' reagent | positive | methanoic acid as well as aldehydes |
| warm acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) | purple to colourless, \(\ce{CO2}\) formed | ethanedioic acid |

### Spectra

:::key What each spectrum tells you
- **Mass spectrum:** the molecular ion peak gives \(M_r\). A peak at M+2 with one-third the height of M shows one Cl; a 1:1 pair shows one Br. The [M+1]⁺ peak gives the number of carbon atoms, \(n = \dfrac{100 \times a(\text{M+1})}{1.1 \times a(\text{M})}\), where \(a\) is the abundance (peak height).
- **Infrared:** C=O (carbonyl, carboxyl) 1670–1740, C=O of an amide 1640–1690, ester 1710–1750, O–H of a carboxylic acid 2500–3000, O–H of an alcohol or phenol 3200–3600, N–H (amine, amide) 3300–3500, C≡N 2200–2250 cm⁻¹. The ring (C=C aromatic) is at 1500–1680.
- **¹³C NMR:** the number of peaks is the number of different carbon environments. Their shifts (Data section) show the type: 160–185 carboxyl; 190–220 aldehyde or ketone; 110–160 ring or C=C; 100–125 nitrile; 0–50 alkyl. See [Chromatography and NMR spectroscopy](#chem.analysis2) and [Infrared spectroscopy and mass spectrometry](#chem.analysis).
:::

Symmetry matters for ¹³C NMR. A benzene ring with two identical or opposite groups in the 1,4-positions has two pairs of equivalent carbons, so it gives fewer peaks than the ring carbons it contains.

:::eg Compound A is a white solid with the molecular ion peak at m/e 121, and an [M+1]⁺ peak with 7.7% of the height of the M⁺ peak. Its IR spectrum has absorptions at 3350 and 1660 cm⁻¹. Warming A with NaOH(aq) gives a gas that turns damp red litmus paper blue and a solution that gives a white precipitate with dilute acid. A has 5 peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum. Identify A. [7 marks]
Number of carbon atoms: \(n = 100 \times 7.7 / (1.1 \times 100) = 7.0\), so A has 7 carbon atoms. [M1] [A1]

The absorption at 3350 cm⁻¹ is N–H and the absorption at 1660 cm⁻¹ is the C=O of an amide. [B1]

The gas is ammonia, so the amide is a primary amide, \(\ce{-CONH2}\). [B1]

The acidified solution gives a white precipitate of a carboxylic acid that is not soluble in water: benzoic acid, so the compound is benzamide, \(\ce{C6H5CONH2}\). [B1]

\(M_r(\ce{C7H7NO}) = 7(12.0) + 7(1.0) + 14.0 + 16.0 = 121.0\), which agrees with the molecular ion peak. [B1]

Benzamide has five carbon environments (the C=O carbon, the ring carbon bonded to it, two equivalent carbons next to it, two equivalent carbons in the 3- and 5-positions and the carbon opposite), which agrees with the 5 peaks. [B1]
:::

## Analysing a given route: types and by-products

In a Paper 4 question you are given a route, usually drawn with structures, and asked three things about each step: the **type** of reaction, the **reagent and conditions**, and sometimes a **by-product**. Work step by step: compare the structure before and after, name the group that changed, and use the reaction tables.

By-products have two origins. Some are the other half of the equation (\(\ce{HCl}\), \(\ce{H2O}\), \(\ce{SO2}\), \(\ce{NH4Br}\)). Others come from a side reaction in which the reagent attacks a different place or goes further than intended.

:::key Typical by-products
| Step | By-products and side reactions |
|---|---|
| nitration of an arene | the other isomers (e.g. 2- as well as 4-), and dinitro compounds; water |
| Friedel–Crafts alkylation | more than one alkyl group on the ring; HCl |
| halogenoalkane + \(\ce{NH3}\) | secondary and tertiary amines and quaternary ammonium salts; \(\ce{NH4X}\) |
| halogenoalkane + NaOH | an alkene by elimination (if the solvent is ethanol); NaX |
| oxidation of a primary alcohol | a carboxylic acid, if the aldehyde is not distilled out |
| esterification | water; an equilibrium, so unreacted acid and alcohol remain |
| acid + \(\ce{PCl5}\) / \(\ce{SOCl2}\) / \(\ce{PCl3}\) | \(\ce{POCl3}\) + HCl / \(\ce{SO2}\) + HCl / \(\ce{H3PO3}\) |
| acyl chloride + nucleophile | HCl (or \(\ce{NH4Cl}\) with excess \(\ce{NH3}\)) |
| diazotisation above 10 °C | phenol and nitrogen |
| reduction with Sn and HCl | tin salts; the amine is present as its salt until NaOH is added |
:::

:::eg Analyse this route to phenyl benzoate. For each step give the type of reaction and the reagent and conditions, and name one by-product. [8 marks]
\[\ce{C6H6 -> C6H5CH3 -> C6H5COOH -> C6H5COCl -> C6H5COOC6H5}\]

Step 1: \(\ce{CH3Cl}\) with \(\ce{AlCl3}\), heat; Friedel–Crafts alkylation (electrophilic substitution). [B1] By-product: HCl, or a ring with more than one methyl group. [B1]

Step 2: hot alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\), then dilute acid; oxidation of the side chain. [B1]

Step 3: \(\ce{SOCl2}\) (or \(\ce{PCl5}\), or \(\ce{PCl3}\) and heat); substitution of OH by Cl. [B1] By-products: \(\ce{SO2}\) and HCl (with \(\ce{SOCl2}\)). [B1]

Step 4: phenol at room temperature; addition–elimination. [B1] By-product: HCl. [B1]

Benzoyl chloride is used in step 4 because phenols do not form esters readily with carboxylic acids themselves, but they react with acyl chlorides at room temperature. [B1]
:::

:::eg (Exam style) 10.0 g of benzene is nitrated in a yield of 85.0%, and the nitrobenzene is reduced to phenylamine in a yield of 70.0%. Calculate the mass of phenylamine obtained. [5 marks]
\(M_r(\ce{C6H6}) = 6(12.0) + 6(1.0) = 78.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C6H5NH2}) = 6(12.0) + 7(1.0) + 14.0 = 93.0\). [B1]

One mole of benzene gives one mole of nitrobenzene, and that gives one mole of phenylamine. \(n(\ce{C6H6}) = 10.0 / 78.0 = 0.1282\) mol. [M1]

Overall yield \(= 0.850 \times 0.700 = 0.595\). [M1]

Moles of phenylamine \(= 0.1282 \times 0.595 = 0.07628\) mol. [M1]

\[m = 0.07628 \times 93.0 = 7.09\,\mathrm{g}\] [A1]
:::

A: (a) Both. (b) \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) only. (c) \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) only.
Q: Name the type of reaction: (a) benzene + \(\ce{CH3COCl}\) with \(\ce{AlCl3}\); (b) propanoyl chloride + \(\ce{NH3}\); (c) propanenitrile → propylamine; (d) phenylamine → benzenediazonium chloride.
A: (a) electrophilic substitution (Friedel–Crafts acylation); (b) addition–elimination; (c) reduction; (d) diazotisation.
Q: Give the product of heating methylbenzene with hot alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\) and then adding dilute acid. Is the carbon count of the molecule changed?
A: Benzoic acid, \(\ce{C6H5COOH}\). The count is unchanged (seven carbons): the \(\ce{CH3}\) carbon has become the COOH carbon. A longer side chain, such as \(\ce{CH2CH3}\), would be cut down to the single COOH carbon.
Q: How many carbon atoms does the product of bromopropane reacting with KCN in ethanol contain?
A: Four. The three carbons of the bromopropane plus the carbon of the \(\ce{CN}\) group.
Q: Describe a test-tube test that distinguishes ethanoic acid from phenol.
A: Add \(\ce{Na2CO3}\)(aq): ethanoic acid gives bubbles of \(\ce{CO2}\); phenol does not, because it is too weak an acid to react with a carbonate.
Q: Describe a test that distinguishes phenol from cyclohexanol.
A: Add \(\ce{Br2}\)(aq): phenol decolourises it and gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol; cyclohexanol does not react.
Q: How many peaks would you expect in the ¹³C NMR spectrum of (a) 4-nitrobenzoic acid and (b) 3-nitrobenzoic acid?
A: (a) 5: the carbon of COOH, the carbon bearing COOH, the two carbons next to it (equivalent), the two next to \(\ce{NO2}\) (equivalent), and the carbon bearing \(\ce{NO2}\). (b) 7: with groups in the 1,3-positions no ring carbons are equivalent.
Q: Explain why phenylamine reacts with \(\ce{Br2}\)(aq) at room temperature but benzene does not.
A: The lone pair on the nitrogen of \(\ce{NH2}\) is delocalised into the ring, which raises the electron density of the ring, so the ring is attacked by a weak electrophile (\(\ce{Br2}\)) without a catalyst. Benzene's ring is less electron-rich and needs \(\ce{Br2}\) with \(\ce{AlBr3}\).
Q: A two-step route has yields of 80.0% and 60.0%. What is the overall yield?
A: \(0.800 \times 0.600 = 0.480\), so 48.0%.

> Phenol's activated ring reacts with bromine water at room temperature, giving a white precipitate. Both phenol and ethanol give hydrogen with sodium, neither reacts with a carbonate, and neither has a C=O group for 2,4-DNPH.

:::recap
- A route is planned by comparing carbon skeletons and groups, working backwards, ordering steps and writing reagent plus conditions for each; each step must leave the other groups alone.
- A Level reaction tables: arenes (halogenation, nitration, Friedel–Crafts, side-chain oxidation, reduction of nitro groups), phenol and phenylamine (diazotisation below 10 °C, coupling in NaOH(aq), bromination), acyl chlorides (made by \(\ce{PCl3}\), \(\ce{PCl5}\), \(\ce{SOCl2}\); room-temperature reactions with \(\ce{H2O}\), alcohols, phenols, \(\ce{NH3}\) and amines), amines, amides and nitriles.
- Reducing agents: \(\ce{NaBH4}\) (aldehydes and ketones only); \(\ce{LiAlH4}\), dry ether (also acids, amides, nitriles); \(\ce{H2}\)/Ni (C=C, ring, nitriles); Sn and concentrated HCl then NaOH (nitro to amino).
- Directing effects: \(\ce{NH2}\), \(\ce{OH}\), alkyl to 2,4,6 (activating); \(\ce{NO2}\), COOH, COR to 3,5 (deactivating). The group on the ring when a new group arrives controls its position, so the order of steps matters.
- Carbon chain grows with KCN (halogenoalkane), HCN (aldehyde or ketone) and Friedel–Crafts; hot alkaline \(\ce{KMnO4}\) shortens an alkyl side chain to COOH.
- Amines: halogenoalkane + \(\ce{NH3}\) (mixture), nitrile reduction (one more carbon), amide reduction; a secondary amine cleanly from acyl chloride + primary amine then \(\ce{LiAlH4}\).
- Racemic mixtures come from attack on planar C=O or carbocations; chiral catalysts make single enantiomers for drugs.
- Unknowns: tests (\(\ce{Br2}\)(aq), \(\ce{Na2CO3}\), NaOH, \(\ce{AgNO3}\), litmus with NaOH) plus MS (M⁺, M+2, [M+1]⁺), IR and ¹³C peak count.
- Analysing a route: name the type, give reagent and conditions, and say what else forms (HCl, \(\ce{SO2}\), isomers, polysubstitution).
:::
