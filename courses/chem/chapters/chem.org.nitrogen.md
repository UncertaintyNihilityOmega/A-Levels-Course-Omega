## Amines: structure, types and names

An **amine** is what you get when one or more hydrogen atoms of ammonia, \(\ce{NH3}\), are replaced by carbon groups. A lone pair of electrons stays on the nitrogen atom, and almost every property of amines comes from it.

:::def Amine
A compound derived from ammonia in which one, two or three hydrogen atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups. A primary amine has one group on the nitrogen (\(\ce{RNH2}\)), a secondary amine two (\(\ce{R2NH}\)) and a tertiary amine three (\(\ce{R3N}\)).
:::

:::warn Not the same as primary, secondary and tertiary halogenoalkanes
For a halogenoalkane you count the carbon groups on the **carbon** that holds the halogen. For an amine you count the carbon groups on the **nitrogen** atom. Butan-2-amine, \(\ce{CH3CH(NH2)CH2CH3}\), has its N on a secondary carbon but is still a **primary amine**, because the nitrogen carries only one carbon group.
:::

Primary amines are named by writing the alkyl group before "amine": \(\ce{CH3CH2NH2}\) is ethylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2NH2}\) is propylamine, and \(\ce{C6H5NH2}\) is **phenylamine** (a benzene ring with an \(\ce{NH2}\) group). You only need to name primary amines. The shortest ones are gases or volatile liquids with a fishy smell, and they dissolve well in water because the N–H bonds and the lone pair on nitrogen form hydrogen bonds with water molecules.

## Making amines

There are four routes to the amines on the syllabus. Two use halogenoalkanes as the starting material, and two are reductions.

:::key Preparation of amines
| Route | Reagent and conditions | Product |
|---|---|---|
| halogenoalkane + ammonia | excess \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure (sealed tube) | primary amine |
| halogenoalkane + primary amine | primary amine in ethanol, heated under pressure (sealed tube) | secondary amine |
| nitrile + hydrogen | \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether, or \(\ce{H2}\) with a nickel catalyst, heated | primary amine |
| amide + hydride | \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether | primary or secondary amine |
:::

**From halogenoalkanes.** The nitrogen lone pair acts as a nucleophile, in the same way as the lone pair in \(\ce{OH-}\) or \(\ce{CN-}\). A second ammonia molecule takes up the hydrogen bromide:

\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br}\]

The product is itself a nucleophile, and a better one than ammonia, so it can attack another bromoethane molecule. That is why ammonia is used in large excess. With a primary amine as the nucleophile you make a secondary amine on purpose:

\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + 2CH3NH2 -> CH3CH2NHCH3 + CH3NH3+Br-}\]

**From nitriles.** A nitrile is reduced by adding four hydrogen atoms across the C≡N bond:

\[\ce{CH3CH2CN + 4[H] -> CH3CH2CH2NH2}\]

The reagent can be lithium tetrahydridoaluminate, \(\ce{LiAlH4}\), in dry ether, or hydrogen gas passed over a nickel catalyst with heating. Since the nitrile is made from a halogenoalkane with KCN, this route adds one carbon atom to the chain.

**From amides.** \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether reduces an amide by replacing the C=O oxygen with two hydrogen atoms. A primary amide gives a primary amine; an N-substituted amide gives a secondary amine:

\[\ce{CH3CONH2 + 4[H] -> CH3CH2NH2 + H2O}\]
\[\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + 4[H] -> CH3CH2NHCH3 + H2O}\]

:::eg Suggest how to make propylamine, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2NH2}\), from bromoethane in two steps.
The amine has three carbons and bromoethane has two, so a carbon must be added: use the cyanide reaction first.

Step 1: heat bromoethane with KCN in ethanol under reflux to give propanenitrile. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2Br + KCN -> CH3CH2CN + KBr}\]
Step 2: reduce the nitrile with \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether (or \(\ce{H2}\) with a nickel catalyst, heated) to give propylamine. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CN + 4[H] -> CH3CH2CH2NH2}\]
Going directly with ammonia would give ethylamine, which has only two carbons.
:::

## Amines as bases

Ammonia accepts a proton using its nitrogen lone pair (see [Acids and bases](#chem.acids)). Amines do the same, and they are the main organic bases.

:::def Basicity of amines
An amine acts as a Brønsted–Lowry base: the lone pair on the nitrogen atom accepts a proton (\(\ce{H+}\)). In water, the equilibrium \(\ce{RNH2 + H2O <=> RNH3+ + OH-}\) lies to the left because the amine is a weak base, but it makes the solution alkaline.
:::

\[\ce{CH3CH2NH2 + H2O <=> CH3CH2NH3+ + OH-}\]

An amine reacts completely with a strong acid to give a salt, in which the nitrogen carries \(\ce{+}\) charge:

\[\ce{CH3CH2NH2 + HCl -> CH3CH2NH3+Cl-}\]

The salt, ethylammonium chloride, is ionic, so it is a white solid that dissolves in water. Adding NaOH(aq) takes the proton back and releases the amine, which is how an amine is separated from a mixture and how phenylamine is freed from its salt later in the chapter:

\[\ce{CH3CH2NH3+ + OH- -> CH3CH2NH2 + H2O}\]

### Ranking the bases

How strong a base is depends on how available the lone pair is to accept a proton. Three structures compare.

:::

- **Ethylamine.** The ethyl group is an electron donor (the inductive effect you used for carbocations in [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen)). It pushes electron density towards the nitrogen, so the lone pair is more available than in ammonia, and the \(\ce{CH3CH2NH3+}\) ion that forms is stabilised by the same donation. Ethylamine is a **stronger base than ammonia**.
- **Ammonia.** The reference point: three hydrogen atoms, no donation and no withdrawal.
- **Phenylamine.** The nitrogen lone pair overlaps with the π system of the benzene ring and is drawn into it, so it is spread over the ring and not held on nitrogen alone. It is less available for a proton, and phenylamine is a **weaker base than ammonia**.

:::key Relative basicity
ethylamine > ammonia > phenylamine. An alkyl group pushes electron density onto N (the lone pair is more available); the benzene ring draws the lone pair into the ring by delocalisation (the lone pair is less available).
:::

:::eg Place ammonia, ethylamine and phenylamine in order of increasing basicity, with a reason for each position. [3 marks]
Phenylamine is the weakest base: the lone pair on N is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available to accept \(\ce{H+}\). [B1]

Ammonia is in the middle: it has neither an electron-donating group nor a ring. [B1]

Ethylamine is the strongest: the electron-donating ethyl group increases the electron density on N, so the lone pair is more available. [B1]

Increasing basicity: phenylamine < ammonia < ethylamine.
:::

:::warn "Phenylamine is a stronger base than ammonia" is a very common error
Phenylamine has an **extra** group on nitrogen, which looks as if it should help. It does the reverse, because the group is a benzene ring that takes the lone pair into the ring. Alkyl groups donate electrons and raise basicity; aryl groups withdraw by delocalisation and lower it.
:::

## Phenylamine

Phenylamine, \(\ce{C6H5NH2}\), is the most useful aromatic amine: it is the starting point for dyes. It is made from benzene in two stages and then released from its salt.

:::steps
1. **Nitrate benzene.** Benzene is warmed with concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulfuric acid (25–60 °C) to give nitrobenzene, \(\ce{C6H5NO2}\), as in [Arenes: benzene and its compounds](#chem.org.arenes).
2. **Reduce the nitro group.** Nitrobenzene is heated under reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid. The tin is the reducing agent. The nitro group becomes \(\ce{-NH2}\), and because the solution is acidic the amine is present as its salt, the phenylammonium ion \(\ce{C6H5NH3+}\).
3. **Release the amine.** Excess NaOH(aq) is added. It removes \(\ce{H+}\) from \(\ce{C6H5NH3+}\) and frees phenylamine, which separates as an oily layer.
:::

\[\ce{C6H5NO2 + 6[H] + H+ -> C6H5NH3+ + 2H2O}\]
\[\ce{C6H5NH3+ + OH- -> C6H5NH2 + H2O}\]

:::lab Making phenylamine
Warm 2 cm³ of nitrobenzene with granulated tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid in a flask under a reflux condenser (see the apparatus in [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen)); the mixture is heated gently at first because the reaction is exothermic. After about 30 minutes cool it, then add NaOH(aq) until strongly alkaline. The phenylamine is then separated by steam distillation or extraction with ethoxyethane. Nitrobenzene and phenylamine are toxic and are absorbed through the skin: wear gloves and eye protection, work in a fume cupboard and keep the concentrated acid away from skin.
:::

Phenylamine is a weak base. It is slightly soluble in water, but it reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid to give the soluble salt \(\ce{C6H5NH3+Cl-}\), and the amine is released again when alkali is added.

### Bromination at room temperature

Benzene needs a halogen carrier (\(\ce{AlBr3}\)) to react with bromine, and does not react with bromine water at all. Phenylamine reacts with **bromine water** at room temperature, with no catalyst, and the \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) is decolourised as a **white precipitate** of 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine forms:

\[\ce{C6H5NH2 + 3Br2 -> C6H2Br3NH2 + 3HBr}\]

The lone pair on the nitrogen is partly delocalised into the ring (the same delocalisation that weakens phenylamine as a base), so the ring is far more electron-rich than benzene's. A more electron-rich ring attacks the weak electrophile more readily. The \(\ce{NH2}\) group directs substitution to the 2-, 4- and 6-positions, and the ring is so reactive that all three positions are substituted in one go. The same thing happens with phenol (see [Phenol and the acidity of organic compounds](#chem.org.phenol)).

:::eg (Exam style) 1.86 g of phenylamine is treated with excess bromine water. 5.43 g of white solid 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine is collected. Calculate the percentage yield.
\(M_r(\ce{C6H5NH2}) = 6(12.0) + 7(1.0) + 14.0 = 93.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C6H2Br3NH2}) = 6(12.0) + 4(1.0) + 3(79.9) + 14.0 = 329.7\). [B1]
\[n(\ce{C6H5NH2}) = \frac{1.86}{93.0} = 0.0200\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
One mole of phenylamine gives one mole of the tribromo product:
\[\text{maximum mass} = 0.0200 \times 329.7 = 6.594\,\mathrm{g}\] [M1]
\[\text{percentage yield} = \frac{5.43}{6.594} \times 100 = 82.3\,\%\] [A1]
:::

## Diazonium salts and azo dyes

Phenylamine undergoes a reaction that no aliphatic amine does so usefully: it reacts with **nitrous acid**, \(\ce{HNO2}\), to give a diazonium salt. Nitrous acid is unstable, so it is made in the flask by adding NaNO2 to dilute hydrochloric acid.

\[\ce{NaNO2 + HCl -> HNO2 + NaCl}\]

### Diazotisation

At **below 10 °C** (an ice bath), phenylamine reacts with the nitrous acid and the excess hydrochloric acid to give the **benzenediazonium** salt:

\[\ce{C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl -> C6H5N2+Cl- + 2H2O}\]

The cation \(\ce{C6H5N2+}\) has a nitrogen–nitrogen triple bond attached to the ring, and can be written \(\ce{C6H5-N#N+}\). It is stable only when it is cold. If the solution is allowed to warm above about 10 °C, the \(\ce{N2}\) leaves as nitrogen gas and the ring picks up an OH group from the water. That is how phenol is made in [Phenol and the acidity of organic compounds](#chem.org.phenol):

\[\ce{C6H5N2+ + H2O -> C6H5OH + N2 + H+}\]

So the temperature decides between two useful outcomes. Keep it **below 10 °C** and the diazonium salt survives for the next reaction. **Warm** it and you get phenol and bubbles of nitrogen.

### Coupling

A diazonium ion is a weak electrophile, and it attacks a very electron-rich ring. In **coupling**, a cold solution of benzenediazonium chloride is added to phenol dissolved in NaOH(aq), also below 10 °C. In alkali, phenol is the phenoxide ion, whose O⁻ group donates strongly into the ring, and the diazonium ion attacks the 4-position, opposite the OH group. An orange-yellow precipitate of an **azo compound** forms:

\[\ce{C6H5N2+Cl- + C6H5OH + NaOH -> HOC6H4N=NC6H5 + NaCl + H2O}\]

:::def Azo compound
A compound containing the azo group, \(\ce{-N=N-}\), joining two aromatic rings (\(\ce{Ar-N=N-Ar'}\)). The N=N group links the π systems of the two rings into one large delocalised system, so azo compounds absorb visible light and are coloured.
:::

The product here is 4-hydroxyazobenzene, a yellow or orange dye. Azo compounds are made in the same way from other coupling partners. 2-Naphthol, for example, couples with benzenediazonium chloride in NaOH(aq) to give an intense orange-red dye. Changing the amine you start from, or the coupling partner, changes the groups on the two rings and so the colour. Azo dyes colour much of the world's fabric, and the indicator methyl orange and many food colourings belong to the same family.

:::warn Coupling needs alkali, and diazotisation needs cold
Two conditions are examined again and again: diazotisation (the amine + NaNO₂ and HCl) must be done **below 10 °C**, and coupling with phenol is done in **NaOH(aq)** (never in acid), so that phenol is present as the more reactive phenoxide ion. If the diazonium salt warms up, you make phenol, not the dye.
:::

:::eg Describe, with reagents and conditions, how benzene is converted into an azo dye by coupling with phenol. [6 marks]
Step 1: benzene to nitrobenzene with concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) and concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), at 25–60 °C. [B1]

Step 2: nitrobenzene to phenylamine by heating under reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid, then adding NaOH(aq). [B1] [B1]

Step 3: phenylamine to benzenediazonium chloride with \(\ce{NaNO2}\) and dilute \(\ce{HCl}\) below 10 °C. [B1]

Step 4: the diazonium salt is added to phenol dissolved in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C. [B1]

The product is 4-hydroxyazobenzene, \(\ce{HOC6H4N=NC6H5}\), an azo compound with the \(\ce{-N=N-}\) group. [B1]
:::

## Amides

An **amide** has the group \(\ce{-CONH2}\) (primary), \(\ce{-CONHR}\) (secondary) or \(\ce{-CONR2}\) (tertiary): a carbonyl group bonded directly to a nitrogen atom. The name is the acid's name with "-oic acid" changed to "-amide": ethanoic acid gives ethanamide, \(\ce{CH3CONH2}\), and propanoic acid gives propanamide, \(\ce{CH3CH2CONH2}\). A group on the nitrogen is shown by N, as in N-methylethanamide.

### Making amides

The best way to an amide is the acyl chloride reaction in [Acyl chlorides and A Level carboxylic acids](#chem.org.acyl):

\[\ce{CH3COCl + 2NH3 -> CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl}\]
\[\ce{CH3COCl + 2CH3NH2 -> CH3CONHCH3 + CH3NH3+Cl-}\]

Both happen at room temperature. Joining two molecules and losing a small molecule (here \(\ce{HCl}\)) is a **condensation** reaction. The product is a **neutral** compound, and the second amine or ammonia molecule only takes the hydrogen chloride.

### Hydrolysis of amides

Amides are hydrolysed by heating under reflux with dilute acid or dilute alkali.

:::key Hydrolysis of amides
| Conditions | Products from \(\ce{CH3CONH2}\) | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| dilute acid (e.g. \(\ce{HCl}\)), heat | ethanoic acid + ammonium ion | \(\ce{CH3CONH2 + H+ + H2O -> CH3COOH + NH4+}\) |
| NaOH(aq), heat | ethanoate ion + ammonia | \(\ce{CH3CONH2 + OH- -> CH3COO- + NH3}\) |

An N-substituted amide gives an amine instead of ammonia: \(\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + OH- -> CH3COO- + CH3NH2}\) in alkali, and \(\ce{CH3NH3+}\) in acid.
:::

The pattern matches the hydrolysis of nitriles in [Carboxylic acids, esters and nitriles](#chem.org.acids): in acid the nitrogen ends up as a positive ion, and in alkali as the neutral gas (ammonia or an amine), because an amine or ammonia cannot exist in acid and a carboxylic acid cannot exist in alkali. Ammonia from an amide in alkali is detected by its smell and by turning damp red litmus paper blue.

### Reduction and weak basicity

Amides are reduced by \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether to amines (see the equations above), and they are **much weaker bases than amines**. They do not turn litmus blue and do not react with dilute acids at room temperature. The reason is in the figure on lone-pair availability: the lone pair on the nitrogen of an amide overlaps with the C=O group and is drawn towards the electronegative oxygen, so it is spread over N, C and O. It is no longer free to accept a proton.

:::eg Give the organic products when N-methylethanamide, \(\ce{CH3CONHCH3}\), is heated under reflux with (a) dilute hydrochloric acid and (b) NaOH(aq). [4 marks]
(a) Ethanoic acid [B1] and the methylammonium ion, \(\ce{CH3NH3+}\) (as methylammonium chloride). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + H+ + H2O -> CH3COOH + CH3NH3+}\]
(b) The ethanoate ion, \(\ce{CH3COO-}\) [B1], and methylamine, \(\ce{CH3NH2}\). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CONHCH3 + OH- -> CH3COO- + CH3NH2}\]
:::

:::eg (Real context) 2.95 g of ethanamide is heated with excess NaOH(aq) and all the ammonia produced is collected as a gas at room temperature. Calculate its volume.
\(M_r(\ce{CH3CONH2}) = 2(12.0) + 5(1.0) + 14.0 + 16.0 = 59.0\). [B1]
\[n(\ce{CH3CONH2}) = \frac{2.95}{59.0} = 0.0500\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
The equation \(\ce{CH3CONH2 + OH- -> CH3COO- + NH3}\) is 1 : 1, so \(n(\ce{NH3}) = 0.0500\,\mathrm{mol}\). [M1]
\[V = 0.0500 \times 24.0 = 1.20\,\mathrm{dm^3}\] [A1]
(The molar volume 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹ is the value at room temperature and pressure.)
:::

## Amino acids

An **amino acid** has an amine group and a carboxylic acid group. The amino acids in proteins are 2-amino acids: the \(\ce{NH2}\) group is on the carbon next to the \(\ce{COOH}\) group (the 2-position, or α-carbon). The general formula is \(\ce{H2N-CHR-COOH}\), where R is a side chain that differs from one amino acid to the next.

| Amino acid | Short name | R group | Isoelectric point (about) |
|---|---|---|---|
| glycine (2-aminoethanoic acid) | Gly | \(\ce{H}\) | 5.97 |
| alanine (2-aminopropanoic acid) | Ala | \(\ce{CH3}\) | 6.00 |
| lysine (2,6-diaminohexanoic acid) | Lys | \(\ce{(CH2)4NH2}\) | 9.74 |
| aspartic acid (2-aminobutanedioic acid) | Asp | \(\ce{CH2COOH}\) | 2.77 |
| glutamic acid (2-aminopentanedioic acid) | Glu | \(\ce{CH2CH2COOH}\) | 3.22 |

All of these except glycine have a chiral carbon, since the α-carbon carries four different groups (H, \(\ce{NH2}\), \(\ce{COOH}\) and R). Glycine has two H atoms on that carbon, so it is not chiral (see [Isomerism](#chem.org.isomerism)). The two amine and acid groups mean that an amino acid has both acidic and basic properties.

### Zwitterions

In the solid and in solution near neutral pH, the proton of the carboxylic acid group moves to the nitrogen lone pair of the amine group, inside the same molecule. The result has a positive and a negative charge, with no overall charge:

\[\ce{H2N-CH2-COOH <=> H3N^+-CH2-COO^-}\]

:::def Zwitterion
A species that carries both a positive and a negative charge in the same molecule, so that its overall charge is zero. In an amino acid it is \(\ce{H3N^+-CHR-COO^-}\).
:::

The charges explain the properties of amino acids: they are ionic solids with high melting points that dissolve in water, much unlike the amines or carboxylic acids they come from.

### Behaviour in acid and alkali

The zwitterion can react with both acids and bases:

- In **acid**, the \(\ce{COO-}\) group accepts a proton and the amino acid becomes a positive ion (cation).
- In **alkali**, the \(\ce{NH3+}\) group loses a proton and the amino acid becomes a negative ion (anion).

:::html

\[\ce{H3N^+-CH2-COO^- + H+ -> H3N^+-CH2-COOH}\]
\[\ce{H3N^+-CH2-COO^- + OH- -> H2N-CH2-COO^- + H2O}\]

:::def Isoelectric point
The pH at which an amino acid has no overall charge, because the positive and negative charges on the molecule cancel. At this pH the zwitterion is the main species and the amino acid does not move in an electric field.
:::

Below its isoelectric point (more acidic) an amino acid has an overall **positive** charge; above it (more alkaline) an overall **negative** charge. For glycine, the isoelectric point is about 5.97. At pH 1 glycine is the cation \(\ce{H3N^+-CH2-COOH}\), at pH 6 it is almost entirely the zwitterion, and at pH 12 it is the anion \(\ce{H2N-CH2-COO^-}\).

Amino acids with extra acidic or basic groups in R behave in the same way, but each extra group changes the charge and moves the isoelectric point. **Lysine** has a second amine group, so it is a basic amino acid and its isoelectric point is high (9.74). **Aspartic acid** and **glutamic acid** have a second carboxylic acid group, so they are acidic amino acids with low isoelectric points. To find the charge of any amino acid at a given pH:

- at very low pH every amine group is protonated (\(\ce{-NH3+}\)) and every carboxylic acid is intact (\(\ce{-COOH}\));
- at very high pH every amine group is \(\ce{-NH2}\) and every carboxylic acid group is \(\ce{-COO-}\);
- in between, compare the pH with the isoelectric point: below it is positive, above it is negative.

:::eg Give the structure and overall charge of (a) lysine at pH 1 and (b) aspartic acid at pH 12, where \(\ce{R}\) is shown in full. [4 marks]
(a) At pH 1 all amine groups are protonated and the \(\ce{COOH}\) is intact: \(\ce{H3N^+-CH((CH2)4NH3^+)-COOH}\). [B1] There are two \(\ce{NH3+}\) groups and no negative charge, so the charge is +2. [B1]

(b) At pH 12 all amine groups are \(\ce{NH2}\) and both acid groups are \(\ce{COO-}\): \(\ce{H2N-CH(CH2COO^-)-COO^-}\). [B1] The charge is −2. [B1]
:::

:::eg Draw glycine at pH 1, at its isoelectric point and at pH 12, and state the overall charge of each. [3 marks]
pH 1: \(\ce{H3N^+-CH2-COOH}\), charge +1. [B1]

At the isoelectric point (pH about 6): the zwitterion \(\ce{H3N^+-CH2-COO^-}\), charge 0. [B1]

pH 12: \(\ce{H2N-CH2-COO^-}\), charge −1. [B1]
:::

## Peptides

Two amino acids join when the \(\ce{COOH}\) of one reacts with the \(\ce{NH2}\) of the other and a molecule of water is lost. The new bond is an amide link, called a **peptide bond** in this context.

\[\ce{H2N-CH2-COOH + H2N-CH(CH3)-COOH -> H2N-CH2-CO-NH-CH(CH3)-COOH + H2O}\]

:::def Peptide bond
The amide link, \(\ce{-CO-NH-}\), formed between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another in a condensation reaction in which water is lost.
:::

Two amino acids make a **dipeptide**, three make a **tripeptide**, and many make a polypeptide or protein. Every peptide has one free \(\ce{NH2}\) end and one free \(\ce{COOH}\) end. The order matters, and by convention the free \(\ce{NH2}\) end is written first, so Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly are different compounds.

:::eg Glycine and alanine can combine to make dipeptides. Draw or describe the dipeptides that can form and state how many different dipeptides can form from the two amino acids together if either can be used twice. [4 marks]
Gly-Ala: the \(\ce{COOH}\) of glycine joins the \(\ce{NH2}\) of alanine: [B1]
\[\ce{H2N-CH2-CO-NH-CH(CH3)-COOH}\]

Ala-Gly: the \(\ce{COOH}\) of alanine joins the \(\ce{NH2}\) of glycine: [B1]
\[\ce{H2N-CH(CH3)-CO-NH-CH2-COOH}\]

The two are isomers: the same atoms, joined in a different order. [B1]

With the amino acids allowed to repeat, Gly-Gly and Ala-Ala also form, so there are 4 dipeptides (\(2 \times 2\)). [B1]
:::

The \(M_r\) of a peptide is the sum of the amino acid \(M_r\) values minus 18.0 for each water lost. For glycylalanine, \(M_r = 75.0 + 89.0 - 18.0 = 146.0\).

Peptides are hydrolysed back to amino acids by the same conditions as amides: heating with dilute acid or with NaOH(aq). In acid, the amino acids are formed as their positive ions; in alkali, as negative ions. Digestion carries out the same reaction at body temperature with enzymes.

\[\ce{H2N-CH2-CO-NH-CH(CH3)-COOH + H2O -> H2N-CH2-COOH + H2N-CH(CH3)-COOH}\]

## Electrophoresis

You can separate amino acids and short peptides because their charge depends on pH. **Electrophoresis** uses this. A drop of the mixture is placed on the middle of a strip of paper or gel soaked in a buffer solution of known pH, and a voltage is applied across the ends. Charged species move; neutral species do not.

:::key Predicting electrophoresis
- **Direction:** positive ions (cations) move towards the **negative** electrode (cathode); negative ions (anions) move towards the **positive** electrode (anode).
- **Charge:** the amino acid's charge depends on pH. Below the isoelectric point it is positive, above it negative; at the isoelectric point it has no net charge and does not move.
- **Speed:** the greater the charge and the smaller the species, the faster it moves.
:::

Dipeptides follow the same rules. A dipeptide has one free \(\ce{NH2}\) end and one free \(\ce{COOH}\) end, plus any acidic or basic groups in the side chains. At a pH well below its isoelectric point every amine group is protonated and the peptide moves to the cathode; well above it, to the anode.

:::eg Predict the movement of alanine (pI 6.00), lysine (pI 9.74) and glutamic acid (pI 3.22) on an electrophoresis strip at pH 6.00. [4 marks]
Alanine: the pH equals its isoelectric point, so it is the zwitterion with no net charge and does not move. [B1]

Lysine: the pH is below its isoelectric point (9.74), so it is positively charged and moves towards the negative electrode (cathode). [B1]

Glutamic acid: the pH is above its isoelectric point (3.22), so it is negatively charged and moves towards the positive electrode (anode). [B1]

At pH 6.00 lysine carries a charge of +1 (two \(\ce{NH3+}\) groups and one \(\ce{COO-}\)) and glutamic acid a charge of −1 (one \(\ce{NH3+}\) group and two \(\ce{COO-}\)), so the two spots move away from the start line at similar speeds in opposite directions. [B1]
:::

:::story A single amino acid changed
Haemoglobin from healthy adults and haemoglobin from people with sickle-cell disease differ by one amino acid in a chain of 146: a glutamic acid is replaced by a valine, which has no acidic group. That removes one negative charge from the protein, so the two forms have different net charges at the same pH and move at different speeds in electrophoresis. Doctors have used this to tell the forms apart. You do not need to know this for the exam: it shows that the rules above work on whole proteins as well as on single amino acids.
:::

:::exam Electrophoresis answers
Say three things: the pH of the buffer relative to the isoelectric point, the sign of the charge (with the species), and the direction of movement to the cathode or anode. Never write that something "moves towards the positive charge" without naming the electrode. Apparatus assembly is not tested.
:::

## Putting it together: routes with nitrogen compounds

| Change | Reagent and conditions |
|---|---|
| halogenoalkane → primary amine | excess \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure |
| nitrile → primary amine | \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether, or \(\ce{H2}\), Ni, heat |
| carboxylic acid → acyl chloride | \(\ce{SOCl2}\) (or \(\ce{PCl5}\), \(\ce{PCl3}\) and heat) |
| acyl chloride → amide | \(\ce{NH3}\) or an amine, room temperature |
| amide → amine | \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether |
| amide → carboxylic acid (or salt) | dilute acid or NaOH(aq), heat under reflux |
| benzene → nitrobenzene | concentrated \(\ce{HNO3}\) + concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\), 25–60 °C |
| nitrobenzene → phenylamine | tin and concentrated \(\ce{HCl}\), heat; then NaOH(aq) |
| phenylamine → diazonium salt | \(\ce{NaNO2}\) + dilute \(\ce{HCl}\), below 10 °C |
| diazonium salt → phenol | warm with water |
| diazonium salt → azo dye | phenol in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C |

:::eg Suggest a route from propanoic acid to propylamine, giving the reagent for each step and the intermediate formed. [6 marks]
Step 1: propanoic acid with \(\ce{SOCl2}\) (or \(\ce{PCl5}\)) gives propanoyl chloride. [B1] [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2COOH + SOCl2 -> CH3CH2COCl + SO2 + HCl}\]
Step 2: propanoyl chloride with ammonia at room temperature gives propanamide. [B1] [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2COCl + 2NH3 -> CH3CH2CONH2 + NH4Cl}\]
Step 3: propanamide with \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether gives propylamine. [B1] [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CONH2 + 4[H] -> CH3CH2CH2NH2 + H2O}\]
The product has the same number of carbon atoms as the acid: the carbonyl carbon becomes the \(\ce{CH2}\) carbon next to the nitrogen.
:::

+ propylamine

> The C≡N bond adds four hydrogen atoms to give \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2NH2}\). The nitrile carbon is already in the chain, so there are still three carbons.

- the diazonium salt is more stable

- an azo dye forms
- nothing happens
> A benzenediazonium salt is stable only below about 10 °C. Above this temperature it reacts with water, giving \(\ce{C6H5OH}\) and \(\ce{N2}\).

- phenol in concentrated sulfuric acid, room temperature
+ phenol in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C
- phenol in ethanol with \(\ce{AlCl3}\)
> The alkali turns phenol into the phenoxide ion, which activates the ring enough for the weak diazonium electrophile, and the cold keeps the diazonium salt stable.
? Why is ethanamide a much weaker base than ethylamine?
- it has no nitrogen atom
- it has no lone pair
+ the nitrogen lone pair is delocalised over the C=O group
- it is an acid
> The amide nitrogen has a lone pair, but it overlaps with the carbonyl π bond and is drawn towards oxygen, so it is no longer free to accept a proton.

:::recap
- Amines are ammonia with H replaced by alkyl or aryl groups: count the carbon groups on N (primary RNH₂, secondary R₂NH, tertiary R₃N).
- Made from halogenoalkane + excess NH₃ in ethanol under pressure (or a primary amine for a secondary amine), by reducing nitriles (LiAlH₄ in dry ether, or H₂/Ni) and by reducing amides (LiAlH₄).
- Amines are bases: RNH₂ + H₂O ⇌ RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻; with acids they give salts (ionic, soluble). Ethylamine > ammonia > phenylamine, because alkyl groups push electrons onto N and the benzene ring delocalises the lone pair.
- Phenylamine: benzene → nitrobenzene (conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄) → Sn + conc. HCl, heat, then NaOH(aq). Bromine water gives a white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenylamine.
- NaNO₂ + dilute HCl below 10 °C gives the benzenediazonium salt; warming with water gives phenol + N₂. Coupling with phenol in NaOH(aq), below 10 °C, gives an azo dye with the –N=N– group.
- Amides: from an acyl chloride + NH₃ or an amine; hydrolysed by dilute acid (acid + NH₄⁺ or RNH₃⁺) or alkali (carboxylate + NH₃ or amine); reduced by LiAlH₄; much weaker bases than amines (lone pair delocalised over C=O).
- Amino acids H₂N–CHR–COOH are zwitterions H₃N⁺–CHR–COO⁻; cation in acid, anion in alkali; isoelectric point = pH of zero net charge. Peptide bond –CO–NH– from loss of water; Mr = sum minus 18.0 per bond.
- Electrophoresis: cations to the cathode, anions to the anode; no movement at the isoelectric point; larger charge and smaller size move faster.
:::
