@summary How Cambridge 9701 Paper 1 works (40 four-choice questions in 1 hour 15 minutes), how to pace it, the kinds of question you will meet, the techniques that win marks, the usual traps by topic, and eight fully worked questions.

:::why
Paper 1 is the only paper where you do not write an answer: you choose one. That sounds easier, but every wrong option has been designed from a real mistake, and 40 questions in 75 minutes leaves no time to think slowly about all of them. The skill is a method: read, predict the answer, then find it in the options. This guide gives you that method, shows you where each topic hides its traps, and works through eight questions line by line.
:::

## The paper at a glance

| Fact | Paper 1 |
|---|---|
| Name | Multiple Choice |
| Time | 1 hour 15 minutes |
| Questions | 40, each with four choices (A, B, C, D) |
| Marks | 40: one mark for each correct answer |
| Content | AS Level topics 1–22 |
| Weighting | 31% of AS Level; 15.5% of A Level |
| Assessment objectives | AO1 (knowledge and understanding) 50%, AO2 (handling, applying and evaluating information) 50% |

Two things follow from the table. First, **every AS topic can appear**, so a gap in any chapter from [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms) to [Infrared spectroscopy and mass spectrometry](#chem.analysis) is a gap in this paper. Second, half the marks are AO2: you will be given information (a table, a graph, a spectrum, an unfamiliar compound) and must apply what you know to it. Memorising facts earns the AO1 half; practising unfamiliar contexts earns the other.

:::exam
A wrong answer scores zero, the same as leaving a question blank. So **never leave a question empty**: if you are unsure, remove the options you know are wrong and choose from the rest. A guess cannot cost you a mark.
:::

## Timing: a plan for 75 minutes

\[\dfrac{75\ \text{minutes}}{40\ \text{questions}} = 1.875\ \text{minutes} = 1\ \text{minute}\ 52\ \text{seconds per question}\]

That is the average, and you should not spend it evenly. Recall questions take 30 to 60 seconds. Calculations and organic questions where you must draw a structure take 2 to 3 minutes. A good plan has three passes:

:::steps
1. **First pass (about 45 minutes).** Work through the paper in order. Answer every question you can do in under two minutes. If a question needs more, circle its number on the question paper, put your best guess on the answer sheet so there is never a blank, and move on.
2. **Second pass (about 20 minutes).** Return to the circled questions, starting with calculations you can see how to do. Take the time to do them properly, with the working beside the question.
3. **Final check (about 10 minutes).** Make sure every question has exactly one answer. Re-check any calculation where you rounded early, and read the questions containing "not", "incorrect" or "least" once more.
:::

:::tip
Do not stay on one question because you have already spent time on it. Time spent is gone whatever you decide next. If you are stuck after two minutes, guess and circle it.
:::

## The kinds of question you will meet

Almost every question falls into one of four kinds. Knowing which one you are looking at tells you what to do first.

| Kind | What it looks like | What to do first |
|---|---|---|
| Recall | "Which compound…", "What is the shape of…" | Answer in your head before reading the options |
| Calculation | Numbers in the question, numbers in the options | Do the calculation, then look for your value; check the distractors for the usual slips |
| Data or graph | A table of ionisation energies, a pH range, a spectrum, a Boltzmann curve | Read the axes or column headings and units, then ask what the data show |
| "Which statements are correct?" | Several numbered statements and four choices such as "1, 2 and 3", "1 and 2 only", "2 and 3 only" or "1 only" | Judge each statement true or false on its own, then choose the matching option |

Some questions use this last format: the answer is not one statement but a combination, so you must decide about every statement. Mark each with T or F beside it as you go; do not stop when you find one that is correct.

## Techniques that win marks

:::steps
1. **Cover the options.** Read the question stem, then cover the choices with your hand and decide what the answer should be. Options are written to attract the half-formed idea. If you have your own answer first, the wrong ones attract you less.
2. **Eliminate.** Cross out any option you can show is wrong. Two left means a 50% guess, and often the reason one of the two is wrong appears when you compare them.
3. **Estimate calculations.** Round the numbers to one figure and get a rough answer first. The options often differ by factors of 10, 2 or 1000, so a rough answer picks the right one even when the arithmetic is awkward.
4. **Check units and powers of ten.** Volumes are in cm³ or dm³ (1 dm³ = 1000 cm³), pressure in kPa or Pa, energies in J or kJ. Many distractors are the right number with the wrong power of ten.
5. **Watch the small words.** "Not", "incorrect", "only", "least", "all". Underline them. For "which is NOT correct", test every option and pick the false one.
6. **Draw it.** For an organic question, sketch the structure or the mechanism step on the paper. For a shape question, count bonding pairs and lone pairs on the central atom before you decide.
7. **Use the Data booklet.** If the question gives you a bond to break or an energy to use, look it up rather than trusting memory (see below).
:::

:::warn
Do not choose an option because it "sounds like" the topic. A distractor is usually a true statement about the wrong thing, such as a correct fact about the rate of a reaction offered as the answer to a question about the position of equilibrium. Answer the question that was asked.
:::

## Traps by topic

These are the mistakes that the wrong options in Paper 1 questions are made from. Each topic links to the chapter that explains it.

| Topic | The trap | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| [Moles](#chem.moles) | Using cm³ where dm³ is needed (a 1000× error), or ignoring the ratio in the equation | Convert volume to dm³ first; write the mole ratio from the balanced equation |
| [Bonding](#chem.bonding) and [shapes](#chem.shapes) | Forgetting lone pairs, so ammonia is called tetrahedral, or calling a molecule with polar bonds polar when its shape cancels the dipoles | Count bonding pairs and lone pairs; check symmetry for polarity |
| [Energetics](#chem.energetics) | The wrong sign of ΔH; using bonds made minus bonds broken | ΔH = energy to break bonds minus energy released making bonds; exothermic is negative |
| [Redox](#chem.redox) | Confusing oxidising agent with the species oxidised | The oxidising agent is reduced; count the change in oxidation number |
| [Equilibria](#chem.equilibria) | Thinking a catalyst changes the position of equilibrium or the value of K | A catalyst speeds both directions equally; only temperature changes K |
| [Kinetics](#chem.kinetics) | Boltzmann curve at a higher temperature: peak higher or area larger | Same area, lower peak moving right; Eₐ unchanged |
| [Periodicity](#chem.periodicity) and [Group 2](#chem.group2) | Mixing up the trends down the group (thermal stability up, hydroxide solubility up, sulfate solubility down) | Learn each trend with its reason |
| [Group 17](#chem.group17) | Mixing up which halide ion reduces concentrated sulfuric acid furthest | Reducing power increases down the group: Cl⁻ < Br⁻ < I⁻ |
| [Organic](#chem.org.intro) | Counting isomers twice or missing branched ones; confusing a reagent with a condition | Systematically build from the longest chain; write reagent and condition separately |
| [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen) | Believing the most polar bond (C–Cl) reacts fastest | Reactivity follows bond strength: C–I is the weakest, so the fastest |
| [Analysis](#chem.analysis) | Reading the IR region wrongly or forgetting the [M+1] peak formula | Use the Data booklet IR table; learn \(n = \dfrac{100 \times \text{abundance of } [\text{M}+1]^+}{1.1 \times \text{abundance of M}^+}\) for the number of carbon atoms |

## Eight questions worked in full

Work each one yourself before opening the answer. The answer gives the method, then says why each wrong option is wrong, because that is how you learn to spot the same trap again.

:::try Eight worked questions
Q: [1 mark] (#chem.moles) 25.0 cm³ of aqueous sodium hydroxide of concentration 0.200 mol dm⁻³ is exactly neutralised by sulfuric acid of concentration 0.125 mol dm⁻³. What volume of the acid is needed?

**A** 10.0 cm³

**B** 20.0 cm³

**C** 40.0 cm³

**D** 80.0 cm³

H: Write the equation first: \(\ce{H2SO4 + 2NaOH -> Na2SO4 + 2H2O}\). Find moles of NaOH, then moles of acid.
A: **B**. \(n(\ce{NaOH}) = 0.0250 \times 0.200 = 5.00 \times 10^{-3}\) mol; the ratio is 1 acid : 2 alkali, so \(n(\ce{H2SO4}) = 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\) mol; volume \(= 2.50 \times 10^{-3} / 0.125 = 0.0200\) dm³ \(= 20.0\) cm³. C ignores the 1 : 2 ratio (\(5.00 \times 10^{-3} / 0.125\)). D multiplies by 2 instead of dividing. A halves the amount twice.

Q: [1 mark] (#chem.shapes) Which species has a pyramidal shape?

**A** \(\ce{BF3}\)

**B** \(\ce{NH4+}\)

**C** \(\ce{CO3^2-}\)

**D** \(\ce{H3O+}\)

H: For each central atom count the bonding pairs, then the lone pairs.
A: **D**. In \(\ce{H3O+}\) oxygen has 3 bonding pairs and 1 lone pair: four pairs arrange tetrahedrally, but the shape (the atoms only) is pyramidal, like ammonia. A, \(\ce{BF3}\), has three bonding pairs and no lone pair: trigonal planar. B, \(\ce{NH4+}\), has four bonding pairs: tetrahedral. C, \(\ce{CO3^2-}\), has three regions of electron density and no lone pair on carbon: trigonal planar.

Q: [1 mark] (#chem.energetics) Use the Data booklet. What is the enthalpy change of the reaction \(\ce{CH4(g) + 2O2(g) -> CO2(g) + 2H2O(g)}\) in kJ mol⁻¹?

**A** −1314

**B** −818

**C** −688

**D** +818

H: Bonds broken: 4 C–H and 2 O=O. Bonds made: 2 C=O (in CO₂) and 4 O–H.
A: **B**. Broken: \(4 \times 410 + 2 \times 496 = 2632\) kJ. Made: \(2 \times 805 + 4 \times 460 = 3450\) kJ (the C=O value in CO₂ is 805). \(\Delta H = 2632 - 3450 = -818\) kJ mol⁻¹. A breaks only one O=O bond. C uses the average C=O value (740) instead of the CO₂ value. D has the sign wrong: it comes from bonds made minus bonds broken. Breaking bonds costs energy and making them releases it, so ΔH = bonds broken − bonds made, which is negative for this exothermic reaction.

Q: [1 mark] (#chem.equilibria) The equilibrium \(\ce{2SO2(g) + O2(g) <=> 2SO3(g)}\) is exothermic in the forward direction. Which change increases the equilibrium amount of \(\ce{SO3}\)?

**A** adding a vanadium(V) oxide catalyst

**B** raising the temperature at constant pressure

**C** raising the pressure at constant temperature

**D** adding argon at constant volume and temperature

A: **C**. There are 3 mol of gas on the left and 2 mol on the right. Raising the pressure moves the position to the side with fewer gas molecules, which is the right. A: a catalyst speeds both directions equally and does not change the position. B: raising the temperature favours the endothermic (reverse) direction. D: argon at constant volume does not change the partial pressures of the reacting gases, so the position does not move.

Q: [1 mark] (#chem.kinetics) The distribution of molecular energies for a gas is drawn at 300 K and again at 320 K. Which statement about the curve at 320 K is correct?

**A** The area under the curve is larger because more molecules are present.

**B** The activation energy is smaller.

**C** The peak is lower and further to the right.

**D** The peak is higher and further to the right.

A: **C**. The number of molecules is the same, so the area is the same. The distribution spreads out towards higher energies, so the peak must be lower and moves to the right, and a larger fraction of molecules has at least the activation energy. A confuses the area (the number of molecules) with the fraction above \(E_a\). B: temperature does not change \(E_a\); only a catalyst does. D: a curve with the same area that moves right and broadens cannot also be taller.

Q: [1 mark] (#chem.org.isomerism) How many structural isomers have the molecular formula \(\ce{C4H9Br}\)?

**A** 2

**B** 3

**C** 4

**D** 5

H: Start with the unbranched carbon chain, move the bromine along it, then do the branched chain.
A: **C**. Unbranched chain: 1-bromobutane and 2-bromobutane (3-bromo and 4-bromo are the same as 2 and 1 read from the other end). Branched chain (2-methylpropane skeleton): 1-bromo-2-methylpropane and 2-bromo-2-methylpropane. That is 4. A forgets the branched chain. B misses one position. D counts the two optical isomers of 2-bromobutane as separate structural isomers; they are stereoisomers with the same structure.

Q: [1 mark] (#chem.org.halogen) Which reagent and condition convert 1-bromopropane into propan-1-ol?

**A** \(\ce{NaOH(aq)}\), heat

**B** NaOH in ethanol, heat

**C** KCN in ethanol, heat

**D** \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heat under pressure

A: **A**. Aqueous hydroxide is a nucleophile and replaces the bromine, giving the alcohol. B gives elimination: propene, because ethanolic hydroxide acts as a base. C replaces Br with CN to give butanenitrile, which has one more carbon. D gives a primary amine, propylamine. The solvent is part of the answer: water gives substitution, ethanol gives elimination.

Q: [1 mark] (#chem.atoms) The first four ionisation energies of an element X, in kJ mol⁻¹, are 577, 1820, 2740 and 11 600. What is the formula of the chloride of X?

**A** XCl

**B** \(\ce{XCl2}\)

**C** \(\ce{XCl3}\)

**D** \(\ce{XCl4}\)

A: **C**. The large jump comes between the third and fourth ionisation energies (2740 to 11 600), so the fourth electron is removed from an inner shell and the atom has three outer electrons. It forms \(\ce{X^3+}\), so the chloride is \(\ce{XCl3}\) (this is aluminium). A would need the large jump after the first electron, B after the second, and D after the fourth.
:::

## The Data booklet

You are expected to use the Data booklet in this paper, so learn where things are rather than learning the values. It contains:

- the constants and the molar gas volume, and the Periodic Table with relative atomic masses
- first and successive ionisation energies, and Pauling electronegativities
- bond energies (exact for diatomic molecules, average for the rest)
- standard electrode potentials
- ¹H and ¹³C NMR chemical shifts, and infrared absorptions

Do not rely on the booklet for formulas such as the [M+1] carbon count: learn them.

:::tip
Paper 1 questions that need the booklet usually say so, or give you an unfamiliar bond or element. Check which state the question wants: bond energies for **gaseous** molecules, and the C=O value in carbon dioxide is different from the average C=O value. Looking up the wrong row is a very common reason for a wrong answer.
:::

## A practice plan

:::steps
1. **Topic by topic.** Finish the quiz at the end of each chapter, then fix what you got wrong by returning to the explanation, not just by reading the answer.
2. **Untimed mixed questions.** Do the AS mixed practice in [Mixed practice: AS Level](#chem.mixed.as) to practise switching topics.
3. **The mock.** Sit [Mock paper: Paper 1 multiple choice](#chem.mock.p1) under exam conditions: 75 minutes, no notes, the Data booklet and a calculator on the desk. Mark it and use the list of chapters to revise.
4. **Real past papers.** The board's own past papers and mark schemes are available through your school, from the Cambridge International website or, sorted by topic, from the Physics & Maths Tutor collection (links below). Do at least a few full papers in 75 minutes, mark them against the mark scheme, and keep a list of the topics where you lost marks.
5. **Fix, then repeat.** For each mistake write down which kind it was: knowledge, misreading, arithmetic or timing. Fix the cause, and a month later redo the questions you missed.
:::

## Six more to try

:::quiz Technique check
? Paper 1 has 40 questions in 1 hour 15 minutes. About how long is there for each question on average?
- about 1 minute
+ just under 2 minutes
- about 2 minutes 30 seconds
- about 3 minutes
> 75 ÷ 40 = 1.875 minutes, which is 1 minute 52 seconds: just under 2 minutes. Spend less on recall questions to leave time for calculations.

? You cannot answer a question and are unsure of every option. What should you do?
- Leave it blank so that you cannot lose a mark.
+ Remove any options you know are wrong and choose one of the rest.
- Choose the longest option.
- Spend five more minutes on it before you move on.
> A wrong answer scores zero, the same as a blank, so a guess cannot cost anything. Never leave a blank; and do not spend five minutes on one mark.

? Roughly how many moles are in 5.0 g of calcium carbonate, \(\ce{CaCO3}\) (\(M_r\) = 100.1)?
- 0.50 mol
+ 0.050 mol
- 20 mol
- 500 mol
> Round \(M_r\) to 100: 5 ÷ 100 = 0.05 mol, and a calculator gives 0.0500 mol. Mass divided by \(M_r\) (not the other way round) gives moles, and 5 g is much less than 100 g mol⁻¹, so the answer must be well below 1.

? A catalyst is added to a mixture at equilibrium. What happens?
- The position of equilibrium moves towards the products.
- The value of K increases.
+ Neither the position of equilibrium nor K changes; equilibrium is reached faster.
- The position moves to the exothermic side.
> A catalyst lowers the activation energy for the forward and the reverse reaction equally. Only a change of temperature changes K.

? A question asks "Which statement about the halogens is NOT correct?" What is the best method?
- Choose the first statement that sounds true.
+ Judge each option true or false, and choose the one that is false.
- Choose the longest statement.
- Choose the statement with the most technical words.
> With "not" questions three options are true and one false. Underline the word "not", test each option and pick the false one.

? 25.0 cm³ of a solution of concentration 0.100 mol dm⁻³ contains how many moles of solute?
- 2.50 mol
+ \(2.50 \times 10^{-3}\) mol
- 0.250 mol
- \(4.00 \times 10^{-3}\) mol
> Convert the volume to dm³ first: 25.0 cm³ = 0.0250 dm³. Then \(n = c \times V = 0.100 \times 0.0250 = 2.50 \times 10^{-3}\) mol. Using 25.0 without converting gives 2.50 mol, a thousand times too large.
:::
