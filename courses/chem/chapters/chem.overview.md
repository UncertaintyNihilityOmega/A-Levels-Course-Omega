## What chemistry is, and who it suits

Chemistry is the science of substances and how they change. Everything you touch, eat, wear or breathe is a substance or a mixture of substances, and a chemist asks two questions about each one: what is it made of, and what will it do? The answers come from the particles inside it (atoms, ions and molecules), how those particles are held together, and how much energy it takes to rearrange them.

The 9701 course splits into three branches that you will meet over and over:

- **Physical chemistry** explains *how much* and *how fast*: moles and calculations, energy changes, equilibrium, acids and bases, rates of reaction, electrochemistry. It is the most mathematical part.
- **Inorganic chemistry** is about the elements and their compounds: patterns across the Periodic Table, Group 2 and Group 17, nitrogen and sulfur, and the transition elements with their colourful complexes. It rewards pattern-spotting and clear explanations.
- **Organic chemistry** is the chemistry of carbon compounds: naming, functional groups, mechanisms, and synthesis routes from one compound to another. It rewards careful drawing and exact reagents.

Two more skills sit across the branches. **Analysis** (infrared and mass spectra, chromatography, NMR, and the qualitative tests for ions and gases) is how chemists find out what a substance is. **Practical skills** are tested in two papers, one in the laboratory and one on paper.

Chemistry is a good fit if you like explaining why things happen, and you are happy to combine memorised facts with calculations. It leads naturally to medicine, pharmacy, dentistry, veterinary science, chemical and materials engineering, biochemistry, environmental science and food science. Be honest with yourself about the workload: chemistry has a lot of facts *and* a lot of numbers, and students who treat it as only one of the two lose marks on the other.

## The Cambridge 9701 papers

This course follows **Cambridge International AS & A Level Chemistry (9701)**. The syllabus has 37 topics: topics 1–22 are AS Level and topics 23–37 are A Level. There are five papers.

| Paper | Duration | Marks | Weighting: AS / A Level | Content |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Paper 1: Multiple Choice | 1 h 15 min | 40 | 31% / 15.5% | AS content; 40 four-choice questions |
| Paper 2: AS Level Structured Questions | 1 h 15 min | 60 | 46% / 23% | AS content |
| Paper 3: Advanced Practical Skills | 2 h | 40 | 23% / 11.5% | practical test in a laboratory |
| Paper 4: A Level Structured Questions | 2 h | 100 | – / 38.5% | A Level content; AS knowledge is assumed |
| Paper 5: Planning, Analysis and Evaluation | 1 h 15 min | 30 | – / 11.5% | written paper, no laboratory |

There are three routes through the papers:

1. **AS Level only**: Papers 1, 2 and 3. Grades run from a to e.
2. **Staged A Level**: the AS papers in the first year, then Papers 4 and 5 later. Your AS marks count towards the A Level.
3. **All in one series**: all five papers in the same exam session. A Level grades run from A* to E.

Notice what the weightings tell you. Paper 4 is the largest single paper at A Level, and it assumes everything from AS, so AS chemistry is never "finished": it stays in the exam until the end.

:::warn Paper 4 tests AS chemistry too
A Level questions regularly mix a new topic (say, lattice energy) with an old one (moles, bonding, organic reactions). Do not drop AS topics once you have passed the AS papers. The mixed-practice chapters at the end of this course exist for this reason.
:::

## Assessment objectives

Every mark in 9701 belongs to one of three assessment objectives (AOs).

| Assessment objective | What it rewards | Share of the whole qualification |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | knowledge and understanding of the science | 40% |
| AO2 | handling, applying and evaluating information: calculations, unfamiliar data, deducing structures | 40% |
| AO3 | experimental skills and investigations | 20% |

Papers 1, 2 and 4 are split evenly between AO1 and AO2 (50% each). Papers 3 and 5 test AO3 only. In practice, AO1 is "do you know it?" and AO2 is "can you use it on something you have not seen before?". Most of this course trains the second skill, because recall alone does not reach the top grades.

## Study order and pace

**Follow the chapter order.** Chemistry builds in layers. [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms) comes first because electrons explain bonding, and [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles) comes second because it is the foundation of every calculation that follows: energetics, equilibria, acids, kinetics, titrations and organic yields all start from moles. Do the physical chapters through to [Reaction kinetics](#chem.kinetics) before you rely on them in the inorganic chapters, and start the organic chapters only after bonding and energetics, because mechanisms need electronegativity, bond polarity and bond energies. Take the A Level chapters after the AS chapters, as the AS course is their starting point.

**A rhythm for every chapter.** A topic chapter takes about 5–7 hours. Split that into sessions of 45–90 minutes, and use this cycle:

| Session | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1 | Read the first half. Attempt every worked example before opening it, then compare. |
| 2 | Read the second half. Learn the definitions and the reagents-and-conditions tables. |
| 3 | Do the practice questions without notes, then mark them against the answers line by line. |
| 4 | Take the quiz and the checklist. Tick only the lines you could do on a blank page. |
| A week later | Re-do the warm-up questions, the quiz and two exam questions from memory. |

At 8 hours a week, one chapter a week is a comfortable pace. A plan with dates is easier to keep than a plan without: [Build your personal study plan](#road.planner) works out one from your exam date.

## How to reach A*

The top grade comes from a handful of habits, not from reading more.

- **Learn definitions word for word.** Terms such as "relative atomic mass", "enthalpy change of formation", "electrode potential" and "rate-determining step" have exact wording. The definition boxes in each chapter give it.
- **Write equations with state symbols.** Balanced, with (s), (l), (g) and (aq), and with ionic equations that leave out spectator ions.
- **Give units and the right number of significant figures.** A correct number with a missing or wrong unit can lose a mark.
- **Name reagents and conditions in full.** "NaOH in ethanol, heat" and "NaOH(aq), heat under reflux" are different answers. Every organic reaction needs its reagent, solvent and condition.
- **Draw mechanisms with exact arrows.** A curly arrow starts at a lone pair or a bond, and ends at the atom or bond that receives the pair.
- **Explain with named causes.** "The C–I bond is weaker" earns a mark. "Iodine is more reactive" does not.
- **Learn the qualitative-analysis notes** (colours of precipitates, results of tests for ions and gases) until you can recite them, then practise them in Paper 3 style.
- **Practise Paper 5 planning** with the structure the chapter gives: variables, method, hazards, and how the results are processed.
- **Use real past papers.** Finish a topic, then do the questions on it, mark them honestly against the mark scheme, and keep a list of every lost mark and why.

:::exam Command words
The syllabus uses the same words in every paper: analyse, calculate, compare, consider, contrast, deduce, define, demonstrate, describe, determine, discuss, evaluate, examine, explain, give, identify, justify, predict, show (that), sketch, state and suggest. A few matter most in chemistry. **State** and **give** want a short answer with no reason. **Explain** wants a cause, so your answer needs the word "because" or its meaning. **Deduce** and **predict** want you to reach an answer from information in the question. **Suggest** means apply what you know to a situation you have not met. Read [Command words decoded](#road.commands) before your first full paper.
:::

:::recap
