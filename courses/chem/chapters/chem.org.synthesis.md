Q: What reagent and conditions convert bromoethane into propanenitrile, and why does the product have one more carbon atom? (from [Halogenoalkanes](#chem.org.halogen))

## A route is a chain of one-group changes

Almost every organic reaction in the AS course changes one functional group into another, and leaves the rest of the molecule alone. A **synthesis** (a *route*) is a series of such changes that turns a starting molecule into a target molecule. Each arrow in a route is one reaction, with its own reagent, its own conditions and its own type.

:::def Synthetic route
A sequence of reactions that converts a starting compound into a target compound, in which the product of each step is the reactant of the next.
:::

You already know which reagent does each job. The difficulty is that the question never says which jobs to do. It gives you a start and a finish, and you must work out the path. The rest of this chapter gives you three things to make that possible: a map of every reaction you need, a method for finding a path on it, and a set of test-tube reactions for finding out what a molecule contains.

## The reaction map

Here is every reaction of the AS organic course, grouped by the compound you start from. Learn it as a map, not as a list: each row is a road from one functional group to another. "Type" is the name an examiner expects in the answer.

### From alkanes and alkenes

| Change | Reagents and conditions | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|
| alkane → halogenoalkane | \(\ce{Cl2}\) or \(\ce{Br2}\), ultraviolet light | free-radical substitution |
| long alkane → shorter alkane + alkene | heat with \(\ce{Al2O3}\) | cracking |
| alkene → alkane | \(\ce{H2(g)}\), Pt or Ni catalyst, heat | addition (hydrogenation) |
| alkene → halogenoalkane | \(\ce{HX(g)}\) at room temperature, or \(\ce{X2}\) | electrophilic addition |
| alkene → alcohol | steam, \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst | electrophilic addition (hydration) |
| alkene → diol | cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) | oxidation |
| alkene → ketone or carboxylic acid (C=C splits) | hot, concentrated, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) | oxidation |
| alkene → poly(alkene) | many alkene molecules join | addition polymerisation |

### From halogenoalkanes

| Change | Reagents and conditions | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|
| halogenoalkane → alcohol | NaOH(aq), heat | nucleophilic substitution (hydrolysis) |
| halogenoalkane → nitrile | KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux | nucleophilic substitution |
| halogenoalkane → amine | excess \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure | nucleophilic substitution |
| halogenoalkane → alkene | NaOH in ethanol, heat | elimination |

### From alcohols

| Change | Reagents and conditions | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|
| alcohol → halogenoalkane | \(\ce{HX(g)}\); or KCl with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) or \(\ce{H3PO4}\); or \(\ce{PCl3}\) and heat; or \(\ce{PCl5}\); or \(\ce{SOCl2}\) | substitution |
| primary alcohol → aldehyde | acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) (or \(\ce{KMnO4}\)), heat, distil off the product | oxidation |
| primary alcohol → carboxylic acid | acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) (or \(\ce{KMnO4}\)), heat under reflux | oxidation |
| secondary alcohol → ketone | acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) (or \(\ce{KMnO4}\)), heat, distil | oxidation |
| tertiary alcohol | not oxidised by these reagents | no reaction |
| alcohol → alkene | heated \(\ce{Al2O3}\), or concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) and heat | dehydration (elimination) |
| alcohol + carboxylic acid → ester | concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, heat under reflux | condensation (esterification) |

### From aldehydes and ketones

| Change | Reagents and conditions | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|
| aldehyde → carboxylic acid | acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) (or \(\ce{KMnO4}\)), heat under reflux | oxidation |
| aldehyde → primary alcohol; ketone → secondary alcohol | \(\ce{NaBH4}\) or \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) | reduction |
| aldehyde or ketone → hydroxynitrile | HCN with KCN catalyst, heat | nucleophilic addition |
| ketone | not oxidised by these reagents | no reaction |

### From carboxylic acids, esters and nitriles

| Change | Reagents and conditions | Type of reaction |
|---|---|---|
| carboxylic acid → primary alcohol | \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether (not \(\ce{NaBH4}\)) | reduction |
| carboxylic acid → salt | reactive metal, alkali or carbonate | acid–base (with a metal, redox) |
| nitrile or hydroxynitrile → carboxylic acid | dilute acid or dilute alkali, heat, then acidify if alkali was used | hydrolysis |
| ester → carboxylic acid + alcohol | dilute acid, or dilute alkali, and heat | hydrolysis |

:::tip Read the map as a chain of functional groups
Mentally put the groups in a row: alkane, alkene, halogenoalkane, alcohol, aldehyde or ketone, carboxylic acid. Oxidation moves right along the oxygen-rich end (alcohol → aldehyde → acid); reduction moves back (acid → alcohol, aldehyde → alcohol). The halogenoalkane is the junction, because the halogen can be swapped for OH, CN or NH₂.
:::

The map shows what is possible between *groups*. Your molecule has a particular carbon skeleton. A ketone, for instance, can only come from a *secondary* alcohol, so a route to propanone must pass through propan-2-ol, not propan-1-ol. Always check that the position of the group in your molecule matches the route.

## Changing the number of carbon atoms

Most reactions keep the carbon skeleton the same: oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, substitution by OH, addition of HX. Only a few reactions change the number of carbon atoms, and they are the ones to look for when the start and the target have different chain lengths.

| Reaction | Effect on the carbon chain |
|---|---|
| halogenoalkane + KCN in ethanol | adds one carbon (the C of CN) |
| aldehyde or ketone + HCN, KCN catalyst | adds one carbon, as a hydroxynitrile |
| alcohol + carboxylic acid (esterification) | joins two molecules into one ester |
| alkene + hot, concentrated, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) | splits the chain at the C=C bond |
| cracking | splits a long chain into shorter ones |
| addition polymerisation | joins many alkene molecules |

This is the first thing to do with any route question: **count the carbon atoms** in the start and the target. If the target has more, a cyanide step is almost certainly needed. If it has fewer, look for the oxidative cleavage of a C=C bond.

:::steps
1. Count the carbons in the start and in the target. A longer target means a cyanide step (KCN or HCN) or an esterification.
2. Compare the functional groups. Which group must be lost, and which gained?
3. Work backwards from the target one step at a time. Ask "what reaction makes this group?" using the map, then repeat for the compound you just named. Stop when you reach the start.
4. Write the route forwards, giving reagents and conditions for each step, then check that no step uses a reagent that would also change another group in the molecule.
:::

Working backwards is the key habit. A target has only a few ways to be made (look up the "made by" list for its group), whereas a start has many things it can become, so the forward search spreads out like a tree and the backward search stays narrow.

## Worked examples: building routes

:::eg Ethene is the only organic starting material. Devise a route to ethyl ethanoate, \(\ce{CH3COOCH2CH3}\), giving reagents and conditions.
**Work backwards.** An ester is made from an alcohol and a carboxylic acid with concentrated sulfuric acid. Ethyl ethanoate is made from ethanol (the "ethyl" part) and ethanoic acid (the "ethanoate" part). Both contain two carbons, so no carbon-chain change is needed: all that is needed is to make an alcohol and an acid from ethene.

**Route forwards.**

Step 1: ethene to ethanol: steam and a \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst (electrophilic addition). [B1]
\[\ce{CH2=CH2 + H2O -> CH3CH2OH}\]
Step 2: ethanol to ethanoic acid: acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), heated under reflux so that the aldehyde that forms first is oxidised on to the acid. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2OH + 2[O] -> CH3COOH + H2O}\]
Step 3: ethanoic acid and *more* ethanol (kept from step 1) heated under reflux with concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) as catalyst. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH <=> CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O}\]

Notice that the route is **convergent**: ethanol is made once, then part of it is taken on to the acid and part is kept to meet the acid in the last step. [B1]
:::

:::eg Suggest a route from propan-1-ol to butanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2COOH}\).
**Count the carbons.** Propan-1-ol has three, butanoic acid has four, so a cyanide step is needed.

**Work backwards.** A carboxylic acid can be made by hydrolysing a nitrile. The nitrile must be butanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN}\), which has the same four-carbon skeleton. A nitrile is made from a halogenoalkane with KCN, and the halogenoalkane must have three carbons: 1-bromopropane. An alcohol turns into a halogenoalkane with HBr. The route is complete.

Step 1: propan-1-ol with \(\ce{HBr(g)}\) gives 1-bromopropane (substitution). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH + HBr -> CH3CH2CH2Br + H2O}\]
Step 2: 1-bromopropane heated under reflux with KCN in ethanol gives butanenitrile (nucleophilic substitution). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2Br + KCN -> CH3CH2CH2CN + KBr}\]
Step 3: butanenitrile heated with dilute acid gives butanoic acid (hydrolysis). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2CN + H+ + 2H2O -> CH3CH2CH2COOH + NH4+}\]

:::eg Convert 2-bromopropane into propanone, and name the type of each step.
A ketone comes from a secondary alcohol, so the intermediate must be propan-2-ol.

Step 1: heat 2-bromopropane with NaOH(aq) to give propan-2-ol (nucleophilic substitution). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CHBrCH3 + OH- -> CH3CH(OH)CH3 + Br-}\]
Step 2: warm propan-2-ol with acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) and distil off the product, propanone (oxidation). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] -> CH3COCH3 + H2O}\]

Both carbons count the same (three), and the OH group sits on carbon 2 throughout, which is what a ketone needs. [B1]

Using NaOH *in ethanol* in step 1 would be the common slip: it gives propene by elimination, not the alcohol. [B1]

## Analysing a route you are given

Some questions hand you the route and ask what is going on. For each step you may be asked for the reagent, the type of reaction, the mechanism and the by-products. Treat each arrow as its own small question. A route is only as good as its worst step: ask of each step what else could happen in the flask.

:::eg The route below is used to make 2-methylpropanoic acid from propan-1-ol. (a) Identify the type of reaction in each step. (b) Suggest the reagent for steps 1 and 2. (c) Name an organic by-product of step 2 and say why it forms. (d) State at which step the number of carbon atoms changes.
\[\ce{CH3CH2CH2OH ->[1] CH3CH=CH2 ->[2] CH3CHBrCH3 ->[3] CH3CH(CN)CH3 ->[4] CH3CH(CH3)COOH}\]

(a) Step 1 is elimination (dehydration), step 2 is electrophilic addition, step 3 is nucleophilic substitution and step 4 is hydrolysis. [B1] [B1]

(b) Step 1: heated \(\ce{Al2O3}\) (or concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) and heat). [B1] Step 2: \(\ce{HBr(g)}\) at room temperature. [B1]

(c) Propene is unsymmetrical, so \(\ce{HBr}\) can add in two ways. Bromine on the middle carbon gives 2-bromopropane through the more stable secondary carbocation, and that is the main product. Bromine on the end carbon gives 1-bromopropane through the less stable primary carbocation. 1-bromopropane is the by-product. [B1]

(d) The carbon count goes from three (propan-1-ol, propene, 2-bromopropane) to four at step 3, where KCN adds one carbon. [B1]

The route gives a *branched* acid because the bromine ends up on the middle carbon. A route to straight-chain butanoic acid could not use this addition step. [B1]
:::

### Possible by-products at each kind of step

Write down by-products by asking what else the reagents could do. These are the ones that appear in questions.

| Step | By-product or problem | Why |
|---|---|---|
| free-radical substitution of an alkane | a mixture: CH₃CH₂Cl, CH₃CHCl₂, CH₂ClCH₂Cl and others | the product also has C–H bonds, so more than one H is replaced |
| addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene | two isomeric halogenoalkanes | the halogen can go on either carbon of C=C; the more stable carbocation gives the major product |
| halogenoalkane with hydroxide | alkene, if the solvent is ethanol | hydroxide acts as a base and eliminates HX |
| halogenoalkane with \(\ce{NH3}\) | secondary and tertiary amines | the amine product is also a nucleophile, so use excess \(\ce{NH3}\) |
| elimination from a halogenoalkane or alcohol | more than one alkene | H can leave from either neighbouring carbon, giving positional and cis/trans isomers |
| oxidation of a primary alcohol | the aldehyde, or the acid | distillation stops at the aldehyde; reflux with excess oxidant goes on to the acid |
| esterification | unreacted acid and alcohol, water | the reaction is reversible and reaches an equilibrium |
| alcohol + \(\ce{PCl5}\) or \(\ce{SOCl2}\) | \(\ce{POCl3}\) and \(\ce{HCl}\); or \(\ce{SO2}\) and \(\ce{HCl}\) | these come from the reagent; with \(\ce{SOCl2}\) both are gases |
| hydrolysis of a nitrile | an ammonium salt (\(\ce{NH4+}\)) | the nitrogen of C≡N leaves as ammonia, which is protonated in acid |

:::warn By-products are not only "waste"
The question "suggest a possible by-product" wants a specific compound and the reason it forms. "Impurities" or "other products" scores nothing. Name the isomer, the over-oxidised compound or the elimination product.
:::

### Yield across several steps

Every step loses some product (side reactions, material left on glassware, equilibrium). In a multi-step route the losses multiply, which is why chemists prefer short routes.

:::formula Overall yield of a route
\[\text{overall yield} = y_1 \times y_2 \times y_3 \times \cdots\]
where each \(y\) is the fractional yield of one step (percentage yield ÷ 100). Convert the answer back to a percentage.
:::

:::eg (Exam style) Ethene is converted to propanoic acid in three steps with yields of 85.0 % (ethene to bromoethane), 70.0 % (bromoethane to propanenitrile) and 80.0 % (propanenitrile to propanoic acid). Calculate the overall percentage yield, and the mass of propanoic acid obtained from 5.00 g of ethene.
Overall fractional yield \(= 0.850 \times 0.700 \times 0.800 = 0.4760\), so the overall yield is 47.6 %. [M1]

\(M_r(\ce{C2H4}) = 2(12.0) + 4(1.0) = 28.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C3H6O2}) = 3(12.0) + 6(1.0) + 2(16.0) = 74.0\). [B1]

One mole of ethene could give one mole of propanoic acid, because each step is one-to-one.
\[n(\ce{C2H4}) = \frac{5.00}{28.0} = 0.1786\,\mathrm{mol}\] [M1]
\[\text{mass of acid} = 0.1786 \times 0.4760 \times 74.0 = 6.29\,\mathrm{g}\] [A1]
:::

## Many groups in one molecule

A real molecule often has more than one functional group. To predict what a reagent does, treat each group separately. Look up each group's reactions, ask whether the reagent is one of them, and remember that **one reagent may act on several groups**.

Two kinds of question follow. In the first you are given a molecule and a reagent and must say what happens. In the second you must choose a reagent that changes one group and leaves another alone. This second kind is called *selectivity*. Some reagents are selective:

- \(\ce{NaBH4}\) reduces aldehydes and ketones, but not carboxylic acids, and it leaves C=C bonds alone.
- \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) reduces aldehydes, ketones and carboxylic acids, and also leaves C=C bonds alone.
- Cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) reacts with C=C, and also with alcohols and aldehydes. Bromine water reacts with C=C and is the better choice when you only want to test for a double bond.
- Acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) oxidises primary and secondary alcohols and aldehydes. At this level treat it as leaving C=C and ketones untouched.
- \(\ce{HBr(g)}\) adds to C=C and also replaces an OH group, so it acts on both.

Molecules with two or three groups need a naming rule that the earlier chapters did not need. The group highest in the order acid, aldehyde, ketone, alcohol, alkene gets the ending and the lowest number. An alcohol that loses out becomes the prefix hydroxy-, and a C=C becomes -en- inside the name, with its own number. So \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH=CHCH2OH}\) is 6-hydroxyhex-4-en-2-one: the ketone wins (carbon 2), the C=C starts at carbon 4 and the OH is on carbon 6.

:::eg Compound Z is \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH=CHCH2OH}\), 6-hydroxyhex-4-en-2-one. Predict what happens when Z reacts with each reagent, and name or draw the organic product where there is one.
Z has three groups: a ketone (C=O, with a \(\ce{CH3CO}\) end), an alkene (C=C) and a primary alcohol (–OH on the end carbon).

| Reagent | What happens | Product or observation |
|---|---|---|
| \(\ce{Br2(aq)}\) | C=C reacts (electrophilic addition) | orange to colourless; \(\ce{CH3COCH2CHBrCHBrCH2OH}\) [B1] |
| \(\ce{NaBH4}\) | ketone is reduced; C=C and OH untouched | \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH2CH=CHCH2OH}\), hex-2-ene-1,5-diol (numbered from the \(\ce{CH2OH}\) end, which gives the OH groups the lower numbers) [B1] |
| acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), heat under reflux | primary alcohol is oxidised to the acid; ketone and C=C untouched | orange to green; \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH=CHCOOH}\) [B1] |
| 2,4-DNPH | ketone C=O reacts | orange precipitate [B1] |
| Tollens' reagent | a ketone is not oxidised | no silver mirror [B1] |
| alkaline aqueous iodine | \(\ce{CH3CO}\) group present | yellow precipitate of \(\ce{CHI3}\) [B1] |
| \(\ce{Na(s)}\) | the O–H bond reacts | bubbles of \(\ce{H2}\) [B1] |
| \(\ce{Na2CO3(aq)}\) | no acid group | no effervescence [B1] |

The iodine reaction cleaves the molecule after the tri-iodomethane forms, so the organic salt left behind is \(\ce{^-OOCCH2CH=CHCH2OH}\), the anion of 5-hydroxypent-3-enoic acid, which has one carbon fewer than Z. [B1]
:::

### Predicting properties

Properties follow from the groups too. The –OH group of Z forms hydrogen bonds with water and with other Z molecules, so Z has a much higher boiling point than a hydrocarbon of similar size, and it dissolves in water better than a hydrocarbon (the carbon chain stays hydrophobic, so the solubility falls as the chain grows). A carboxylic acid group is acidic and hydrogen-bonds even more strongly. A C=C group makes a molecule unsaturated and reactive towards electrophiles. Naming the group is not enough: **say which property comes from which group and why**, using hydrogen bonding, polarity or the π bond.

## Finding out which groups a molecule contains

A bottle with no label is a standard organic question: use test-tube reactions to find out what is in it. Each test detects one group. The tests all come from reactions you have met; here they are in one place.

| Test | Positive result | Group detected |
|---|---|---|
| aqueous bromine (bromine water), room temperature | orange to colourless | C=C |
| cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) | purple to colourless | oxidisable group: C=C, primary or secondary alcohol, aldehyde |
| warm acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) | orange to green | primary or secondary alcohol, or aldehyde (tertiary alcohols and ketones: no change) |
| 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH) | orange precipitate | C=O of an aldehyde or ketone |
| Tollens' reagent, warm | silver mirror (or black precipitate) | aldehyde (ketone: no reaction) |
| Fehling's solution, heat | orange or red precipitate (copper(I) oxide) | aldehyde (ketone: no reaction) |
| alkaline aqueous iodine, warm | yellow precipitate of \(\ce{CHI3}\) | \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) or \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\) |
| \(\ce{Na2CO3(aq)}\) (or any carbonate) | bubbles of \(\ce{CO2}\) (limewater turns milky) | carboxylic acid |
| sodium metal | bubbles of \(\ce{H2}\) (lighted splint pops) | O–H: alcohol or carboxylic acid |
| \(\ce{AgNO3(aq)}\) in ethanol, warm | white (AgCl), cream (AgBr) or pale yellow (AgI) precipitate | halogen in a halogenoalkane |

Three of these deserve a note.

- **Tollens' and Fehling's** reagents are mild oxidising agents. They oxidise an aldehyde to the carboxylic acid (a carboxylate in the alkaline reagent) while they themselves are reduced: \(\ce{Ag+}\) to silver metal, \(\ce{Cu^2+}\) to copper(I) oxide. A ketone is not oxidised, so the pair distinguishes the two carbonyl compounds.
- **Acidified dichromate(VI)** only separates alcohols into oxidisable (1° and 2°) and not (3°). To tell a primary from a secondary alcohol, test the oxidation product: an aldehyde gives a silver mirror, a ketone does not.
- **The silver nitrate test** needs the halogen to leave the organic molecule as a halide ion, which is why it works on halogenoalkanes (hydrolysis in the tube). The rate also tells you something: iodoalkanes give a precipitate fastest. If the sample is first boiled with NaOH(aq), you must acidify with dilute nitric acid before adding silver nitrate, because hydroxide ions would otherwise give a brown precipitate of silver oxide that hides the halide result.

Some groups have no single test. An ester has no simple reagent; you recognise it by its sweet smell and by hydrolysis to an acid and an alcohol. An amine or a nitrile needs hydrolysis or other reactions you meet at A Level.

:::lab Safe testing
Do the 2,4-DNPH test with a few drops of sample only: the reagent is a skin irritant. Tollens' reagent must be freshly made and the leftover solution rinsed away at once, because it can form explosive solids on standing. Warm tests use a water bath, never a naked flame near ethanol.
:::

:::eg A liquid X has the formula \(\ce{C4H8O}\). It gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH, no silver mirror with Tollens' reagent and a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine. Identify X.
2,4-DNPH positive means X contains a C=O group of an aldehyde or ketone. [B1]

No silver mirror rules out an aldehyde, so X is a ketone. [B1]

The yellow precipitate shows a \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) group. [B1]

The only straight-chain or branched ketone with the formula \(\ce{C4H8O}\) is butanone, \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH3}\), and it does have the \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) group. X is butanone. (Its aldehyde isomer, butanal, would have given a silver mirror and no yellow precipitate.) [B1]
:::

:::eg Describe a test to distinguish each pair, stating the result for each compound: (a) propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol; (b) propanal and propanone; (c) ethanoic acid and ethyl ethanoate; (d) butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol.
(a) Warm with alkaline aqueous iodine. Propan-2-ol, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CH3}\), gives a yellow precipitate. Propan-1-ol has no \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\) group and gives none. [B1]

(b) Warm with Tollens' reagent. Propanal gives a silver mirror; propanone gives none. (Alkaline iodine also works the other way round: propanone gives the yellow precipitate, propanal does not.) [B1]

(c) Add sodium carbonate solution. Ethanoic acid effervesces (\(\ce{CO2}\)); ethyl ethanoate does not. [B1]

(d) Warm with acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\). Butan-2-ol, a secondary alcohol, turns the solution green; 2-methylpropan-2-ol, a tertiary alcohol, leaves it orange. [B1]
:::

## Choosing a reagent: reduction

Reduction questions are the commonest place where a student chooses a reagent that is too weak.

:::eg Give a reagent for each conversion, with a reason: (a) ethanal to ethanol; (b) ethanoic acid to ethanol.
(a) \(\ce{NaBH4}\) (in water or ethanol) or \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether. Either reduces an aldehyde to a primary alcohol. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CHO + 2[H] -> CH3CH2OH}\]
(b) \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether only. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3COOH + 4[H] -> CH3CH2OH + H2O}\]
\(\ce{NaBH4}\) is not strong enough to reduce a carboxylic acid. A carboxylic acid is harder to reduce than an aldehyde, so only the stronger reducing agent works. [B1]
:::

## Planning in practice: two harder routes

:::eg Ethanal can be converted into 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH}\), in two steps. Give the reagents and conditions, and name the mechanism of the first step.
**Count carbons.** Ethanal has two, lactic acid has three, so a cyanide step is needed. Because the product has an OH on the same carbon as the new C, the step is the addition of HCN to the C=O.

Step 1: ethanal with HCN and a little KCN, heated, gives 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}\). [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CHO + HCN -> CH3CH(OH)CN}\]
The mechanism is nucleophilic addition: the cyanide ion attacks the \(\ce{C^{\delta+}}\) of the carbonyl group. [B1]

Step 2: heat with dilute acid (hydrolysis), which turns the nitrile into the carboxylic acid group. [B1]
\[\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN + H+ + 2H2O -> CH3CH(OH)COOH + NH4+}\]
:::

A route can also be completed from the middle: a question may give you steps 1 and 3 and ask for step 2. The method is the same, but you now name the *intermediate* first and then ask what reagent links it to each neighbour.

:::eg Spot the error: describe how propan-1-ol can be converted into butanoic acid in three steps, naming the intermediate in each step. [4 marks]
A student wrote this answer.

Line 1: Step 1: react with HBr(g) to make 1-bromopropane.
Line 2: Step 2: heat 1-bromopropane under reflux with KCN in ethanol to make butanenitrile.
Line 3: Step 3: heat butanenitrile with dilute sodium hydroxide solution to make butanoic acid.

Line 1 scores [B1], line 2 scores [B1], and the three named intermediates and product are correct [B1]. **Line 3 is wrong: heating a nitrile with dilute alkali gives the sodium salt, sodium butanoate, not the acid. The mark for the final step is lost [B0].**

Correct line 3: heat with dilute sulfuric acid (or hydrochloric acid) to give butanoic acid directly; or heat with dilute alkali and then add dilute acid to release the free acid. [B1]

The student scores 3 out of 4. When a hydrolysis uses alkali, the extra "acidify" step is part of the answer.
:::

:::exam How to answer a synthesis question
Write each step on its own line: the reagent, the conditions (including the solvent, and "reflux" or "distil" where they matter), and the name or formula of the intermediate. One mark is usually given for each reagent and each intermediate. If the question says "in two steps", do not use three. If it asks for "the type of reaction", use the exact term (nucleophilic substitution, electrophilic addition, elimination, oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis, condensation). Check carbon numbers against the question before you hand it in.
:::

Q: Give the reagent and conditions to convert (a) ethene to ethane-1,2-diol, (b) bromoethane to ethylamine, (c) propan-2-ol to propene.
A: (a) Cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\). (b) Excess \(\ce{NH3}\) in ethanol, heated under pressure. (c) Heated \(\ce{Al2O3}\), or concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) and heat.
Q: How many carbon atoms does the product have when 2-bromobutane reacts with KCN in ethanol? Name it.
A: Five. The product is 2-methylbutanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(CH3)CN}\): four carbons of the chain plus the carbon of CN.
Q: Name the type of reaction in each: (a) propene + HBr; (b) bromoethane + NaOH(aq); (c) propanal + HCN; (d) ethanol → ethene.
A: (a) electrophilic addition. (b) nucleophilic substitution. (c) nucleophilic addition. (d) elimination (dehydration).
Q: A student wants butan-2-one from but-1-ene in two steps. Suggest the steps.
A: But-1-ene with steam and \(\ce{H3PO4}\) gives butan-2-ol (the OH goes on the middle carbon because the secondary carbocation is more stable; see [Hydrocarbons: alkanes and alkenes](#chem.org.hydrocarbons)). Warm with acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) and distil to give butanone.
Q: A compound gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent and decolourises bromine water. Which two groups are present?
A: An aldehyde (silver mirror) and a C=C group (bromine water).
Q: Which of ethanol, propanone and ethanal turns warm acidified dichromate(VI) from orange to green? Explain.
A: Ethanol and ethanal. A primary alcohol and an aldehyde are oxidised, which turns orange \(\ce{Cr2O7^2-}\) to green \(\ce{Cr^3+}\). A ketone is not oxidised.
Q: Write an equation for the hydrolysis of propanenitrile with dilute hydrochloric acid.
A: \[\ce{CH3CH2CN + HCl + 2H2O -> CH3CH2COOH + NH4Cl}\]

A: (a) Cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\). [B1]
(b) Acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) (or \(\ce{KMnO4}\)), heated under reflux. [B1]

A: (a) \(\ce{NaBH4}\) or \(\ce{LiAlH4}\). [B1]
(b) \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether. [B1]
\(\ce{NaBH4}\) is not a strong enough reducing agent to reduce a carboxylic acid. [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ Propene is converted into propanone in two steps. Give the reagent and conditions for each step, and name the intermediate.
H: A ketone comes from a secondary alcohol. Which alcohol forms when water adds to propene?
A: Step 1: steam and \(\ce{H3PO4}\) catalyst. [B1]
The intermediate is propan-2-ol: the OH goes on the middle carbon, because the secondary carbocation is more stable. [B1]
Step 2: acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\) (or \(\ce{KMnO4}\)), heat and distil off the product. [B1]
The product is propanone, \(\ce{CH3COCH3}\). [B1]
Q: [5 marks] ★★ 1-Bromopropane is converted into compound C in three steps: step 1 NaOH(aq), heat, gives A; step 2 acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), heat and distil, gives B; step 3 HCN with KCN catalyst gives C. (a) Name A, B and C. (b) Name the mechanism of step 3. (c) Give the reagent and conditions that would convert 1-bromopropane into propene.
A: (a) A is propan-1-ol. [B1] B is propanal. [B1] C is 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH(OH)CN}\). [B1]
(b) Nucleophilic addition. [B1]
(c) NaOH in ethanol, heat. [B1]
Q: [6 marks] ★★ Three compounds J, K and L all have the formula \(\ce{C5H10O}\) and all give an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH. Only J gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent. Only K gives a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine. Identify the group present in all three, and suggest a structure for each of J, K and L.
H: K is a ketone with a \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) group; L is a ketone without one.
A: All three contain the C=O group of an aldehyde or ketone. [B1]
J is an aldehyde (silver mirror); for example pentanal, \(\ce{CH3CH2CH2CH2CHO}\). [B1] [B1]
K is a methyl ketone, \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) present: pentan-2-one, \(\ce{CH3COCH2CH2CH3}\) (or 3-methylbutan-2-one). [B1] [B1]
L is a ketone with no methyl group on the C=O: pentan-3-one, \(\ce{CH3CH2COCH2CH3}\). [B1]
Q: [4 marks] ★★ (#chem.moles) 7.40 g of butan-2-ol, \(\ce{C4H9OH}\), is oxidised to butanone, \(\ce{C4H8O}\), with a yield of 78.0 %. Calculate the mass of butanone formed.
H: Both compounds contain four carbon atoms and one mole of alcohol gives one mole of ketone.
A: \(M_r(\ce{C4H9OH}) = 74.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C4H8O}) = 72.0\). [B1]
\(n(\ce{C4H9OH}) = 7.40 / 74.0 = 0.1000\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
Theoretical mass \(= 0.1000 \times 72.0 = 7.20\,\mathrm{g}\) [M1]
Actual mass \(= 7.20 \times 0.780 = 5.62\,\mathrm{g}\) [A1]
Q: [7 marks] ★★★ Compound M is \(\ce{HOOCCH2CH=CHCH2CH2OH}\). State what you would observe, and give the organic product, when M reacts with (a) aqueous sodium carbonate, (b) bromine water, (c) \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether, followed by dilute acid, and (d) acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), heated under reflux.
H: List the three groups in M first: carboxylic acid, alkene, primary alcohol.
H: \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) reduces a carboxylic acid but leaves C=C alone; dichromate oxidises a primary alcohol but at this level leaves C=C alone.
A: (a) Effervescence (\(\ce{CO2}\)). [B1] The product is the sodium salt, \(\ce{NaOOCCH2CH=CHCH2CH2OH}\). [B1]
(b) The orange colour disappears, giving \(\ce{HOOCCH2CHBrCHBrCH2CH2OH}\). [B1]
(c) The carboxylic acid is reduced to a primary alcohol: \(\ce{HOCH2CH2CH=CHCH2CH2OH}\), hex-3-ene-1,6-diol. [B1] [B1]
(d) The primary alcohol is oxidised to a carboxylic acid: \(\ce{HOOCCH2CH=CHCH2COOH}\), hex-3-enedioic acid, and the solution turns from orange to green. [B1] [B1]
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ Devise a route from ethene to 2-hydroxypropanoic acid, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)COOH}\), in four steps. Give the reagents and conditions and name each intermediate, and name the mechanism of the step that adds the extra carbon.
H: The earlier worked example went from ethanal to the target in two steps. You need to make ethanal from ethene first.
H: Ethanal comes from a primary alcohol by distillation.
A: Step 1: ethene with steam and \(\ce{H3PO4}\) gives ethanol. [B1]
Step 2: ethanol with acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), heated, with the product distilled off as it forms [B1], gives ethanal. [B1]
Step 3: ethanal with HCN and KCN catalyst, heated [B1], gives 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)CN}\). [B1] The mechanism is nucleophilic addition. [B1]
Step 4: heat with dilute acid (hydrolysis) [B1] to give 2-hydroxypropanoic acid. [B1]

Q: [7 marks] ★★★ Butanedioic acid, \(\ce{HOOCCH2CH2COOH}\), can be made from ethene. (a) Suggest a three-step route with an intermediate that contains two bromine atoms, giving reagents and conditions, the intermediate and the type of reaction in each step. (b) A chemist needs 10.0 g of butanedioic acid and the overall yield of the route is 40.0 %. Calculate the mass of ethene needed.
H: The product has four carbons and two COOH groups. Each COOH comes from a CN group, and each CN group replaces a bromine atom.
H: Ethene with \(\ce{Br2}\) gives 1,2-dibromoethane. Hydrolysing a dinitrile makes two acid groups.
A: (a) Ethene with \(\ce{Br2}\) at room temperature gives 1,2-dibromoethane (electrophilic addition). [B1]
Heat under reflux with excess KCN in ethanol gives butanedinitrile, \(\ce{NCCH2CH2CN}\) (nucleophilic substitution, at both carbons). [B1]
Heat with dilute acid (hydrolysis) gives butanedioic acid. [B1]
(b) \(M_r(\ce{C4H6O4}) = 4(12.0) + 6(1.0) + 4(16.0) = 118.0\) and \(M_r(\ce{C2H4}) = 28.0\). [B1]
\(n(\text{acid}) = 10.0 / 118.0 = 0.08475\,\mathrm{mol}\) [M1]
Moles of ethene needed \(= 0.08475 / 0.400 = 0.2119\,\mathrm{mol}\), since one mole of ethene gives one mole of the acid. [M1]
Mass of ethene \(= 0.2119 \times 28.0 = 5.93\,\mathrm{g}\) [A1]

H: A diester has two ester groups, so two ethanoic acid molecules react with one molecule of a diol.
H: You must make both the diol and the ethanoic acid from ethene.
A: Diol: ethene with cold, dilute, acidified \(\ce{KMnO4}\) gives ethane-1,2-diol. [B1] [B1]

Esterification: ethane-1,2-diol with at least two moles of ethanoic acid per mole of diol, concentrated \(\ce{H2SO4}\) catalyst, heat under reflux. [B1]

:::quiz
? Which step in a route must be present if the target compound has more carbon atoms than the start?
- hydrolysis of an ester
- oxidation with acidified dichromate(VI)
+ a reaction with cyanide, such as KCN with a halogenoalkane or HCN with a carbonyl compound
- electrophilic addition of HBr to an alkene
> Only cyanide reactions (and esterification, which joins two molecules) increase the number of carbons in the AS course. Oxidation, hydrolysis and HBr addition keep the skeleton the same.
? A student plans to reduce propanoic acid to propan-1-ol with sodium tetrahydridoborate(III), \(\ce{NaBH4}\). What is wrong?
- The product would be propanal.
- It would also reduce any C=C in the molecule.
+ \(\ce{NaBH4}\) is too weak to reduce a carboxylic acid; \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) is needed.
- Reduction cannot form a primary alcohol.
> A carboxylic acid is reduced to a primary alcohol by \(\ce{LiAlH4}\) in dry ether. \(\ce{NaBH4}\) reduces only aldehydes and ketones.
? A route has three steps with yields of 90.0 %, 80.0 % and 50.0 %. What is the overall yield?
- 73.3 %
- 50.0 %
+ 36.0 %
- 220 %
> Multiply the fractions: \(0.900 \times 0.800 \times 0.500 = 0.360\). Averaging the yields (73.3 %) is a common error; yields multiply because each step starts with what the last step produced.
? Which test distinguishes propanal from propanone?
- 2,4-DNPH
- aqueous bromine
+ Tollens' reagent
- sodium carbonate solution
> Both compounds give an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH, and neither reacts with bromine water or carbonate. Only the aldehyde is oxidised by Tollens' reagent to give a silver mirror.
? A compound gives no colour change with warm acidified \(\ce{K2Cr2O7}\), but reacts with sodium metal to give a gas. What is it likely to be?
- a primary alcohol
- an aldehyde
+ a tertiary alcohol
- a ketone
> A tertiary alcohol is not oxidised, and it has an O–H bond, so it reacts with sodium to give hydrogen. A primary alcohol turns dichromate green; aldehydes and ketones do not react with sodium.
? Which compound gives a yellow precipitate with alkaline aqueous iodine?
- propan-1-ol
- propanal
+ propan-2-ol
- 2-methylpropan-2-ol
> Propan-2-ol contains \(\ce{CH3CH(OH)}{-}\), which is oxidised to \(\ce{CH3CO}{-}\) and then gives \(\ce{CHI3}\). Propan-1-ol, propanal and the tertiary alcohol do not have either group.
:::

:::recap
- A route is a chain of one-group changes. Plan it by counting carbons, comparing groups, working backwards from the target and checking that no reagent damages another group.
- The carbon count only increases with KCN on a halogenoalkane, HCN on a carbonyl compound, or esterification. It decreases by cleaving C=C with hot concentrated acidified KMnO₄ or by cracking.
- Oxidation: 1° alcohol → aldehyde (distil) → acid (reflux); 2° alcohol → ketone; 3° alcohol and ketones are not oxidised. Reduction: NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ for aldehydes and ketones; LiAlH₄ only for acids.
- Tests: bromine water (C=C), 2,4-DNPH (C=O), Tollens'/Fehling's (aldehyde only), warm acidified dichromate (1°/2° alcohol, aldehyde), alkaline iodine (CH₃CO– or CH₃CH(OH)–), carbonate (acid), sodium (O–H), AgNO₃ in ethanol (halogenoalkane).
- Name by-products specifically: the isomer, the over-oxidised product, the alkene from elimination, secondary amines, ammonium salts.
- Overall yield = product of the step yields.
:::
