## chem.lattice · Lattice energy, entropy and Gibbs free energy (`@time 8–10 hours`)
All of topic 23 (the old id chem.entropy now points here). Warm-up from [Chemical energetics](#chem.energetics)
(Hess's law), [Atomic structure](#chem.atoms) (IE) and [Chemical bonding](#chem.bonding). Outcomes (checklist):
- 23.1 1 define and use (a) enthalpy change of atomisation ΔHat (the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms is
  formed from the element in its standard state), (b) lattice energy ΔHlatt (the enthalpy change when one mole of an
  ionic compound is formed from its gaseous ions under standard conditions: the syllabus direction, gas ions → solid,
  so lattice energies are negative); 2 (a) define and use first electron affinity (the enthalpy change when one electron
  is added to each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms to form one mole of gaseous 1− ions), (b) factors affecting EA,
  (c) trends in EA of Group 16 and 17 (first EA exothermic, generally less exothermic down the group; F and O anomalies
  as small atoms with electron repulsion: say Cl's first EA is more exothermic than F's; second EA of O is endothermic,
  adding an electron to a negative ion); 3 construct and use Born–Haber cycles (+1 and +2 cations, −1 and −2 anions);
  4 calculations; 5 effect of ionic charge and radius on the magnitude of lattice energy (higher charge, smaller ions →
  more exothermic).
- 23.2 1 define and use ΔHhyd (the enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions dissolves in water to form an
  infinitely dilute solution) and ΔHsol (the enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid dissolves in water to form
  an infinitely dilute solution); 2 energy cycle linking ΔHsol, lattice energy and ΔHhyd: ΔHsol = −ΔHlatt + ΣΔHhyd;
  3 calculations; 4 effect of ionic charge and radius on ΔHhyd.
- 23.3 1 entropy S = the number of possible arrangements of the particles and their energy in a given system;
  2 sign of ΔS for (a) melting, boiling, dissolving and their reverse, (b) temperature rise, (c) a change in the number
  of gaseous molecules; 3 ΔS⦵ = ΣS⦵(products) − ΣS⦵(reactants) (units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; the ΔSsys + ΔSsurr form is not required).
- 23.4 1 ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵; 2 calculations (convert ΔS to kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: divide by 1000); 3 feasible when ΔG ≤ 0
  (Cambridge: a reaction is feasible when ΔG is negative); 4 effect of temperature on feasibility (the four sign
  combinations; T at which ΔG = 0: T = ΔH/ΔS).
Sections: why ionic compounds are stable; the definitions (each a `:::def`, with an equation and state symbols:
Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s)); a Born–Haber cycle for NaCl drawn as a level diagram (hand SVG `fig`, 600 × 380, COURSE.md
"Energy cycles": elements at the zero level, ΔHf down to NaCl(s), the upward steps ΔHat(Na), IE₁(Na), ΔHat(Cl), then
EA₁(Cl) down, lattice energy down); embed `<div class="widget" data-w="chem-bornhaber" data-compound="NaCl"></div>`;
data for cycles must be given in each question: use the Data-booklet IE values (FACTS.md) and, for other terms, standard
data-book values you are sure of or label them "data for this question" (the check script recomputes whatever you use);
MgO cycle (two IEs, two EAs: second EA endothermic); calculating lattice energy and calculating an unknown term;
lattice energy trends (NaCl vs MgO vs NaF vs KCl, qualitatively: charge and radius); hydration and solution cycles
(figure: a triangle cycle with gaseous ions at the top); worked ΔHsol; entropy (gases > liquids > solids; a figure
of particle arrangements; sign predictions); S⦵ data in questions (label "data for this question" unless sure);
Gibbs free energy and feasibility (a `:::plot` of ΔG against T as a straight line ΔG = ΔH − TΔS with the crossing at
T = ΔH/ΔS, e.g. thermal decomposition of CaCO₃ with ΔH = +178 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ given as data:
T ≈ 1106 K; check it); kinetic stability vs feasibility (a feasible reaction may be slow: high Eₐ).
Worked examples (≥8): lattice energy of NaCl from a cycle; first EA from a cycle; ΔHf of MgO; why MgO's lattice energy is
much more exothermic than NaCl's; ΔHsol of a salt from lattice energy and hydration enthalpies; predict signs of ΔS for
four changes; ΔS⦵ from standard entropies; minimum temperature for feasibility; link to Group 2 solubility
([Group 2](#chem.group2), A Level section).
Pitfalls: lattice energy sign (gas ions → solid is exothermic); using ½ΔHat(Cl₂) vs ΔHat(Cl) (atomisation is per mole
of atoms, so no halving needed when the value is ΔHat); forgetting two IEs for Mg²⁺; J vs kJ in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; T in °C;
"ΔG negative so the reaction is fast".
Board box: leave it out unless sure.

## chem.electrochem · Electrochemistry: electrolysis and electrode potentials (`@time 8–10 hours`)
Topic 24. Warm-up from [Redox and oxidation numbers](#chem.redox), [Moles and stoichiometry](#chem.moles) and the
Gibbs section of [Lattice energy, entropy and Gibbs free energy](#chem.lattice). Outcomes:
- 24.1 1 predict products of electrolysis from the state of the electrolyte (molten or aqueous), position in the redox
  series (E⦵) and concentration (e.g. concentrated NaCl(aq) gives Cl₂ at the anode, dilute gives O₂; H₂ at the cathode
  for reactive metals); 2 F = Le (6.022 × 10²³ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 9.64 × 10⁴, which agrees with the booklet's 9.65 × 10⁴ C mol⁻¹
  within the 3 s.f. precision of e; say so rather than claiming an exact match); 3 (a) Q = It, (b) mass and/or
  volume of substance liberated (moles of electrons = Q/F, then the half-equation ratio); 4 determination of L by an
  electrolytic method (electrolyse CuSO₄(aq) with copper electrodes, weigh the cathode gain, measure I and t: L = Q /
  (n(e⁻) × e)).
- 24.2 1 define standard electrode (reduction) potential (the potential of a half-cell relative to the standard
  hydrogen electrode under standard conditions: 298 K, 1 mol dm⁻³ ions, 101 kPa gases) and standard cell potential;
  2 the standard hydrogen electrode (H₂ at 101 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺, platinum electrode coated with platinum black,
  298 K); 3 measuring E⦵ of (a) metals or non-metals with their ions, (b) ions of the same element in different
  oxidation states (Pt electrode in a solution with both ions at 1.00 mol dm⁻³); 4 E⦵cell = E⦵(positive, reduction) −
  E⦵(negative); 5 (a) polarity and electron flow in the external circuit (from the more negative electrode), (b)
  feasibility (E⦵cell positive → feasible, but may be slow or concentrations non-standard); 6 relative strength as
  oxidising/reducing agents from E⦵; 7 redox equations from half-equations; 8 qualitative effect of concentration on E;
  9 Nernst: E = E⦵ + (0.059/z) log([oxidised]/[reduced]) (for a metal ion/metal, [reduced] = 1: E = E⦵ + (0.059/2) log[Cu²⁺]);
  10 ΔG⦵ = −nE⦵cell F (n = moles of electrons transferred; ΔG in J mol⁻¹, convert to kJ).
Sections: electrolysis recap and products (table for molten and aqueous electrolytes; selective discharge by E⦵ and
concentration); Faraday calculations (worked: mass of copper deposited by 0.500 A for 30.0 min; volume of O₂ at room
conditions); Avogadro determination as a `:::lab`; half-cells and the SHE (figure: hand SVG of a standard cell:
SHE on the left, Cu²⁺/Cu on the right, salt bridge (KNO₃(aq) filter paper), high-resistance voltmeter, labels;
COURSE.md apparatus rules); E⦵ table from FACTS.md (a selection as a Markdown table, ordered most negative to most
positive); cell potentials and electron flow; embed `<div class="widget" data-w="chem-cell" data-left="Zn" data-right="Cu"></div>`;
feasibility predictions with E⦵ (e.g. will Fe³⁺ oxidise I⁻? +0.77 vs +0.54: yes; will Cl₂ oxidise Br⁻?); limits
(rate, concentration); Nernst with worked numbers (Cu²⁺ at 0.0100 mol dm⁻³: E = 0.34 + 0.0295 × (−2) = +0.28 V; Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺
ratio 10 : 1: E = 0.77 + 0.059 = +0.83 V); ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵ (Daniell cell: E⦵ = 1.10 V, n = 2: ΔG⦵ = −212 kJ mol⁻¹; check).
Worked examples (≥9, a real context: e.g. electroplating or the lithium-ion battery idea described only qualitatively;
fuel cells are not in 9701): electrolysis products of three solutions; Faraday mass and gas volume; L from data;
E⦵cell and polarity for Zn/Cu and for Fe³⁺,Fe²⁺/I₂,I⁻; full redox equation from two half-equations (MnO₄⁻/Fe²⁺);
feasibility of Cu + H⁺; Nernst ★★; ΔG⦵ from E⦵cell ★★.
Pitfalls: E⦵cell = E(right) − E(left) applied to whichever is drawn right; adding E⦵ values after multiplying
half-equations by coefficients (never multiply E⦵); using z wrongly in Nernst; "the salt bridge carries electrons"
(it carries ions); Q in coulombs from minutes not converted to seconds.
Board box (sure): AQA includes commercial cells and fuel cells (such as the hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell), which 9701 does
not; use one board only.

## Widget `chem-bornhaber` (widgets/chem-bornhaber.js)
`<div class="widget" data-w="chem-bornhaber" data-compound="NaCl"></div>` (also `MgO`, `KCl`, `MgCl2`, `CaO`, `custom`).
- Editable fields (kJ mol⁻¹) for each step: ΔHf, ΔHat(metal), IE₁ (and IE₂ for 2+ cations; defaults from the Data
  booklet values in FACTS.md, generated or copied exactly), ΔHat(non-metal) per mole of atoms (× 2 for MCl₂), EA₁ (and
  EA₂ for 2− anions), lattice energy. A select chooses which term is unknown; the widget computes it by Hess's law.
- Defaults for the other terms: put the values you use in the chapter's worked example (and label them "example data:
  edit them to match your question"). Never claim they are official values unless they come from FACTS.md.
- Canvas level diagram drawn to scale (y-range follows the values), arrows up for endothermic and down for exothermic,
  each labelled with its name and value; the unknown highlighted; formulas with state symbols as plain text
  (Na(g), Na⁺(g) + e⁻ …).
- Probe defaults, `data-compound="MgO"`, `data-compound="custom"`, an empty field (friendly message).

## Widget `chem-cell` (widgets/chem-cell.js)
`<div class="widget" data-w="chem-cell" data-left="Zn" data-right="Cu"></div>`; `data-tab="nernst"` for the Nernst tab.
- **Data**: generate the half-cell list with Python by parsing the FACTS.md "E° / V" line (never type E⦵ values).
  Each entry: label (e.g. "Cu²⁺/Cu"), half-equation text, E⦵, z, and whether it is metal/ion, gas, or two ions (Pt).
- Tab "Cell": two selects (left, right half-cell). Draw the cell (two beakers, electrodes, salt bridge, voltmeter) on
  canvas with labels; mark + and − electrodes, the direction of electron flow in the wire, E⦵cell = E⦵(+) − E⦵(−)
  (2 d.p., V), the overall equation (balanced by the electrons), ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵cell (kJ mol⁻¹, 3 s.f.) and "feasible".
- Tab "Nernst": choose a half-cell with z and concentrations ([oxidised], [reduced]; for metal/ion the [reduced] box is
  fixed at 1), slider over 10⁻⁴ to 10 mol dm⁻³ (log scale), E = E⦵ + (0.059/z) log([ox]/[red]) to 3 d.p., and a small
  plot of E against log[ox].
- Probe defaults, `data-left="Cu" data-right="Cu"` (E = 0, note "same half-cell"), `data-tab="nernst"`, an unknown label.
