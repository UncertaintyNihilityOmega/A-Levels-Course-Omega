## What the subject is, and who it suits

Computer Science is the study of how to solve problems with computers: how to describe a problem exactly, how to write the steps that solve it, and how the machines and networks that run those steps work. The course has three strands.

- **Problem solving.** You break a problem into smaller parts, find a method (an algorithm) and describe it precisely, in pseudocode or a flowchart.
- **Programming.** You turn that method into a working program and test it. This course teaches Python.
- **How computers work.** Data representation, hardware, the processor, networks, the internet, operating systems, security, databases and the ethics of technology.

It suits students who enjoy logic puzzles, patient step-by-step reasoning and finding why something does not work. You do not need to be good at advanced mathematics: there is no calculator in any paper, and the numbers are always small enough to work by hand.

You do not need any programming experience. This course starts from zero in [Programming from zero: Python and pseudocode](#cs.f.basics). Cambridge recommends previous study of IGCSE or O Level Computer Science but does not require it.

:::tip The most important habit
Write code every day, even for ten minutes. Reading about programming teaches you the vocabulary; only writing programs teaches you to program. Two of the four papers test this directly.
:::

## The qualification and its four papers

This course follows the Cambridge International AS & A Level Computer Science **9618** syllabus for exams in 2027, 2028 and 2029. There are four papers. Calculators must not be used in any of them.

| Paper | Title | Time | Marks | Syllabus sections | AS weighting | A Level weighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Theory Fundamentals | 1 h 30 min | 75 | 1–8 | 50% | 25% |
| 2 | Fundamental Problem-solving and Programming Skills | 2 h | 75 | 9–12 | 50% | 25% |
| 3 | Advanced Theory | 1 h 30 min | 75 | 13–20 | none | 25% |
| 4 | Practical | 2 h 30 min | 75 | 19–20, except low-level and declarative programming | none | 25% |

How each paper is answered:

- **Paper 1** is written on the question paper. The questions are built on short scenarios (a shop, a video-conference company, a database for a furniture company). Where a question uses assembly language, the paper prints the part of the instruction set it needs, so you never memorise the whole instruction set.
- **Paper 2** is written on the question paper, in **pseudocode**: you are not asked to write programming code. A separate **insert** lists the built-in functions and operators (such as `MID`, `LENGTH`, `DIV` and `MOD`), and the front page of the paper says you will need it.
- **Paper 3** is written on the question paper. It covers the advanced theory: floating point, protocols, Boolean algebra, operating systems, encryption, artificial intelligence, and the design of data structures and classes.
- **Paper 4** is taken **on a computer** with no internet or email. You write and test real programs in Python, Java or Visual Basic (console mode) and paste your code and evidence of testing into an evidence document. This course teaches Python. A mark of zero is given if another language is used than the three allowed.

:::exam Two rules printed on the papers
"No marks will be awarded for using brand names of software packages or hardware." Write "a word processor" or "a laptop computer", never a product name. And Papers 1, 2 and 3 all print: calculators must not be used.
:::

### AS Level, A Level and when you sit the exams

- **AS Level** is Papers 1 and 2 only. It is graded a to e. About 180 guided learning hours.
- **A Level** is all four papers, graded A* to E. About 360 guided learning hours.
- Three routes: take the AS only; take the A Level in stages (AS first, with its marks carried forward, then the rest a year later); or take the whole A Level in one series.
- There are two exam series each year, June and November.

AS and A Level share the same AS papers, so the AS chapters of this course are the foundation of both.

## Assessment objectives

Every mark in every paper is awarded for one of three **assessment objectives**.

- **AO1: knowledge and understanding.** Recall facts and explain ideas ("State the purpose of the MAR").
- **AO2: application.** Analyse a problem in computational terms and apply knowledge to a new scenario ("A video-conference company wants … Explain why it should use …").
- **AO3: design, programming and evaluation.** Design, write, test and evaluate programs and systems.

| | AO1 | AO2 | AO3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| AS Level overall | 30% | 40% | 30% |
| A Level overall | 30% | 30% | 40% |
| Paper 1 | 60% | 40% | none |
| Paper 2 | none | 40% | 60% |
| Paper 3 | 60% | 40% | none |
| Paper 4 | none | none | 100% |

The pattern to notice: the two theory papers (1 and 3) are 60% recall and 40% applying that knowledge to a scenario, and the two programming papers (2 and 4) test whether you can design, write and test. Paper 4 is entirely programming.

## Board differences

This course is for Cambridge 9618. If a friend studies a different board, these two facts help you understand how the others differ.

:::board
Cambridge 9618 has no coursework: programming is tested in Paper 2 (pseudocode on paper) and Paper 4 (on a computer, in the exam). AQA A Level Computer Science (7517) and OCR A Level Computer Science (H446) each include a non-exam assessment, a programming project. AQA's Paper 1 is taken on screen with a skeleton program released before the exam.
:::

## Study order and pace

The chapters are in teaching order, and each one assumes only the chapters before it, so working from the top works. A better plan for AS is to alternate theory and programming:

1. Start with [Programming from zero](#cs.f.basics) and do not move on until you can write the two small programs at its end without help.
2. Then take one AS theory chapter, then one AS programming chapter, and repeat. Theory is memory and explanation; programming is a skill. Alternating gives each its own practice time every week.
3. Program a little every day. Twenty minutes a day beats a three-hour session once a week.
4. For the A Level, finish the AS chapters first, then A Level theory and A Level programming side by side, and use the Python chapter for Paper 4 as soon as you have started the A Level programming chapters.

The syllabus is about 180 guided learning hours for AS and 360 for the whole A Level. At 6 hours a week, that is about 30 weeks for the AS content and 60 for the A Level, so a one-year AS or a two-year A Level course is realistic. Plan backwards from your exam series and leave the last six to eight weeks for past papers.

## How to reach A*

1. **Write code every day.** In Paper 2 you write pseudocode and in Paper 4 you write Python. Both are skills.
2. **Learn the pseudocode guide.** Paper 2 and Paper 3 answers must follow its layout: keywords in capitals, `←` for assignment, three-space indents. Every pseudocode block in this course is written in that style.
3. **Dry-run with trace tables.** Pseudocode that traces correctly on paper is pseudocode you understand. Trace tables appear in the first programming chapter and in almost every chapter after it.
4. **Use the exact term.** Theory marks are awarded for the precise word: "The **MAR** holds the address …", not "a part of the computer".
5. **Do past papers under time.** Do them in the order in the paper guides, mark your own work against the mark scheme and fix every lost mark.
6. **Read the mark points.** Cambridge mark schemes give one mark per point. In this course every model answer shows its mark points as `[B1]`, so you learn to write one clear point per mark.

### Command words

The first word of a question tells you what to write. These are the Cambridge meanings.

| Command word | What the examiner wants |
|---|---|
| State | express the answer in clear terms: a short answer, no reasons |
| Identify | name, select or recognise the item |
| Describe | state the points, giving the characteristics and main features |
| Explain | set out the purposes or reasons: say why or how, with evidence |
| Justify | support a case with evidence from the scenario |
| Complete | add to a diagram or table that is given |
| Write | write the answer in a specific way (for example in pseudocode) |
| Draw | in matching questions, draw a line to match a term with its description |

:::warn Describe is not Explain
"Describe the cache" needs what it is and does. "Explain why a cache speeds up the computer" needs reasons. Writing a description when the question says explain loses the marks, however accurate it is.
:::
