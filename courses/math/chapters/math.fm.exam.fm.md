:::why
Paper 3 is the applied Further Mathematics paper that builds on 9709 Mechanics: projectiles, rigid bodies, elastic strings, circular motion, variable forces and collisions. It is 40% of an AS and 20% of an A Level. It is short (90 minutes, 50 marks), so there is little slack, and its questions depend on a clear diagram and a consistent set of signs. A plan for the time and a routine for the diagram turn what you know into marks.

## What the front page says

The June 2025 (9231/31) and June 2026 front pages are the model:

- You write your answers on the question paper, in the space provided, and use the extra lined page at the end (with the question number) if you need more. The specimen paper for examination from 2027 says answers go in a separate printed answer booklet, so read your own paper's instructions.
- Black or dark blue pen, with an HB pencil for diagrams and graphs; no erasable pen or correction fluid.
- The list of formulae MF19 is provided.
- Use a calculator where appropriate, but show all necessary working: **no marks are given for unsupported answers from a calculator**.
- Give non-exact answers to 3 significant figures, or 1 decimal place for angles in degrees, unless the question specifies otherwise.
- **Where a numerical value for the acceleration due to gravity is needed, use \(10\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\).** Using 9.8 or 9.81 changes your answer in the third significant figure and can lose the accuracy mark.

:::exam The value of g
Paper 3 tells you to use \(g = 10\). Write "\(g = 10\)" in your first line of working so that it is visible, and do not switch to 9.8 because a textbook or another exam board does. If a question gives answers "in terms of \(g\)" or "in terms of \(a\) and \(g\)", keep the symbol: the number appears only when a numerical value is asked for.
:::

## A timing plan

The paper gives 90 minutes for 50 marks, which is **1.8 minutes per mark**. Plan for **1.6 minutes per mark on a first pass**: 80 minutes for the whole paper, leaving about 10 minutes to return to gaps and to check.

| Question size | Marks | Time at 1.6 min per mark |
|---|---|---|
| a short question | 5 | about 8 minutes |
| a medium question | 6 | about 10 minutes |
| a medium to long question | 7 | about 11 minutes |
| a long question | 9 | about 14 minutes |
| the longest question seen in 2025 | 10 | about 16 minutes |
| the whole paper | 50 | about 80 minutes, leaving 10 to check |

Rules of thumb:

1. **Read first, for 2 minutes.** Mechanics questions are verbal: a good picture of each one before you start saves time inside it.
2. **Draw before you write.** A large diagram with every force, the positive direction and the unknowns takes a minute and prevents most lost marks. Two-part questions often reuse it.
3. **Split the marks as you go.** A 4-mark part is two ideas (an equation and its solution); a 9-mark question is about three. If you have used twice the time in the table, leave a gap.
4. **Use "show that" results.** A given result such as \(v^2 = \dots\) can be used in the next part even if you could not prove it.
5. **Check units and reasonableness in the last 10 minutes:** a speed of \(300\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) for a ball, a tension that is negative, an angle above \(90^\circ\) for a projectile.

## Formulas: what MF19 gives you and what you must learn

**You are given** (MF19) for Paper 3:
- the trajectory equation \(y = x\tan\theta - \dfrac{gx^2}{2V^2\cos^2\theta}\);
- Hooke's law \(T = \dfrac{\lambda x}{l}\) and the elastic energy \(E = \dfrac{\lambda x^2}{2l}\);
- the acceleration of circular motion, \(r\omega^2\) or \(\dfrac{v^2}{r}\), towards the centre;
- the centres of mass: a triangular lamina (two thirds of the way along the median from the vertex), a solid hemisphere (\(\tfrac38r\) from the centre), a hemispherical shell (\(\tfrac12r\)), a circular arc (\(\dfrac{r\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\)), a sector (\(\dfrac{2r\sin\alpha}{3\alpha}\), angle \(2\alpha\)) and a solid cone or pyramid (\(\tfrac34h\) from the vertex);
- the four constant-acceleration formulae from 9709 Mechanics (\(v = u + at\), \(s = \tfrac12(u + v)t\), \(s = ut + \tfrac12at^2\), \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\)).

**You must learn** (not on MF19): the friction law \(F \le \mu R\); \(W = mg\); kinetic and gravitational potential energy; work done and the work-energy principle; \(P = Fv\); momentum and impulse; Newton's laws; \(v = r\omega\) and the period \(\dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}\); the moment of a force; the conditions for a vertical circle (tension non-negative, normal reaction non-negative); Newton's experimental law \(v_2 - v_1 = e(u_1 - u_2)\); the method for oblique impact (the component along the line of centres obeys the law, the perpendicular one is unchanged); \(v\dfrac{dv}{dx}\) as the acceleration; and the formula for combining centres of mass, \(\bar x = \dfrac{\sum mx}{\sum m}\).

:::exam Quote, then use
MF19 gives the trajectory equation, Hooke's law, elastic energy and the standard centres of mass. Quote the formula you use ("\(E = \dfrac{\lambda x^2}{2l}\)") and substitute straight away. The marks are for the equation you set up, and the equation is what connects the formula to the question.
:::

## Diagrams, signs and modelling words

Almost every lost mark in Mechanics starts in the diagram. This is the routine:

1. **Draw large.** A diagram that fills a quarter of a page leaves room for forces.
2. **Draw every force**, including the ones you think are small: weight, normal reaction, friction (opposing motion or the tendency to move), tension, thrust. Do not draw the resultant and its components together.
3. **State the positive direction** by an arrow in the diagram. Unknown velocities after an impact are drawn in the positive direction, so a negative answer means "the other way".
4. **Mark the lengths and angles** you use, such as the radius \(l\sin\theta\), the natural length, the extension, the distances to a centre of mass.
5. **Write the equation in words, then in symbols**: "resolving along the string:", "moments about \(A\):", "conservation of momentum:". The words earn the method mark when the symbols have a slip.

The modelling words in the questions have exact meanings:

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| particle | a point with mass: no size, so no rotation |
| smooth | no friction |
| light | no mass: the tension is the same all along a light string |
| inextensible | the length does not change (so no EPE) |
| elastic string | obeys Hooke's law when taut, and has no tension when slack |
| just taut | tension is zero but the string is at its natural length |
| limiting equilibrium | friction has its maximum value, \(F = \mu R\) |
| about to topple | the normal reaction acts at the edge of the base |
| leaves the surface | the contact force is zero |
| perfectly elastic | \(e = 1\) (no energy lost in the impact) |

## Setting out the standard models

Two layouts that cover a lot of the paper. The first is the vertical circle with the string going slack (the shape of the 2026 question 5), the second an elastic string with a falling particle (the shape of the 2026 question 6).

:::eg (Exam style) A particle \(P\) of mass \(0.4\ \mathrm{kg}\) is attached to one end of a light inextensible string of length \(0.9\ \mathrm{m}\). The other end is fixed at \(O\). \(P\) is held at rest vertically below \(O\) and then projected horizontally with speed \(\sqrt{27}\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). When the string makes an angle \(\theta\) with the downward vertical and is taut, \(P\) has speed \(v\). Take \(g = 10\). (a) Show that the tension in the string is \(4(1 + 3\cos\theta)\) newtons. (b) The string goes slack at the point where \(\theta\) is obtuse. Find the speed of \(P\) at that instant.
(a) Energy: the height gained is \(0.9(1 - \cos\theta)\), so
\[\tfrac12(0.4)v^2 = \tfrac12(0.4)(27) - 0.4(10)(0.9)(1 - \cos\theta)\] [M1]
\[v^2 = 27 - 18(1 - \cos\theta) = 9 + 18\cos\theta\] [A1]
Resolving along the string towards \(O\):
\[T - 0.4(10)\cos\theta = \frac{0.4\,v^2}{0.9}\] [M1]
\[\begin{aligned}
T &= 4\cos\theta + \frac{0.4(9 + 18\cos\theta)}{0.9} \\
&= 4\cos\theta + 4 + 8\cos\theta \\
&= 4(1 + 3\cos\theta)
\end{aligned}\] [A1]
(b) The string is slack when \(T = 0\), so \(\cos\theta = -\tfrac13\). [B1]
Then \(v^2 = 9 + 18\left(-\tfrac13\right) = 3\). [M1]
The speed is \(\sqrt3 = 1.73\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
:::

Notice: the mass stays in the first lines and cancels only when you simplify, the sign of \(\cos\theta\) comes from one fixed convention (angle from the downward vertical), and the condition for slack, \(T = 0\), is stated.

:::eg (Exam style) A light elastic string has natural length \(0.6\ \mathrm{m}\) and modulus of elasticity \(24\ \mathrm{N}\). One end is fixed at \(O\) and a particle of mass \(0.5\ \mathrm{kg}\) is attached to the other end. The particle is released from rest at \(O\) and falls vertically. Take \(g = 10\). When the extension of the string is \(x\) metres, the speed of the particle is \(v\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). (a) Show that \(v^2 = 12 + 20x - 80x^2\). (b) Find the greatest speed. (c) Find the greatest extension.
(a) Energy, from \(O\) to the point where the extension is \(x\): the particle has fallen \(0.6 + x\).
\[\tfrac12(0.5)v^2 = 0.5(10)(0.6 + x) - \frac{24x^2}{2(0.6)}\] [M1] [A1]
\[\begin{aligned}
0.25v^2 &= 3 + 5x - 20x^2 \\
v^2 &= 12 + 20x - 80x^2
\end{aligned}\] [A1]
(b) The speed is greatest where the acceleration is zero, that is where the tension equals the weight:
\[\frac{24x}{0.6} = 0.5(10)\] [M1]
so \(x = 0.125\). [A1]
Then \(v^2 = 12 + 2.5 - 1.25 = 13.25\), so the greatest speed is \(3.64\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). [A1]
(c) The greatest extension is where \(v = 0\): \(80x^2 - 20x - 12 = 0\), that is \(20x^2 - 5x - 3 = 0\). [M1]
\[x = \frac{5 + \sqrt{265}}{40} = 0.532\ \mathrm{m}\] [A1]
(the negative root is rejected because \(x \ge 0\).) 
:::

Notice: the fall is \(l + x\), not \(x\), because the particle starts at the fixed end; EPE is counted only once the string is taut; and (b) uses either equilibrium or the derivative \(\dfrac{d(v^2)}{dx} = 0\), which give the same \(x\).

## The marks students lose most often

These come from the warning boxes in the Paper 3 chapters.

- **Projectiles:** giving only one of the two angles when the target can be reached twice, and not checking that the other root is in range.
- **Centres of mass:** using the heights of the parts as if they were masses; measuring from different points; the cone's centre of mass is \(\tfrac34h\) from the vertex (MF19), which is \(\tfrac14h\) from the base; the slip is to measure \(\tfrac14h\) from the vertex.
- **Rigid bodies:** using the length of the ladder where a perpendicular distance is needed (for a ladder of length 6 m at angle \(\theta\) to the ground, the smooth wall's reaction acts at height \(6\sin\theta\) above the ground, not 6); finding only one critical value and not saying which of topple or slide happens first.
- **Elastic strings:** writing the length where the extension belongs; counting EPE when the string is slack; forgetting the natural length in the drop (a particle falling from the fixed end falls \(l + x\)).
- **Horizontal circles:** an outward "centrifugal force" in the equation; the radius is \(l\sin\theta\), not \(l\).
- **Vertical circles:** the wrong height (\(r(1 - \cos\theta)\), and \(2r\) at the top); two different angle conventions in one solution.
- **Variable force:** writing \(m\dfrac{dv}{dx} = F\) and losing the \(v\); the sign of a resistance; assuming the particle turns round when the force changes sign.
- **Direct impact:** writing the law the wrong way round so the separation is negative; using conservation of energy in a collision with \(e < 1\).
- **Oblique impact:** the angle measured from the wall or from the normal (the components swap); writing "speed after \(= e\times\) speed before"; applying the experimental law to speeds instead of components along the line of centres.

:::warn Sign and unit slips
Many "wrong answer" marks in mechanics are signs and units. Check that a tension is positive, a speed is positive, a time is positive, and that \(g = 10\) and the SI units (metres, kilograms, seconds) are used throughout, with kilometres per hour and grams converted first.
:::

## Reading the marks

Mark schemes for 9231 use M1, A1, B1, DM1 and DB1 (marks that depend on an earlier starred method or independent mark), A1 FT and B1 FT (follow-through from your own earlier wrong value), and abbreviations such as SOI (seen or implied), AWRT (answer which rounds to), OE (or equivalent) and ISW (ignore subsequent working). Marks are awarded positively and are not deducted for errors, and wrong working after a correct answer is ignored, so write the answer clearly, in the form the question asks for, and stop. A "show that" question gives the final answer, so the marks go to the lines of working that lead to it.

## How to practise

1. **By topic, once.** Finish the chapter practice for each topic, with a diagram on every question.
2. **Mixed.** Use [Mixed practice: Further Mechanics and Statistics](#math.fm.mixed.applied) to practise choosing the model.
3. **One timed paper.** Take [Mock Paper 3: Further Mechanics](#math.fm.mock.p3) in one sitting of 1 hour 30 minutes, with your calculator and MF19 only. Mark it line by line.
4. **Real papers.** Use the 9231 Paper 3 past papers (the links box) after the chapters are done, one at a time, timed and marked with the mark scheme.
5. **Error log.** For each lost mark note the topic and the slip (a diagram, a sign, a missing term in an energy equation). Redo the question a week later.

## Final week

:::checklist Final week
- [ ] I have done at least two full timed Paper 3 papers in the last 14 days, and marked them with the mark scheme
- [ ] I can solve a projectile problem with the trajectory equation, find both angles and the speed and direction at a point
- [ ] I can find the centre of mass of a composite body from the MF19 results, measuring all distances from one point

- [ ] I can use energy and the radial equation for a vertical circle, and find where the string goes slack or the contact stops

- [ ] I can use conservation of momentum and Newton's experimental law for a direct impact, including successive impacts
- [ ] I can resolve along and perpendicular to the line of centres for an oblique impact with a wall or another sphere
- [ ] I know which formulas MF19 gives (the trajectory, Hooke's law, EPE, circular acceleration, centres of mass) and which it does not
- [ ] I use \(g = 10\) and draw a labelled diagram on every question
