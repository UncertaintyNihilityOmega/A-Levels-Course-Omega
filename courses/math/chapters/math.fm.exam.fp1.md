:::why
Paper 1 is the compulsory paper. It is 60% of an AS in Further Mathematics and 30% of an A Level, and Paper 2 assumes everything in it. It is also the paper that asks you to write proofs and to write long, careful working for 13 to 16 marks at a time. ## What the front page says

The front page of the June 2025 Paper 1 (9231/11) is the model; the June 2026 Papers 2, 3 and 4 say the same on these points. In summary:

- You write your answers on the question paper, in the space provided. If you run out of space, use the extra lined page at the end and write the question number clearly. For examinations from 2027 the specimen paper says answers go in a separate printed answer booklet, including sketches, so read your own paper's instructions carefully.
- Use a black or dark blue pen, and an HB pencil for diagrams or graphs. Do not use an erasable pen or correction fluid.
- The list of formulae MF19 is provided. Nothing else is printed: there is no separate booklet of tables for this paper.
- You should use a calculator where appropriate, but you must show all necessary working: **no marks are given for unsupported answers from a calculator**.
- Give non-exact numerical answers to 3 significant figures, or 1 decimal place for angles in degrees, unless the question specifies otherwise.

:::exam Unsupported calculator answers earn nothing
A calculator answer with no working shows nothing about method. Write the equation you solve, the values you substitute and the line before the final number. If a question says "show that", every line that leads to the printed answer must be on the page. If it says "exact", answer with fractions, surds, \(\pi\) or logarithms, never a decimal.
:::

## A timing plan

The paper gives 120 minutes for 75 marks, which is **1.6 minutes per mark**. Plan for **1.4 minutes per mark on a first pass**, so that the whole paper takes 105 minutes and you keep about 15 minutes to return to questions you skipped and to check.

| Question size | Marks | Time at 1.4 min per mark |
|---|---|---|
| a short question | 5 | about 7 minutes |
| a medium question | 8 | about 11 minutes |
| a medium to long question | 9 | about 13 minutes |
| a long question | 13 | about 18 minutes |
| a long question | 15 | about 21 minutes |
| the longest question seen in 2025 | 16 | about 22 minutes |
| the whole paper | 75 | about 105 minutes, leaving 15 to check |

Rules of thumb for the paper:

1. **Spend the first 3 minutes reading.** Read every question stem and the parts. Mark the ones you can start at once. The paper is not in order of difficulty, so a later question may be easier for you than an earlier one.
2. **Do your strongest topics first** if that settles you, but come back to every question: a missed question costs far more than a long one.
3. **Stop when you have used about twice the planned time in the table** (for example 14 minutes on a 5-mark question) on a question you cannot finish. Leave a gap, write "see later" and move on. Method marks are awarded for a correct start, so write the first lines of any question you attempt.
4. **Use "hence" and "deduce".** These words say that the next part uses the previous one. If you cannot do a part, but a later part says "hence", use the result printed in the earlier part: when it is a "show that" result, you may use it even if you could not prove it.
5. **Keep 15 minutes** for returning to gaps, and for the checks listed in the final-week checklist (a determinant by a second method, a root by substitution, \(n = 1\) in a series).

## What the questions looked like

The June 2025 paper (9231/11) had 7 questions worth 8, 7, 7, 9, 13, 16 and 15 marks, which is 75. They were, in order:

| Question | Marks | Topic of the question |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 8 | series: standard results from MF19, and a method of differences sum |
| 2 | 7 | roots of a cubic: a substitution to find a new cubic, then sums of powers |
| 3 | 7 | induction on a recurrence (5 marks), then a deduction (2 marks) |
| 4 | 9 | a matrix as two transformations, then the line of invariant points |
| 5 | 13 | a polar spiral: sketch, area, then a numerical check |
| 6 | 16 | three-dimensional planes: equation of a plane, angle between planes, distance between skew lines |
| 7 | 15 | a rational function: asymptotes, stationary points, sketches and an inequality |

The specimen paper (for examination from 2027) also had 7 questions worth 75: a method of differences with a deduced infinite sum, induction on a recurrence, a polar curve (sketch, area, a Cartesian form), cubic roots with sums of cubes and fourth powers, a singular matrix with invariant lines, a vector question with skew lines and a plane, and a rational function with a related sketch and an inequality.

Two things to take from this, and only two. First, the questions are **several linked parts** and the long ones are worth 13 to 16 marks, so working has to be set out and carried forward. Second, the topics a paper contains change from series to series, and these two papers are not a forecast: every chapter of the syllabus can appear, so revise all of them.

## Formulas: what MF19 gives you and what you must learn

**You are given** (MF19) for Paper 1: \(\sum r\), \(\sum r^2\) and \(\sum r^3\) in closed form; the binomial expansion, arithmetic and geometric series formulae from 9709; the trigonometric identities; the derivatives and standard integrals of 9709 (including \(\dfrac{1}{x^2 + a^2}\), \(\dfrac{1}{a^2 - x^2}\) and integration by parts); and the scalar product \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b} = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta\).

**You must learn** (not on MF19):
- the relations between roots and coefficients for quadratics, cubics and quartics, and the substitution method for a new equation;
- the determinant and inverse of a 2 × 2 and a 3 × 3 matrix, and \((\mathbf{AB})^{-1} = \mathbf{B}^{-1}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\);
- the matrix of each standard transformation (or, better, how to build it from the images of \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\)), and that \(|\det \mathbf{M}|\) is the area scale factor;
- the polar-Cartesian conversions and the polar area \(\tfrac12\int r^2\,d\theta\) (the syllabus says "recall");
- the vector product \(\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}\) in components and as \(|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\sin\theta\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}\), the three forms of the equation of a plane, and the distance and angle formulae for lines and planes;
- the layout of an induction proof, and the method of differences (it is a method, not a formula).

:::exam Look in MF19 before you try to remember
MF19 holds the three sums \(\sum r\), \(\sum r^2\) and \(\sum r^3\). Use it, and spend your memory on what it leaves out. It never tells you when to use a formula: that is the skill the questions test, and the one this guide and the chapters train.
:::

## Setting out proofs and "show that" results

Paper 1 is the paper where **written reasoning earns the marks**. The mark schemes for the induction question award the base case, the assumption, the inductive step and the conclusion as separate marks (B1 for the base case, B1 for the assumption, then M1 A1 for the step and A1 for the conclusion). A solution that proves the right thing but leaves out "assume true for \(n = k\)" or the final sentence loses marks it was close to getting. This is the layout, applied to a recurrence:

:::eg (Exam style) The sequence \(u_1, u_2, \ldots\) is defined by \(u_1 = 5\) and \(u_{n+1} = 2u_n + 3\). Prove by induction that \(u_n = 2^{n+2} - 3\) for all positive integers \(n\).
**Base case**, \(n = 1\): \(2^{1+2} - 3 = 8 - 3 = 5 = u_1\). So the result is true for \(n = 1\). [B1]

**Assumption**: assume the result is true for \(n = k\), that is \(u_k = 2^{k+2} - 3\). [B1]

**Inductive step**: the target is \(u_{k+1} = 2^{k+3} - 3\). Use the recurrence and then the assumption:
\[\begin{aligned}
u_{k+1} &= 2u_k + 3 \\
&= 2\left(2^{k+2} - 3\right) + 3 \end{aligned}\] [M1]
\[\begin{aligned}
&= 2^{k+3} - 6 + 3 \\
&= 2^{k+3} - 3
\end{aligned}\] [A1]
This is the formula with \(n = k + 1\).

**Conclusion**: true for \(n = 1\), and if true for \(n = k\) then true for \(n = k + 1\). So by induction it is true for all positive integers \(n\). [A1]
:::

Habits that keep these marks:

- Write the target for \(n = k + 1\) before the working, so that you know where the algebra must end.
- The assumption must be **visible in the first line of the step**. If a step could be done without it, it is not an induction step.
- The last sentence names the base case, the step and "for all positive integers \(n\)". When a divisibility result is asked, write the expression as a multiple of the divisor (as in \(6(7m + 1)\)).
- If a question has a part after the induction (a "deduce"), it uses the proved formula: do not redo the induction.

The method of differences has its own layout. Show the partial fractions, write the terms for the first few and the last few values of \(r\), cross out what cancels, and say what remains. The count of surviving terms is where marks are lost.

:::eg (Exam style) Show that \(\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{n}\frac{1}{r(r + 2)} = \frac34 - \frac{2n + 3}{2(n + 1)(n + 2)}\). Hence find the sum to infinity.
Partial fractions: \(\dfrac{1}{r(r + 2)} = \dfrac12\left(\dfrac1r - \dfrac{1}{r + 2}\right)\). [B1]

Write out the terms:
\[\begin{aligned}
r = 1: &\quad \tfrac12\left(1 - \tfrac13\right) \\
r = 2: &\quad \tfrac12\left(\tfrac12 - \tfrac14\right) \\
r = 3: &\quad \tfrac12\left(\tfrac13 - \tfrac15\right) \\
&\ \ \vdots \\
r = n - 1: &\quad \tfrac12\left(\tfrac{1}{n - 1} - \tfrac{1}{n + 1}\right) \\
r = n: &\quad \tfrac12\left(\tfrac1n - \tfrac{1}{n + 2}\right)
\end{aligned}\] [M1]
The terms \(\tfrac13, \tfrac14, \ldots, \tfrac1n\) cancel in pairs. Four terms survive, because the gap is 2:
\[S_n = \tfrac12\left(1 + \tfrac12 - \tfrac{1}{n + 1} - \tfrac{1}{n + 2}\right)\] [A1]
Combine the last two fractions:
\[\begin{aligned}
S_n &= \tfrac34 - \tfrac12\cdot\frac{(n + 2) + (n + 1)}{(n + 1)(n + 2)} \\
&= \tfrac34 - \frac{2n + 3}{2(n + 1)(n + 2)}
\end{aligned}\] [A1]
As \(n \to \infty\), \(\dfrac{2n + 3}{2(n + 1)(n + 2)} \to 0\), so the sum to infinity is \(\dfrac34\). [B1]
:::

Check with \(n = 1\): the formula gives \(\tfrac34 - \tfrac{5}{12} = \tfrac13\), and the sum is \(\tfrac{1}{1 \times 3} = \tfrac13\). Always keep the \(n = 1\) check for the margin.

## Layouts for the other question types

| Type | What the working should show |
|---|---|
| Roots of an equation | write \(\sum\alpha\), \(\sum\alpha\beta\), \(\alpha\beta\gamma\) with the signs from the coefficients before anything else; every "sum of squares" is built from them; for a substitution say which new variable you use |
| Sketch of a rational function | the equations of the asymptotes (with the division shown for an oblique one), the axis crossings, the turning points, and which side of each asymptote the curve lies |
| Set of values by a discriminant | rearrange to a quadratic in \(x\), say that \(x\) is real so \(b^2 - 4ac \ge 0\), solve the inequality in \(y\), and test any special value of \(y\) by substitution |
| Matrix and transformation | state the images of \((1, 0)\) and \((0, 1)\), then the matrix; test a product on a point; for invariant lines, say which type you are finding |
| Polar curve and area | tabulate \(r\) at key angles, sketch for \(r \ge 0\), state the limits that cover the curve once, use radians |
| Vectors and planes | a normal from \(\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2\), the plane through a point in the form \(\mathbf{r} \cdot \mathbf{n} = p\); check the result by substituting a point of the plane |
| Skew-line distance | the direction \(\mathbf{b}_1 \times \mathbf{b}_2\), a vector between two points, and the scalar product with the unit normal |

## The marks students lose most often

These come from the warning boxes in the Paper 1 chapters.

- **Roots:** the sum and the product carry a minus sign and the pairs do not; a missing power has coefficient 0 (so \(\sum\alpha = 0\) for \(x^3 - 7x + 6 = 0\)); a negative \(\sum\alpha^2\) shows non-real roots but a positive one proves nothing; to find the equation with roots \(2\alpha + 1, 2\beta + 1, \ldots\), put \(y = 2x + 1\), so \(x = \dfrac{y - 1}{2}\); do not replace \(x\) by \(2x + 1\).
- **Series:** to start at \(r = m\), subtract the sum to \(m - 1\), not \(m\); writing out only the first lines and guessing the end; losing a surviving term when the gap is 2 or 3; forgetting a factor such as \(\tfrac12\); deciding convergence from the terms instead of from \(S_n\).
- **Matrices:** swapping the order of a product; \(\det(k\mathbf{A}) = k^n\det\mathbf{A}\) for an \(n \times n\) matrix; a missing transpose or division by the determinant when inverting.
- **Transformations:** "A followed by B" is \(\mathbf{BA}\), with the second transformation on the left; confusing an invariant line with a line of invariant points.
- **Induction:** writing "assume true for all \(n\)"; not using the assumption; leaving out "for all positive integers \(n\)" in the conclusion.
- **Rational functions:** writing "horizontal asymptote \(y = 0\)" when the numerator is one degree higher, instead of dividing for the oblique asymptote; the direction of the discriminant inequality (outside the roots, not between); an unexamined special value.
- **Graph relations:** moving the \(x\)-coordinate instead of the height for \(y = 1/f\); drawing a curve for \(y^2 = f\) where \(f < 0\); multiplying an inequality by a quantity of unknown sign.
- **Polar:** using the calculator's \(\tan^{-1}\) without checking the quadrant; degrees in \(\tfrac12\int r^2\,d\theta\); counting a curve twice; forgetting the \(\tfrac12\) or integrating \(r\) instead of \(r^2\).
- **Vectors:** using \(\sin\theta\) from the vector product to find an angle (it cannot say acute from obtuse, so use the scalar product); two parallel directions in a plane equation; the line-plane angle with \(\cos\) instead of \(\sin\); when finding a point on the line of intersection of two planes, setting \(x = 0\) fails if the direction of the line has \(x\)-component 0, because the line never reaches \(x = 0\): choose another coordinate.

:::warn Method before arithmetic
In all of these, the slip is cheap and the lost mark is large. Slow down on the first line of each question, write the formula before you substitute and check the sign of every answer before you move on.
:::

## Reading the marks

Mark schemes for 9231 use these labels. Knowing them tells you what to write.

| Label | What it rewards |
|---|---|
| M1 | a correct method, even if the arithmetic goes wrong |
| A1 | an accurate answer, which depends on the M1 before it |
| B1 | an independent mark: a correct fact, value or statement, with no method needed |
| DM1 | a method mark that depends on an earlier method mark being earned |
| A1 FT, B1 FT | follow-through: correct use of your own earlier wrong answer |
| SOI, AWRT, ISW | "seen or implied", "answer which rounds to", "ignore subsequent working" |

Examiners award marks positively and do not deduct them for errors, so a correct method line scores even when the answer that follows is wrong. Cross out neatly (you may not use an erasable pen or correction fluid), and never leave a part blank if you can write a first line.

## How to practise

1. **By topic, once.** Finish the chapter practice for each topic before using real papers.
2. **Mixed.** Use [Mixed practice: Further Pure](#math.fm.mixed.pure) to practise choosing the method, not just using it.
3. **One timed paper.** Take [Mock Paper 1: Further Pure Mathematics 1](#math.fm.mock.p1) in one sitting of 2 hours, with your calculator and MF19 only. Mark it line by line with the mark scheme.
4. **Real papers.** Use the 9231 Paper 1 past papers (the links box) after the chapters are done, one timed paper at a time, and mark each with its mark scheme.
5. **Error log.** After each paper write down every lost mark: the topic, what you did and what to do instead. Redo those questions a week later from a blank page.

- [ ] I have done at least two full timed Paper 1 papers in the last 14 days, and marked them with the mark scheme
