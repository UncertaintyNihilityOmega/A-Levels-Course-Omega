## What the front page says

The June 2025 (9231/41) and June 2026 front pages are the model:

- You write your answers on the question paper, in the space provided, and use the extra lined page at the end (with the question number) if you need more. The specimen paper for examination from 2027 says answers go in a separate printed answer booklet, so read your own paper's instructions.
- Black or dark blue pen, with an HB pencil for diagrams and graphs; no erasable pen or correction fluid.
- The list of formulae MF19 is provided, and **it holds the statistical tables**: there is no separate booklet of tables for this paper.
- Use a calculator where appropriate, but show all necessary working: **no marks are given for unsupported answers from a calculator**.
- Give non-exact answers to 3 significant figures unless the question specifies otherwise.

:::exam Tables from MF19, not from memory
MF19 holds the normal distribution function and its critical values, the t-distribution table, the \(\chi^2\) table, and both Wilcoxon tables. State the table value you read ("\(t_8 = 1.860\) for \(p = 0.95\)"), with its degrees of freedom, because the mark is for the value and for the right column. A critical value that is correct but unlabelled can lose the mark.
:::

## A timing plan

The paper gives 90 minutes for 50 marks, which is **1.8 minutes per mark**. Plan for **1.6 minutes per mark on a first pass**: 80 minutes for the whole paper, leaving about 10 minutes to return to gaps and to check.

| Question size | Marks | Time at 1.6 min per mark |
|---|---|---|
| a short question | 6 | about 10 minutes |
| a medium question | 7 | about 11 minutes |
| a medium to long question | 8 | about 13 minutes |
| a long question | 10 | about 16 minutes |
| the longest question seen in 2025 | 12 | about 19 minutes |
| the whole paper | 50 | about 80 minutes, leaving 10 to check |

Rules of thumb:

1. **Read first, for 2 minutes.** Mark which questions are tests (they need the layout), which are algebra and calculus (continuous random variables, PGFs), and which are tables.
2. **The wording marks are cheap.** Hypotheses, a stated assumption and a conclusion in context are one or two marks each and do not depend on getting the number right. Write them even when you are unsure of the statistic.
3. **Do not skip table work.** Reading the right table value is quick: the comparison and conclusion are marked on your own test statistic against the **correct** critical value, so a wrong critical value is not followed through (see "Reading the marks"). Check the degrees of freedom, the column and the tail before you compare.
4. **Use "show that" and "verify" results.** A given value (such as a median to verify, or a given \(\mathrm{E}(X)\)) may be used later even if you could not do the part.
5. **Check in the last 10 minutes:** probabilities between 0 and 1, a variance that is positive, degrees of freedom that match the table row, a CDF that reaches 1.

## Formulas and tables: what MF19 gives you and what you must learn

**You are given** (MF19) for Paper 4:
- the pooled two-sample variance \(s^2 = \dfrac{\sum(x_1 - \bar x_1)^2 + \sum(x_2 - \bar x_2)^2}{n_1 + n_2 - 2}\);
- the probability generating function \(G_X(t) = \mathrm{E}(t^X)\), with \(\mathrm{E}(X) = G'(1)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X) = G''(1) + G'(1) - \{G'(1)\}^2\);
- the normal distribution function \(\Phi(z)\) and the critical values of the normal distribution;
- the **t-distribution table**: for \(\nu = 1\) to \(30\), \(40\), \(60\), \(120\) and \(\infty\), the value \(t\) with \(P(T \le t) = p\) for \(p\) = 0.75, 0.90, 0.95, 0.975, 0.99, 0.995, 0.9975, 0.999 and 0.9995;
- the **\(\chi^2\) table**: for \(\nu = 1\) to \(25\), then \(30, 40, \ldots, 100\), the value \(x\) with \(P(X \le x) = p\) for \(p\) = 0.01, 0.025, 0.05, 0.9, 0.95, 0.975, 0.99, 0.995 and 0.999;
- the **Wilcoxon signed-rank table**: the largest value of \(T\) leading to rejection, for \(n = 6\) to \(20\), one-tailed 5%, 2.5%, 1% and 0.5% (two-tailed 10%, 5%, 2%, 1%), with \(T\) the smaller of \(P\) and \(Q\); and the normal approximation (mean \(\tfrac14n(n + 1)\), variance \(\tfrac1{24}n(n + 1)(2n + 1)\)) for larger \(n\);
- the **Wilcoxon rank-sum table**: the largest value of \(W\) leading to rejection, for \(m\) and \(n\) from 3 to 10 with \(m \le n\), where \(W\) is the smaller of \(R_m\) and \(m(n + m + 1) - R_m\); and the normal approximation (mean \(\tfrac12m(m + n + 1)\), variance \(\tfrac1{12}mn(m + n + 1)\));
- the 9709 entries (mean and standard deviation, binomial, geometric and Poisson probabilities, \(\mathrm{E}(X)\) and \(\mathrm{Var}(X)\) for continuous variables, the unbiased estimates \(\bar x\) and \(s^2\), the central limit theorem and the distribution of a sample proportion).

**You must learn** (not on MF19): the t-test statistics and confidence interval formulas (one-sample, paired, two-sample, and the large-sample normal form with separate variances); the \(\chi^2\) statistic \(\sum\dfrac{(O - E)^2}{E}\), the degrees of freedom rules and the rule that every expected frequency must be at least 5; the expected frequency \(\dfrac{\text{row total} \times \text{column total}}{N}\); the sign test (the number of plus signs is \(B(n, \tfrac12)\) under \(H_0\)); how to rank and form \(P\) and \(Q\) in the Wilcoxon tests; the CDF and PDF relationships and \(\mathrm{E}(g(X)) = \int g(x)f(x)\,dx\); and the PGFs of the standard distributions and the product rule for independent variables.

:::exam Which column?
For a one-tailed test at the \(\alpha\) level, read the column \(p = 1 - \alpha\). For a two-tailed test, or a \(100(1 - \alpha)\%\) confidence interval, read \(p = 1 - \dfrac{\alpha}{2}\). A 5% two-tailed test is \(p = 0.975\), not \(0.95\). For the \(\chi^2\) table the upper tail is used: a 5% test reads \(p = 0.95\).
:::

## Choosing the right test

This is the decision the questions test most. Ask in order: what is the data, how many samples, what can you assume?

| Situation | Test | Assumption to state |
|---|---|---|
| a mean, one small sample, \(\sigma^2\) unknown | one-sample t, \(\nu = n - 1\) | the population is normal |
| a mean, one large sample | normal test, with \(s^2\) in place of \(\sigma^2\) | none beyond the central limit theorem |
| a mean, population variance given (even for a small sample) | normal test (\(z\)) with \(\sigma^2\), not a t-test | the population is normal |
| two independent small samples, equal variances | pooled two-sample t, \(\nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2\) | both populations normal with a common variance |
| two independent large samples | normal test with \(\dfrac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{s_2^2}{n_2}\) | none beyond the central limit theorem |
| two independent samples, both population variances given | normal test with \(\dfrac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1} + \dfrac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}\), even for small samples | both populations normal |
| paired data (the same units twice, or matched) | t-test on the differences, \(\nu = n - 1\) | the differences are normal |
| a median, any distribution | sign test | a random sample; independent observations |
| a median or paired differences, symmetric distribution | Wilcoxon signed-rank | the distribution is symmetric |
| two independent samples, identical populations | Wilcoxon rank-sum | the distributions are symmetric, as for the signed-rank test |
| counts against a model | \(\chi^2\) goodness of fit | a random sample; every expected frequency at least 5 |
| counts in a two-way table | \(\chi^2\) test of independence | a random sample; every expected frequency at least 5 |

If both a parametric and a non-parametric test could be used, the question says (or the data say) which: a clearly skewed set of differences makes the Wilcoxon signed-rank test unsuitable, as in the June 2026 question where one mark is for saying so.

## Setting out a test

Every test uses the same seven lines. The marks are spread across them, so skipping a line costs a mark even when the calculation is right.

:::steps
1. **Hypotheses** in words and symbols, with the parameter defined: "\(H_0: \mu = 60\), \(H_1: \mu > 60\), where \(\mu\) is the population mean lap time".
2. **The level and the number of tails** (from \(H_1\)).
3. **The assumption** the test needs, stated in context.
4. **The test statistic**, with the formula and the values, and the distribution it follows under \(H_0\).
5. **The critical value** (with \(\nu\), the column and the table value) or the \(p\)-value.
6. **The comparison**: "\(2.1 > 1.860\), so reject \(H_0\)".
7. **The conclusion in context**, in "evidence" language.
:::

**The conclusion wording.** The 2026 mark scheme accepts a conclusion in context that says there is **sufficient evidence to support (or suggest)** the alternative claim, or **insufficient evidence** to support it, with a level of uncertainty in the language. It does **not** accept "no evidence". "Accept \(H_0\)" and "do not reject \(H_0\)" are both allowed, but a conclusion that stops at "reject \(H_0\)" with no context loses the final mark. Never write that the test "proves" anything.

| Result | A conclusion that earns the mark |
|---|---|
| reject \(H_0\) | "There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level to suggest that the mean lap time is more than 60 seconds." |
| do not reject \(H_0\) | "There is insufficient evidence at the 5% level to suggest that the mean mass differs from 500 g." |
| a confidence interval | "The interval is \((a, b)\), so the true mean difference is between \(a\) and \(b\) with 95% confidence." |
| a \(\chi^2\) test | "There is sufficient evidence to suggest that reliability is associated with the company." |

:::eg (Exam style) A coach claims that the mean time for one lap of a track is 60.0 seconds. A runner thinks that the true mean is higher. The runner records 9 lap times, which have mean 62.1 s and an unbiased estimate of population standard deviation \(s = 3.0\) s. Assuming the times are a random sample from a normal distribution, test at the 5% significance level whether the mean time is greater than 60.0 s.
\(H_0: \mu = 60\) and \(H_1: \mu > 60\), where \(\mu\) is the population mean lap time in seconds. [B1]

The test is one-tailed at the 5% level. The population variance is unknown and the sample is small, so assume the lap times are normally distributed and use a t-test with \(\nu = 9 - 1 = 8\). [B1]
\[\begin{aligned}
t &= \frac{\bar x - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt n} \\
&= \frac{62.1 - 60}{3.0/\sqrt9} = 2.1
\end{aligned}\] [M1] [A1]
From MF19, \(P(T \le t) = 0.95\) with \(\nu = 8\) gives the critical value \(1.860\). [B1]

Since \(2.1 > 1.860\), the statistic is in the critical region, so reject \(H_0\). [M1]

There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level to suggest that the mean lap time is more than 60.0 seconds. [A1]
:::

The second worked layout is the \(\chi^2\) test of independence: a table of counts, expected frequencies from the totals, then the statistic.

:::eg (Exam style) A survey asked 170 people which of three types of film, \(A\), \(B\) or \(C\), they preferred. The results are in the table. Test at the 5% level whether the preference is independent of the age group.

| | A | B | C | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 30 | 28 | 22 | 20 | 70 |
| 30 and over | 22 | 38 | 40 | 100 |
| Total | 50 | 60 | 60 | 170 |

\(H_0\): the type of film preferred is independent of the age group. \(H_1\): the type of film preferred is not independent of (is associated with) the age group. [B1]

Expected frequency = \(\dfrac{\text{row total} \times \text{column total}}{170}\): [M1]

| | A | B | C |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under 30 | 20.6 | 24.7 | 24.7 |
| 30 and over | 29.4 | 35.3 | 35.3 |

All expected values are greater than 5, so no classes need combining. [A1]
\[X^2 = \sum\frac{(O - E)^2}{E} = 2.668 + 0.296 + 0.896 + 1.868 + 0.208 + 0.628 = 6.56\] [M1] [A1]
\(\nu = (2 - 1)(3 - 1) = 2\), and the 5% critical value (\(p = 0.95\)) is \(5.991\). [B1]

Since \(6.56 > 5.991\), reject \(H_0\). [M1]

There is sufficient evidence at the 5% level to suggest that the preferred type of film is associated with the age group. [A1]
:::

The largest contribution (\(2.668\), the under-30 group preferring \(A\)) says where the association comes from: many more under-30s prefer \(A\) than independence predicts. Questions ask for this in a two-mark part, as in the June 2026 paper's "which company" question. Use the contributions: say which cell and whether the observed is above or below the expected.

## Setting out the other question types

| Type | What the working should show |
|---|---|
| Continuous random variable | state the pieces and the ranges; use \(F(x) = 0\) below and \(F(x) = 1\) above; integrate the second piece from its own start and add \(F\) at the join; test that the pieces join |
| CDF of \(Y = h(X)\) | write \(G(y) = P(Y \le y) = P(X \le h^{-1}(y))\), reverse the inequality if \(h\) is decreasing, give the range of \(Y\) from the range of \(X\), differentiate for the PDF |
| Confidence interval | the formula with \(\bar x\), the table value with \(\nu\) and column, the arithmetic, and the interval written as \((a, b)\) |
| Goodness of fit | the expected frequencies from \(N \times P(\text{class})\), merge classes below 5 first, then calculate; \(\nu\) with parameters estimated |
| Sign test | \(S\) is \(B(n, \tfrac12)\) under \(H_0\); the tail the data lean towards, doubled for a two-tailed test; the conclusion in context |
| Wilcoxon | the differences from the median or between pairs, ranks of the absolute values, \(P\) and \(Q\), the right statistic for \(H_1\), the table value (equal is in the critical region) |
| PGF | \(G(1) = 1\) as a free equation; \(G'(1)\) and \(G''(1)\) for the mean and variance; the product of the PGFs for a sum of independent variables, the coefficient of \(t^k\) for \(P(X = k)\) |

## The marks students lose most often

These come from the warning boxes in the Paper 4 chapters.

- **Continuous random variables:** a CDF that stops at the end of its main piece (no 0 and 1 outside); integrating the second piece from its own start and forgetting to add \(F\) at the join; the reversed inequality for a decreasing function; a CDF given with no range for \(Y\); stopping at \(G(y)\) when the PDF is asked.
- **One-sample t:** reading the one-tailed column for a two-tailed test (\(1.833\) instead of \(2.262\) for \(\nu = 9\)).
- **Two means:** using an independent-sample test on paired data (or the reverse); pooling variances in a large-sample normal test, when each variance is divided by its own sample size.
- **Goodness of fit:** calculating \(\dfrac{(O - E)^2}{E}\) for small classes and combining afterwards; \(\nu = n - 1\) when a parameter was estimated; rounding expected frequencies early.
- **Independence:** using percentages instead of the actual counts.
- **Sign test:** comparing a single tail with the full \(\alpha\) in a two-tailed test; using the wrong tail (the one the data do not lean towards); using \(P(S = s)\) instead of the tail.
- **Wilcoxon:** forgetting that the sign test does not need symmetry; using the wrong statistic for a one-tailed test (the data must lean the way \(H_1\) says); the wrong continuity correction (\(88.5\), not \(87.5\), for \(P(P \le 88)\)); saying "must be less than" when the table value itself is in the critical region; ranking the larger sample.
- **PGFs:** \(G''(1) = \mathrm{E}(X^2) - \mathrm{E}(X)\), not \(\mathrm{E}(X^2)\); forgetting \(G(1) = 1\); multiplying PGFs for dependent variables.

:::warn Wording is part of the answer
Four phrases cost marks: "no evidence", "proves", a conclusion with no context and a conclusion with no level. Write "sufficient evidence at the 5% level to suggest that \(\ldots\)" or "insufficient evidence \(\ldots\)", name the quantity in words, and state the assumption (normality, equal variances, symmetry, independence) when the question asks for it.
:::

## Reading the marks

Mark schemes for 9231 use M1, A1, B1, DM1 and DB1 (marks that depend on an earlier starred mark), A1 FT and B1 FT (follow-through from your own earlier wrong value; in a test the comparison M1 is for "their" statistic against the correct critical value, and the final A1 is for a correct conclusion from correct working, so a wrong critical value usually costs both and is not followed through; a scheme sometimes names one common slip as allowed for the comparison mark, such as the critical value for the other tail or for one degree of freedom too many, but never just any value of your own), and abbreviations such as SOI (seen or implied), AWRT (answer which rounds to), OE (or equivalent) and ISW (ignore subsequent working). Marks are awarded positively and are not deducted for errors, so write every line. A mark scheme may allow a conclusion in either of two forms, so use the one you are sure of.

## How to practise

1. **By topic, once.** Finish the chapter practice for each topic, writing the full seven-line layout for every test.
2. **Mixed.** Use [Mixed practice: Further Mechanics and Statistics](#math.fm.mixed.applied) to practise choosing the test, not just using it.
3. **One timed paper.** Take [Mock Paper 4: Further Probability and Statistics](#math.fm.mock.p4) in one sitting of 1 hour 30 minutes, with your calculator and MF19 only. Mark it line by line.
4. **Real papers.** Use the 9231 Paper 4 past papers (the links box) after the chapters are done, one at a time, timed and marked with the mark scheme. Read the mark scheme's wording for the conclusions.
5. **Error log.** For each lost mark note the topic and the slip (a column, a hypothesis, a conclusion). Redo the question a week later.

:::recap
- Paper 4: 1 h 30, 50 marks, all questions compulsory; 40% of an AS and 20% of an A Level; calculator, ruler and MF19, which holds the tables.
- Plan 1.6 minutes per mark, which is 80 minutes and leaves 10 minutes for checking; the longest question in 2025 was 12 marks.
- MF19 gives the pooled variance, the PGF results and the normal, t, \(\chi^2\) and Wilcoxon tables. Learn the t statistics and intervals, the \(\chi^2\) statistic and rules, the sign test, the ranking method and the standard PGFs.
- Set out every test in seven lines: hypotheses, level and tails, assumption, statistic, critical value or \(p\)-value, comparison, conclusion in context.
- The 2026 mark scheme wants "sufficient evidence" or "insufficient evidence", in context, with a level; "no evidence" is not accepted.
- Most lost marks are wrong column, wrong tail, combining classes after calculating, percentages for counts, a CDF with no 0 and 1, \(G(1) = 1\) forgotten.
- Do timed papers in the final weeks and keep an error log.
:::
