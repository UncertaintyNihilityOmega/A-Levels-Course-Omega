## What Further Mathematics is, and who it suits

Cambridge International **Further Mathematics 9231** is a separate qualification from Mathematics 9709. It is a second mathematics course for students who enjoy the subject and want more of it: more algebra, more calculus, more proof, and applications that go beyond 9709 mechanics and statistics. This section of the course follows the 9231 syllabus for 2026 to 2027, with the 2028 to 2030 syllabus covered at the end of this chapter.

It suits you if you like to know why a method works, you are comfortable with the 9709 Pure content (algebra, trigonometry, calculus, complex numbers, vectors), and you are happy to spend time on long, multi-step questions in which each part builds on the one before. Some university courses in mathematics, physics, engineering and computer science value it or ask for it, so check the entry requirements of the courses you want. If you only want a taste, the one-chapter tour [Further Mathematics: a guided tour](#math.further) shows a small example from each major topic before you commit.

:::tip How the chapters are built
Every topic chapter follows one pattern. A **warm-up** of three questions from earlier chapters, then teaching in small steps with worked examples at 9231 depth (proof and "show that" questions are written with the marks an examiner gives), a **Spot the error** example, graded exam practice from one star to three stars, a **stretch** for A*, a quiz, an "I can" checklist and a recap. The three-star questions have several linked parts like the real papers.
:::

## How it builds on 9709

Further Mathematics assumes the 9709 content. The assumption is real: the Further Pure papers use the P3 content of 9709 freely, which is why the AS is not designed to be taken in the same year as AS Mathematics.

## The four papers

| Paper | Name | Time | Marks | Questions | Weighting |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Further Pure Mathematics 1 | 2 h | 75 | 6 to 8, all compulsory | 60% of AS, 30% of A Level |
| 2 | Further Pure Mathematics 2 | 2 h | 75 | 7 to 9, all compulsory | 30% of A Level (A Level only) |
| 3 | Further Mechanics | 1 h 30 min | 50 | 5 to 7, all compulsory | 40% of AS, 20% of A Level |
| 4 | Further Probability & Statistics | 1 h 30 min | 50 | 5 to 7, all compulsory | 40% of AS, 20% of A Level |

You answer every question on every paper. The two Pure papers carry 75 marks each over two hours, which is 1.6 minutes per mark. The two applied papers carry 50 marks over an hour and a half, which is 1.8 minutes per mark. Questions are structured: a stem and parts (a), (b), (c) that usually build on each other, from about 5 marks to about 16 marks in the papers of June 2025 and June 2026. Paper 2 assumes the content of Paper 1. Each paper has its own survival guide in this course: [Paper 1](#math.fm.exam.fp1), [Paper 2](#math.fm.exam.fp2), [Paper 3](#math.fm.exam.fm) and [Paper 4](#math.fm.exam.fs).

:::exam The front page is the same on every paper
The June 2025 and June 2026 front pages say: answer all questions; use a black or dark blue pen and an HB pencil for diagrams and graphs; show all necessary working, because no marks are given for unsupported answers from a calculator; give non-exact answers to 3 significant figures, or 1 decimal place for angles in degrees, unless the question says otherwise; the list of formulae MF19 is provided. The Mechanics paper adds that where a value of the acceleration due to gravity is needed you use 10 m s⁻². The specimen paper for examination from 2027 says answers go in a separate printed answer booklet, so check the instructions in the paper you sit.
:::

## Routes through the qualification

| Qualification | Papers | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| AS Further Mathematics | Paper 1 and Paper 3, or Paper 1 and Paper 4 | Paper 1 is compulsory. Paper 3 or Paper 4 is your choice. Graded a to e. |
| A Level Further Mathematics | all four papers | Graded A* to E. Papers can be taken in one series or staged. |
| Staged A Level | AS papers first (Paper 1 plus Paper 3 or 4), then Paper 2 and the other applied paper in a later series | Your centre sets the entry rules for each series. |

Papers are set in the June and November series. Whether you take all four in one sitting or stage them is a decision for you and your centre; the course works either way, because each paper has its own chapters and guide. What does not change is the order of dependence: **Paper 2 needs Paper 1**, so FP1 is always studied first. The AS is not designed to be taken after one year alongside AS Mathematics, because Paper 1 assumes 9709 P3 content that is normally taught in the second year of 9709.

:::board Other Further Mathematics qualifications
UK Further Mathematics A Levels (AQA 7367, Edexcel 9FM0 and OCR H245) are organised as core pure papers plus optional applied papers, so the topics are grouped differently from the four 9231 papers. The 9231 applied papers include the t-distribution, the Wilcoxon tests and probability generating functions. If you are following another board, check its topic list against the chapter list below before you rely on this course.
:::

## Assessment objectives, MF19 and the calculator

Every paper, and both the AS and the A Level, split its marks the same way: **AO1 (knowledge and understanding) is 45%** and **AO2 (application and communication) is 55%**. In practice AO1 is carrying out a standard method accurately; AO2 is choosing the method for an unfamiliar situation, building a chain of reasoning, interpreting a result in context and communicating it clearly. Proof, "show that" and conclusions in statistics are AO2 skills: they are marks for what you write, not only for what you calculate.

The command words in the papers are calculate, deduce, derive, describe, determine, evaluate, explain, identify, interpret, justify, prove, show (that), sketch, state and verify. "Show that" gives you the answer: every line must be there, because the marks are for the working. "Deduce" and "hence" tell you to use the previous part.

**MF19** is the list of formulae printed with every 9709 and 9231 paper. It is one shared list, and "all formulae in the list may be needed". For Further Mathematics it adds, among others: \(\sum r\), \(\sum r^2\) and \(\sum r^3\); the Maclaurin series of the standard functions; the hyperbolic identities and the logarithmic forms of the inverse hyperbolic functions; derivatives and integrals of inverse trigonometric and hyperbolic functions; the trajectory equation; Hooke's law and elastic energy; the centres of mass of the standard shapes; the pooled variance; the probability generating function relations; and the normal, t, \(\chi^2\) and Wilcoxon tables. It does **not** give the relations between roots and coefficients, the vector product formula, plane equations, de Moivre's theorem, matrix inverses, reduction formulae, arc length and surface area formulae, integrating factors, \(v = r\omega\), Newton's experimental law, the \(\chi^2\) statistic or the sign test rules. Each chapter's `:::exam` box lists what is given for its topic, and each paper guide collects them: [Paper 1](#math.fm.exam.fp1), [Paper 2](#math.fm.exam.fp2), [Paper 3](#math.fm.exam.fm), [Paper 4](#math.fm.exam.fs).

**Calculator and accuracy.** You need a scientific calculator and a ruler. Graphical, symbolic and computer algebra calculators are not allowed, and no marks are given for unsupported answers from a calculator, so show the working that the calculator completes (the equation you solved, the values you substituted). Give non-exact answers to 3 significant figures and angles in degrees to 1 decimal place unless the question says otherwise. Keep full calculator values through a calculation and round once, at the end.

## The 2028 to 2030 syllabus

The 2028 to 2030 syllabus has the same four papers with the same times, marks, weightings and subject content, and the syllabus states that there are no significant changes which affect teaching. The chapters of this section stay valid for it. The syllabus years change which version of the specification and the front-matter you read, not the mathematics.

## Study order and pace

Follow the sidebar order within each paper. Between papers the rules are short:

1. **FP1 first.** Everything in FP2 assumes it (induction, matrices, roots and polar ideas return).
2. **FM and FS are independent** of each other and of FP2. Do them in parallel with the Pure papers, or choose one for the AS.
3. Within FP1, take the sequence as written: roots, series, matrices and transformations, induction (the series and matrix chapters feed it), then graphs and polar, then vectors.
4. Finish the topic chapters of a paper, then use its survival guide and its mock paper, then the mixed practice.

For pace, a steady 6 hours a week is a realistic load for someone also studying other subjects. At that rate the chapters of each paper take about the following (at the head-of-chapter study times):

| Paper | Study hours | Weeks at 6 hours a week |
|---|---|---|
| Paper 1 (10 chapters) | 55 to 74 | about 9 to 12 |
| Paper 2 (12 chapters) | 65 to 84 | about 11 to 14 |
| Paper 3 (9 chapters) | 46 to 55 | about 8 to 9 |
| Paper 4 (8 chapters) | 40 to 51 | about 7 to 9 |
| All four | 206 to 264 | about 34 to 44 |

These are the hours of learning and practice inside the chapters. Add two to four weeks per paper for the survival guide, the mixed practice and a timed mock, and allow time at the end for revising whichever paper you did first. An AS route (Paper 1 plus one applied paper) is therefore a first-year or one-term commitment on top of your other subjects. If time is short, the order of value is: finish the chapters of FP1 completely, then the applied paper you chose, then add FP2.

:::tip A weekly rhythm that works
Two sessions of teaching (one new section each, with every worked example covered and then redone from a blank page) and one session of questions. Before moving on from a chapter, do the Exam practice and the Stretch without looking at the answers, mark yourself line by line against the marks shown, and note each lost mark in an error log (chapter, question, what you did, what to do instead).
:::

## How to reach A*

Cambridge does not publish a mark for a grade in advance, so there is no figure to aim at. The habits that earn the top grades in Further Mathematics are the same ones that earn full marks on a single question.

- **Write the proof, not just the answer.** In induction, "show that", identity proofs and statistics conclusions, the marks are for each line of reasoning. The paper guides give the layouts.
- **Know the formulae MF19 does not give.** The list above is long. Put each on a card the day you meet it, and recall it from memory every week.
- **Use the structure of a question.** Parts are linked: "hence" and "deduce" mean the next part is easier if you use the last one. A result given in "show that" can be used in later parts even if you could not prove it.
- **Practise with real papers once you have the chapters.** The past papers listed under Resources, with their mark schemes, show the form of the questions. Use them after the chapter practice, as timed attempts.
- **Check answers with a second route.** A determinant can be checked by the area scale factor, a root by substitution, a sum by \(n = 1\), a probability by its sum to 1. A check takes a minute and rescues an accuracy mark.
- **Keep the error log**, and redo every question you lost marks on, a week later, from a blank page.

## Are you ready? A readiness quiz

Eight questions from the 9709 skills used most in Further Mathematics. If you slip on one, read its explanation, which names the chapter to revise before you start the Further chapters that use it.

:::quiz Readiness quiz
? What is \(\arg(-1 + \mathrm{i})\), in radians, with \(-\pi < \arg z \le \pi\)?
- \(\dfrac{\pi}{4}\)
- \(-\dfrac{\pi}{4}\)
+ \(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\)
- \(-\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\)
> The point \((-1, 1)\) is in the second quadrant. \(\tan^{-1}(-1) = -\dfrac\pi4\) from a calculator is the wrong quadrant: add \(\pi\) to get \(\dfrac{3\pi}{4}\). Revise [Complex numbers](#math.p3.complex).

? Find \(\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b}\) for \(\mathbf{a} = \begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}\) and \(\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\).
+ 4
- 8
- 6
- \(\begin{pmatrix}2\\-2\\4\end{pmatrix}\)
> \(1 \times 2 + 2 \times (-1) + 2 \times 2 = 2 - 2 + 4 = 4\). Dropping the minus gives 8; the products by components without adding make a vector, not a number. Revise [Vectors](#math.p3.vectors).

? Find \(\displaystyle\int x\mathrm{e}^{2x}\,dx\).
- \(\tfrac12x^2\mathrm{e}^{2x} + c\)
+ \(\tfrac14\mathrm{e}^{2x}(2x - 1) + c\)
- \(\tfrac14\mathrm{e}^{2x}(2x + 1) + c\)
- \(\tfrac12\mathrm{e}^{2x}(x - 1) + c\)
> Take \(u = x\) and \(dv = \mathrm{e}^{2x}\,dx\): \(\tfrac12x\mathrm{e}^{2x} - \tfrac14\mathrm{e}^{2x}\). Integrating each factor separately is not a rule, and a sign slip in the second step gives \(+1\). Differentiate your answer to check. Revise [Integration techniques: parts, substitution and partial fractions](#math.p3.inttech).

? Write \(\dfrac{1}{r(r + 2)}\) as partial fractions.
- \(\dfrac1r - \dfrac1{r + 2}\)
+ \(\dfrac12\left(\dfrac1r - \dfrac1{r + 2}\right)\)
- \(\dfrac12\left(\dfrac1r + \dfrac1{r + 2}\right)\)
- \(\dfrac{1}{2r} - \dfrac{1}{r + 2}\)
> \(1 = A(r + 2) + Br\): \(r = 0\) gives \(A = \tfrac12\), \(r = -2\) gives \(B = -\tfrac12\). The first option forgets the \(\tfrac12\), which is the factor that survives in the method of differences. Revise [Partial fractions and the binomial series](#math.p3.partial).

? Solve \(\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2xy\) with \(y = 3\) when \(x = 0\).
- \(y = \mathrm{e}^{x^2} + 2\)
- \(y = 3\mathrm{e}^{2x}\)
+ \(y = 3\mathrm{e}^{x^2}\)
- \(y = 3x^2\)
> Separate: \(\int\dfrac1y\,dy = \int 2x\,dx\), so \(\ln y = x^2 + c\) and \(y = A\mathrm{e}^{x^2}\). The condition gives \(A = 3\). Adding the constant after exponentiating, or integrating \(2x\) as \(2x^2\)-type terms, are the common slips. Revise [Differential equations](#math.p3.diffeq).

? \(X \sim \mathrm{Po}(3.2)\). Find \(P(X = 2)\).
- 0.380
+ 0.209
- 0.130
- 0.223
> \(\mathrm{e}^{-3.2} \times \dfrac{3.2^2}{2!} = 0.2087\). 0.380 is \(P(X \le 2)\), 0.130 is \(P(X = 1)\) and 0.223 is \(P(X = 3)\). Revise [The Poisson distribution](#math.s2.poisson).

? A 3 kg particle moving at \(4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) hits a stationary 5 kg particle and they stick together. What is their speed?
- \(2.4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
+ \(1.5\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
- \(0.75\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
- \(4\ \mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\)
> Momentum is conserved: \(3 \times 4 = (3 + 5)v\), so \(v = 1.5\). Dividing by only the mass at rest gives 2.4; keeping the speed unchanged forgets the extra mass. Revise [Momentum and collisions](#math.m.momentum).

? A two-tailed test at the 5% level uses a normal distribution with known variance. Which critical value of \(z\) do you use?
- 1.645
+ 1.960
- 2.326
- 0.975
> Two-tailed at 5% leaves 2.5% in each tail, so \(\Phi(z) = 0.975\) and \(z = 1.960\). 1.645 is the one-tailed 5% value, and 0.975 is the probability, not the \(z\)-value. Revise [Hypothesis testing](#math.s2.hypothesis).
:::
