:::why
The Nazis did not want only to rule Germany: they wanted to remake the Germans. Women were to bear children, boys to become soldiers, churches to bend or be crushed, and a "people's community" was to be purified of everyone the regime classed as alien. This chapter asks how far that happened between 1933 and 1939, and its answers differ sharply from area to area: the schools and youth movements were reshaped, women's lives changed in contradictory ways, the churches resisted more than the unions had, and the persecution of Jews and other minorities was carried out step by step with terrible effect. Because this is a question about people, it must be handled with care: the victims are named as human beings, and the evidence is weighed, not ranked.
:::

## Impact on women

### The ideal: Kinder, Küche, Kirche

:::def Kinder, Küche, Kirche
"Children, kitchen, church": an older German saying (usually linked to the Kaiser's time) for the idea that a woman's place is in the family. It is used for the Nazi view of women, though the regime's own propaganda stressed motherhood and the home, with little use of the church.
:::

The Nazi view of women came from the party's ideas about race and the nation. A woman's main duty was to bear healthy "Aryan" children. Hitler put it in a speech to the National Socialist Women's League in September 1934, in the words of the standard English translation: "For her world is her husband, her family, her children, and her home." (From Hitler's speech of 8 September 1934, in Noakes and Pridham, *Nazism 1919–1945*, vol. 2, 2000, translated from the *Frankfurter Zeitung* of 9 September 1934.) In the same speech he went on to argue that the larger world of the state rested on the smaller world of the family, so that the two complemented each other. The party's women's organisation, the **NS-Frauenschaft**, led by Gertrud Scholtz-Klink from 1934 as "Reich Women's Leader", had about two million members by 1938.

The ideal was an attack on the Weimar "new woman", who had the vote (since 1919), worked in offices and shops, dressed in the modern style and smoked in public. It was also a population policy: the Nazis were afraid that the falling birth rate of the 1920s and the Depression would weaken the "race" and the army.

### Attempts to reduce women's work

In 1933–36, when millions were unemployed, the regime wanted women out of the jobs that a man could do.
- **Marriage loans.** The Law for the Reduction of Unemployment (1 June 1933) offered interest-free loans of up to 1,000 marks, in vouchers for furniture, to newly married couples, on condition that the wife left her job and did not return while the loan lasted. A quarter was cancelled for every child.
- **Dismissals.** Under laws of 1933, married women could be dismissed from the civil service where the husband had work, and women doctors, lawyers and teachers were pushed out of public posts. From 1936 women were excluded from the judiciary (as judges and prosecutors).
- **Education.** The law of 25 April 1933 against overcrowding in the schools and universities set limits on the admission of new students, including a limit on the share of women among new university entrants (about 10 per cent), though the limits were largely ignored. The party said that girls should be trained to be wives and mothers, with courses in domestic science and eugenics.

### Efforts to raise the birth rate

- **Marriage loans** (above) were meant to raise the marriage rate as well as employment.
- **Honours and benefits.** The **Mother's Cross** (*Ehrenkreuz der Deutschen Mutter*), established on 16 December 1938 and first presented in May 1939, was a medal in three classes: bronze for mothers of four or five children, silver for six or seven and gold for eight or more. Families with several children received allowances and tax relief, and large families had priority for housing.
- **Restrictions.** Abortion and the sale of contraceptives were restricted for "Aryan" women, and the penalties for abortion were increased. At the same time sterilisation and abortion were forced on women judged "unfit" (see the section on minorities below), so the policy meant compulsion in two opposite directions.
- **Lebensborn.** In December 1935 the SS under Heinrich Himmler founded the *Lebensborn* ("spring of life") association, which ran maternity homes for the births of children thought to be "racially valuable", including those of unmarried mothers.

How well did this work? The marriage rate rose sharply, from about 517,000 marriages in 1932 to about 740,000 in 1934, and the birth rate rose from about 14.7 per thousand in 1933 to about 20.3 in 1939. But the causes are disputed: the end of the Depression helped, and couples who took loans on average had one child, not the four that would cancel the loan. The birth rate was still short of the level of the early twentieth century, and the regime did not reach its aim of large families.

### Changes to attitudes to women in work from 1937

Rearmament changed the policy. By 1936–37 the Four Year Plan had created a shortage of labour (see [Hitler's Germany (3)](#hist.d.hitler.3)), and the regime needed women in the factories, offices and farms. The condition that a wife must leave work to receive a marriage loan was dropped in 1937. Propaganda was turned around, and women were urged to take jobs. In 1938 a **duty year** (*Pflichtjahr*) was introduced, which required young unmarried women under twenty-five to spend a year in farming or domestic work before taking a job elsewhere. The number of women in paid work grew in all sectors during the 1930s and reached about 14 million in 1939, according to a report of the Reich Labour Ministry.

Even so the ideology held. In September 1939 the Labour Ministry rejected the compulsory call-up of married women for war work, in line with Hitler's wish. The result was that the regime used far fewer women in war industry than Britain did.

### What the evidence shows

Nazi policy was inconsistent. It praised the housewife and mother, and yet rearmament pulled women into work. It tried to raise the birth rate, but did so through a system in which the state decided which women should have children and which should not. Historians differ. Jill Stephenson, in *Women in Nazi Germany* (2001), stresses the contradictions between ideology and the needs of the economy, and that women's lives were affected in uneven ways. Claudia Koonz, in *Mothers in the Fatherland* (1986), argued that women were not only victims, since many took part in the regime's welfare and racial work. The best judgement is that **the regime changed the legal and ideological position of women in a conservative direction, but its practical effect was limited and was reversed by the needs of rearmament**, so the "transformation" was less than the propaganda suggested.

## Impact on young people

### Education

The Nazis wanted to shape the minds of the young, and the schools were an early target. Hitler saw the child as raw material for the nation, and he said in a speech at Reichenberg in December 1938 (paraphrased) that the children of his opponents already belonged to the new Germany.

- **Teachers.** The Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service (7 April 1933) allowed the dismissal of Jewish teachers and of those thought politically unreliable. Most teachers were then brought into the **National Socialist Teachers' League** (NSLB), led by Hans Schemm, and attended courses and camps. Teachers swore an oath to Hitler as civil servants (from August 1934) and had to teach what the state wanted. Some, especially older teachers, grumbled, but few resisted openly.
- **The syllabus.** Subjects were reshaped for ideology. **Biology** taught race theory and eugenics. **History** began with the humiliation of 1918 and the rise of the Nazi movement and glorified the German past. **German**, **geography** and **maths** were used to teach nationalism (for example by problems about the cost to the state of caring for disabled people, or the bombing of cities). **Physical education** was given a greater share of time, to prepare for military service. Religious instruction survived but under pressure.
- **Textbooks.** New textbooks were issued, and children's books such as *Der Giftpilz* ("The Poisonous Mushroom", 1938), published by Julius Streicher's *Der Stürmer* press, taught children to see Jews as dangerous.
- **New schools.** From 1933 the **Napolas** (National Political Educational Institutes), run under the Education Minister Bernhard Rust, took boys for a military-style, ideological education, and from 1937 the **Adolf Hitler Schools**, run by the Labour Front's Robert Ley and the Hitler Youth, took selected boys of twelve to eighteen to train a party elite. They were few in number, and most children stayed in ordinary schools.
- **Universities.** In May 1933 students and SA men burned thousands of books by Jewish, left-wing and liberal authors in the university cities, on 10 May in Berlin. Jewish and politically opposed lecturers were dismissed, many leading scientists left Germany, and the Nazi Students' League took control of student life. The universities were brought into line, and research was directed towards the aims of the regime.

### Youth groups: the Hitler Youth and the League of German Girls

The **Hitler Youth** (*Hitler-Jugend*, HJ) had been founded in 1922. Its leader from June 1933 was **Baldur von Schirach**, a young Nazi who was made Reich Youth Leader and who brought the other youth organisations (including the Protestant and the Catholic ones) under his control or closed them down. Membership rose from about 108,000 at the end of 1932 to about 2.3 million at the end of 1933, mostly because other groups were taken over.

- **Structure.** Boys and girls were organised by age: the *Jungvolk* for boys of ten to thirteen or fourteen, the Hitler Youth proper for boys of fourteen to eighteen, and for girls the *Jungmädel* and the **League of German Girls** (*Bund Deutscher Mädel*, BDM), parallel in structure, up to eighteen.
- **Activities.** Sport, hiking, camping, singing, uniforms and parades; many young people enjoyed them. For a boy from a poor family the movement offered adventure, comradeship and the chance to lead, as an older boy over younger ones. For girls the BDM stressed health, the home, and motherhood.
- **Indoctrination.** Meetings taught the Nazi view of history, the cult of Hitler and race. Loyalty to the movement was meant to come before loyalty to the family and the church.
- **Military training.** For boys, drill, map reading, shooting and marching were central, and special branches trained boys as glider pilots or sailors. In 1938 about 1.5 million boys were trained in rifle shooting (a reported figure).
- **Compulsion and attendance.** At first membership was voluntary but under strong pressure from teachers, employers and the community. The law of **1 December 1936** declared that "all of the German youth in the Reich is organised within the Hitler Youth", making it the only legal youth organisation, and the **Youth Service Duty decree of 25 March 1939** made membership compulsory, even over parents' objections. By 1939 about 8.7 million young people were enrolled, but the figures include every young person enrolled on paper, and many were less than keen. Complaints from parents and from teachers about long hours of duty, tired and absent pupils and the tough behaviour of the young leaders were common, and some young people turned to groups outside the regime (see the Edelweiss Pirates and the Swing Youth in [Hitler's Germany (2)](#hist.d.hitler.2)).

:::def Hitler Youth (*Hitler-Jugend*)
The Nazi youth organisation, led from 1933 by Baldur von Schirach. It trained boys of fourteen to eighteen (and younger boys in the *Jungvolk*), with a parallel League of German Girls (BDM) for girls. Made the only legal youth organisation by the law of 1 December 1936, and compulsory by the decree of 25 March 1939.
:::

### How successful was the Nazi effort with the young?

Michael Kater's *Hitler Youth* (2004) is the standard scholarly history of the movement; it shows both the attractions the movement had for young people and its compulsion and abuses, and it makes clear that the experience of the young varied with class, region and age. Detlev Peukert, in *Inside Nazi Germany* (1982; English edition 1987), argued that the regime's control was much less complete than its image suggests, because many young people drifted away from it or against it.

:::key Young people: how far were they transformed?
- **Change.** The schools were purged and the syllabus rewritten. The youth movements were unified, and by 1939 almost all young people were in the Hitler Youth or the BDM, either by choice or by law. Many young Germans were drilled, indoctrinated and prepared for war. Their view of the world, and especially of race and the Jews, was shaped by the school and the movement.
- **Limits.** Teachers were mostly conservative and not fully converted. Many young people joined for the sport and the friends, and some, in the late 1930s, were bored or annoyed by drill and by interference. Some formed groups of their own. Parents, churches and local culture still counted. Membership figures show enrolment, not belief.
- **Judgement.** The Nazis transformed the *organisation* of youth almost completely and the *content* of what they were taught to a large extent. How far they transformed young people's beliefs, in the short time of six years before the war, is much harder to show.
:::

## Religious policies

### The Catholic Church and the Concordat

Hitler was a Catholic by birth, but he disliked both churches, since they claimed a loyalty that rivalled the claim of the state. In 1933 he needed to calm Catholic fears. A **Concordat** between the Reich and the Vatican was signed in Rome on **20 July 1933** (by Cardinal Pacelli, later Pope Pius XII, and by Franz von Papen for Germany). It guaranteed the Catholic Church's freedom to worship, its schools and religious organisations (as long as they were not political), and its right to run its own affairs, in return for the Church's withdrawal from politics. The Catholic Centre Party had dissolved itself on **5 July 1933**. The Concordat gave the regime international respectability and removed the Catholic party from German politics.

:::def Concordat (1933)
An agreement signed on 20 July 1933 between Nazi Germany and the Vatican. The Church was guaranteed freedom of worship and the continuation of its schools and organisations, and in return priests and bishops withdrew from politics and the Catholic Centre Party had dissolved. The Nazis broke its terms within months.
:::

The Nazis soon broke the terms. They arrested priests and closed Catholic newspapers and youth groups, they put monks and priests on trial in 1935–37 on charges of immorality and currency smuggling (with a lot of publicity), they replaced church schools with "community schools", and there was a struggle over crucifixes in classrooms, in which the Nazis backed down after local protests in Oldenburg in 1936. On **14 March 1937** Pope Pius XI issued the encyclical ***Mit brennender Sorge*** ("With burning concern"), written in German and smuggled into Germany, and read from Catholic pulpits on 21 March. It accused the regime of breaking the Concordat and criticised the worship of race and state. The regime responded with arrests and the seizure of the presses that had printed it. The Catholic Church's resistance in matters of faith was firmer than its stand in politics.

### The Protestant churches: the Reich Church and the German Christians

About two thirds of Germans were Protestants, in a number of regional churches, and many were nationalist and conservative. The Nazis wanted a single, united "Reich Church" under their control.

- **German Christians.** The *Deutsche Christen* movement mixed Christianity and Nazi ideology, with an "Aryan" Jesus and the rejection of the Jewish Old Testament. After Hitler spoke on the radio in support, it won about two thirds of the votes in the church elections of **23 July 1933**.
- **Bishop Müller.** **Ludwig Müller**, a former army chaplain and a friend of Hitler, was made **Reich Bishop** at the national synod at Wittenberg on **27 September 1933**. At a German Christian rally at the Berlin Sports Palace on **13 November 1933**, a speaker called for the removal of the Old Testament and the "Jewish" parts of the New, and the outcry that followed weakened the movement.
- **The Pastors' Emergency League.** Martin Niemöller, a Berlin pastor and a First World War U-boat commander, formed the Pastors' Emergency League in September 1933, which about 7,000 pastors joined by the start of 1934, in protest against the "Aryan paragraph" being applied to the Church.
- **The Confessing Church.** At the **Barmen Synod** (29–31 May 1934) the opposing Protestants, led by theologians such as Karl Barth, issued the Barmen Declaration, which said that the Church could not take its orders from any state and that Christ was the only Lord. This created the **Confessing Church**, which was a minority of Protestants and was divided about how far to resist.
- **The failure of the Reich Church.** Müller's authority collapsed under the opposition of the Confessing Church and the lack of enthusiasm of most Protestants, and in July 1935 Hitler appointed Hanns Kerrl as Minister for Church Affairs to try to control the churches by committees. Niemöller was arrested on **1 July 1937**, and after a trial was held as Hitler's personal prisoner in Sachsenhausen and Dachau until 1945. The regime did not manage to unite the Protestants in a Nazi church.

### The German Faith Movement

:::def German Faith Movement
A small neo-pagan movement founded in 1933 and led by the scholar Jakob Wilhelm Hauer, which rejected Christianity as foreign to the "Germanic" spirit and tried to put a faith based on race, nature and old Germanic myths in its place. Some in the SS, such as Himmler, sympathised, but the movement attracted only a small following and Hitler did not support it.
:::

Hitler never made a firm choice between the churches and an alternative religion, because open warfare with the churches in the 1930s would have cost him support, and as the war approached he put the clash off. The Nazis concentrated on weakening the churches' role in schools, youth groups and public life, not on abolishing them.

:::key Religion: how far was Germany transformed?
- **Change.** The Catholic Church withdrew from politics (July 1933), and the Centre Party disappeared. The Protestant churches were split. Church schools and youth groups were closed or taken over, and the Hitler Youth took Sunday time. Many churchmen were arrested, and the public place of religion shrank.
- **Limits.** The churches kept the loyalty of most Germans. The attempt to impose a Reich Church failed, the German Faith Movement stayed small, and the Confessing Church and the encyclical of 1937 showed open protest. Most Germans remained Christian in belief and practice.
- **Judgement.** The Nazis weakened the *institutions* and public role of the churches but did not transform the *beliefs* of ordinary believers, and they failed to put a Nazi religion in their place. Compared with the trade unions, which were smashed in a day, the churches proved a much harder target.
:::

## Persecution of Jews and other minorities

This section deals with the years 1933–39. The people who suffered were individuals and families: shopkeepers and doctors, children excluded from their schools, men sent to camps. The facts are presented without graphic detail, and the numbers carry the sources they come from.

### Nazi race theory

:::def Social Darwinism
The application of Charles Darwin's theory of evolution to human societies, in the form of the phrase "survival of the fittest" (from Herbert Spencer), to argue that nations and "races" are in a natural struggle in which the strong win. It was used by Nazis to claim that "Aryan" Germans must keep their "blood" pure and win living space.
:::

:::def Eugenics
The belief, founded by the Englishman Francis Galton in the 1880s, that a population can be improved by encouraging "fit" people to have children and by preventing "unfit" people from doing so. In Germany it was called "racial hygiene" and under the Nazis led to compulsory sterilisation and later to the killing of disabled people.
:::

Nazi ideas combined these two. **Hitler's *Mein Kampf*** (1925–26) divided humanity into races, put the "Aryan" (or "Nordic") race at the top as the creator of civilisation and the Jews at the bottom as parasites and the enemy, and said that the nation's strength depended on keeping the race "pure". This set of ideas was used to define who belonged to the *Volksgemeinschaft* ("people's community") and who did not. Those who belonged were praised, and those who did not (Jews first, then others) were to be excluded, controlled or removed. After 1933 a Racial Policy Office of the party, doctors, professors and civil servants put the theory into law.

### Why were the Jews persecuted? Other reasons

Race theory alone did not explain why the persecution began in 1933 and why it was so widely accepted. Several other reasons mattered.
- **Older antisemitism.** Hatred of Jews had existed in Europe for centuries in Christian teaching and in nineteenth-century political antisemitism. Jews were about 500,000 people in 1933, under one per cent of the population, mostly living in towns, and many Germans knew Jews personally while still accepting stereotypes.
- **The First World War.** The myth of the "stab in the back" blamed the defeat of 1918 on traitors at home, including Jews and socialists. In 1916 the army had counted Jewish soldiers in a "Jewish census" (*Judenzählung*) on the charge that they were shirkers, although about 100,000 Jews served and about 12,000 died. After 1918 many nationalists blamed "Jewish-Bolshevik" revolutionaries for the revolution of November 1918 and for the Treaty of Versailles.
- **The Great Depression.** In the crisis of 1929–33 the Nazis blamed "Jewish" bankers and department stores for ruin and unemployment, and used the Jews as a scapegoat for fears and resentment.
- **Opportunism and pressure.** Some Germans gained from the removal of Jews: jobs in the professions, businesses sold at low prices ("Aryanisation"), flats and property. Party radicals, such as the SA, Julius Streicher and Joseph Goebbels, pressed for more severe measures, and the regime used antisemitism to unite the party.

| 12–15 November 1938 | Decrees after Kristallnacht | A fine of 1 billion marks on the Jewish community; Jews excluded from shops and businesses; Jewish children excluded from state schools |
| January 1939 | Reich Central Office for Jewish Emigration | Forced emigration run by the state, under Göring and the SS |

The Nuremberg Laws were announced at the party rally in Nuremberg on 15 September 1935. The **Reich Citizenship Law** said that only people "of German or related blood" could be citizens, so that Jews, a "separate race", could not be full citizens, and the **Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour** banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans. A decree of 14 November 1935 defined who was a Jew by the religion of the grandparents, which meant that people who thought of themselves as Germans, and some who had been baptised, were now classed as Jews. The laws turned persecution into law.

### Kristallnacht, 9–10 November 1938

On 7 November 1938 Herschel Grynszpan, a seventeen-year-old Jewish refugee whose family had been among those expelled to Poland, shot the German diplomat Ernst vom Rath at the German embassy in Paris, and vom Rath died on 9 November. That evening, at a gathering of Nazi leaders in Munich, Goebbels gave a speech that was understood as a call for revenge, and in the night of 9–10 November the SA, party members and some others, mostly in civilian clothes, attacked Jews in Germany and in Austria.

The figures below are estimates from the Holocaust Encyclopedia of the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum (USHMM) and standard accounts.

- **Violence.** More than 1,400 synagogues and prayer rooms were destroyed or damaged, and more than 7,000 Jewish shops and businesses were smashed or looted (USHMM and standard accounts). At least **91 Jews were killed** at once, and the toll rose into the hundreds, because many died in the camps afterwards or took their own lives.
- **Arrests.** Between about 26,000 and more than 30,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to the camps at Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen, most released after weeks or months on condition that they emigrate.
- **The aftermath.** On 12 November Göring chaired a meeting at which the regime imposed a **fine of 1 billion marks** on the Jewish community (called an "atonement payment"), ordered that insurance money for damaged property go to the state, and issued decrees that drove Jews out of the economy and out of the schools.
- **Reactions.** The press abroad reported the pogrom, and it was widely condemned in Britain and the United States. In Germany some people helped their neighbours and some criticised the destruction, but the regime met no open opposition. The regime noticed that many Germans disliked the open violence, and in the following months it turned to persecution by decree.

The pogrom was a turning point. It showed that no Jew in Germany was safe, and it pushed thousands to emigrate. By the autumn of 1939 about 280,000 of the roughly 500,000 Jews of 1933 had left Germany, many of them leaving everything behind. But most countries restricted the number of refugees they would accept, and those who remained in Germany found it harder and harder to leave.

### Other minorities

The Nazis defined other groups as threats to the "health" of the people's community.

- **Homosexual men.** The Nazis saw male homosexuality as a threat to the birth rate and to the "manly" state. **Paragraph 175** of the Reich penal code, which had been in force since 1871, made sexual acts between men a crime. Gay clubs and the Berlin Institute for Sexual Science of Magnus Hirschfeld were raided and wrecked in 1933, and Röhm's homosexuality was used as part of the excuse for the Night of the Long Knives on 30 June 1934. In **1935** the law was widened, so that any "indecent" act between men could be punished, and from 1936 a Reich Central Office for the Combating of Homosexuality and Abortion co-ordinated the persecution. Estimates (which vary) are that about 100,000 men were arrested between 1933 and 1945, about 50,000 were convicted, and about 5,000–6,000 were sent to concentration camps, where many died. Lesbians were not covered by the law.
- **Jehovah's Witnesses.** They refused to give the Hitler salute, to join Nazi organisations, to swear an oath of loyalty or, after conscription began in 1935, to serve in the armed forces. Their organisation was banned in the German states in 1933 and nationally by 1935, and many were imprisoned. Estimates of the number sent to camps range from about 2,000 to about 10,000, and of the dead from about 1,200 to 5,000; about 250 were executed for refusing military service in the war. Because they could be released if they signed a declaration giving up their faith, and many would not, they showed a stubborn resistance.
- **Roma and Sinti.** Roma and Sinti ("Gypsies") were seen as "racially inferior" and as "asocial". A decree of 26 November 1935 applied the Nuremberg Laws to them, in 1936 a camp for Roma was set up at Marzahn near Berlin before the Olympic Games, and a research unit studied them as a "race". In December 1938 Himmler ordered the "fight against the Gypsy plague", leading to registration, arrests and later deportations. Their persecution in 1939–45 belongs to the later chapters.
- **Disabled people.** The Law for the Prevention of Hereditarily Diseased Offspring of 14 July 1933 (in force in January 1934) ordered compulsory sterilisation of people with conditions judged to be inherited, such as severe mental illness, epilepsy, hereditary blindness or deafness and severe alcoholism, by order of Hereditary Health Courts. More than **400,000** people were sterilised by 1945. From **1939**, in the programme known as **Aktion T4** and a parallel programme for children, disabled people in institutions were killed: the first children in 1939 and adults from the autumn of that year. About 70,000 had been killed by August 1941, when the killings were officially stopped after protests, notably the sermon of Bishop von Galen on 3 August 1941.
- **"Asocials".** People labelled work-shy, beggars, tramps, prostitutes and habitual criminals were persecuted. In June 1938, in a police action against the "work-shy", about 10,000 men were arrested and sent to concentration camps. They wore the black triangle.
- **Black Germans.** Several hundred children of German mothers and African soldiers of the French occupation army in the Rhineland, called "Rhineland bastards" by the Nazis, were sterilised in 1937.

:::key Why were minorities persecuted? The main reasons
- **Race theory.** The belief in "Aryan" superiority, Social Darwinism and eugenics, which defined Jews, Roma and Black Germans as racially harmful, and the disabled as a "burden" on the "healthy" nation.
- **Scapegoats.** For the lost war and the Depression, especially the Jews.
- **Control and conformity.** Jehovah's Witnesses, "asocials" and homosexuals were persecuted because they would not conform or did not fit the picture of the obedient, productive, child-bearing citizen.
- **Population and economic aims.** The birth-rate policy (homosexuals), the cost of care (disabled people), the labour supply (the "work-shy") and the wish for Jewish property (Aryanisation, the fine after Kristallnacht).
- **The regime's own dynamic.** Party radicals and rivals competed to propose harsher measures, and Hitler let them go on, so that the persecution moved in steps and at times in sudden bursts.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. A [30] essay gets one mark out of 15 for analysis and judgement (Table A) and one out of 15 for knowledge (Table B). Read the commentary as an examiner would.

:::eg 'Nazi policies towards young people were successful.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 2 (4–6): some understanding, descriptive, criteria implicit. Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, but the argument lacks balance; weak judgement. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained balanced argument, a clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): a good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Successful" must be defined by the regime's aims: control of organisation, indoctrination in belief, and preparation for war. Criteria: (1) control of organisations; (2) the content of education; (3) the beliefs and behaviour of the young; (4) the war aim. For: Schirach (June 1933); membership from about 108,000 (1932) to about 2.3 million (1933) to about 8.7 million (1939); the law of 1 December 1936; the decree of 25 March 1939; the new syllabus; military training. Against: membership enrolled on paper; compulsion shows lack of voluntary support; teachers' conservatism; Edelweiss Pirates and Swing Youth; parents and churches. Judgement: success in organisation and in some beliefs, less in the depth of commitment.

**A Level 2/3 paragraph (about Table A Level 2, 5 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "The Nazis were successful with young people. They set up the Hitler Youth and the BDM and most young people joined. They changed what was taught in schools so that children learnt about race. This was successful because young people grew up supporting the Nazis."

*Commentary.* Accurate and clear, and it names the main organisations. But it is a **list followed by an assertion**: no definition of "successful", no dates or figures, no mention of compulsion or of any limit. "Grew up supporting the Nazis" is asserted without evidence. Table A Level 2; Table B Level 3 for the correct but thin detail.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and give the other side.** "If the test is control of organisation, the Nazis were almost completely successful. Baldur von Schirach was made Reich Youth Leader in June 1933 and took in or closed the other youth groups, so that membership of the Hitler Youth rose from about 108,000 at the end of 1932 to about 2.3 million at the end of 1933. The law of 1 December 1936 made it the only legal youth organisation, and the Youth Service Duty decree of 25 March 1939 made membership compulsory, even over parents' objections, so that about 8.7 million were enrolled by 1939. But the need for compulsion suggests that the success was incomplete, since many joined because of pressure, and the figures count everyone enrolled, not everyone who attended. On this test the regime was successful in organisation, but organisation is not the same as belief."

*Commentary.* Now the knowledge is selected to prove a point, with a criterion and a qualification. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. It needs other criteria (what young people were taught and what they believed) and an overall judgement to reach Level 5.

**A Level 5 conclusion (and the paragraph that sets it up).**

"On the second test, the effect on young people's beliefs and behaviour, the evidence is more mixed. The schools were purged (the Civil Service law of 7 April 1933 removed Jewish and politically unreliable teachers), the syllabus was rewritten so that biology taught race theory and history taught the humiliation of 1918, and children's books such as *The Poisonous Mushroom* (1938) taught hatred of Jews. For many young people the movement was a source of adventure, status and friendship, and Kater's study of the Hitler Youth shows how strong its appeal was to many who joined. But the evidence of limits is equally real. Compulsion in 1936 and 1939 suggests that voluntary membership had levelled off, parents and teachers complained of the demands made on pupils, and some young people formed groups such as the Edelweiss Pirates and the Swing Youth that rejected the regime's control. Peukert argued that the regime's hold on the young was weaker than its image.

Overall, the Nazis were very successful in controlling the organisation of young people, quite successful in shaping what they were taught, and less demonstrably successful in changing what they believed. The judgement depends on the test: by organisation the success was almost total; by belief, it was partial. The more important test is the second, because the regime's aim was to make a generation that would think as Nazis, and six years before the war was too short a time to know whether it had succeeded. The outbreak of the war in 1939 means that the best evidence of success, how the generation behaved as soldiers, lies outside the period."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies **criteria** (organisation, content, belief), weighs them, uses historians to argue instead of to list, and reaches a supported judgement. With the earlier paragraph on organisation, this is Level 5: Table A 14, Table B 13, about 27 out of 30.
:::

:::eg Assess the reasons for the persecution of minorities in Nazi Germany, 1933–39 [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the reasons" asks you to weigh *why* it happened and which reasons mattered most, so you need a range of reasons, links between them, criteria for ranking them, and a judgement.

**Plan (4 minutes).** Reasons: (1) race theory (Social Darwinism, eugenics, *Mein Kampf*); (2) scapegoating (the stab in the back, the Depression); (3) the nature of the regime (party radicals, competition, Hitler's tolerance); (4) practical gain (Aryanisation, the fine of November 1938; the birth rate; the cost of care); (5) control and conformity (Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexuals, "asocials"). Evidence: boycott (1 April 1933), Civil Service law (7 April 1933), sterilisation law (14 July 1933), Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935), Paragraph 175 (1935), Aryanisation, Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938), the decrees of 12 November. Judgement: race ideology was the main cause, but the timing and form of persecution depended on the other reasons.

**A Level 3 paragraph (about Table A Level 3, 7 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "The Nazis persecuted minorities because of their race theory. They believed in the Aryan race and thought that Jews and others were inferior. They passed the Nuremberg Laws in 1935 and had Kristallnacht in 1938. They also persecuted homosexuals and Jehovah's Witnesses. So the main reason was racism."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with some dates, but it states one reason and lists events. It does not explain why race theory led to action in 1933–39, does not weigh other reasons, and treats different minorities as one. Table A Level 3; Table B Level 3.

**Moving to Level 4: a range of reasons and criteria.** "Race theory explains the aims of the persecution, but not all of its timing or form. The sterilisation law of 14 July 1933, for example, drew on eugenics, a belief that was not only Nazi (Francis Galton had founded eugenics in the 1880s) and which targeted people judged to have inherited illnesses, of whom more than 400,000 were sterilised. The persecution of Jews drew on race theory too, but also on the memory of the 'stab in the back' of 1918, the scapegoating of Jews in the Depression and the gains made by non-Jews from Aryanisation. Other groups, such as Jehovah's Witnesses, were persecuted for what they did, not for their 'race': they refused the Hitler salute and military service after 1935."

*Commentary.* A good Level 4 paragraph: several reasons applied to different groups, with a distinction between race and behaviour. To reach Level 5, the essay would rank the reasons with a stated criterion and show how they interacted.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The main reason for the persecution of minorities in Nazi Germany between 1933 and 1939 was the regime's racial ideology, but ideology does not explain why the persecution took the forms and had the timing that it did. A full explanation needs three further reasons: the use of minorities as scapegoats, the practical gains that the regime and many Germans drew from persecution, and the way in which the regime itself worked, with party radicals pressing for harsher measures while Hitler allowed them to go on. The best judgement is that ideology set the direction, and the other reasons set the pace and the targets.

The case for ideology as the main reason is strong. Hitler's *Mein Kampf* put the Aryan race at the top of a hierarchy and the Jews at the bottom, and the party's programme was built on the idea of a pure *Volksgemeinschaft*. Social Darwinism, the idea that nations and races struggle for survival, and eugenics, the idea that a population can be improved by selective breeding, gave the ideas a scientific cover that many doctors and civil servants accepted. These ideas explain the laws: the sterilisation law of 14 July 1933, under which more than 400,000 people with supposed hereditary illnesses were sterilised, the Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935, which took citizenship from Jews and banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans, the decree of 26 November 1935 which applied the same laws to Roma, and the later campaigns against 'asocials' (about 10,000 men sent to camps in June 1938). Without the belief that people could be sorted by 'blood', none of these laws would have been written.

But ideology alone does not explain the timing, or why so many Germans went along. Antisemitism was older than the Nazis, and it had gained new force from the defeat of 1918. The 'stab in the back' myth blamed Jews and socialists for the defeat, although about 100,000 Jews had served and about 12,000 had died, and in the Depression the Nazis blamed Jewish bankers and department stores for ruin. This explains why the first measures of 1933, the boycott of 1 April and the Civil Service law of 7 April, were aimed at Jews. Practical gain mattered too. As Jews were driven out of the professions and their businesses were sold at low prices in the 'Aryanisation' of 1936–38, others got their jobs and property, and after Kristallnacht the regime itself took 1 billion marks by a fine on the Jewish community, at a time when rearmament was straining the finances. Birth-rate policy explains why homosexual men were prosecuted with more force after Paragraph 175 was widened in 1935, and why the disabled were sterilised, since the regime considered them a burden on a nation that needed healthy children and soldiers.

The way the regime worked explains the stages. There was no single plan from 1933 to 1939. The boycott of 1933 was started by the party but called off after a day, partly because it had little support among ordinary Germans. The Nuremberg Laws were drawn up in a hurry at the Nuremberg rally in 1935, under pressure from party members who demanded action. In 1936 the regime held back during the Olympic Games to avoid foreign criticism, and in 1938, after the Anschluss and with the Four Year Plan in need of money and raw materials, it pressed harder, culminating in Kristallnacht on 9–10 November 1938, in which more than 1,400 synagogues were destroyed or damaged, about 26,000 to 30,000 men were arrested and at least 91 people were killed at once. This pattern of bursts of violence from below and legal measures from above, with Hitler approving but not always leading, is why Evans and others describe a radicalisation in which party members and officials competed with each other in anticipating what Hitler wanted. Friedländer's view that Hitler's own 'redemptive antisemitism' was the driving force fits the direction, if not every step.

In conclusion, race theory was the main reason, because it chose the victims and made persecution a duty. But the stages and the scale depended on scapegoating (the memory of 1918 and the Depression), on gain (Aryanisation, the fine of 1938, the cost of care) and on a system in which party radicals pushed and Hitler let them. The reasons also differed between groups: for Jews and Roma the cause was race, for Jehovah's Witnesses their refusal to obey, and for the disabled a mixture of eugenics and cost. The more important test of 'main reason' is whether the persecution would have happened without it, and the answer is that it would not; the other reasons would not have produced it by themselves."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): the answer gives a range of reasons, ranks them against a stated test (would it have happened without it?), explains how they link (ideology sets the direction, other reasons the pace), distinguishes the minorities, uses historians for argument and reaches a supported judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates (1 April 1933, 7 April 1933, 14 July 1933, 15 September 1935, 26 November 1935, 9–10 November 1938), figures (400,000; about 10,000; 1,400; 26,000 to 30,000; 91) and named laws used for the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 800–900 words in 40 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Assess the impact of Nazi policies on women in the years 1933–39 [30] (plan and Level 4 paragraph)
**Plan.** Criteria: (1) work; (2) the family and birth rate; (3) political and legal position; (4) the regime's aims. Change: marriage loans (1 June 1933); dismissals; women excluded from the judiciary (1936); the Mother's Cross (16 December 1938). Continuity or reversal: rearmament and the labour shortage (1936–37); the loan condition dropped (1937); the *Pflichtjahr* (1938); about 14 million women in paid work by 1939. Judgement: the ideology was conservative, but the practical effects were limited and partly reversed.

**A Level 4 paragraph.** "Nazi policy towards women's work was a contradiction. From 1933 the regime wanted women out of work: the Law for the Reduction of Unemployment of 1 June 1933 gave marriage loans of up to 1,000 marks only if the wife gave up her job, and from 1936 women could no longer be judges or prosecutors. But rearmament created a shortage of labour from 1936–37, so the loan condition was dropped in 1937, a duty year was imposed on young women in 1938, and about 14 million women were in work by 1939. The regime's ideology was Kinder, Küche, Kirche, but its economic needs pushed women into the factory, and in 1939 it still refused to conscript married women for war work, which shows how far the ideology held."

*Commentary.* A good Level 4 paragraph: it applies a criterion (work), uses precise knowledge and reaches a mini-judgement through a contradiction. To reach Level 5, the essay would compare work with the family and birth rate (which rose, but for disputed reasons), consider different groups of women (married and unmarried, "Aryan" and persecuted) and use Stephenson and Koonz to argue.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Evaluate the view that the Nazis failed to change the religious beliefs of the German people." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Nazis tried to destroy the churches but they failed. Hitler signed a Concordat with the Pope. There was a Reich Church and the Confessing Church. Many Germans stayed Christian. So the Nazis failed to change their beliefs."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): relevant and in the right direction, but it is a **list with an assertion**. "Tried to destroy the churches" is not what the regime did in the 1930s (it weakened them but avoided open war), the Concordat is mentioned with no date or purpose, and the Reich Church is mentioned without saying that it failed. There is no criterion for "beliefs" and no evidence on the other side. Table B Level 2–3: some relevant names, but no dates, no figures and some imprecision.

**The fix.** Define the test and weigh the evidence: "The Nazis changed the *place* of religion in German life more than the *beliefs* of believers. The Concordat of 20 July 1933 took the Catholic Church out of politics and the Centre Party dissolved on 5 July, and in the Protestant churches the German Christians won about two thirds of the votes on 23 July 1933 and Müller became Reich Bishop on 27 September 1933. But the Reich Church failed: the Pastors' Emergency League, the Barmen Synod of 29–31 May 1934 and Pope Pius XI's encyclical *Mit brennender Sorge* of 14 March 1937 show that many believers would not accept Nazi control of the faith, and the German Faith Movement stayed small. Church schools and youth groups were closed, so the Nazis reduced the churches' public influence, but there is no evidence that most Germans stopped being Christians." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

Q: What did Hitler say about women's role in 1934, and how did Nazi policy try to reduce women's work in 1933?
A: In a speech to the National Socialist Women's League (8 September 1934) he said that a woman's world was her husband, her family, her children and her home. In 1933 the marriage loan (up to 1,000 marks, from the law of 1 June 1933) required the wife to leave work, and married women could be dismissed from the civil service.
Q: What were the Mother's Cross and the Lebensborn?
A: The Mother's Cross (established 16 December 1938, first awarded May 1939) was a bronze, silver or gold medal for mothers of four or five, six or seven, and eight or more children. The Lebensborn (December 1935) was an SS association running maternity homes for children thought to be racially valuable.
Q: Why did attitudes to women's work change from 1937?
A: Rearmament and the Four Year Plan created a labour shortage, so the marriage-loan condition was dropped (1937), women were urged to take jobs and a duty year (*Pflichtjahr*) was introduced for young women (1938). About 14 million women were in paid work by 1939.
Q: What was the Hitler Youth, and how did membership become compulsory?
A: The Nazi youth organisation, led from 1933 by Baldur von Schirach, with a parallel BDM for girls. Other youth groups were taken over or closed. The law of 1 December 1936 made it the only legal youth organisation, and the decree of 25 March 1939 made membership compulsory. About 8.7 million were enrolled in 1939.
Q: Name three changes to schools under the Nazis.
A: Jewish and politically unreliable teachers were dismissed (7 April 1933) and teachers joined the NSLB; biology taught race theory, and history and other subjects were reshaped, with new textbooks such as *The Poisonous Mushroom* (1938); and new schools (Napolas from 1933, Adolf Hitler Schools from 1937) trained an elite.
Q: What was the Concordat of 1933 and how did the Nazis treat it?
A: An agreement of 20 July 1933 with the Vatican that guaranteed the Catholic Church's worship, schools and organisations in return for the Church's withdrawal from politics (the Centre Party dissolved on 5 July). The Nazis broke it with arrests, trials of priests and the closing of Catholic groups, and Pius XI protested in *Mit brennender Sorge* (14 March 1937).
Q: Who were the German Christians and the Confessing Church?
A: The German Christians mixed Christianity with Nazi ideas and won about two thirds of the votes at the church elections of 23 July 1933; Ludwig Müller was their Reich Bishop. The Confessing Church, formed at the Barmen Synod (29–31 May 1934) with leaders such as Niemöller and Barth, rejected Nazi control of the Church.
Q: What did the Nuremberg Laws do?
A: On 15 September 1935 the Reich Citizenship Law took citizenship from Jews, and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans. A decree of 14 November 1935 defined a Jew as a person with three or four Jewish grandparents.
Q: What happened on 9–10 November 1938, and what followed?
A: Kristallnacht: after Ernst vom Rath's death, the SA and party members attacked Jews and Jewish property in Germany and Austria, destroying more than 1,400 synagogues and prayer rooms and smashing more than 7,000 businesses. At least 91 people were killed and about 26,000 to 30,000 men were sent to camps. On 12 November a fine of 1 billion marks was imposed on Jews and decrees excluded them from business and from the schools.
Q: Name three other minorities persecuted by the Nazis and one reason for each.
A: Homosexual men (a threat to the birth rate; Paragraph 175 widened in 1935); Jehovah's Witnesses (they refused the salute, oaths and military service); disabled people (eugenics: the sterilisation law of 14 July 1933 and later the T4 killings). Others: Roma and Sinti (race), "asocials" (work-shy) and Black Germans.

Stages: the boycott and Civil Service law (April 1933); the Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935); Aryanisation and the licence withdrawals (1936–38); the Anschluss violence (March 1938); the J stamp and the Polish expulsion (October 1938); Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938) and the decrees after it. Level 5 shows both the escalation and the pauses (the 1936 Olympics) and explains them.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Race theory was the main reason why minorities were persecuted in Nazi Germany.' Evaluate this view.
H: Treat Jews, disabled people and Jehovah's Witnesses separately before you judge.
H: What other reasons are there: scapegoating, gain, the nature of the regime?
A: Indicative content. Race theory: *Mein Kampf*, Social Darwinism and eugenics; the Nuremberg Laws; sterilisation (400,000 by 1945). Other reasons: the "stab in the back" and the Depression; Aryanisation and the fine of November 1938; the birth-rate and the cost of care; the refusal of Jehovah's Witnesses to obey; party radicals. Level 5 ranks the reasons and shows how they link.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the Nazis transformed German society more in appearance than in reality.
H: Choose a test of "reality": organisations, laws, practice or belief.
H: Use at least three of the four areas: women, young people, religion, persecution.
A: Indicative content. Appearance: the Hitler Youth statistics (nominal membership); the propaganda of the mother and the housewife; the Reich Church. Reality: the destruction of the Jews' position in German life; sterilisation (over 400,000); the schools; the compulsion of 1936 and 1939. Mixed: the churches' survival; women's work. Level 5 judges area by area against a stated test, perhaps judging that the change was real for the persecuted and the young and limited for women and the churches.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Kristallnacht marked a turning point in the Nazi persecution of the Jews.' Evaluate this view.
H: What was new about it, and what was not?
H: Compare 1933–35 with 1938–39.
A: Indicative content. Turning point: the scale of violence in one night; the arrest of 26,000 to 30,000 men; the fine of 1 billion marks; the decrees of 12–15 November excluding Jews from business and schools; forced emigration run by the state (January 1939). Continuity: the earlier stages (boycott, Nuremberg Laws, Aryanisation); the radicalisation after the Anschluss; the way the persecution moved in steps. Level 5 reaches a judgement with a criterion (new methods, scale, aim).
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Nazi social policy was driven more by the needs of the regime than by ideology.' How far does the evidence on women, young people and minorities in 1933–39 support this view?
H: What would count as an "ideological" policy and what as a "need of the regime"?
H: Look for places where the two clashed.
A: Support: women's work reversed because of rearmament (1937, 1938); the marriage loan and the aim of fewer unemployed; the Hitler Youth as a source of soldiers; Aryanisation and the fine of 1 billion marks as sources of money; the labour needs that shaped the persecution of "asocials". Challenge: ideology set the targets (race theory, eugenics; Nuremberg Laws; sterilisation); the regime refused to conscript married women in 1939 despite needs; Kristallnacht and the persecution of the disabled had little economic gain. A* judgement says that ideology fixed the goals and needs shaped the means and pace, and tests this on at least two areas.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Compare the effects of Nazi policy on the churches with its effects on young people. In which was the regime more successful?
H: Define success for each: control or belief?
H: Use dates and evidence for both.
A: Young people: control by the Hitler Youth (1936, 1939), the schools; limits in belief. Churches: Concordat (1933), the Reich Church failure, Barmen (1934), *Mit brennender Sorge* (1937); institutions weakened, beliefs mostly intact. A* judgement says that the regime was more successful with the young, who were its own to organise from 1933, than with the churches, which had independent institutions and a loyalty that predated the regime, while noting that for neither group can belief be measured before 1939.
:::

:::recap
- **Women**: Kinder, Küche, Kirche; Hitler's speech of 8 September 1934; marriage loans (1 June 1933, up to 1,000 marks, quarter cancelled per child); Lebensborn (December 1935); Mother's Cross (16 December 1938); birth rate 14.7 (1933) to 20.3 (1939) per thousand; loan condition dropped 1937; *Pflichtjahr* 1938; about 14 million women in work by 1939.
- **Education**: Civil Service law (7 April 1933); NSLB; biology and history reshaped; *The Poisonous Mushroom* (1938); Napolas (1933); Adolf Hitler Schools (1937); book burning (10 May 1933).
- **Youth**: Hitler Youth (Schirach, June 1933); about 108,000 (1932), 2.3 million (1933), 5.4 million (1936), 8.7 million (1939); law of 1 December 1936; Youth Service Duty decree of 25 March 1939; BDM; military training; Kater (2004), Peukert.
- **Religion**: Centre Party dissolved (5 July 1933); Concordat (20 July 1933); German Christians (23 July 1933 election); Müller, Reich Bishop (27 September 1933); Sports Palace (13 November 1933); Barmen (29–31 May 1934); Kerrl (1935); *Mit brennender Sorge* (14 March 1937); Niemöller arrested (1 July 1937); German Faith Movement (Hauer).
- **Race theory**: Social Darwinism, eugenics, *Mein Kampf*; other reasons: the stab in the back, the First World War, the Depression, gain.
- **Persecution of Jews**: boycott (1 April 1933); Civil Service law (7 April 1933); Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935; decree of 14 November); Aryanisation; Anschluss (March 1938); J stamp (5 October 1938); Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938: more than 1,400 synagogues, more than 7,000 businesses, at least 91 killed, about 26,000 to 30,000 arrested, fine of 1 billion marks); about 280,000 emigrated by 1939.
- **Other minorities**: homosexual men (Paragraph 175 widened 1935; about 100,000 arrested, about 5,000–6,000 in camps); Jehovah's Witnesses; Roma and Sinti (decree of 26 November 1935; Marzahn 1936; December 1938); disabled people (sterilisation law 14 July 1933, over 400,000; Aktion T4 from 1939); "asocials" (June 1938).
- **Judgement**: transformation was greatest in the organisation of young people and in the exclusion and persecution of minorities; it was limited for women, whose work was needed, and for the churches, whose believers largely held to their faith.
