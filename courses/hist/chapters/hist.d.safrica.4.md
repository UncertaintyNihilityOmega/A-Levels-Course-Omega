:::why
In November 1965 a small white government in a British colony in central Africa declared itself independent so that it would not have to share power with the African majority. Britain called it a rebellion but did not fight it. For fourteen years the regime survived sanctions and a guerrilla war, and then in 1979 it agreed to a settlement in London and in 1980 the state of Zimbabwe was born under a Black prime minister, Robert Mugabe. The story matters for Paper 4 because it is a second case of minority rule ending, one that came sooner and by a different route than in South Africa, and because it raises the question of who ended it: Britain, the guerrillas, South Africa, the frontline states or the Rhodesians themselves. You need the events in order and a way of weighing the causes.
:::

## What is the question really asking?

The key question asks *how the ending was achieved*, which is a question about causes and about the **process**: a declaration of independence, a long war, two failed settlements and a third that worked. Sort the causes into five groups and test each.
1. **The guerrilla war.** Did the fighting force the government to concede, and how?
2. **Britain's policies.** Sanctions, the refusal to use force, the settlement attempts and, in 1979, Lancaster House.
3. **The role of other states.** South Africa, Mozambique, Zambia, the other frontline states, the USA, the USSR and China.
4. **The economy and white morale.** The effects of sanctions and of conscription and emigration.
5. **Leadership and politics.** Smith's decisions, the divisions among the nationalists, the choices of Mugabe and Nkomo, and the diplomacy of Carrington.

:::def Unilateral Declaration of Independence (UDI)
The declaration made by the Rhodesian government under Ian Smith on 11 November 1965, announcing that Rhodesia was independent of Britain without Britain's agreement. Britain and almost every other state treated it as illegal, and no country formally recognised Rhodesia.
:::

:::def NIBMAR
"No independence before majority African rule": the principle that Britain would not grant independence to Rhodesia until the African majority could govern. African and Asian Commonwealth members insisted on it after UDI, and British governments of both parties moved towards it, although they did not always say so in those words.
:::

:::def Patriotic Front
The alliance formed in October 1976 by Robert Mugabe's ZANU and Joshua Nkomo's ZAPU to negotiate together, and to fight together in principle. They remained two parties with separate armies, ZANLA and ZIPRA, and in 1980 they stood against each other in the election.
:::

## Background: a colony ruled by its settlers

### Southern Rhodesia

Southern Rhodesia was named after Cecil Rhodes, whose British South Africa Company occupied it in the 1890s (see [Imperialism and world powers (1): why imperialism was a significant force in Africa from 1870](#hist.int.empire.1)). White settlers had been given self-government in 1923, which meant that Britain left most decisions to a parliament elected by a voting roll that was almost entirely white. By the mid-1960s there were about 4 million Africans and about 220,000 Europeans, roughly 5 per cent of the population. The settlers held most of the land through the Land Apportionment Act of 1930, which reserved about half of it, including the best farmland, for whites; the rest was shared between the African reserves and other uses. The Land Tenure Act of 1969 divided the land into two roughly equal halves, one for each group. Africans were also excluded from skilled jobs and from the vote in practice, since the franchise had property and education qualifications.

### The Federation and the rise of African nationalism

From 1953 to 1963 Southern Rhodesia was joined with Northern Rhodesia and Nyasaland in the **Federation of Rhodesia and Nyasaland**, which white leaders hoped would be a "partnership" under white control. African opinion in the north saw it as a way of extending white rule. Britain dissolved it at the end of 1963, and Nyasaland became independent as Malawi in July 1964 and Northern Rhodesia as Zambia in October 1964, under Black governments. The white leaders of Southern Rhodesia were left to ask the British government for the independence that they felt they were owed, since they had governed themselves for forty years.

African nationalism had grown in the same years. The African National Congress of Southern Rhodesia (1957) was banned in 1959, and its successor, the National Democratic Party (1960), in 1961. **ZAPU**, the Zimbabwe African People's Union, was founded in December 1961 under **Joshua Nkomo**. In August 1963 a group led by the Reverend **Ndabaningi Sithole**, with **Robert Mugabe** as secretary-general, broke away to form **ZANU**, the Zimbabwe African National Union. The split came from disputes about Nkomo's leadership and tactics, and it had an ethnic side, since Nkomo's support was strongest among the Ndebele of the west and Sithole's and Mugabe's among the Shona of the north and east, who were by far the larger group. ZAPU was banned in 1962 and ZANU in 1964, and their leaders were detained, Mugabe for ten years until 1974.

### The Rhodesian Front and Ian Smith

In December 1962 white voters elected the **Rhodesian Front (RF)**, a new party formed that year to defend white rule against the pressure for change. Its first leader, Winston Field, was replaced by **Ian Smith**, a farmer and former Royal Air Force fighter pilot, on **13 April 1964**, when Field failed to persuade Britain to grant independence. Smith, who was born in Rhodesia, was a man of plain speech, popular with whites, and he led a government that held that Africans were not yet ready for majority rule and that "standards" had to be kept up.

## Demanding independence, 1964–65

### The dispute

White Rhodesia wanted independence on the **1961 constitution**, which gave Africans a small number of seats and kept power with the whites for the foreseeable future. Britain would not grant it on those terms. After Labour's election victory in October 1964, the new Prime Minister, **Harold Wilson**, set out what became known as the **Five Principles**: unimpeded progress to majority rule; guarantees against any change that would reverse it; an improvement in the political status of the African population; progress towards ending racial discrimination; and an assurance that any basis for independence was acceptable to the people of Rhodesia as a whole. The last was the sticking-point. Smith said it was already satisfied, since he had an **indaba** (a meeting) of chiefs and headmen in October 1964 and a referendum of the mostly white electorate on **5 November 1964**, in which 89 per cent voted yes. African nationalists, whose leaders were in detention, were not consulted, and Britain did not accept that this was the "people as a whole". In May 1965 the Rhodesian Front won all 50 of the "A roll" seats in a general election.

### The talks

Talks went on through 1965 and failed. Wilson offered a Royal Commission to test African opinion and suggested that Britain should withdraw its powers over legislation, and Smith refused; Wilson went to Salisbury himself at the end of October. A British government with a small majority in the House of Commons, and the Commonwealth's African members pressing it, could not accept a settlement that left the African majority without a clear path to power. Smith, for his part, believed that Britain would not use force and that sanctions, if they came, would fail, as the actions against South Africa and Portugal's empire suggested, and he was under pressure from his own right wing.

### UDI, 11 November 1965

On **11 November 1965**, at 11 am, during the silence kept for Armistice Day, Smith's cabinet signed the **Unilateral Declaration of Independence**. The text was modelled on the American Declaration of Independence of 1776, and it kept loyalty to Queen Elizabeth II: Smith claimed that his government was acting to defend justice, civilisation and Christianity, and not to rebel against the Crown. The Governor, Sir Humphrey Gibbs, dismissed the ministers, and they ignored him; he stayed at Government House for four years as the Queen's representative, still treated as such by Britain.

### Reactions

**Rhodesians.** Most whites supported the declaration, and the RF won the general elections of 1970 and 1974 by large majorities. The government declared a state of emergency, which it renewed each year, and it censored the press. Africans had no say. Many had been detained; others were angry, but with ZAPU and ZANU banned, their leaders in prison or abroad and the security forces strong, there was no way to resist openly. Church leaders and some Africans spoke against UDI, and the more radical nationalists began to prepare for armed struggle.

**Britain.** Wilson called the declaration illegal and the Smith regime a rebel one. He announced economic measures at once, among them a ban on Rhodesian sugar and tobacco and, in December 1965, an **oil embargo**, and he is reported to have told the Commonwealth, in January 1966, that sanctions would end the rebellion in "weeks rather than months". But he ruled out the use of force. The reasons were practical and political. Rhodesia was landlocked, a long way from Britain, and had its own well-trained army and air force; British troops would have had to fight "kith and kin", many of them British-born, and ministers and military advisers doubted that such a war could be won quickly; Zambia, a Black state, was dependent on Rhodesian railways and power; and the British government was reluctant to start a war in Africa. To the African states of the Commonwealth, this decision was a betrayal, and African anger at Britain's refusal to use force lasted until 1980.

**The United Nations.** The General Assembly and the Security Council condemned UDI at once (Resolution 217 of 20 November 1965). In April 1966, **Resolution 221** allowed Britain to use force to stop oil tankers from reaching Rhodesia through the port of Beira, in Portuguese Mozambique, and the Royal Navy carried out the **Beira Patrol** from March 1966 to June 1975. On **16 December 1966** the Security Council passed **Resolution 232**, which made sanctions on a list of Rhodesian exports and imports **mandatory**: it was the first time that the UN had imposed mandatory economic sanctions. In May 1968 **Resolution 253** extended them to almost all trade.

**No recognition.** No state formally recognised Rhodesia, although Portugal and South Africa traded with it and kept consular offices.

### Did sanctions work?

:::key Why sanctions did not end the Smith regime quickly
1. **South Africa and Portugal.** Rhodesia's oil, goods and exports went through South Africa and, until 1975, through Mozambique. Their governments, which had their own reasons for backing white rule, ignored the UN's measures.
2. **Smuggling and "sanctions-busting".** Companies in other countries sold to Rhodesia through third parties, and in the USA the **Byrd Amendment** of 1971 allowed the import of Rhodesian chrome until Congress repealed it in March 1977.
3. **Import substitution.** Rhodesia made at home many of the goods it could no longer import and, in the late 1960s and early 1970s, its economy grew.
4. **Britain would not use force** and the Beira Patrol only covered one route.
5. **They hurt Africans too.** The poorest people lost most when the economy was cut off, and the regime blamed outside enemies, which gave it a cause to rally its supporters.
:::

But sanctions were not without effect. They denied Rhodesia the foreign capital and equipment that it needed, drove up costs, cut the earnings of its main exports (tobacco and minerals), and left it dependent on South Africa. After 1974 the effect of the oil crisis, the war and the loss of Mozambique's ports made the economy shrink. Sanctions mattered less as a way of making the government give in at once than as a weight that made the long war harder to bear.

## Talks that failed, 1966–72

Britain did not give up looking for a settlement, and the attempts show what each side would accept.
- **HMS *Tiger*, 2–4 December 1966.** Wilson and Smith met on a Royal Navy cruiser off Gibraltar. The terms would have returned Rhodesia to legality under a transitional government in which the Governor had real powers, and they included independence on a new constitution with a slow route to majority rule. Smith took the terms home, and the Rhodesian cabinet rejected them on 5 December. On the same day Wilson told the House of Commons that Britain would ask the UN for mandatory sanctions and would not now agree to independence before majority rule.
- **HMS *Fearless*, October 1968.** A second round, again off Gibraltar. Smith again refused to accept guarantees that would let Britain or the courts reverse an unfair constitution, and no agreement followed.
- **The republic, 1970.** After a white referendum in 1969, Rhodesia adopted a republican constitution and was declared a republic on **2 March 1970**, cutting its last link with the Crown. The new constitution gave Africans a small number of seats in a house with white control, and did not allow majority rule even in the long term. The Land Tenure Act (1969) made the division of land between the races permanent.
- **The Home–Smith proposals, November 1971.** With a Conservative government in Britain, the Foreign Secretary, Sir Alec Douglas-Home, and Smith reached an agreement that would have given Africans more seats over many years, with a slow path towards majority rule, in return for British recognition. Britain insisted that the "people of Rhodesia as a whole" should be asked, and sent the **Pearce Commission**, led by Lord Pearce, which toured the country from January to March 1972.
- **The Pearce Commission, 1972.** African opinion, led by the new **African National Council (ANC)** of **Bishop Abel Muzorewa**, a Methodist bishop who was chosen to lead the campaign against the proposals, spoke out against them. The commission reported in May 1972 that most Europeans accepted the terms and that most Africans rejected them, so that the condition of the Five Principles was not met. It was the first time that Africans had been asked, and the result showed that the government's claim that Africans accepted white rule was false. Britain dropped the plan, and the RF government had nothing to offer except a continued war.

:::key What the failed talks showed
1. **The sides were too far apart.** Smith would not give a clear path to majority rule, and Britain could not accept a settlement that did not.
2. **Britain's position hardened** between 1966 and 1972, towards majority rule as the condition for any settlement.
3. **Africans had to be consulted.** The Pearce Commission of 1972 made it clear that no settlement made without the nationalists would hold, which led Britain and others to deal with them later.
4. **They showed British weakness.** Britain could stop a settlement but could not impose one without using force, and so the war was left to the Rhodesians and the guerrillas.
:::

## The civil war, 1964–79

### The aims and methods of the Rhodesian government

The Rhodesian Front's aim was to keep **white political control** of the country for the foreseeable future, which it described as the maintenance of "civilised standards" and of "partnership" in which Africans would advance only as fast as the government judged them ready. Its methods, over fifteen years, were these.
- **Laws and detention.** The Law and Order (Maintenance) Act and emergency regulations allowed the banning of organisations, detention without trial, and, from the late 1960s, the death penalty for acts of terrorism. Nkomo, Mugabe and Sithole and hundreds of others were detained for ten years or more.
- **Strong security forces.** A well-trained army (including the Rhodesian African Rifles, whose African soldiers served under white officers), the Rhodesian Light Infantry, the Special Air Service, the British South Africa Police and a powerful air force. Conscription of white men was lengthened, to 18 months by 1976, and reservists were called up again and again; from 1979 Black men were also conscripted. From 1967 to 1975 units of the **South African Police** fought in Rhodesia.
- **Special forces.** The **Selous Scouts**, formed in 1973, were a mixed unit that included Black soldiers and white officers and often operated in disguise in the countryside, to find guerrillas and to carry out raids; they were involved in some of the most notorious actions of the war.
- **Control of the countryside.** From 1973–74 the government moved large numbers of Africans in the war zones into **protected villages** (or "keeps") surrounded by fences, so that guerrillas could not get food or help from them. Conditions were poor, and the policy turned many villagers against the government.
- **Raids across the borders.** After Mozambique became independent in 1975, Rhodesian forces attacked guerrilla camps and refugee camps in Mozambique and Zambia. The raid on Nyadzonya in Mozambique in August 1976 killed hundreds of people, and Rhodesia said that they were guerrillas; Mozambique said that they were refugees. Rhodesia carried out other large attacks in 1977–79.
- **Propaganda and reform.** It tried to win African support through chiefs, development schemes and the promise of limited political rights. The **Internal Settlement** (see below) was the last of these.

### The nationalist movements

| | ZAPU and ZIPRA | ZANU and ZANLA |
|---|---|---|
| Founded | ZAPU December 1961; its army, the Zimbabwe People's Revolutionary Army (ZIPRA) | ZANU August 1963 (split from ZAPU); its army, the Zimbabwe African National Liberation Army (ZANLA) |
| Leader | Joshua Nkomo | Ndabaningi Sithole at first; Robert Mugabe emerged as leader in the mid-1970s |
| Main support | The Ndebele of the west and south-west (Matabeleland) | The Shona of the north and east |
| Outside backers | The USSR (arms and training), Zambia (bases), Cuba and other states | China (arms and training), Tanzania and, after 1975, Mozambique (bases) |
| Strategy | A more conventional army, with the aim of a larger force that could take the field | "People's war": political work in villages, small groups of guerrillas living among peasants |
| Role in the war | Wankie 1967 (with the ANC of South Africa); raids from Zambia; built its army for a final offensive | Chinhoyi 1966; the offensive from the east from 1972 and especially after 1975 |

Both movements were banned and had to operate from abroad, and the rivalry between them was bitter. In 1975 the ZANU chairman, Herbert Chitepo, was killed by a bomb in Lusaka, and the quarrels inside ZANU that followed gave Mugabe, who had been freed from detention in November 1974 and went to Mozambique in 1975, the chance to take over its leadership. The frontline states (Zambia, Tanzania, Mozambique and Botswana) tried again and again to bring the two together, and in October 1976 they formed the **Patriotic Front**.

### The nature of the fighting

The war had two phases.
1. **1966–72: small-scale incursions.** The first clash came on **28 April 1966** at **Chinhoyi** (Sinoia), where Rhodesian forces killed seven ZANLA fighters in the first major engagement, and the second the Wankie campaign of 1967–68, when ZIPRA and the South African ANC's MK sent a mixed force from Zambia (see [End of minority rule (2)](#hist.d.safrica.2)). Rhodesian forces defeated both, and the regime felt confident.
2. **From December 1972: a real war.** On **21 December 1972** ZANLA attacked Altena Farm in the north-east, and the attacks that followed marked the start of a longer and larger war in the north-east. Rhodesia's control of the countryside slowly weakened. The decisive change came after the Portuguese revolution of 1974: when Mozambique became independent under Frelimo on **25 June 1975**, Rhodesia faced a hostile neighbour with a border of about 1,300 kilometres, and ZANLA could enter almost anywhere on the east. Mozambique's President, Samora Machel, closed the border in March 1976, which cost Rhodesia its route through Mozambique's ports. By 1979 ZANLA and ZIPRA had tens of thousands of men under arms, the government held the cities and the main roads, and large areas of the countryside at night were controlled by the guerrillas.

The war was largely fought in rural areas, where civilians were caught between the two sides. Landmines killed many people, and guerrillas and government forces both attacked villagers whom they suspected of helping the other side. Schools and mission stations were attacked or closed, the government declared martial law in many areas in 1978, and hundreds of thousands of rural people were displaced. Guerrillas shot down two civilian airliners, Air Rhodesia Viscounts, near Kariba, with missiles, in September 1978 and February 1979; the first attack killed 38 people and ten survivors were murdered on the ground, and the second killed 59. These attacks shocked many white Rhodesians, as did the killing of missionaries and farmers, and pushed many to leave. Estimates of the total number killed in the war run from about 20,000 to 30,000, most of them Africans, and the war left displaced families, refugees and a legacy of bitterness.

:::key Why the war mattered
1. **It ended the regime's confidence.** The early incursions had been defeated, but the war after 1972 was not one that the Rhodesian army could win, since it had no way to stop guerrillas entering from Mozambique, and the cost in men, money and morale kept growing.
2. **It strained the white population.** Conscription lengthened, reservists left their jobs and farms for months, the economy shrank and, from 1976, more whites left Rhodesia each year than arrived.
3. **It made the nationalists' case.** The war showed that the Rhodesian government could not keep the African majority out of politics by force.
4. **It could not end the war alone.** The guerrillas did not win the war: the security forces held the cities, were never defeated in a major battle and killed many guerrillas. What the war did was to make a political settlement the only way out.
5. **It brought in outside powers.** China, the USSR and Cuba helped the guerrillas, South Africa and others the regime, and the more the war became part of the Cold War, the more Britain and the USA wanted a settlement that would exclude Soviet influence.
:::

## The pressure of other states, 1974–76

### South Africa, Mozambique and the frontline states

For nine years Rhodesia relied on South Africa. John Vorster's government sold it oil, bought its goods and from 1967 sent police units. But in 1974–75 South Africa's own position changed (see [End of minority rule (2)](#hist.d.safrica.2)). After the Portuguese collapse, Vorster, who feared that the Rhodesian war would spread, pursued a policy of **détente** in southern Africa, working with Zambia's President Kenneth Kaunda to get Smith and the nationalists to talk. In August 1975 Smith, Nkomo, Sithole and others met on a railway bridge at the Victoria Falls, in a train compartment that straddled the border, under the eyes of Vorster and Kaunda, but the talks collapsed. South Africa then withdrew its police units, and in 1976 Vorster let it be known that he would reduce the supply of weapons and oil unless Smith made a settlement.

The **frontline states** were Zambia (Kaunda), Tanzania (Julius Nyerere), Mozambique (Machel) and Botswana (Seretse Khama), later joined by Angola. They gave the guerrillas bases and training grounds, and they pressed the nationalists to unite and to accept a negotiated settlement. They paid a heavy price: Rhodesia's raids killed many people in Mozambique and Zambia and damaged their economies, and for Machel and Kaunda a settlement was a way to end the damage.

### Kissinger, 1976

The United States had not been very interested in Rhodesia, but in 1976, after Cuban troops arrived in Angola, the Secretary of State, **Henry Kissinger**, feared that Soviet and Cuban influence would spread to Rhodesia and Namibia, and he took up the idea of a settlement. He saw Vorster and then, on **19 September 1976**, Smith in Pretoria, and the proposals that followed were that Rhodesia would accept majority rule within two years; an interim government would be set up with Black and white members; the British would organise a constitution conference; and there would be a fund to help Rhodesia's economy. On **24 September 1976**, in a television broadcast, Smith said that he accepted the proposals. Many Rhodesians saw it as a betrayal, and Smith's government later said that he had accepted majority rule in two years only as a part of a complete package, so that what he had agreed to remained disputed. A conference met in Geneva from 28 October to 14 December 1976, and was adjourned without agreement, since Smith and the nationalists disagreed on who would control the interim government and the army and police. The Patriotic Front had meanwhile formed, in October 1976, to speak with one voice.

Kissinger's plan had failed, but it had shown that Smith's government had accepted the idea of majority rule, that South Africa and the USA were ready to press him, and that the nationalists would not accept a settlement that left the whites in control of the security forces. In 1977 the new British Foreign Secretary, **David Owen**, and the American ambassador to the UN, **Andrew Young**, produced the "Anglo-American proposals", which Smith rejected.

:::key How important were South Africa and the frontline states in ending white rule?
1. **South Africa was Rhodesia's lifeline,** so its decision to withdraw direct support from 1975 and its pressure on Smith in 1976 were crucial; without it, Rhodesia could not have fought on.
2. **But South Africa did not want a radical Black government,** so it supported a moderate solution (Muzorewa) and kept some links to the end.
3. **The frontline states made the war possible,** by giving bases to the guerrillas, and then helped end it, by pressing the Patriotic Front to accept the Lancaster House terms in 1979.
4. **The superpowers made it a Cold War issue.** Soviet and Chinese backing for the guerrillas and the American fear of it pushed Kissinger, Carter and Thatcher towards a settlement.
:::

## The Internal Settlement and the ending of white minority rule, 1978–79

### Why Smith agreed

After the failure of Geneva and of the Anglo-American proposals, Smith turned to a different strategy: a settlement with the African leaders who were inside the country, and not in exile or in the guerrilla armies, in the hope of winning recognition for a moderate Black-led government that the whites could live with. He had several reasons.

:::key Reasons why Smith agreed to the Internal Settlement
1. **The war was getting worse.** By 1977 guerrillas operated in most rural areas, conscription was lengthened and reserve service was heavy, and the cost in lives and money was rising.
2. **The economy was weaker.** The effect of sanctions was added to that of the oil price, the loss of Mozambique's ports and the cost of defence, and more whites were leaving than arriving.
3. **Outside pressure.** South Africa had cut back support and urged a settlement; Britain and the USA had made it clear after Kissinger's plan and the Owen–Young plan that Rhodesia could not hold out for ever. In September 1976 Smith had already accepted the principle of majority rule.
4. **The aim was to divide the nationalists.** Smith hoped that an agreement with Muzorewa, Sithole and Chief Jeremiah Chirau would split the African opinion from the Patriotic Front, would let the West recognise a Black-led government and would lift sanctions.
5. **He believed that he could keep white interests safe.** The terms kept whites in key posts and left the army, the police and the courts largely unchanged.
:::

### The agreement

On **3 March 1978** Smith and three African leaders, **Bishop Abel Muzorewa** (the United African National Council, UANC), the Reverend **Ndabaningi Sithole** (a faction of ZANU, later ZANU-Ndonga) and **Chief Jeremiah Chirau** (the Zimbabwe United People's Organisation), signed the **Internal Settlement** (also called the Salisbury Agreement). It provided for a transitional government, with an executive council of the four men, which would draft a new constitution and hold elections by universal adult suffrage. Under the new constitution a parliament of 100 members would have 72 seats elected by Black voters and 28 reserved for whites, enough to block constitutional change. White officials would stay in the civil service, the courts, the army and the police for a transition period. Smith kept the title of Prime Minister until the elections.

The agreement did not end the war. Nkomo and Mugabe, who were excluded, said that it was a sham to preserve white power under a Black front, and the Patriotic Front intensified the war. The frontline states called it a sell-out, and the UN Security Council declared it "illegal and unacceptable" (Resolution 423, 14 March 1978). Sithole's ZANU-Ndonga had little support among the guerrilla forces. In September 1978 and February 1979 the Viscount airliners were shot down (see above), and Rhodesia's forces made some of their largest raids into Mozambique, Zambia and Angola. The government declared martial law in many areas, and the number of refugees and deaths rose.

### The 1979 election and the Muzorewa government

The common-roll election took place in April 1979: the white roll voted on 10 April and the common roll (open to Black voters) in the days up to 21 April. The Patriotic Front urged a boycott and threatened to disrupt the voting, and much of the country was under martial law. The government claimed a turnout of about 64 per cent. **Muzorewa's UANC won 51 of the 72 common-roll seats**, Sithole's ZANU won 12 and a third party, the United National Federal Party, won 9. The RF won the white seats. On **1 June 1979** Muzorewa became Prime Minister of **Zimbabwe Rhodesia**, with Smith in the cabinet as a minister without portfolio. Observers from some Western groups reported no serious problems in the counting, but said that the election was not free, since the main parties had been excluded, and many Africans in the war zones were obliged to vote.

### Rejection by Britain, ZAPU and ZANU

:::key Why the Internal Settlement failed
1. **It did not include the Patriotic Front.** The two armies that were doing the fighting, ZANLA and ZIPRA, were not parties, so the war went on, and Muzorewa could not end it.
2. **Real power stayed with whites.** Whites kept 28 seats and a veto, and the army, police and courts remained largely in their hands. To many Africans Muzorewa was a front for Smith, and some called him "Smith's puppet".
3. **It was not recognised.** The UN Security Council declared the election null and void (Resolutions 445 and 448), the frontline states and the Organisation of African Unity rejected it, and in June 1979 President Carter decided not to lift American sanctions. Britain had told Smith that any settlement had to be acceptable to the people as a whole, which meant including the Patriotic Front.
4. **Sanctions continued,** and so the economic benefits that Smith had hoped for did not come.
5. **It did, however, show that white rule was over.** The election of a Black prime minister, with a white minority accepting it, showed that Smith could not turn back, and so it prepared the way for the next stage. It also gave Muzorewa a place at the table in London.
:::

## The Lancaster House Agreement, 1979, and a final settlement

### Why the fighting ended

By mid-1979 a number of pressures came together.
- **War weariness and the military position.** Neither side could win. The guerrillas controlled much of the countryside and had tens of thousands of fighters; the Rhodesian forces held the towns and the roads and had never been defeated in a major battle, but they could not defeat the guerrillas, and the cost in lives, money and men was too much. Many white Rhodesians had left or wanted to.
- **The economy.** Sanctions, the war and the loss of Mozambique's trade had left Rhodesia poorer and indebted, and without recognition it could not borrow.
- **South Africa.** Pretoria had its own reasons for ending the war. It did not want a long conflict on its border or a military victory for a ZANLA government that was armed by the Chinese. It preferred a moderate government in Salisbury, and it let it be known that it would not support Rhodesia without a settlement.
- **The frontline states.** The war had cost Mozambique and Zambia dearly, through Rhodesian raids and economic losses. Machel and Kaunda urged the Patriotic Front to accept a settlement, and Machel made clear that he would not go on supporting the war indefinitely.
- **British and Commonwealth diplomacy.** The Conservative government of **Margaret Thatcher**, elected on 3 May 1979, had been expected to recognise Muzorewa's government and to lift sanctions. But the Foreign Secretary, **Lord Carrington**, argued that recognition would not end the war, would split the Commonwealth (Nigeria nationalised British oil interests in the summer of 1979, a warning of what Africans might do) and would leave Britain to deal with a continuing conflict. At the **Commonwealth Heads of Government meeting in Lusaka**, from **1 to 7 August 1979**, Thatcher agreed that Britain would call a conference of all the parties to draw up a constitution acceptable to all, and to hold free elections under British supervision.

### The negotiations in London

The Lancaster House conference met from **10 September to 15 December 1979**, chaired by Carrington, with the delegation of Zimbabwe Rhodesia led by Muzorewa (with Smith as a member) and the Patriotic Front delegation led by **Robert Mugabe** and **Joshua Nkomo**. It went in three stages.
1. **The constitution (September–October).** Britain proposed a constitution for an independent Zimbabwe with a parliament of 100 members, **80 elected on a common roll and 20 reserved for whites** for seven years, a Prime Minister with executive power and a bill of rights. Muzorewa accepted in October 1979; the Patriotic Front objected to the reserved seats and to the protections for white property, and bitter argument followed.
2. **The land question.** The most difficult point was land, since the Patriotic Front wanted to take land from white farmers without compensation. The constitution provided that land could be bought only from **willing sellers, with compensation**, for ten years, and Britain and the USA indicated that they would help to pay for land purchase, an understanding that became a long dispute after 1980. Carrington told the Patriotic Front that Britain would go ahead without them if they refused, and Machel and Kaunda pressed them to give way. After weeks of argument they did.
3. **The transition (November–December).** The agreement for the return to British rule and a ceasefire: Lord **Soames** was appointed Governor in December 1979, with full executive and legislative powers, the UDI was revoked, sanctions were lifted, the forces of both sides were gathered in assembly points under the supervision of a Commonwealth monitoring force of over a thousand soldiers, and elections would be held in February 1980.

The agreement was signed on **21 December 1979**, and the ceasefire began on 28 December.

:::key What the Lancaster House Agreement settled
1. **A new constitution:** 80 common-roll seats and 20 reserved for whites for seven years; a bill of rights; majority rule.
2. **A transition under British authority:** the Governor, Soames, in charge until independence; the sanctions lifted; the UDI revoked.
3. **A ceasefire** with the guerrillas and Rhodesian forces assembled and monitored.
4. **Land:** purchase from willing sellers, with compensation, for ten years.
5. **Elections** under British supervision, in which all parties, including the Patriotic Front, could stand.
:::

:::key Reasons why the Lancaster House talks succeeded
1. **Exhaustion.** After fifteen years, both sides had reasons to stop: the Rhodesian government could not win and the guerrillas could not take the cities by force.
2. **The frontline states pressed the Patriotic Front,** and South Africa pressed the Rhodesian side.
3. **Britain acted as an honest broker.** Carrington's patient approach, his insistence on a settlement that included all parties, and Thatcher's decision, against the expectation of her own supporters, not to recognise Muzorewa.
4. **Each side got something it could present as a win.** The whites kept 20 seats, property protection and a transition under British law; the Patriotic Front won majority rule, the end of the UDI regime and an election it hoped to win; Muzorewa's government gave up power in return for a place in the new state.
5. **The Commonwealth.** The Lusaka meeting and the pressure of African states made it impossible for Britain to ignore the demand for an inclusive settlement.
:::

The election was held in February 1980, with British officials and observers from the Commonwealth and other countries, and many allegations of intimidation, especially by ZANLA fighters and by Rhodesian auxiliary forces. The Patriotic Front split: **Mugabe's ZANU-PF** and **Nkomo's PF-ZAPU** stood separately. The white roll chose 20 white members, all from the Rhodesian Front.

The result surprised many people, since most had expected a coalition in which ZAPU would have a larger part. On **4 March 1980** the Governor, Soames, invited Mugabe to form a government, and on **18 April 1980** Rhodesia became independent as **Zimbabwe**, with Mugabe as Prime Minister and the Reverend Canaan Banana as the (ceremonial) President. Britain was relieved to have ended a problem that had lasted fifteen years.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: who ended white rule in Rhodesia?)
**Martin Meredith**, a journalist, published *The Past Is Another Country: Rhodesia 1890–1979* in 1979, as the settlement was reached, and a later biography, *Mugabe: Power and Plunder in Zimbabwe* (2002); both give weight to the political choices of Smith, the nationalist leaders and Carrington. **David Martin and Phyllis Johnson**, in *The Struggle for Zimbabwe* (1981), wrote a detailed and sympathetic history of the guerrilla war, which they present as the main force that ended white rule, and which draws on the guerrillas' and the nationalists' own accounts. **Robert Blake**, a British historian, published *A History of Rhodesia* in 1977, before the Internal Settlement, so that it cannot say how the story ended, and it must be used as a view from the years of the war. A student can use these to make a point about evidence. Historians who stress the **war** see Lancaster House as the result of guerrilla pressure; historians who stress **diplomacy and the outside powers** see the end as a settlement made by Britain, South Africa and the frontline states when each had its own reasons to stop the war. Most accounts say that both were needed.
:::

## How was white minority rule ended?

:::key A judgement you can defend
White rule ended because the Smith government **lost the ability and the will to go on fighting**, and because the outside powers that might have kept it going, above all South Africa, withdrew their support and urged a settlement. The **guerrilla war** was the main pressure, since it made the cost of resistance higher every year after 1972, and the opening of the Mozambique border in 1975 made that cost unbearable. **Sanctions** were weaker than their supporters hoped, but, with the war, the oil price and the loss of Mozambique's ports, they turned slow growth into decline. **Britain's policies** (no force, the failed talks, the refusal to recognise the Internal Settlement and, finally, Lancaster House) allowed the regime to last for fourteen years and then gave it a way out. The **frontline states** and **South Africa** pressed both sides. The **Internal Settlement** shows that Smith had accepted that white rule would end, but not how, and Lancaster House provided what the Internal Settlement could not: an agreement that included the people who were fighting. A defensible ranking puts the **war** first, **outside pressure from South Africa and the frontline states** second, **British diplomacy** as the means by which an end was reached, and **sanctions** as the weakest of the main causes.
:::

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant (the refusal of force, the sanctions, the two sets of talks), so Table B is Level 3. The talks are mentioned without dates or terms. Table A Level 3 at the bottom (7).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If a reason is 'main' when the regime could not have survived without it, Britain's refusal to use force qualifies, since a British military operation in 1965 would probably have ended the rebellion quickly, and Wilson ruled it out on the day. But the survival of the regime for fourteen years cannot be put down to British inaction alone. The oil embargo of December 1965 and the Beira Patrol (1966–75) had little effect because Rhodesia's oil and exports went through South Africa and Mozambique, whose governments, with the support of the white regimes, ignored UN Resolution 232 of 16 December 1966, which made sanctions mandatory. So Britain was a necessary condition for survival, but the active help that kept the economy going came from outside Britain."

*Commentary.* Two criteria are applied and tested against accurate detail, with a clear distinction between "necessary" and "main". Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs the regime's own strength, the weakness of the opposition, a view of how things changed after 1974–75 and a judgement that is sustained to the end.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is only partly valid. Britain's refusal to use force made it possible for white rule to survive after the declaration of independence on 11 November 1965, and its repeated attempts to reach a settlement gave the regime time. But the main reasons why it lasted until 1980 were the support that Rhodesia received from South Africa and Portugal, and the military and political strength of the white state until the middle of the 1970s. I shall test the view against four criteria: what Britain did, outside support for Rhodesia, the strength of the regime, and the weakness of the opposition.

On **what Britain did**, the case is strong. Harold Wilson called UDI an act of rebellion on 11 November 1965, but he ruled out force at once. Rhodesia was landlocked and far away, the army and air force were well trained, many Britons felt for the Rhodesians as 'kith and kin', and the Commonwealth's African members were in a position only to protest. Britain's sanctions began with the oil embargo of December 1965, and the Royal Navy's Beira Patrol (from March 1966) stopped oil tankers at one port only. Wilson's hope that sanctions would end the rebellion in 'weeks rather than months' proved wrong. Britain also tried to settle: on HMS Tiger in December 1966, on HMS Fearless in October 1968 and in the Home–Smith agreement of November 1971, each of which would have given Africans a slow path to majority rule. Smith turned down the first two, and in 1972 the Pearce Commission found that most Africans rejected the third. In these ways British policy kept the problem unsolved and gave the regime fourteen years.

On **outside support**, though, the evidence points elsewhere. The UN made sanctions mandatory on 16 December 1966 (Resolution 232), and extended them in 1968 (Resolution 253), but South Africa and Portuguese Mozambique kept supplying Rhodesia with oil and carrying its exports until 1975. From 1967 to 1975 South African police units fought in Rhodesia. The American Byrd Amendment of 1971 allowed the import of Rhodesian chrome until its repeal in March 1977. The economy grew in the late 1960s and early 1970s, as import substitution replaced goods that could no longer be bought. Without this help, British sanctions might well have worked.

On **the strength of the regime**, the Rhodesian Front won the elections of 1965, 1970 and 1974 by large majorities. Its army, air force and special forces defeated the first guerrilla incursions at Chinhoyi in April 1966 and in the Wankie campaign of 1967, and it ran a security state with detention without trial. And on **the opposition**, ZAPU and ZANU had split in 1963, were banned in 1964 and had their leaders detained; Mugabe was in prison for ten years until 1974, and the guerrillas, who were based abroad, could do little before the attack on Altena Farm in December 1972.

The importance of these other factors is shown by what happened when they changed. In 1974–75 Portugal left Mozambique; Mozambique became independent on 25 June 1975, opening a border of about 1,300 kilometres to ZANLA and closing Rhodesia's route to the sea in March 1976; South Africa withdrew its police units in 1975 and put pressure on Smith; and the war after 1972 turned into a conflict that the army could not win. Britain's policy was the same in 1976 as in 1966, yet within four years white rule had ended. It was Lancaster House, in 1979, in which Britain, under Carrington, at last played a decisive role, by refusing to recognise the Muzorewa government and bringing in all the parties.

I conclude that Britain's policies were a necessary condition but not the main reason. Had Wilson used force in 1965, or had Britain accepted a settlement that left whites in power, history would have been different, so Britain's policy mattered. But the regime survived because it had friends and strength, and it ended because both were lost. British policy gave Rhodesia the chance to last; South Africa and Portugal gave it the means; and it lasted until 1980 because the loss of those means, together with the war, did not come until the second half of the 1970s."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): four explicit criteria, a balanced argument in which each side is tested against the other, evidence from the change after 1974–75 used as a test, and a sustained judgement that separates "necessary condition" from "main reason". Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise, selected and dated detail (11 November 1965; December 1965; 16 December 1966; December 1966 and October 1968; November 1971; 1972; 1971–77; 25 June 1975; 1,300 km). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 850 words, 35 to 40 minutes.

Question: "Evaluate the view that the Internal Settlement of 1978 failed." [30]

Q: What did the Rhodesian government demand in 1964–65, and what were the main points of disagreement with Wilson?
A: Independence on the 1961 constitution, which kept power with the white minority. Wilson insisted on the Five Principles, above all that any basis of independence must be acceptable to the people of Rhodesia as a whole. Smith held an indaba of chiefs (October 1964) and a referendum of the mostly white electorate (5 November 1964, 89 per cent yes) as proof, and Britain did not accept them.
Q: What was UDI, and how did Britain, the UN and white Rhodesians react?
A: The Unilateral Declaration of Independence of 11 November 1965. Britain called it a rebellion, imposed sanctions (an oil embargo in December 1965) and ruled out force. The UN condemned it (Resolution 217), allowed the Beira Patrol (Resolution 221) and made sanctions mandatory on 16 December 1966 (Resolution 232). Most whites supported it, and no state recognised Rhodesia.
Q: Why did Britain not use force in 1965?
A: Rhodesia was landlocked and far away, had a strong army and air force and the loyalty of many "kith and kin"; Zambia depended on Rhodesian railways and power; ministers and military advisers doubted that a war could be won quickly; and the British government with its small majority did not want a war in Africa.
Q: What happened at the talks on HMS Tiger and HMS Fearless, and why did they fail?
A: Wilson and Smith met off Gibraltar in December 1966 (Tiger) and October 1968 (Fearless). Smith's cabinet rejected the Tiger terms on 5 December 1966, and he again refused the Fearless proposals, because both would have kept a British role in guarding against changes to the constitution and led to majority rule more clearly than he would accept.
Q: What did the Pearce Commission find in 1972?
A: It tested the Home–Smith proposals of November 1971 among Rhodesians in January–March 1972 and reported in May that most Europeans accepted them and most Africans rejected them, so that the condition of the Five Principles was not met. The African National Council under Bishop Muzorewa led the opposition.
Q: What were ZAPU and ZIPRA, and ZANU and ZANLA?
A: ZAPU (1961, Joshua Nkomo) and its army ZIPRA, backed by the USSR and based in Zambia, mainly Ndebele; ZANU (1963, Ndabaningi Sithole, with Robert Mugabe as secretary-general and later leader) and its army ZANLA, backed by China and, from 1975, based in Mozambique, mainly Shona.
Q: Why did the war change after 1972, and especially after 1975?
A: ZANLA's attack on Altena Farm on 21 December 1972 began a much more serious war in the north-east. When Mozambique became independent under Frelimo on 25 June 1975, Rhodesia had a hostile neighbour with a border of about 1,300 km, which ZANLA could use as a base.
Q: What methods did the Rhodesian government use against the guerrillas?
A: Detention and emergency laws; the army, air force, Special Air Service and, from 1973, Selous Scouts; conscription; protected villages for rural Africans; cross-border raids (for example Nyadzonya, August 1976); and political schemes with chiefs and moderate Africans. South African police helped from 1967 to 1975.
Q: What did Kissinger propose in September 1976, and why did it not succeed?
A: After meeting Vorster and Smith (19 September 1976) he proposed majority rule within two years, an interim government and a development fund. Smith announced on 24 September that he accepted, but the Geneva conference (28 October–14 December 1976) failed over control of the interim government and the security forces.
Q: What was the Internal Settlement of 3 March 1978?
A: An agreement between Smith and Muzorewa, Sithole and Chirau for a transitional government and elections by universal suffrage; 72 of 100 seats would be elected by Black voters and 28 reserved for whites. It led to the April 1979 election, which Muzorewa's UANC won (51 of 72 common-roll seats) and to Zimbabwe Rhodesia (1 June 1979), but Britain, the UN, the Patriotic Front and the frontline states rejected it.
Q: What was agreed at Lancaster House?
A: A constitution with 80 common-roll seats and 20 reserved for whites for seven years; land purchase from willing sellers with compensation; a ceasefire; a return to British rule under Governor Soames; and elections in February 1980. The agreement was signed on 21 December 1979.
Q: What were the results of the 1980 election, and what followed?
A: ZANU-PF won 57 of the 80 common-roll seats, PF-ZAPU 20 and Muzorewa's UANC 3; the Rhodesian Front took the 20 white seats. Soames invited Mugabe to form a government on 4 March 1980, and Zimbabwe became independent on 18 April 1980.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why Ian Smith declared independence in November 1965.
A: Indicative content. The demand for independence on the 1961 constitution; the Five Principles and the sticking-point of acceptability to the people as a whole; the failure of talks in 1965 (Wilson's offer of a Royal Commission and withdrawal of powers, Smith's refusal); the RF's election victory (May 1965); the independence of Zambia and Malawi (1964) and fear of majority rule; the belief that Britain would not use force; pressure from the RF's right wing; the support expected from South Africa and Portugal. Level 5 uses criteria (immediate, underlying, the role of belief) and judges that the aim of keeping white control, together with the expectation that Britain would not use force, was the main reason.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Sanctions failed to end white minority rule in Rhodesia.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: sanctions began slowly (December 1965 oil embargo; mandatory sanctions only on 16 December 1966); South Africa and Mozambique supplied oil and took exports; the Byrd Amendment (1971–77); import substitution and growth; the regime lasted fourteen years. Challenge: sanctions deprived Rhodesia of capital, equipment and markets, drove up costs and made it dependent on South Africa; after 1974 the loss of Mozambique's ports, the oil price and the war made the economy shrink; sanctions helped to deny the Internal Settlement recognition. Level 5 judges that sanctions did not end the regime by themselves but weakened it for the war and the settlement.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the aims and methods of the Rhodesian government in the war of 1964–79.
A: Indicative content. Aims: to keep white control and "standards", to defeat the guerrillas, to win African support through chiefs and, after 1976, a moderate settlement. Methods: emergency laws and detention; the army, air force, SAS and Selous Scouts; conscription; protected villages; raids on camps in Mozambique and Zambia (Nyadzonya, 1976); martial law from 1978; the Internal Settlement. Evaluation: methods contained the war for years but could not stop infiltration from Mozambique after 1975, and protected villages and raids turned civilians against the government. Level 5 judges that the methods were effective in the short term and failed to solve the political problem.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that Joshua Nkomo and ZAPU did more than Robert Mugabe and ZANU to end white minority rule.
A: Indicative content. Support for Nkomo: the older movement (1961); ZIPRA's army; Soviet backing; Wankie (1967); his talks with Smith in 1975–76. Challenge: ZANU split in 1963; ZANLA carried the main war after 1972; Mozambique's base from 1975; Mugabe led the Patriotic Front delegation and won the election of 1980 with 57 seats against 20. Level 5 sets a criterion (military and political contribution) and judges that the two were partners, with ZANLA the main military force and Nkomo's role strongest before 1972.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Internal Settlement of 1978 was a step towards majority rule, not a barrier to it.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which of its terms went towards majority rule, and which protected white power?
H: What did it lead to in 1979?
A: Indicative content. Step: Smith's acceptance of majority rule in principle; the April 1979 election with universal suffrage and a Black Prime Minister; it showed that the whites could not turn back and led to Lancaster House. Barrier: 28 seats and a veto for whites; the Patriotic Front excluded; the war continued; Britain and the UN refused recognition; it might have fixed a system with Muzorewa as a front. Level 5 judges that it was both, since it made a Black-led government the next step but kept control and so could not end the war.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which South Africa's actions caused the end of white minority rule in Rhodesia.
H: Separate South Africa's support (1965–75) from its pressure (1975–79).
H: What did South Africa want in 1979?
A: Indicative content. Support: oil, trade and loans, police units from 1967 to 1975, helicopters; this kept the regime alive. Pressure: after Portugal left Mozambique, détente and the Victoria Falls conference (August 1975); the withdrawal of police; Vorster's pressure on Smith in 1976 (Kissinger); the preference for a moderate settlement. Limits: South Africa wanted Muzorewa in power and did not want the Patriotic Front to win; the end came because of the war and the frontline states. Level 5 judges that South Africa's withdrawal of support was a necessary condition for change, but the war and the frontline states were more important.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the guerrilla war was the main reason why white minority rule ended in Rhodesia.
H: What did the war change after 1972 and after 1975?
H: Compare it with sanctions, South African pressure and British diplomacy.
A: Indicative content. Support: Altena Farm (21 December 1972); the opening of Mozambique's border (25 June 1975); conscription and reserve service; deaths of about 20,000–30,000; white emigration; the economy; the Internal Settlement and the Patriotic Front's refusal to accept it; the guerrillas' control of the countryside. Challenge: the security forces were never defeated in a major battle and held the cities; the guerrillas did not win; Lancaster House was a negotiation that Britain organised; the frontline states and South Africa pressed both sides; sanctions and the economy. Level 5 judges that the war was the main pressure, since it made the political settlement necessary, but not the only one, because the form of the end was set by diplomacy.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The end of white minority rule in Zimbabwe was the result of a compromise, not of a victory by either side.' Evaluate this view.
H: Who gave up what at Lancaster House: whites, Muzorewa, the Patriotic Front, Britain?
H: Compare the outcome with what each side wanted in 1965 and in 1978.
A: Support: the security forces were never defeated; the guerrillas did not take power by force; the agreement gave 20 white seats for seven years, protected property and land purchase from willing sellers, a British governor and a ceasefire; Mugabe agreed to a constitution that limited his powers at first; Smith gave up UDI and Muzorewa gave up office. Challenge: majority rule, the aim of the nationalists since 1960, was achieved; the Rhodesian state ceased to exist; ZANU-PF won the 1980 election with 57 of the 80 common-roll seats and the Rhodesian Front kept only the white seats; many see the result as a victory for the guerrillas' cause. A* judgement: it was a negotiated settlement reached because neither side could win outright, but it favoured the nationalists on the main issue (political power), and the compromises (seats, land) were limited in time and left questions open that shaped later events.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that white minority rule ended earlier in Zimbabwe than in South Africa because the Rhodesian state was weaker.
H: Compare the size of the white population, the economy and the armed forces of the two states.
H: Consider the effect of geography: Mozambique's independence, the border, and the role of South Africa as a backer.
A: Support: Rhodesia's white population was about 220,000 against more than 4 million in South Africa; it was landlocked and depended on South Africa; the war began in earnest in 1972 and the opening of the Mozambique border in 1975 made it unwinnable; UN sanctions applied to Rhodesia from 1966; Britain could negotiate, as the legal authority. Challenge: South Africa's economy, army and nuclear and arms industry were far stronger and it needed outside pressure; South African resistance had a mass internal base and years of unrest; Rhodesia lasted 15 years under sanctions; change in South Africa came only after the internal crisis of the 1980s, the end of the Cold War and the leaders' decisions. A* judgement: weakness mattered, but so did the different structure: Rhodesia was a rebel colony that Britain could negotiate with, while South Africa was a sovereign state with a stronger economy, so that the end required a different kind of pressure.
:::

:::recap
- **Background**: Southern Rhodesia self-governing from 1923; about 220,000 whites and 4 million Africans; Federation 1953–63; ZAPU 1961 (Nkomo), ZANU 1963 (Sithole, Mugabe); Rhodesian Front 1962; Smith Prime Minister 13 April 1964.
- **UDI**: Five Principles; referendum 5 November 1964 (89 per cent yes); UDI 11 November 1965; Britain: sanctions, oil embargo, no force; UN Resolutions 217, 221 (Beira Patrol 1966–75), 232 (16 December 1966, mandatory), 253 (1968).
- **Failed talks**: Tiger (2–4 December 1966), Fearless (October 1968), republic (2 March 1970), Home–Smith (November 1971), Pearce Commission (1972: Africans reject).
- **War**: Chinhoyi 28 April 1966; Wankie 1967; Altena Farm 21 December 1972; protected villages, Selous Scouts (1973); Mozambique independent 25 June 1975; deaths about 20,000–30,000; Viscount shootdowns September 1978 and February 1979.
- **Pressure**: Victoria Falls August 1975; Kissinger 19 and 24 September 1976; Geneva 28 October–14 December 1976; Patriotic Front October 1976; Anglo-American proposals 1977.
- **Internal Settlement**: 3 March 1978 (Smith, Muzorewa, Sithole, Chirau); 72 Black and 28 white seats; April 1979 election (UANC 51 of 72); Zimbabwe Rhodesia 1 June 1979; rejected by Britain, the UN, the Patriotic Front.
- **Lancaster House**: Lusaka 1–7 August 1979; talks 10 September–15 December; agreement 21 December 1979; 80 common-roll and 20 white seats for seven years; land from willing sellers; Governor Soames.
- **1980**: ZANU-PF 57, PF-ZAPU 20, UANC 3 (of 80); Mugabe invited to form a government 4 March; independence 18 April 1980.
- **Judgement**: the war and the loss of outside support (South Africa, Mozambique) made white rule unsustainable; British diplomacy and the frontline states made the settlement possible; sanctions were the weakest main cause.
