Includes a mark-band explorer.

:::why
Paper 4 is the biggest paper of the A Level: 60 marks, 30% of the grade, and two extended essays on your depth study. It tests two skills at once. One is **knowledge**: precise, selected detail on a period you have studied for months. The other is **argument**: weighing causes, successes, failures or claims against a standard, and reaching a judgement. Most students have the knowledge. The marks above Level 3 go to the students who use it to **argue**, with a standard of judgement that they choose and keep to. This chapter shows how, and how the examiners reward it.
:::

## What the paper looks like

**The front page.** It is the same front page as every 9489 paper. It says you answer on the enclosed answer booklet, and the instruction is "Answer two questions from one section only". The three sections are the three options: **A, the European option; B, the American option; C, the International option**. The information box says the total mark is 60 and that the marks for each question are shown in brackets. Nothing else is supplied: you have no sources, no map and no list of dates.

**The questions.** In the 2027 specimen paper each section has **three** questions, one for each of the three topics of the option, and each question is for 30 marks. You answer **two of the three**. That means:
- you must study **at least two** topics of your option, and a third gives you a real choice;
- the question on a topic can be about **any** of its four key questions, so you must be ready for all four of each topic you study.

**Two things to notice about the specimen questions.**
1. They are on **narrow, specific issues** and not on whole topics: a policy, a decision, a cause, a result. The skill is to use the whole topic for one precise question.
2. Their **date limits** matter. A question about "the years 1933 and 1934" is not an invitation to write about 1935 or about 1929. Knowledge outside the date range or the focus earns little.

:::warn The old papers had a different shape
In the 2024–26 syllabus Paper 4 had four questions in each section (twelve in all), including topics now removed (such as Britain, 1919–39), and its command words included "Discuss" and "Analyse". The 2027 syllabus defines four command words: **Assess**, **Evaluate**, **Compare** and **Explain**, and the specimen uses only the first two. Old papers are excellent practice for topics that survive, but check each question against your syllabus, and treat "Discuss" and "Analyse" as the same task: weigh the issue and judge.
:::

## What the examiners are testing

:::def AO2: Historical explanation, analysis and judgement
Identify, explain and analyse the past using historical concepts: **cause and consequence**, **change and continuity**, and **significance**; explain and analyse **connections** between different aspects of the past; and **reach a judgement**. On Paper 4 it is half of the marks, and Table A is the table that tests it.
:::

:::def AO1: Historical knowledge
Recall, select and use appropriate historical knowledge. On Paper 4 it is the other half of the marks (Table B). It does not mean writing everything you know: it means choosing facts that **prove the point you are making**.
:::

## Command words and question forms

:::def Assess
Make an informed judgement. You weigh the factors or arguments and decide.
:::

:::def Evaluate
Judge the quality, importance, amount or value of something. You weigh evidence for and against, and decide how good, how important or how large.
:::

On Paper 4 the two words ask for the same skill. In both, you **weigh** and then **judge**. The difference is only in the wording: "Assess" invites a judgement of how much or how far; "Evaluate" invites a judgement of how good or how true. The specimen paper uses both, in six forms.

| Form of question | Example (written for this guide) | What you weigh |
|---|---|---|
| "'…' Evaluate this view." | "'Opposition to apartheid grew mainly because of the Soweto uprising.' Evaluate this view." | The claim's factor against the other factors |
| "Evaluate the view that …" | "Evaluate the view that Kennedy's presidency achieved little for civil rights." | How far the statement is true, and for what |
| "'…' Assess this view." | "'Sukarno's Guided Democracy was a response to weakness rather than a show of strength.' Assess this view." | The two readings the claim sets against each other |
| "Assess the reasons for …" | "Assess the reasons why the Soviet Union built the Berlin Wall in 1961." | The reasons, ranked against each other |
| "Evaluate the reasons for …" | "Evaluate the reasons for the survival of the apartheid state in the 1970s." | As above: weigh and rank the reasons |
| "Assess the extent of …" | "Assess the extent of Fascist control over Italian society by 1939." | A matter of degree: where it was large, where it was limited |

Other forms, such as "Assess the success of …", "Assess the importance of …" or "Evaluate the impact of …", are possible (the old papers used several). Treat them the same way: find a standard, weigh, and judge.

**Reasons questions and view questions are different jobs.** A question about reasons asks you to build the list of causes and rank them. A question with a view in it already contains one answer, and you are asked how far it holds. For a view you must decide what the claim means, give the case for it and the case against it, and say which is stronger. You are never asked simply to agree: "I mostly agree", "I partly agree" and "I disagree" are all acceptable if the argument earns them.

## Criteria: the idea that carries the marks

:::def Criteria
The standards you use to judge. A criterion is a **question you ask of every factor** ("did it decide the outcome, or only make it easier?") so that the factors can be compared fairly. Table A asks for "valid criteria", and at the top level "valid, wide-ranging criteria".
:::

Without criteria, an essay lists points on each side and then says "overall, it was a mixture". With criteria, every point is weighed against the same standard, so the conclusion follows from the argument.

| If the question is about ... | Criteria you might choose |
|---|---|
| **Reasons or causes** ("the main reason", "assess the reasons") | **Necessity**: would it have happened without this? **Timing**: underlying cause or trigger? **Scale**: how many people, how much power? **Durability**: how long did it matter? **Interaction**: did it make the others possible or stronger? |
| **Success or failure** | The **aims** of the person or government; the **results** against each aim; **who** gained or lost; **short** and **long** term; the **cost** |
| **Extent of control, change or growth** | **Breadth** (how many areas of life); **depth** (real or superficial); **permanence**; the **evidence of resistance** or of acceptance |
| **Importance, significance, impact** | **What changed** because of it, and for whom; how **lasting**; how it compares with other changes |
| **The role of an individual** | How much was due to **choice and character**, how much to **circumstances** and other people |

State your criteria **early** (in the introduction), apply them in **every paragraph**, and use them in the **judgement**. A criterion that appears only in the last paragraph is not a criterion.

## The levels of Table A and Table B

**Table A: analysis and judgement (AO2), out of 15**

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 13–15 | **Full understanding, balanced and analytical.** Establishes valid and wide-ranging criteria; consistently analytical of the key features of the period; a focused, balanced argument with a sustained line of reasoning; a clear, sustained and supported judgement |
| 4 | 10–12 | **Good understanding, mostly analytical.** Valid criteria; analysis may be uneven; an attempt at balance that may lack coherence or precision in places; a supported judgement, though some evaluations may be only partly substantiated |
| 3 | 7–9 | **Understanding with some analysis; the argument lacks balance.** Attempts at criteria; some analysis but also descriptive passages; an argument that lacks balance, coherence and precision; the beginning of a judgement with weak substantiation |
| 2 | 4–6 | **Some understanding, descriptive.** Criteria that may be implicit; limited analysis; an inconsistent argument; an assertion rather than a judgement |
| 1 | 1–3 | **Addresses the topic, but not the question.** Descriptive; no analysis or argument; no relevant judgement |
| 0 | 0 | No creditable response |

**Table B: supporting knowledge (AO1), out of 15**

| Level | Marks | What the knowledge is like |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 13–15 | A **high level of relevant detail**: carefully selected, fully focused on supporting the argument, wide-ranging, consistently precise and accurate |
| 4 | 10–12 | A **good level** of relevant detail: appropriately selected, mostly focused on the argument, a range of points but uneven depth, mostly precise and accurate |
| 3 | 7–9 | An **adequate level**: mostly appropriate, may not fully support the points made, descriptive in places, a narrow range, occasionally imprecise |
| 2 | 4–6 | **Some** relevant detail: presented as narrative, not directly linked to the argument, limited in range and depth, frequently imprecise |
| 1 | 1–3 | **Limited** knowledge: limited relevance, inaccurate or vague |
| 0 | 0 | No creditable response |

**How the marking works.** The examiner finds the best-fit level for each table. Within a level, an essay that **convincingly** meets it gets the top mark, one that **adequately** meets it a middle mark, and one that **just** meets it the lowest mark. Marks are whole numbers and are never deducted. The two marks are added to give the mark out of 30, and your two essays are added to give the mark out of 60.

| If both tables are at | The mark out of 30 is |
|---|---|
| Level 5 | 26–30 |
| Level 4 | 20–24 |
| Level 3 | 14–18 |
| Level 2 | 8–12 |
| Level 1 | 2–6 |

The two tables need not match. A narrative essay with excellent detail might score Level 2 for Table A (4–6) and Level 4 for Table B (10–12): 14 to 18 in all. A tightly argued essay with thin facts has the opposite problem. Both halves count equally.

:::key The step from level to level in Table A
- **Level 1 to 2: answer the question.** Use its key words. Stop writing about the topic and start writing about the question.
- **Level 2 to 3: analyse.** Explain **how** and **why**, with some argument, and begin to judge.
- **Level 3 to 4: balance, with valid criteria.** Give both sides a real argument, say what standard you are using, and reach a supported judgement.
- **Level 4 to 5: wide-ranging criteria, applied throughout.** Keep one line of reasoning from the first paragraph to the last, so that the judgement is **sustained** and not tacked on, and make sure every part of the essay is analytical.
:::

:::key The step from level to level in Table B
- **Level 1 to 2: relevant detail**, in place of vague statements.
- **Level 2 to 3: link the detail to the argument.** A narrative of events is Level 2. Detail that proves a point is Level 3.
- **Level 3 to 4: range and focus.** More points, each chosen to support the argument.
- **Level 4 to 5: precision and wide range, throughout.** Dates, names, figures and places that are accurate, chosen with care, and covering the whole question.
:::

## Choosing your two questions

You have six minutes, and the choice is worth as much as any paragraph. A student who picks the question on the topic they like best and finds it is about a narrow issue they have not revised has lost ten marks before writing a word.

:::steps
1. **Read all three questions.** For each, underline three things: the **command word and form** (reasons? a view? extent?), the **focus** (the issue itself), and the **date limits**.
2. **Test your facts.** Can you write five precise facts (a date, a name, a figure) for this exact focus in one minute? If you cannot, the question is dangerous.
3. **Test your argument.** Can you state two criteria and two sides at once? If you can argue only one side, you will be held at Level 3.
4. **Compare the focus with your revision**, not the topic title. A question on a topic you know well can still ask about the one policy you skipped.
5. **Choose the two where you have the most precise knowledge and an argument.** Do not choose by topic alone, and do not choose a view question just because you agree with the view: you are never required to.
6. **Jot a five-line plan for each** before you write the first. The rest of the time is for writing.
:::

## Building a 30-mark essay

An essay of this kind has one job: **to answer the question as a whole, and to prove the answer.** Paragraphs are steps in one argument.

1. **Introduction (four or five sentences).** Say what the key words mean, state your **criteria**, and give your line of argument ("I shall argue that ..."). Do not retell the background.
2. **Body paragraphs (three or four).** Each has the same four moves: the **point** (the factor, and what it claims), the **evidence** (dates, names, figures), the **analysis** (how it affected the outcome, judged by your criteria) and a **mini-judgement** (what the paragraph shows about the question). Start with the strongest factor, or the one in the question.
3. **The challenge** (one paragraph or woven in). The case against the claim, or the rival reasons, with the same quality of evidence. Do not tack it on at the end: weigh it in the same terms.
4. **Connections.** Say how the factors link ("the war made the second problem fatal"; "without the first, the second would not have mattered"). Level 5 is **analytical of the key features of the period**, and links are a large part of that.
5. **Conclusion (four to six sentences).** Answer the question, apply the criteria, rank or weigh the factors, and give a clear judgement. Do not add new evidence, and do not repeat the whole essay.

:::exam Technique for a Paper 4 essay
- **Answer the question every paragraph.** Finish each with a sentence that uses the question's own words ("so the Alliance was the necessary condition for independence, but not its only cause").
- **Use precise evidence**: a date, a name, a figure, a place. "In the 1930s" is not evidence; "on 30 July 1937" is.
- **Cover the whole focus.** If the question is "the reasons for ...", give a range. If it is a view, give both sides, and make the second as strong as the first.
- **Keep to the date limits** in the question.
- **Make the judgement early and keep it going.** Level 5 asks for a "sustained line of reasoning throughout" and a "sustained" judgement, so state your argument in the introduction and let every paragraph move it on.
- **Never narrate.** If a sentence tells what happened without saying how it bears on the question, cut it or rewrite it.
:::

## Worked examples built level by level

The questions below were written for this guide and are not past-paper questions. The history in them is taught in the topic chapters named, where you will find the facts in full.

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the reasons why Malaya became independent in August 1957.
The history is in [The route to independence (2)](#hist.d.malaysia.2).

**Plan (four minutes).**
- *Terms:* "reasons" = the causes of independence in 1957, not of independence at some later date.
- *Criteria:* (1) **Necessity**: which reason, if it had been missing, would have stopped independence in 1957, and which only made it easier? (2) **Who decided**: how far was it won by Malayan leaders, and how far granted because it suited Britain?
- *Reason 1, the Alliance:* UMNO and the MCA won 9 of 12 seats in the Kuala Lumpur municipal election (February 1952); the MIC joined in 1954; 51 of 52 elected seats on 81.7 per cent of the votes, 27 July 1955; the Tunku's London talks, 18 January to 6 February 1956; the Reid Commission (March 1956) and the Merdeka Constitution.
- *Reason 2, the Emergency:* the communists' claim that only war would bring independence; the Briggs Plan (1950), about 500,000 people in New Villages; Templer, High Commissioner from 1952; the failed Baling Talks (December 1955); the MCA (1949) as a legal channel for Chinese.
- *Reason 3, British interests and decolonisation:* India (1947), Burma (1948), Ghana (March 1957); tin and rubber; the Anglo-Malayan defence agreement of 1957.
- *Judgement:* the Alliance was the necessary condition; the Emergency decided to whom Britain would hand over; British interests explain Britain's willingness but had existed for years without producing independence.

**Level 2 (Table A 4–6; Table B 4–6; about 10 of 30): narrative.**
"The Emergency began in June 1948 when three planters were murdered at Sungai Siput. The British brought in the Briggs Plan in 1950 and moved squatters to New Villages. General Templer became High Commissioner in 1952. The Alliance won the election in 1955 and the Tunku went to London in 1956. Malaya became independent on 31 August 1957. The Emergency ended in 1960."

*Commentary.* Accurate, and in the right order, but it is a **narrative**: it never says how any of this led to independence, and it uses no criteria. The conclusion is only the last event. Table A Level 2 (descriptive, an implicit approach, no judgement). Table B Level 2: relevant detail, but "presented as a narrative, not directly linked to the argument".

**Level 3 (Table A 7–9; Table B 7–9; about 16 of 30): one factor explained, no balance.**
"The main reason was the Alliance. UMNO and the MCA joined together in 1952 and were joined by the MIC in 1954. In the 1955 election the Alliance won 51 of the 52 seats, which showed Britain that the communities could work together. Britain wanted to hand over power to a government that could govern all communities, so the Tunku was able to get agreement for independence in 1956. The Emergency and Britain's other interests also played a part."

*Commentary.* One reason is **explained**, with some analysis, and it is linked to the outcome. But the others are only named, there is no balance, and the "judgement" ("the main reason") is not argued against any standard. Level 3.

**Level 4 (Table A 10–12; Table B 10–12; about 22 of 30): three reasons explained, partly compared.**
Add to the Level 3 paragraph two more: "The Emergency also mattered. The Briggs Plan moved about half a million people into New Villages and cut off the guerrillas, and Templer joined it to a political effort. By 1954 the guerrillas were retreating to the Thai border, so Britain could now leave without handing the country to the communists. Britain's own interests were a third reason. India and Burma had become independent in 1947 and 1948 and Ghana was to follow in 1957, and Britain was short of money. Britain could keep its interests in tin and rubber and its bases under the 1957 defence agreement. So all three reasons were important, but the Alliance was probably the most important because it gave Britain someone to hand over to."

*Commentary.* A range of reasons, each explained with accurate detail, and a supported judgement. This is **Level 4**. It stays there because the criteria are implied rather than set, the reasons are in separate boxes, and the final "probably the most important" is only partly substantiated. It does not say why the Alliance was more important than the others.

**Level 5 (Table A 13–15; Table B 13–15; about 27 of 30): a full answer.**

"Malaya became independent on 31 August 1957, peacefully and by negotiation, while a communist insurgency was still being fought. I shall weigh the reasons by two tests. The first is necessity: which reason, if it had been missing, would have stopped independence in 1957, and which only made it easier? The second is who decided: how far independence was won by Malayan leaders and how far it was granted because it suited Britain. I shall argue that the Alliance was the necessary condition, that the Emergency decided to whom Britain would hand over, and that British interests explain Britain's willingness but not the date.

The Alliance was the necessary condition. The Malayan Union of 1946 had failed because Malays would not accept it, and Britain's question for the next decade was whether the communities could govern together. The Alliance answered it. UMNO and the MCA won 9 of the 12 seats in the Kuala Lumpur municipal election of February 1952, the MIC joined in 1954, and on 27 July 1955 the Alliance won 51 of the 52 elected seats on 81.7 per cent of the votes. That gave the Tunku, Tunku Abdul Rahman, the mandate he used in London from 18 January to 6 February 1956 to secure agreement that independence would come by August 1957. Without a partner Britain could trust, there was nobody to hand power to, and the Reid Commission, appointed in March 1956, could not have drawn up a constitution that protected the special position of the Malays and offered citizenship to the others. By the test of necessity this is the strongest reason, and by the test of who decided it shows Malayan leaders acting.

The Emergency decided what kind of Malaya Britain would leave. The communists argued that independence could be won only by war, and a government that gave way to an insurgency would have encouraged others. The Briggs Plan of 1950 moved about half a million squatters into New Villages and cut the guerrillas off from food, and Templer, High Commissioner from 1952, joined it to a political effort. By 1954 the guerrillas were retreating to the Thai border, and at the Baling Talks in December 1955 the Tunku refused Chin Peng's demand for legal recognition of his party. Britain could now leave without appearing to yield to the communists. The Emergency also helped the Alliance: the MCA, founded in 1949, gave Chinese a legal channel, and the promise of independence took away the communists' strongest argument. So the Emergency was not the cause of independence but the condition of its form: it made Britain choose the Alliance and not the MCP. By the test of necessity it ranks second.

British interests explain why Britain said yes, and are the weakest reason on their own. India (1947) and Burma (1948) had gone, Ghana was to follow in March 1957, and a Britain short of money had little reason to hold down a people who did not want to be held. Britain also expected to keep what it valued: tin and rubber, and the bases it secured by the defence agreement of 1957. But these interests had existed in 1948 and in 1952, and Britain had not left then. What had changed was a government in Kuala Lumpur that would protect them. British interest was a reason to agree; it did not make the request.

The three reasons therefore form a chain and not a list. The Emergency, by defeating the communists' claim, made the Alliance the only serious partner; the Alliance's victory in 1955 turned British willingness into a date; and British interests made the answer yes. If one link is removed the result differs, but only the Alliance was a necessary condition in its own right, since the other two could not have produced independence without someone to receive it. Malayan leaders, working inside the conditions that the Emergency and British interests created, therefore did most to decide the timing and the terms of Merdeka."

*Commentary.* The introduction sets **criteria** (necessity; who decided) and a line of argument. Each paragraph applies them and ends with a mini-judgement, so the reasoning is **sustained**. The reasons are **ranked** and linked, and the conclusion is a clear, supported judgement that uses the criteria. Knowledge is precise and wide-ranging: dates, figures, named bodies and people, chosen to prove the point. Table A Level 5 (14); Table B Level 5 (13): about 27 of 30. About 700 words, which is what 40 minutes of writing produces.
:::

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'Stalin's rule in the 1930s depended more on persuasion than on terror.' Evaluate this view.
The history is in [Stalin's Russia (2)](#hist.d.stalin.2) and [Stalin's Russia (3)](#hist.d.stalin.3). This example is a **plan and the key paragraphs**, built level by level, because the skill is the one you need in every view question: weighing two things against a criterion.

**Plan (four minutes).**
- *Terms:* "depended" means what the regime could not have done without. "Persuasion" is the cult, propaganda, rewards and real benefits. "Terror" is arrests, shootings, camps and fear.
- *Criteria:* (1) **Reach**: which touched more people and more groups? (2) **Decisive moments**: which did the regime use when it was most at risk? (3) **Dependence**: which could the regime have done without, and which depended on the other?
- *For the view:* the cult (the fiftieth birthday, 21 December 1929; the *Short Course* of 1938); the 1936 Constitution (5 December 1936), announced as the most democratic in the world; the Stakhanovite movement (30–31 August 1935) and its rewards; the promoted officials and workers who rose after 1937 and believed sincerely; letters to Stalin.
- *Against the view:* about 1.6 million arrested and 681,692 shot in 1937–38 (353,074 and 328,618); Order 00447 (30 July 1937) with quotas of 75,950 to be shot and 193,000 for the camps; a Gulag of about 1.5 million; force in the countryside during collectivisation; the cult itself protected by law, since criticism could be punished as "anti-Soviet agitation".
- *Judgement:* terror was the more fundamental, but persuasion explains the quality of the support.

**A Level 3 paragraph (one-sided).** "Stalin relied on persuasion. He had a cult of personality, which began with his fiftieth birthday in 1929, and Soviet newspapers praised him as the father of the nation. In 1936 a new constitution was announced and Stakhanovite workers were given rewards. Many people loved Stalin and wrote to him as a father. So Stalin's rule depended on persuasion."

*Commentary.* Accurate, with some analysis, and it begins to judge. But it argues **one side only**, and the judgement ("so ... depended on persuasion") is only an assertion. Level 3.

**A Level 4 paragraph (both sides, side by side).** Add: "However, there was also terror. In 1937 and 1938 about 1.6 million people were arrested and 681,692 were shot, and about 1.5 million were in the Gulag. This shows that Stalin used terror as well as persuasion. Overall both persuasion and terror were important to Stalin's rule."

*Commentary.* Now both sides are **explained** with precise evidence. This is Level 4: the last sentence is a "balance" that says nothing about which side mattered more, and there is no standard. The answer is held at Level 4 because its balance is a list of two sides, not a comparison.

**A Level 5 paragraph (compared, by a criterion).** "The test of reach favours persuasion at first sight: the cult, the press and the Constitution of 1936 addressed everyone, while the shootings of 1937–38 fell on a minority, about 681,692 of a population of some 160 million. But terror reached the groups that mattered for the regime's survival. It struck the peasantry in collectivisation, the Party elite and army officers in 1937–38, and every official, who knew from Order 00447 and the show trials that a post could end with a bullet. Persuasion mostly worked on those who had already gained: the promoted workers and managers who rose into the places left vacant after 1937, and the young who believed sincerely. In this sense persuasion and terror were not alternatives, since the terror created many of the beneficiaries whom propaganda then persuaded."

*Commentary.* It uses a criterion (reach), applies it to **both** sides, shows how they **interact**, and builds the judgement into the paragraph. Evidence is precise. This is the Level 5 paragraph: balance, analysis and judgement in one.

**A Level 5 conclusion.** "The view is therefore mostly unconvincing, though it identifies something real. Persuasion won the sincere support of the promoted and the young, and it gave the regime a language in which to speak to everyone. But on each test terror was the more fundamental. It reached the groups on which the regime's survival depended; it was the regime's answer in its two most dangerous moments, the countryside in the early 1930s and the elite in 1937–38; and the cult itself could not be questioned without risk, so that persuasion depended on terror for its credibility. Stalin's rule depended on both, but persuasion needed terror more than terror needed persuasion."

*Commentary.* A clear, sustained judgement, which uses all three criteria that the introduction set and ranks the two factors. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the reasons why Malaya became independent in August 1957." [30]

A student wrote this as their main paragraph:

"The Emergency was the main reason for independence. It began in June 1948 when planters were killed at Sungai Siput. In 1950 the British brought in the Briggs Plan and resettled squatters in New Villages. Templer came in 1952. The guerrillas lost and the Emergency ended in 1960. Independence was in 1957. So the Emergency was the main reason for independence."

**The level.** Table A **Level 2** (4–6): the paragraph is **descriptive**. It tells the story of the Emergency and ends with an **assertion** ("so the Emergency was the main reason"). It never explains how the Emergency led to independence, uses no criterion, and does not compare it with another reason. It also contains a date problem: the Emergency ended in 1960, after independence, so the student has not noticed that the Emergency's defeat cannot be the whole story. Table B is Level 2 to 3: the facts are accurate but are narrative.

**The fix.** Start with the argument and test it. "The Emergency helped to bring independence, but only by shaping who Britain would hand power to. The communists said that only war would win independence. By 1954 the Briggs Plan and Templer had driven the guerrillas back to the Thai border, so Britain could leave without appearing to yield. But the Emergency did not end until 1960, and independence came in 1957, so the war cannot have been the necessary condition. What Britain needed was a partner, and that was the Alliance." Now every fact has a job, the date is used as evidence, and a second reason is introduced and compared.
:::

## Timing

105 minutes for 60 marks. A suggested plan, which Cambridge does not set:

| Minutes | Task | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | Read the three questions; choose two; jot five facts and two criteria for each | n/a |
| 46 | Question 1: plan (4 minutes), write (42 minutes) | 30 |
| 46 | Question 2: plan (4 minutes), write (42 minutes) | 30 |
| 7 | Check both: finish any unfinished conclusion; add the missing date or name | n/a |
| **105** | **Total** | **60** |

Practise the times at home. The two commonest timing errors are to write a wonderful first essay in 65 minutes and rush the second, and to run out of time before the conclusion. An unfinished second essay loses far more marks than a slightly shorter first one, because the second essay is worth exactly as much. If time runs short, write the conclusion even in three sentences: a judgement is the step to Level 4 and Level 5.

## Common mistakes

These come from the descriptors: each is a feature that the mark scheme says does **not** earn a higher level.

:::warn Ten ways to lose marks on Paper 4
1. **Narrative** (Table A and Table B, Level 2). Telling what happened, in order, instead of arguing. Every paragraph needs a claim.
2. **Writing about the topic, not the question** (Level 1). A prepared essay on "Hitler's rise" for a question on "the Night of the Long Knives".
3. **Ignoring the date limits or the focus.** Knowledge outside them earns little.
4. **One side only** (Level 3). "The argument lacks balance." Give the case against with the same care.
5. **"On the one hand ... on the other hand" with no verdict** (Level 4). Both sides, but no comparison.
6. **Criteria that appear only at the end**, or not at all. Set them in the introduction and use them in every paragraph.
7. **A judgement that only says "both were important".** A sustained, supported judgement ranks the factors and says why.
8. **Imprecise knowledge.** "A lot of people", "in the 1930s", "around then". Table B Level 5 is "consistently precise and accurate".
9. **Unselected knowledge.** Everything you remember, whether or not it proves the point. Level 5 detail is "carefully selected" and "fully focused on the argument".
10. **Poor timing.** An unfinished second essay, or no conclusion.
:::

## Revising for Paper 4

You study at least two topics of your option for months. The revision problem is that each topic has four key questions and examiners can ask about any narrow issue in any of them.

:::steps
1. **Turn each key question into questions.** For each chapter, write three "Assess the reasons ..." questions and three view questions, using the section headings and the key boxes of the topic chapter.
2. **Build a fact sheet per key question**: the five or six main factors, each with two precise facts (a date, a name, a figure) and the links between the factors.
3. **Choose criteria for each type of question** (the table above) and write them on one card.
4. **Plan, do not write, ten questions a week**: four minutes each, with criteria, both sides, and a judgement.
5. **Write timed essays** of 42 minutes and mark them with the tables above, Table A and Table B separately. Write down what would take each to the next level.
6. **Rewrite the weakest paragraph** of each essay at the next level up, with the descriptor open.
7. **Test your facts**: for each topic, list thirty dates and figures and check that you can give each from memory.
:::

Each topic chapter in this course ends with Paper 4 practice and worked examples in the real style. The skills are taught in [Skills: writing the analytical essay (Papers 2 and 4)](#hist.skills.essays), and Paper 4 is the A Level step up from the Paper 2 essay: see [Paper 2: Outline study](#hist.exam.p2).

## Using past papers and mark schemes

- **The 2027 specimen Paper 4** has nine questions, three in each section, each for 30 marks. It is the best guide to what the live papers will look like. Cambridge's past-papers page has it with its mark scheme.
- **Papers from the old syllabus (2024–26)** have the same front page and the same mark tables, with twelve questions and some topics that no longer exist. Use them for topics that survive.
- **The mark scheme's indicative content** for each question is a list of points that a response might cover, and it says that the best responses reach "a clear judgement". It always adds that other valid points can earn credit. Compare your plan with it after you have written your own, and note which points and criteria you missed.
- **Mark yourself honestly.** Decide the level for Table A first, then Table B, then "just", "adequately" or "convincingly" within each. Then write down what you would add to go up a level.

:::recap
- Paper 4: **1 h 45 min, 60 marks**; two questions from **one section** (your option), from a choice of three (one per topic in the specimen), each for **30 marks**; no sources; booklet only. 30% of A Level; AO1 and AO2 each half.
- Sections: **A European**, **B American**, **C International**. Revise at least two topics, all four key questions of each.
- Command words: **Assess** and **Evaluate**: both ask you to weigh and judge. Forms in the specimen: a quotation with "Evaluate this view" or "Assess this view"; "Evaluate the view that"; "Assess the reasons for"; "Evaluate the reasons for"; "Assess the extent of".
- **Criteria** are the standards of judgement (necessity, scale, durability, aims and results, breadth and depth). State them early, use them in every paragraph and in the conclusion.
- **Table A (AO2), out of 15:** Level 1 topic not question; Level 2 descriptive; Level 3 some analysis, lacks balance; Level 4 valid criteria, supported judgement, uneven; Level 5 wide-ranging criteria, sustained reasoning, sustained judgement. **Table B (AO1), out of 15:** limited; some, as narrative; adequate; good; high level of relevant, precise detail.
- Both tables are added: both at Level 5 gives 26–30 for an essay. Within a level: **just** (lowest), **adequately** (middle), **convincingly** (highest).
- Essay shape: introduction with criteria and a line of argument; point, evidence, analysis and mini-judgement in each paragraph; the challenge weighed in the same terms; connections; a conclusion that judges.
- Suggested timing: 6 min to choose, then 46 + 46 minutes, 7 minutes to check.
:::
