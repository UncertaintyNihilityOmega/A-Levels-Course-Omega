## Industrial expansion and economic recessions

### The nature and extent of industrial growth

Chapter 1 showed the scale of growth. For this key question you need two further points about its *nature*.

First, growth was **uneven in time**. The long-run trend was upward, but it came in surges followed by collapses. The economy contracted for 65 months from October 1873 to March 1879, the longest contraction the National Bureau of Economic Research records for the nineteenth century, and fell sharply again from 1893 to 1897. Between the slumps came booms: the 1880s, the late 1890s and the 1900s.

Second, growth was **uneven in place and in people**. The North-East and the Midwest industrialised; the South stayed poor and mainly agricultural; the West sold raw materials. In 1870 about half of the workforce worked on farms; by 1900 about 40 per cent did, and the rest worked in factories, mines, railroads, shops and offices. Output per worker rose and many prices fell, so many families were better off than their parents. But the gains went disproportionately to the owners: the economist Charles Spahr estimated in 1896 that the richest one per cent of families owned more than half of the nation's wealth, and the fortunes of Rockefeller and Carnegie were beyond anything seen before. Hundreds of thousands of workers earned barely enough to live on, and a depression could take their work away.

:::def Business cycle
The regular rise and fall of the economy through booms, in which output, jobs and profits rise, and recessions or depressions, in which they fall. In the Gilded Age the swings were large and sudden, and there was no government welfare or central bank to soften them.
:::

### The reasons for, and consequences of, the Panics

:::def Panic
The Gilded Age word for a sudden financial crisis: investors rush to sell shares and withdraw money from banks, banks close and businesses fail, often leading to a depression. The syllabus calls them "the Panics".
:::

**The Panic of 1873.**
- *Causes.* The railroad boom had gone too far. Companies had built lines ahead of traffic, financed by bonds that bankers sold to investors at home and in Europe. Jay Cooke and Company, the leading bank of the Civil War, had taken on the Northern Pacific Railroad, which was building across the empty North-West; when investors stopped buying its bonds, the bank was stuck with debts it could not pay. European credit tightened after a stock-market crash in Vienna in May 1873, and falling prices made borrowers' debts harder to pay. Cooke's bank failed on **18 September 1873**, banks and brokers followed, and the New York Stock Exchange closed for ten days.
- *Consequences.* Dozens of railroads failed and construction stopped; thousands of businesses went under; and unemployment rose to hundreds of thousands, with wages cut for those who kept jobs. The slump lasted until 1879. Cities saw bread lines and "tramps": unemployed men on the road looking for work. Many people blamed bankers and the Republican government. The Democrats won the House of Representatives in the elections of 1874. In July 1877, wage cuts on the railroads set off the Great Railroad Strike (below). Farmers' prices fell, which swelled the Grange and Greenback movements. The Coinage Act of 1873, passed the same year, became a symbol of financial injustice to those who wanted more money in circulation.

**The Panic of 1893.**
- *Causes.* Again railroads had been overbuilt. The immediate sign was the failure of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad in February 1893, followed by the collapse of National Cordage in May and a stock-market crash. Several deeper causes combined: falling wheat and cotton prices that left farmers unable to pay their debts; foreign investors, worried about American silver policy and unsettled by a banking crisis in London in 1890, pulling out their gold; and the Treasury's gold reserve falling below $100 million, the level thought safe, partly because the Sherman Silver Purchase Act (1890) obliged the government to buy silver with notes redeemable in gold. Fear that America would leave the gold standard drove investors to cash in their notes for gold.
- *Consequences.* About 500 banks and some 15,000 businesses failed, and about a quarter of the country's railroad track went into the hands of receivers (court-appointed managers) as companies including the Union Pacific, the Northern Pacific and the Santa Fe went bankrupt. Estimates of national unemployment at the worst point range from about 12 to about 18 per cent, and in some cities it was far higher. Jacob Coxey led an "army" of unemployed men from Ohio to Washington in 1894 to demand public works. The Pullman strike (1894) was one result. President Grover Cleveland persuaded Congress to repeal the Sherman Silver Purchase Act in November 1893, which angered the silver states and farmers, and in 1895 the Treasury had to borrow gold from a syndicate led by J.P. Morgan (a deal for $62 million of gold). In the elections of 1894 the Democrats lost over a hundred seats in the House, and the discontent fed the Populist movement and the election of 1896. Recovery began only in 1897.

**The Panic of 1907** shows the same weaknesses a decade later. On 22 October 1907 the Knickerbocker Trust Company in New York failed after an attempt to corner the stock of a copper company collapsed; runs on trust companies spread. Morgan gathered the leading bankers in his library and organised a rescue, and the Treasury deposited money in banks. The crisis showed that the country still relied on one private banker to save its financial system, and it led to the Aldrich–Vreeland Act (1908) and, in 1913, to the Federal Reserve ([Gilded Age and Progressive Era (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3)).

:::key Why the Panics happened, and what they did
1. **Causes**: overbuilding and speculation (railroads above all); banks without a lender of last resort or a central bank; a gold standard that limited money and made foreign investors nervous; falling farm prices and heavy debts.
2. **Economic consequences**: bank and business failures, years of unemployment, falling prices and wages, and a pause in building.
3. **Social consequences**: tramps and bread lines; strikes in 1877 and 1894; evictions and hardship for farmers and workers.
4. **Political consequences**: Democratic gains in 1874; the Greenback and Populist parties; the Silver issue; the election of 1896; the later demand for banking reform.
Growth *and* instability came from the same system.
:::

## The social impact of industrialisation and urbanisation

### The growth of the cities

Between 1870 and 1900 the share of Americans living in towns and cities rose from about 26 to about 40 per cent. Chicago grew from about 300,000 people in 1870 to about 1.7 million in 1900, and New York, with its neighbouring boroughs, to about 3.4 million. The people came from the farms and from Europe: in New York City in 1900 more than a third of residents had been born abroad. Cities were built with little planning around the factories, the railroad stations and the docks, and local governments were not equipped for the speed of the change.

### Living and working conditions

- **Hours and pay.** A normal working week in a factory in 1880 was ten hours a day, six days a week. In steel mills the furnaces never stopped: workers did twelve-hour shifts, often seven days a week. Real wages rose over the long run because prices fell, but pay for unskilled men and for women was low, work was often seasonal, and a layoff or illness could push a family into debt. Families depended on several incomes, so wives took in washing or sewing and children went out to work.
- **Child labour.** The census of 1900 counted about 1.75 million children aged 10 to 15 in work, about 18 per cent of that age group, in mines, mills, canneries, textile factories and city streets (as newsboys and messengers). They were paid a fraction of an adult's wage.
- **Danger.** Machines had no guards, mines had little ventilation and railroad work was among the most dangerous jobs in the country. Congress passed the Safety Appliance Act in 1893 to require automatic couplers and air brakes after thousands of railroad workers had been killed or injured. At the Monongah mine in West Virginia in December 1907 an explosion killed 362 men. If a worker was injured, the employer usually owed no compensation: courts held that a worker accepted the risks of the job, and that he could not sue if a fellow worker had caused the accident.
- **Company towns.** Some employers owned the housing, shops and even churches. George Pullman built a model town for his railroad-car workers near Chicago, but charged high rents and prices and controlled the residents' lives.
- **Women.** Large numbers of women worked in textile mills, laundries and the garment trade, where "sweating" (piecework at home or in cramped workshops for the lowest pay) was common, and as domestic servants. The new offices opened clerical jobs to women (see chapter 1).

### Housing

The new city workers needed housing near their jobs, and builders crammed families into small plots.

:::def Tenement
A multi-storey building divided into small flats, usually let to the poor, with poor light, ventilation and sanitation. The word came to mean the overcrowded slum buildings of immigrant neighbourhoods such as New York's Lower East Side.
:::

In New York, builders put up "dumbbell" tenements after a law of 1879: five or six storeys on a 25-foot lot, with four flats on each floor, many rooms without windows and a narrow airshaft for the middle rooms. Whole families, often with lodgers, lived in one or two rooms. There was often one water tap in the yard, and shared outdoor toilets. The Lower East Side was among the most crowded districts in the world, with several hundred people to the acre. Most of the population of New York lived in such buildings.

The journalist and photographer **Jacob Riis**, a Danish immigrant and police reporter, exposed these conditions in *How the Other Half Lives* (1890), with flash photographs of children asleep on fire escapes and families in cellars. He wrote in his preface: "Long ago it was said that 'one half of the world does not know how the other half lives.' That was true then. It did not know because it did not care." He argued that landlords' greed and city neglect, not the character of the poor, produced the slums, and his book led to inquiries and new building laws (the Tenement House Act of 1901). Riis was not a neutral observer: he campaigned for reform, and some passages show the prejudices of his time about the nationalities he described. But his statistics and photographs are corroborated by official reports.

### Health

Crowding, no clean water and poor drains meant disease. Cholera epidemics in 1832, 1849 and 1866 killed thousands in American cities; typhoid, diphtheria, measles and smallpox were common in the summer heat; tuberculosis ("consumption"), spread in dark crowded rooms and workplaces, was the leading killer of the working population. Infant death rates were far higher in the tenement districts than in richer ones. Overall life expectancy at birth in the United States was about 47 years in 1900. City governments and reformers responded slowly: boards of health were set up from the 1860s, water filtration and sewers spread in the 1890s, and milk and food laws came after 1900 (see [the next chapter](#hist.us.gilded.3)).

:::key Living and working conditions: the picture
1. **Work**: long hours, low pay for the unskilled, danger, child labour and the threat of unemployment.
2. **Housing**: overcrowded tenements, high rents and poor sanitation.
3. **Health**: infectious disease, high infant mortality, a life expectancy of about 47.
4. **Variation**: skilled workers, and workers in some industries, earned a decent living; the worst conditions were for immigrants, women, children and the unskilled.
These conditions were the reasons for unions, for settlement houses and, later, for Progressive reform.
:::

### The "Boss" system

City government struggled to provide services for fast-growing populations. A system of **political machines** filled the gap.

:::def Political machine and Boss
A political machine is an organisation, usually run by a single leader, the "Boss", that controls a party in a city, wins elections by supplying jobs and favours in return for votes, and profits from city contracts. The best-known was **Tammany Hall**, the Democratic organisation of New York City.
:::

**How a machine worked.**
- A network of **ward and precinct captains** knew every family in a neighbourhood. They found jobs on the city payroll or with contractors, handed out coal in winter, paid for funerals, helped with a family's rent or a son's bail, and spoke for them to the police, in exchange for votes.
- Newly arrived immigrants were helped to become citizens (and voters) quickly, and machines cultivated the Irish, German, Italian and Jewish communities.
- The machine's leaders paid for this with the city's money: they gave contracts to firms that paid kickbacks, sold licences, protected gambling and saloons, and sold **franchises** (the right to run streetcars, gas, water and telephone services) to businessmen. Business gained, because franchises were valuable, and the machines gained, because businessmen paid for them.

**The Tweed Ring.** William M. "Boss" Tweed ran Tammany Hall and New York City in the late 1860s with a ring of officials. Their best-known project was the new County Courthouse, which cost more than $12 million, far above its first estimate, because contractors paid inflated bills and returned part of the money to the Ring. Estimates of the total stolen range widely, from about $30 million to well over $100 million. In July 1871 the *New York Times* published copies of the accounts, and the cartoonist Thomas Nast attacked the Ring in *Harper's Weekly*. Tweed was convicted in 1873, escaped from custody in 1875, was recaptured in Spain and died in a New York jail in 1878. But Tammany Hall survived him and went on to win elections under later leaders, including Richard Croker, because it still delivered favours that the poor needed.

Machines existed in many cities: Chicago, Philadelphia, Boston and San Francisco. In 1905 a Tammany district leader, George Washington Plunkitt, told a journalist that he had made a fortune from "honest graft" (profiting from inside knowledge, such as buying land that the city planned to turn into a park) while condemning "dishonest graft" such as bribery and blackmail.

:::key The Boss system: why it lasted
1. **A real need**: rapid growth, immigrants who needed help and weak city governments.
2. **Mutual benefit**: voters got jobs and relief; bosses got power and money; businessmen got franchises and contracts.
3. **Costs**: corruption, high taxes and debt, poor services, and contempt for the law.
4. **Opposition**: newspapers, reformers and the middle classes, whose campaigns became part of the Progressive Movement.
A good answer shows both sides: machines were corrupt *and* a social service for the poor.
:::

## The growth of trusts and monopolies

### What a trust was

:::def Trust
Strictly, a legal device by which shareholders in several companies handed their shares to a board of trustees in exchange for certificates, so the trustees could run the firms as one. The word came to mean any large combination that controlled a whole industry. Standard Oil's trust agreement of 1882 was the model.
:::

:::def Monopoly
Control of the supply of a good or service by a single firm or group, so that buyers have no choice and the seller can set the price. In the Gilded Age a firm that controlled most of an industry, such as Standard Oil in refining, was called a monopoly.
:::

Firms grew bigger and combined in two ways, and the best examples used both.

- **Horizontal combination:** buying or driving out competitors in the same business so as to control the market. Rockefeller's Standard Oil, founded in Ohio in 1870, did this in refining. In 1872 he used a scheme called the South Improvement Company to win secret **rebates** (discounts) from the railroads for shipping oil, and bought up rivals who could not compete. By about 1880 Standard Oil refined, on the usual estimate, some 90 per cent of American oil. In 1882 its owners put their shares into a trust; after Ohio's Supreme Court ordered the trust dissolved in 1892, the company reorganised as a **holding company** under the lenient laws of New Jersey in 1899.
- **Vertical integration:** owning every stage from raw material to finished product to cut costs and secure supplies. Carnegie's steel works bought coal fields, coke ovens, iron-ore mines, a fleet of lake ships and a railroad to carry the ore.

### Why trusts grew

- **Economies of scale.** Steelworks, refineries and railroads had enormous fixed costs, so the more they produced, the lower the cost per unit.
- **Ruinous competition.** In the depression after 1873, firms cut prices to keep customers and went bankrupt; combining was a way to stop the price war.
- **Railroad rebates and tariffs.** Big shippers won discounts from the railroads that small firms could not match, and tariffs kept foreign competition out.
- **Law.** States such as New Jersey (1889 and 1896) passed laws that let one company own shares in others, and courts defended property and the freedom of contract.
- **Investment bankers.** J.P. Morgan and others arranged mergers and reorganised railroads, preferring ordered markets to cut-throat competition.
- **The great merger wave of 1897–1904.** After the depression of the 1890s ended, hundreds of firms merged into giants. The biggest was **U.S. Steel**. In 1901 J.P. Morgan bought Carnegie Steel for about $480 million and combined it with other firms, forming a company capitalised at about $1.4 billion, the first company in the world worth more than a billion dollars. It produced about two-thirds of the nation's steel.

### Impact on political parties, states and legislatures

Business wanted favourable laws, and had the money to get them. Companies and their lawyers lobbied state legislatures for charters, franchises and land; paid for the campaigns of both parties; and sometimes bribed members outright. The writer **Henry Demarest Lloyd** accused Standard Oil of corrupting legislatures in *Wealth Against Commonwealth* (1894). In 1896 the Republican organiser Mark Hanna raised a campaign fund for William McKinley that was several times larger than the Democrats', much of it from industrialists and banks frightened by Bryan's silver policy. Until the Seventeenth Amendment (1913), senators were chosen by state legislatures, so a business that controlled a legislature could place its own men in the Senate. Several senators were millionaires, such as Leland Stanford of California, a railroad owner, and Nelson Aldrich of Rhode Island. The Senate came to be called a "millionaires' club". On 23 January 1889 the humorous magazine *Puck* (which backed the Democrats) published a famous cartoon by Joseph Keppler, "The Bosses of the Senate", showing giant money bags labelled with trusts (steel, copper, oil, iron, sugar, tin, coal and others) towering over tiny senators at their desks, while the visitors' "People's Entrance" is bolted and barred. Both parties were under the influence of business, and neither was a simple tool of the trusts, but few politicians dared to attack them.

States passed their own anti-trust laws (Kansas and others), but they could not control companies operating across state lines, and states competed to offer the most lenient incorporation laws.

### Impact on the Federal government and the economy

**The Interstate Commerce Act (4 February 1887)** was the first federal regulation of business. It barred railroads from charging "unjust and unreasonable" rates, from giving rebates and from pooling traffic, and set up the **Interstate Commerce Commission (ICC)** of five members. It was weak: the ICC could not set rates and had to go to the courts, which often ruled for the railroads.

**The Sherman Anti-Trust Act (2 July 1890)** declared "every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce among the several States" illegal, and made it a crime to "monopolize". It was passed by overwhelming majorities, partly to answer voters' anger, but its wording was vague, and the Justice Department was small. In *United States v. E.C. Knight Co.* (1895) the Supreme Court held that the sugar trust, which controlled some 98 per cent of sugar refining, was not covered, because manufacturing was not "commerce". The government brought few cases in the 1890s, and one of its first successes was to use the Act against the American Railway Union in the Pullman strike (*In re Debs*, 1895). Not until Theodore Roosevelt's presidency (1901–09) did the Act begin to be used against large combinations (see [the next chapter](#hist.us.gilded.3)).

**The economy.** The effects of the trusts were the subject of argument even then.

:::cols
:::col The case that trusts were harmful
- They destroyed competitors by rebates and price-cutting, and reduced choice.
- They could charge high prices and cut output once a market was controlled.
- They paid low wages and crushed unions with their wealth.
- They corrupted politics and gave a few men power over the economy.
- Their financial manipulation caused crises such as 1893.
:::
:::col The case that trusts brought benefits
- They cut costs by economies of scale; steel rails fell to about a tenth of their 1860s price and kerosene prices fell by well over half.
- They invested in research, new plant and better management.
- They built the national industries on which America's rise as a power depended.
- Their owners gave huge sums to libraries, universities and hospitals.
- Competitive markets were unstable and led to bankruptcies and unemployment.
:::
:::

### The Robber Barons

:::def Robber Baron
A critic's label for the great industrialists and financiers of the Gilded Age, who were said to have made their fortunes by ruthless and dishonest means: bribery, stock manipulation, rebates and crushing rivals. The name recalls medieval German lords who robbed travellers on the Rhine. Defenders prefer "captains of industry".
:::

| Person | Business | Why they matter |
|---|---|---|
| Cornelius Vanderbilt | New York Central and other railroads | Built a railroad empire; left about $100 million in 1877 |
| Jay Gould | Railroads and Wall Street | Attempted a corner in gold (Black Friday, 24 September 1869); symbol of speculation |
| John D. Rockefeller | Standard Oil | Dominated oil refining by rebates and takeovers; later a huge philanthropist |
| Andrew Carnegie | Carnegie Steel | Cut costs through vertical integration; wrote the Gospel of Wealth; Homestead 1892 |
| Henry Clay Frick | Coke and Carnegie Steel | Ran the Homestead works; hired the Pinkertons |
| J.P. Morgan | Investment banking | Reorganised railroads, formed U.S. Steel, rescued the Treasury in 1895 and Wall Street in 1907 |
| James J. Hill | Great Northern Railway | Built a transcontinental line without a federal land grant; often held up as a "captain" |
| Collis Huntington and Leland Stanford | Central Pacific and Southern Pacific | Used public land and loans; later accused of controlling California politics |

The label was popularised by the journalist **Matthew Josephson** in *The Robber Barons* (1934), written in the depression, which presented the industrialists as plunderers. Later historians such as Allan Nevins and **Alfred Chandler** answered that these men also built efficient enterprises and cut prices, and that the term hides differences: Hill and Carnegie created much of what they owned, while Gould largely manipulated stock. A balanced view uses both labels and judges each man on his record. Two of the most famous remarks about the age, Vanderbilt's "The public be damned" (reported in 1882), and Gould's boast that he could hire half the working class to kill the other half, are not reliable evidence: the first was reported in a newspaper and Vanderbilt later denied the sense in which it was used, and the second is probably legend.

:::key Trusts and monopolies: the argument to learn
1. **Growth**: trusts grew out of economies of scale, price wars, rebates, tariffs, friendly state laws and investment bankers.
2. **Politics**: they gave business great influence on parties, state legislatures and the Senate.
3. **Federal response**: the Interstate Commerce Act (1887) and the Sherman Act (1890) were weak; the Supreme Court narrowed them (*E.C. Knight*, 1895); the Sherman Act was used first against strikers.
4. **Economy**: lower costs and prices and new industries, but monopoly power, rebates and instability.
5. **Judgement**: the question is not "good or bad" but "good for whom and in what way". Efficiency benefits were real; the harm lay in power over markets and politics.
:::

## The rise of organised labour

### Why workers organised

Workers faced employers who were large, rich and united. A single worker could not bargain with a steel company. Employers used **blacklists** (lists of union members that firms agreed not to hire), **lockouts** (shutting the plant), the **"yellow-dog" contract** (a promise not to join a union) and private detective agencies such as the Pinkertons. Governors sent state militia to strikes, and federal judges issued **injunctions** (court orders banning strike activity). Workers were also divided by skill, race, language and religion, which employers exploited.

### The National Labor movement

The **National Labor Union (NLU)**, founded at Baltimore in August 1866, was the first attempt at a national federation. Led by the iron moulder **William H. Sylvis**, it campaigned for the eight-hour day, for cooperatives that workers would own and for greenbacks (cheap money). It welcomed women and reformers, as well as trade unionists, but its Black members formed their own Colored National Labor Union in 1869. It collapsed after Sylvis's death in 1869 and the Panic of 1873.

### The Knights of Labor

The **Knights of Labor** were founded in Philadelphia on 28 December 1869 by Uriah Stephens and a group of garment cutters. They began as a secret society, with rituals, to protect members from being blacklisted. Under **Terence Powderly**, Grand Master Workman from 1879, they dropped the secrecy. Their key features:
- They organised **all workers**, skilled and unskilled, men and women, Black and white, native-born and immigrant, in local assemblies by area and not by craft. They excluded bankers, lawyers, liquor dealers and gamblers, and, in practice, the Chinese, whom they supported excluding.
- Their aims were broad and reforming: an eight-hour day, an end to child labour and convict labour, equal pay for equal work, cooperatives, and an end to the wage system itself in favour of workers owning their enterprises. Powderly preferred arbitration, education and politics to strikes.
- In 1885 a strike against the railroads of Jay Gould on the Wabash and Southwest systems forced Gould to restore wage cuts, and was hailed as a victory. In the next year membership surged, from about 100,000 in mid-1885 to **about 700,000 or more in 1886**.

### Haymarket and the decline of the Knights

In 1886 a national campaign for the eight-hour day led to strikes across the country, with some 300,000 workers involved at the start of May. In Chicago, on 3 May, police fired on strikers at the McCormick Reaper Works, killing at least one. The next evening, 4 May, anarchists called a rally at **Haymarket Square**. It was peaceful until, as police ordered the crowd to disperse, someone threw a bomb. It killed one policeman at once; in the bomb and the shooting that followed, seven police officers and at least four civilians died. No one proved who had thrown the bomb. Eight anarchists were tried on the theory that their speeches had incited it; seven were sentenced to death, and four were hanged on 11 November 1887. In 1893 Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three survivors, saying the trial had been unfair.

Haymarket was not the work of the Knights, but newspapers and employers treated it as proof that unions meant violence and foreign radicalism. Employers counter-attacked: Gould's railroad crushed the Southwest strike of 1886 and blacklisted the strikers. The Knights lost members as fast as they had gained them, falling below 100,000 by 1890.

### The American Federation of Labor

The **American Federation of Labor (AFL)** grew from the Federation of Organized Trades and Labor Unions (1881) and was reorganised at Columbus, Ohio, in December 1886. Its president for almost all of the next 38 years was **Samuel Gompers**, a cigar-maker born in London.
- It was a federation of **craft unions** of skilled workers, each controlling its own members and funds: carpenters, cigar-makers, printers, iron moulders.
- Its approach, called **"pure and simple" unionism** or "bread-and-butter" unionism, aimed at immediate gains: higher wages, shorter hours (the eight-hour day) and better conditions, through collective bargaining and, where needed, strikes backed by strike funds. It did not seek to abolish the wage system or to form a labour party; it rewarded its friends and punished its enemies among the major parties.
- It grew steadily: about half a million members by 1900 and about 1.7 million by 1904.
- Its limits: it excluded most unskilled workers, most women and many Black workers (several affiliated unions had colour bars), and even in 1904 it represented only a minority of the industrial workforce.

:::key Knights of Labor and AFL compared
1. **Membership**: Knights, all workers including unskilled, women and Black workers; AFL, skilled craft workers organised by trade.
2. **Aims**: Knights, to reform society and end the wage system; AFL, immediate improvements in wages, hours and conditions.
3. **Methods**: Knights, education, cooperatives and arbitration (strikes unwelcome); AFL, collective bargaining and the strike.
4. **Outcome**: Knights, a sudden rise in 1885–86 and a rapid collapse; AFL, slow, steady growth to 1914.
:::

### The great strikes

| Date | Place and industry | Cause | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| July 1877 | Railroads, from Martinsburg, West Virginia, to Pittsburgh, Chicago and St Louis | Wage cuts by the Baltimore and Ohio and other railroads in a depression | Spread across the country; militia and federal troops called in; over 100 deaths on most estimates; the cuts were not reversed; states built armouries |
| 1886 | Gould's Southwest railroad system | Knights' demand for union recognition | Defeated; strikers blacklisted |
| 4 May 1886 | Haymarket, Chicago | Eight-hour-day strikes; police killing of strikers | Bomb and executions; Knights discredited |
| 1892 | Homestead steelworks, Pennsylvania | Wage cut and lockout by Carnegie Steel | Union broken; steel stayed largely non-union until the 1930s |
| 1894 | Pullman Palace Car Company and the railroads | Wage cuts of about a quarter with no cut in rents; the ARU boycott | Broken by court injunction and federal troops; Debs jailed |

**The Great Railroad Strike (July 1877)** began on 16 July when workers of the Baltimore and Ohio Railroad at Martinsburg stopped trains after the second wage cut within a year. It spread along the lines to Baltimore, Pittsburgh, Chicago and St Louis. Crowds of strikers and unemployed people joined in; rail yards were burned and, at Pittsburgh, a clash with militia left many dead. President Rutherford Hayes sent federal troops to protect the railroads. The strike ended in early August and the railroads kept their wage cuts, but the strike showed that industrial workers could stop the national economy, and frightened the middle classes.

**Homestead, 1892.** The Carnegie Steel works at Homestead, near Pittsburgh, were run by the chairman, **Henry Clay Frick**, while Andrew Carnegie was in Scotland. The skilled workers belonged to the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers; only about 800 of some 3,800 workers were members, but nearly all of them backed the union. When the contract expired at the end of June 1892, Frick proposed a pay cut, refused to negotiate with the union, built a fence of barbed wire around the works and locked the workers out. He hired about 300 armed guards from the Pinkerton Agency. When the Pinkertons came up the Monongahela River on barges before dawn on **6 July**, thousands of strikers and townspeople fought them in a battle that lasted most of the day. Seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed; the Pinkertons surrendered and were beaten as they were marched through the town. Governor Robert Pattison sent about 8,000 militia on 12 July, and the works reopened with non-union men. On 23 July an anarchist, Alexander Berkman, who had no connection to the workers, tried to kill Frick; Frick survived, but the attack turned public opinion against the strikers. The strike collapsed in November. The union was broken, wages were cut and steelworkers faced twelve-hour days with no union for over forty years. In his autobiography, published in 1920, Carnegie wrote: "No pangs remain of any wound received in my business career save that of Homestead." Congress investigated the use of Pinkertons and in 1893 barred the federal government from employing them (the Anti-Pinkerton Act).

**Pullman, 1894.** The Pullman Palace Car Company built sleeping cars and owned the town of Pullman, Illinois, where its workers had to live. After the Panic of 1893 it cut wages by about a quarter but did not cut rents and prices in the town. A delegation of workers who complained was sacked. On 11 May 1894 the Pullman workers struck. They were members of the **American Railway Union (ARU)**, founded in 1893 by **Eugene V. Debs** as an industrial union open to all railroad workers. On 26 June the ARU began a boycott: members refused to handle Pullman cars, which stopped trains on twenty-nine railroads and involved about 125,000 workers. The railroad owners, organised in the General Managers' Association, held out. Attorney General Richard Olney, who had been a railroad lawyer, obtained a federal court injunction against the boycott under the Sherman Act and for blocking the mails. When Debs refused to call it off, President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops to Chicago on 4 July, over the objection of the governor of Illinois, John Peter Altgeld. Rioting followed, about thirty people died, and the strike collapsed. Debs was jailed for six months for contempt of court, and in *In re Debs* (1895) the Supreme Court upheld the use of injunctions against strikes. He emerged a convert to socialism. In June 1894, in the middle of the dispute, Cleveland had signed a law making Labor Day a national holiday.

:::key What the great strikes show
1. **Employers were stronger**: they had money, blacklists, private armies and the support of the courts and the army.
2. **Government sided with employers**: troops in 1877 and 1894, an injunction in 1894, militia in 1892. It did so to defend property and order, and on the grounds that strikes stopped the mails and interstate trade.
3. **Workers were divided**: by skill (Homestead's skilled and unskilled), race and nationality, and by the choice between reform politics (the Knights) and bargaining (the AFL).
4. **Violence discredited labour in the eyes of the middle class**: Haymarket, Berkman's attack and the Pullman riots.
5. **But the strikes also changed attitudes**: they led to a federal inquiry, to Labor Day and to arguments that the courts and the law should treat unions more fairly.
:::

## Farm organisations and populist movements

### The farmers' grievances

Farmers in the South and on the Great Plains were producers of crops sold on a world market and customers of an industrial economy that they felt cheated them. They had five main grievances:
1. **Falling prices.** Cotton fell to about five cents a pound in the mid-1890s, less than half its price of the mid-1870s, and wheat to under 50 cents a bushel, about half its early-1870s price, because of expanding production in America and abroad.
2. **Debt and the money supply.** Farmers had borrowed to buy land and machines; falling prices meant that they had to repay with dearer dollars. They blamed the gold standard and the shortage of money. In the South, the crop-lien system tied tenant farmers to storekeepers who lent against the next crop at high prices and interest.
3. **Railroad rates.** Railroads were the only way to market, and charged more where they had no competition. Grain elevator owners and middlemen added their own charges.
4. **The tariff.** It raised the prices of manufactured goods farmers bought, but did not protect the crops they sold.
5. **A sense of political neglect.** Farmers felt that bankers, railroads and trusts bought the legislatures that should have helped them.

### The Grange and the Granger laws

The **National Grange of the Patrons of Husbandry** was founded in 1867 by Oliver Hudson Kelley, a clerk in the Department of Agriculture, as a social and educational order for farm families. After the Panic of 1873 it grew into a movement, with more than 800,000 members in 1875. It set up cooperative stores, grain elevators and factories (most failed), and its members took political action. Midwestern states passed **Granger laws** in the early 1870s that set maximum rates for railroad freight and grain-elevator storage and set up commissions: Illinois (1871 and 1873), Wisconsin, Iowa and Minnesota.

The railroads challenged the laws in the courts, but in *Munn v. Illinois* (1 March 1877) the Supreme Court, under Chief Justice Morrison Waite, upheld an Illinois law fixing grain-elevator charges. Property "affected with a public interest", the Court said, could be regulated for the public good. This opened the way to regulation. But in *Wabash, St Louis and Pacific Railway v. Illinois* (1886) the Court ruled that states could not regulate rates on traffic that crossed state lines, which created a gap that only the federal government could fill: the Interstate Commerce Act of 1887. The Grange declined in the later 1870s as its cooperatives failed and farm prices recovered, but its laws and the principle of public regulation lasted.

### The Greenback Party

Farmers and workers who wanted cheap money formed the **Greenback Party** (1874, renamed the Greenback-Labor Party in 1878). Its demands were that the government stop withdrawing greenbacks from circulation and print more paper money, to raise prices and make debts easier to pay. The government rejected this and resumed payment of greenbacks in gold on 1 January 1879. The party won about a million votes and more than ten seats in Congress in the elections of 1878, and its 1880 presidential candidate, James B. Weaver of Iowa, won about three per cent of the vote. It faded as prosperity returned in the 1880s and as the Bland–Allison Act (1878) appeared to meet some demands with silver, but it linked farmers, workers and the issue of money, and its ideas passed to the Populists.

### The Farmers' Alliances

In the 1880s the Southern Farmers' Alliance, founded in Texas in the 1870s, and the Northern Alliance spread across the South and the Plains, organising cooperatives, lectures and a newspaper network. By 1890 the Southern Alliance claimed over a million members. Its leader Charles Macune proposed the **sub-treasury plan** (1889): the government would build warehouses where farmers could store crops and borrow up to 80 per cent of their value in paper money, and so escape the crop lien. A separate Colored Farmers' Alliance enrolled Black farmers. The Alliances elected candidates in 1890 and prepared the ground for a third party.

### The People's Party and the Populists

The **People's Party** (the "Populists") was formed in 1891–92 by the Alliances, the Knights of Labor and reform groups. At its founding convention at **Omaha, Nebraska, on 4 July 1892**, it adopted a platform written by Ignatius Donnelly that began: "We meet in the midst of a nation brought to the verge of moral, political, and material ruin." It declared that "Corruption dominates the ballot-box, the Legislatures, the Congress, and touches even the ermine of the bench," and that "we breed the two great classes—tramps and millionaires." Its demands included:
- **free and unlimited coinage of silver** at the ratio of 16 to 1, to increase the money supply;
- a **graduated income tax**;
- government **ownership of the railroads**, telegraph and telephone;
- the **sub-treasury** system of farm credit;
- the **direct election of senators**, the secret ballot and the eight-hour day.
These went far further than the AFL and, in several cases, were later adopted by the Progressives.

In the election of **1892** its candidate James B. Weaver won about one million votes (about 8.5 per cent) and 22 electoral votes, carrying Kansas, Colorado, Idaho and Nevada. He was the first third-party candidate since 1860 to win any electoral votes. In the South, some Populists such as Tom Watson of Georgia tried to unite poor Black and white farmers, but the effort ran into racist violence and fraud by the Democrats.

The depression of 1893 strengthened the party, which gained votes in 1894. But in 1896 the issue of silver split it. The Democratic convention in Chicago adopted a free-silver platform, and the 36-year-old William Jennings Bryan of Nebraska won the nomination with his **"Cross of Gold" speech** (8 July 1896), saying: "You shall not press down upon the brow of labor this crown of thorns, you shall not crucify mankind upon a cross of gold." The Populists then had to choose between running their own candidate and backing Bryan. They backed him ("fusion"), nominating Tom Watson for vice-president. The Republican William McKinley, with Mark Hanna's huge fund, ran a campaign that presented Bryan as a danger to property and to jobs, and won with about 51 per cent of the vote and 271 electoral votes to Bryan's 176. McKinley carried the Northeast and the industrial Midwest, where factory workers and city voters feared free silver. The Populists split and collapsed; the Gold Standard Act of 1900 and the return of prosperity (with new gold discoveries in Alaska and South Africa raising prices) took away their main issue.

:::note The historians' debate: what were the Populists?
- **Richard Hofstadter**, in *The Age of Reform* (1955), saw the Populists as backward-looking small farmers, nostalgic for an agrarian past, prone to conspiracy theories and prejudice.
- **Lawrence Goodwyn**, in *The Populist Moment* (1978), argued the opposite: that the movement grew from the Alliance's cooperative experiment, was a democratic mass movement of farmers who challenged the financial system, and was defeated by the bankers and by the Democrats' take-over of silver in 1896.
Later historians stress the movement's diversity and its mix of forward-looking and backward-looking ideas. For an exam answer, the point is that the Populists' programme (income tax, direct election of senators, regulation of railroads) was largely realised by the Progressives and the New Deal.
:::

:::key The Populists: strengths and weaknesses
1. **Why they grew**: falling prices, debts, railroad rates, depression and distrust of the two parties.
2. **What they achieved**: a national platform, about a million votes in 1892, and ideas adopted by later reformers.
3. **Why they failed**: they were a movement of farmers in a country that was becoming urban and industrial; they could not win workers in the East; the Democrats took over silver; they split over fusion; the South's racial politics and fraud divided and defeated them; recovery after 1897 removed the grievance.
:::

## Weighing the consequences: who gained and who paid?

Questions on this key question often ask whether growth did more good than harm, or which group was most affected. A table by group helps you see that the answer depends on who you ask.

| Group | Gains | Costs |
|---|---|---|
| Owners and investors | Vast fortunes; the fortunes of Rockefeller and Carnegie; new industries | Panics, bankruptcies (railroads in 1873 and 1893), political attack |
| Skilled workers | Rising real wages in many trades; the AFL won shorter hours and better pay for some | Deskilling by machines; layoffs in depressions; the loss at Homestead |
| Unskilled workers and children | Jobs, often for immigrants | Low pay, danger, long hours, no union, child labour |
| Farmers | Railroads to market; cheap machines | Falling prices, debt, high freight rates, the tariff |
| City dwellers | Jobs, shops, entertainment, cheap goods | Tenements, disease, corrupt government |
| Consumers | Cheaper steel, kerosene, cloth and food | Monopoly prices in some trades |
| The nation | World leadership in output | Inequality, class conflict, concentrated power |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Rapid growth made the United States richer and more powerful, but it did so **unevenly and unstably**. The economy suffered two depressions in 20 years; many workers' conditions were dangerous and insecure; and a few firms and bankers gained power over markets and politics. The response was a long argument about whether the market could correct itself, as the Gospel of Wealth and Sumner claimed, or whether workers, farmers and government should act. In this period the answer was mainly *no regulation*: governments sided with property, strikes failed and the Populists lost. That failure is the reason the **Progressive Movement** of 1890–1920 arose.
:::

:::warn Common mistakes
- **Do not treat "the Panics" as one event.** 1873 (railroad speculation, Jay Cooke) and 1893 (railroads, gold reserve, silver) had related but different causes.
- **Do not confuse the Knights and the AFL.** The Knights organised all workers and aimed to reform society; the AFL organised skilled craft workers and aimed at wages, hours and conditions.
- **Do not say the Sherman Act "broke up the trusts".** In the 1890s it was weakly enforced and was used against strikers; break-ups came later.
- **Do not say Homestead was won by the workers or the Pinkertons.** The Pinkertons surrendered, but the state militia and the company won the strike.
- **Do not describe the Populists as a farmers' party only.** They were an alliance of farmers, with some workers and reformers, and their ideas on railroads, income tax and senators were adopted later.
- **Do not forget the benefits.** Cheaper steel, kerosene and goods are as much a "consequence" as slums.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the Knights of Labor declined after 1886.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The Knights of Labor grew very fast and had about 700,000 members in 1886. Then the Haymarket bomb went off in Chicago on 4 May 1886 and killed police. The Knights lost some strikes. By 1890 they had under 100,000 members. The AFL was set up in December 1886. So the Knights declined because of Haymarket and the AFL."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but every sentence describes an event. "So" claims a cause without explaining how Haymarket or the AFL reduced the Knights. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The first reason was Haymarket. The Knights had not organised the rally or thrown the bomb, but employers and the newspapers presented it as proof that unions meant violence and foreign anarchism. Public opinion turned against organised labour, and the Knights, as the largest and best-known union, suffered most. A second reason was the failure of strikes. In 1885 the Knights had forced Jay Gould to restore wage cuts, which brought a flood of new members, but in 1886 Gould crushed their strike on the Southwest railroads and blacklisted the strikers, and employers across the country copied him with lockouts and blacklists. Members who had joined expecting quick wins left when the strikes failed."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason lay in the Knights' own organisation. Powderly disliked strikes and preferred arbitration, cooperatives and political reform, but local assemblies struck anyway, and the order grew from about 100,000 to about 700,000 members in a year with no way to train or control them. Skilled workers, who could win wages and hours through a union of their own craft with a strike fund, found the AFL, founded in December 1886 under Samuel Gompers, a better fit.

These factors were linked. The Knights' rapid growth rested on a few strike victories; once Haymarket and the employers' counter-attack turned public opinion against them and defeated the strikes, an organisation that had grown too fast and was divided about aims had nothing to hold its members. The most important reason was the employer counter-attack after Haymarket, because without it the Knights' organisational weaknesses might have been survivable; the rise of the AFL was a result of the Knights' decline as much as a cause, since skilled workers left because the Knights were failing."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected**, and a supported conclusion about the most important factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: To what extent did the growth of trusts harm the American economy in the years 1870–1900?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Harm: rebates and the South Improvement Company (1872) squeezed out small refiners; Standard Oil controlled about 90 per cent of refining; monopoly could raise prices; low wages and crushing of unions (Homestead 1892); corruption of legislatures (Lloyd 1894); manipulation of railroad finance helped cause the Panics.
- Benefits: economies of scale cut costs (steel rails about a tenth of their 1860s price; kerosene down by more than half); Carnegie's integration and Standard Oil's efficiency; investment and research; stability instead of price wars.
- Distinguish: harm to rivals, workers and politics versus harm to the economy's output and prices.
- Judgement: the economy as a whole grew fast; the harm was real but fell more on competitors, workers and democracy than on output. Criterion: did output and living standards rise or fall?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The growth of trusts harmed the American economy. Rockefeller's Standard Oil used rebates from the railroads to drive its rivals out of business, and by about 1880 it controlled about 90 per cent of oil refining, so there was no competition and it could set prices. Carnegie's company defeated the union at Homestead in 1892 and cut wages. Trusts also bribed legislators, as Lloyd's book of 1894 said, and the Sherman Act of 1890 did not stop them. So trusts harmed the economy."

*Commentary.* Detailed and accurate, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts. It does not say what "harm to the economy" means, and does not look at the other side. A paragraph like this, with the benefits merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the trusts also lowered costs and prices, which is evidence against harm to the economy. The price of steel rails fell to about a tenth of its 1860s level, and kerosene prices fell by more than half, because large works, vertical integration and constant cost-cutting made production cheaper. Carnegie bought his own coal, coke and ore, and his works produced steel at lower cost than any in Britain. Rockefeller's refineries wasted less and shipped in bulk. Combination also ended the price wars that had bankrupted firms in the depression of the 1870s. And America's total output grew faster than any other country's in the period, so whatever the trusts did, they did not prevent growth.

The harm, therefore, was less to total output than to the way the gains were shared and to the conditions of competition. Rebates were a form of favouritism that small firms could not match, and the monopoly power of Standard Oil depended on them as much as on efficiency. Workers at Homestead lost their union and faced twelve-hour days; consumers in markets with no rival paid whatever the firm decided. The greatest damage was to the political system: Lloyd's charges of bribed legislatures, the 'millionaires' club' Senate and the Supreme Court's narrowing of the Sherman Act in *E.C. Knight* (1895) meant that the law could not restrain the firms.

Overall, the trusts harmed the economy only to a limited extent. If the test is what happened to output, prices and investment, they probably helped. If the test is competition, the position of small business, workers' bargaining power and the independence of government, they did harm. Since the question asks about the *economy*, the better judgement is that the harm was real but secondary to the benefits of scale, and that the case against the trusts is strongest on fairness and politics rather than on economic efficiency."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (output and prices versus competition and fairness) and distinguishes between kinds of harm. With an opening paragraph on the case for harm, this is Level 5 (19–20). Note that the best answers define "harm to the economy" before judging it.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

Before their cable reached me in Scotland, the Governor of the State had appeared on the scene with troops. ... Suits had been entered against many of the strikers, but upon my return these were promptly dismissed. ... All the old men who remained, and had not been guilty of violence, were taken back. ... No pangs remain of any wound received in my business career save that of Homestead.

*Adapted from the Autobiography of Andrew Carnegie, published in 1920 after his death, chapter on the Homestead strike. Sentences from different parts of the chapter are joined.*

**Source B**

Hereafter no employee of the Pinkerton Detective Agency, or similar agency, shall be employed by the Government of the United States or the District of Columbia.

*Adapted from the Anti-Pinkerton Act, passed by Congress as part of an appropriations law, 3 March 1893.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the consequences of the Homestead strike of 1892.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is by Carnegie and says he was in Scotland and the Governor sent troops. Source B is a law that stops the government using Pinkertons. Both are about Homestead. They are different because A is a person and B is a law. A is biased because Carnegie owned the company."

*Commentary.* One difference identified by type. No similarity explained; Source B is never linked to the events; the comment on bias is a label with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"Both sources show that Homestead left lasting regret or unease about how the dispute was handled, and both link it to the use of force. Source A says that 'no pangs remain of any wound received in my business career save that of Homestead', which accepts that the affair hurt Carnegie, and its mention of troops and of 'suits' against strikers recalls the state's heavy involvement. Source B's ban on Pinkertons in federal employment shows that Congress, too, found their use to be unacceptable.

They differ in perspective and scope. Source A is personal and defensive. It stresses distance ('in Scotland'), leniency (suits 'promptly dismissed', the old men 'taken back') and that those taken back 'had not been guilty of violence', which puts the blame on violent strikers and leaves out the Pinkerton barges and the 3,800-man lockout. Source B is impersonal and legal; it does not mention Homestead or the strike, and it says nothing about the workers' grievances.

The context explains the differences. Carnegie wrote in his old age, when he had given away most of his fortune and wanted to be remembered as a friend of working people; in 1886 he had written that workers had a right to form unions. Most historians conclude that he had approved Frick's non-union policy in advance, so his claim of distance serves his reputation. Source B was passed in 1893, after a congressional investigation of the Pinkertons at Homestead, in a mood of public anger at a private army fighting citizens. Its limits are clear: it bound only the federal government, and employers could still hire Pinkertons. Source A is therefore strong evidence of how Carnegie chose to remember the strike, but weak on events, and Source B is strong evidence of the political reaction, but only indirect evidence about Homestead itself."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through the nature, origin and purpose of each source and the context of 1892–93; and a final comparison of their value for the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: C (trusts as "bosses" of the Senate); B ("millionaires" and corruption of the Legislatures and Congress). Challenge or qualify: A (corruption is the politicians' own opportunity, in a city machine, with no business "boss"); D (the criminal class is "Congress", not business); B (blames "governmental injustice" as well as wealth).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a Tammany leader boasting about his own profit; he is recorded by a journalist and edited, and he wrote in 1905 to justify the machine by separating "honest" from "dishonest" graft, so it is self-serving. But it is a rare insider's account, and it shows that city politicians made money from public improvements with no pressure from a trust.
- **B**: a party platform written to win farmers' votes, dramatic by design ("tramps and millionaires"). It shows what a movement of about a million voters in 1892 believed, not what was proved. Its blame is on "governmental injustice", which fits both explanations.
- **C**: a visual source from *Puck*, a humorous magazine that supported the Democrats, published weeks before the Republican Benjamin Harrison took office after an election in which business money was important. It is satire, with exaggeration. But senators were then chosen by state legislatures, and many were wealthy: Stanford, a railroad owner, and Aldrich among them.
- **D**: a joke by a famous satirist, in 1897, who had co-written *The Gilded Age* about congressional bribery. It is not evidence, but a reliable sign of the contempt in which Congress was held.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources B and C support the view that business was the cause of political corruption, and C, as a visual source, dramatises it: the trusts are the 'bosses', the people are locked out. But they are partisan documents, Puck for the Democrats and the Populist preamble for a party with no power, and both exaggerate for effect. Sources A and D point elsewhere. In A the corruption belongs to the politician who takes his 'opportunities' in city government, where the machine's power came from votes and patronage; D blames Congress itself. My own knowledge supports both sides. Business did corrupt: the Crédit Mobilier scandal of 1872 gave shares to congressmen, Lloyd accused Standard Oil of buying legislatures, and Hanna raised a huge fund for McKinley in 1896. But the spoils system, in which jobs went to party loyalists, and the machines like Tweed's, which grew out of immigration and weak city government, would have produced corruption without the trusts, and Plunkitt's world of city contracts had little to do with the Senate. On balance, the sources support the view only partly: big business was a major source of corruption in state and federal politics, but it was not the main cause in the cities, where the machine system and the lack of civil-service rules were at least as important. The word 'main' overstates it."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and context; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify, brings in own knowledge, and the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "'The Populists failed because of the issue of silver.' How far do you agree?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Populists were founded in 1892 at Omaha. Their candidate, James Weaver, won 22 electoral votes. They wanted free silver, a graduated income tax and government ownership of the railroads. In 1896 William Jennings Bryan made the Cross of Gold speech and the Populists backed him as their candidate. McKinley won the election. After that the Populists faded away. So the Populists failed because of silver."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story of the party without saying why silver mattered, and it does not consider other reasons for failure. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "Silver was the main reason for the Populists' collapse because it destroyed their independence. By 1896 the Democrats had adopted free silver and nominated Bryan, so the Populists, who had won about a million votes in 1892 on a wider programme, had to choose between a hopeless separate candidate and a fusion that swallowed their party; they backed Bryan and, when he lost to McKinley, they had no distinct cause left. However, silver was not the only cause. The party had never won the industrial workers of the East, who feared that free silver would raise prices, and in the South its attempt to unite Black and white farmers was broken by fraud and violence. Recovery after 1897 removed the farmers' main grievances." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give two causes and two consequences of the Panic of 1893.
A: Causes: overbuilt, over-borrowed railroads (the Philadelphia and Reading failed in February 1893); the drain on the gold reserve and fear that the dollar would leave gold (partly because of the Sherman Silver Purchase Act); falling farm prices and debts; foreign investors withdrawing. Consequences: about 500 banks and 15,000 businesses failed; a quarter of the railroads went bankrupt; high unemployment; the Pullman strike and Coxey's Army; Democratic defeat in 1894; the silver issue and 1896.
Q: What was a political machine, and why did the poor support it?
A: An organisation led by a Boss that controlled a city party, won elections with jobs and favours for votes, and profited from contracts and franchises (Tammany Hall). The poor supported it because it gave them jobs, coal, help with rent and funerals, and a route to citizenship that no one else provided.
Q: Explain the difference between horizontal and vertical combination, with an example of each.
A: Horizontal combination buys or beats competitors at one stage of production (Standard Oil in refining). Vertical integration owns all the stages from raw material to product (Carnegie Steel: ore, coal, coke, ships, railroad and mills).
Q: What did the Interstate Commerce Act (1887) and the Sherman Act (1890) do, and why were they weak?
A: The Interstate Commerce Act banned unreasonable railroad rates and rebates and set up the ICC; the Sherman Act banned combinations in restraint of trade and monopolising. They were weak because the ICC could not set rates and courts often overruled it, and the Sherman Act was vague, was little enforced, was narrowed by *E.C. Knight* (1895) and was first used against strikers (*In re Debs*).
Q: Name three differences between the Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor.
A: The Knights recruited all workers (skilled and unskilled, women, Black and white), the AFL skilled craft workers; the Knights wanted to reform society and end the wage system, the AFL concentrated on wages, hours and conditions; the Knights favoured arbitration, cooperatives and politics, the AFL collective bargaining and strikes; the Knights rose in 1885–86 and collapsed, the AFL grew steadily.
Q: What happened at Homestead in 1892?
A: Carnegie Steel (under Frick) locked out members of the Amalgamated Association after a dispute over wages; 300 Pinkerton guards arrived by barge on 6 July and fought strikers (seven workers and three Pinkertons were killed); the state militia reopened the works; the strike failed by November and the union was broken.
Q: Why did farmers join the Grange, the Greenback Party and the People's Party?
A: They suffered falling crop prices, heavy debts, high railroad and elevator charges and the tariff; the Grange gave them cooperation and Granger laws; the Greenbackers wanted more paper money to raise prices; the Populists wanted silver, an income tax, public ownership of railroads and the sub-treasury.
Q: What did the Munn v. Illinois decision (1877) decide?
A: That a state could regulate a business "affected with a public interest" (grain elevators, and by extension railroads), upholding the Granger laws, although in 1886 the Court said states could not regulate interstate rates.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Panic of 1873 led to a long depression.
A: Indicative content: the failure of Jay Cooke and Company (18 September 1873) and the closure of the Stock Exchange destroyed confidence and credit; railroad construction, the leading industry, stopped and dozens of railroads failed, which cut demand for steel, coal and labour; banks failed or called in loans, so businesses went bankrupt; prices fell and the shortage of money made debts heavier; there was no central bank or government relief to restore confidence; the contraction lasted 65 months. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: the collapse of railroad finance spread through banks and industry because they were linked, and the lack of a lender of last resort made recovery slow. It ends with a judgement on the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why living conditions in the tenements of New York were so poor in the 1880s.
A: Indicative content: rapid immigration and a shortage of housing near jobs; landlords and builders crammed families into small lots to maximise rents (dumbbell tenements: few windows, shared water and toilets); the 1879 law set minimum standards that were low and little enforced; city government was weak and often corrupt (Tammany); poverty and irregular work meant families could not afford better and took in lodgers; no public transport at low cost to live further out. Level 4: connect the pressure of numbers and poverty to landlords' profit and weak regulation and judge which mattered most (e.g. landlord profit and lack of regulation made overcrowding possible, while poverty and immigration made it necessary).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why political machines such as Tammany Hall kept winning elections.
A: Indicative content: they gave concrete help (jobs, coal, funerals, bail, rent) to the poor in return for votes, when city government and charities did little; they helped immigrants to become citizens and voters; precinct captains knew every family; they controlled patronage and contracts, which paid for the system; ethnic loyalty (Irish leaders for Irish voters) and sometimes fraud; reformers were divided and middle-class. Level 4 connects the help given to voters with the money from contractors and judges which mattered most (the personal service that built loyalty).
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far was the American Federation of Labor more successful than the Knights of Labor?
A: For the AFL: steady growth (about half a million members by 1900, about 1.7 million by 1904) against the Knights' collapse after 1886; it won higher wages and shorter hours for many skilled workers; it survived depressions with strike funds and kept clear aims; it avoided Haymarket-style associations. Against: it represented only a minority of skilled workers and excluded the unskilled, most women and many Black workers, who were most in need; the Knights briefly organised 700,000, including many excluded by the AFL, and their reforms (eight-hour day, end of child labour) were later achieved; craft unions did not avoid defeat either: at Homestead (1892) the skilled steelworkers' union was broken. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "durability and gains for members" versus "range of workers helped", and judges, e.g., that the AFL was more successful in survival and results for its members, while the Knights were more successful in breadth and vision, though they failed to turn it into lasting organisation.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'In the years 1870–94, the government supported employers against workers.' How far do you agree?
H: List the occasions when troops, courts or injunctions were used in labour disputes (1877, 1892, 1894), and the occasions when government did the opposite.
H: Consider the courts (*In re Debs*, the Sherman Act used against unions), the Anti-Pinkerton Act, Labor Day and the Contract Labor Law (1885).
A: For: federal troops in 1877 and 1894; the Pullman injunction and *In re Debs*; state militia in 1892; the Sherman Act used against a union and not against trusts; no laws on hours, safety or child labour; the Supreme Court's defence of property. Against: Congress investigated the Pinkertons at Homestead and passed the Anti-Pinkerton Act (1893); Labor Day became a federal holiday in 1894; the Contract Labor Law (1885) answered union demands; Governor Altgeld refused to ask for troops in 1894; some state laws on hours and child labour; military intervention was justified as upholding law and the mails, not simply taking sides. Level 5 judges, e.g., that in the great strikes the government acted on the employers' side, but it was not the direct agent of employers and some policy responded to workers' pressure.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Populists failed because the Democrats took over their cause.' How far do you agree?
A: For: the Democrats adopted free silver and nominated Bryan in 1896, leaving the Populists with no distinct issue; fusion with Bryan split the party; Bryan lost to McKinley. Against: the Populists never won the industrial vote of the East; in the South, racism, fraud and violence divided and defeated them; recovery after 1897 removed grievances; the Republicans' campaign, funded by Hanna, frightened workers; the Populists' agrarian base was shrinking relative to the country. Level 5 uses a criterion (what decided the outcome in 1896), judging, e.g., that the Democratic take-over was the immediate cause of collapse, but the deeper reason was that the movement could not win beyond the farm states.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the regulation of the railroads in the 1870s and 1880s.
A: Similarities: both justify regulating charges in the public interest ("for the common good"; "reasonable and just"); both are legal texts. Differences: A is a court opinion setting a principle that states may regulate private property affected with a public interest, while B is a statute applying to charges in interstate transport; A upholds state power, B reflects the federal action needed after the Court said in 1886 that states could not regulate interstate rates. Provenance: A shows the Court's view of the Granger laws; B shows Congress's response to farmers' and shippers' pressure. Evaluation: A is strong evidence of the principle; B is strong evidence of what Congress decided but weaker evidence about regulation in practice because the ICC lacked power to set rates. Level 4 explains the link between the two through the Wabash decision and judges their value.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources agree that government should control private wealth and business in the public interest?
H: Decide which sources support control (A, B, C, D) and how far each goes: regulation of charges, ownership, taxation.
H: Use your own knowledge to ask what the Sherman Act and the weak ICC show about whether the control was effective.
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose: a court, a statute, a party platform, a steelmaker.
A: All four support some control, but they differ in degree. A (regulation of businesses affected with a public interest); B (a ban on unreasonable charges and discrimination); C (public ownership of the railroads: the most radical); D (taxation of large estates, from a leading industrialist). Challenge and qualification: the sources agree in principle, not in method, and they come from groups with different aims: the court and Congress offer limited regulation, the Populists a challenge to private ownership, and Carnegie a way to protect his idea of wealth as a trust for the public while avoiding regulation of business. Own knowledge: the ICC lacked power; the Sherman Act was weakly enforced (*E.C. Knight*); the Populists lost in 1896; inheritance taxes did not come until the 1910s. Level 5 judges how far: the sources agree that some public control was legitimate, but disagree on its limits, and in practice the control was far weaker than the sources suggest.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Lawrence Goodwyn, in *The Populist Moment* (1978), argued that Populism was a democratic movement that grew from the Alliance's cooperative experiment, while Richard Hofstadter, in *The Age of Reform* (1955), saw the Populists as nostalgic and prone to conspiracy theories. How far does the evidence of the Omaha Platform and of 1892–96 support Goodwyn's view?
H: Which planks of the Omaha Platform look forward (income tax, direct election of senators, regulation) and which look backward?
H: How did the sub-treasury plan arise from the cooperative movement, and why did the Populists lose in 1896?
A: Support: the Alliance's cooperatives and sub-treasury plan show a practical, collective response to credit; the platform's income tax, public ownership of utilities, direct election of senators and secret ballot were later adopted by Progressives and the New Deal; the movement tried to unite Black and white farmers; about a million votes in 1892. Challenge: some Populist rhetoric blamed bankers and foreign plots ("Wall Street conspiracy"), and some Populists, including Tom Watson later, were nativist or racist; the focus on silver, a simple cure, shows a narrow remedy; the platform stressed the "producing classes" more than workers' needs. A* judgement: Goodwyn is right that Populism was a serious democratic movement with a forward-looking programme, but Hofstadter's point about conspiracy language and the movement's limits (silver, race, a farm base) is also supported; the best view sees both elements.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Robber barons' and 'captains of industry' are both labels used for the businessmen of the Gilded Age. Evaluate which label is more useful, with reference to Josephson's *The Robber Barons* (1934).
H: Choose three men (e.g. Gould, Carnegie, Hill) and test each label against their records.
H: Ask what each label leaves out and when and why Josephson wrote.
A: Robber baron: Gould's gold corner (1869) and watered railroad stock; the Crédit Mobilier; Rockefeller's rebates; Frick and Carnegie at Homestead; Morgan's control of the Treasury in 1895. Captain of industry: Carnegie's costs and steel prices; Hill's Great Northern built without land grants; Rockefeller's efficiency and philanthropy; new industries and jobs. A* judgement: both labels are generalisations; the useful question is which methods created wealth (production) and which only transferred it (speculation and favours); Josephson wrote in the depression, when business was discredited, so his book reflects his time; a more accurate approach assesses each man separately, with Gould near the first label, Hill near the second and Carnegie and Rockefeller needing both.
