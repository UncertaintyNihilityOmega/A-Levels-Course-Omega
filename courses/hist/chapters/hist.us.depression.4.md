:::why
By the end of 1934 the New Deal had stopped the collapse but not ended the Depression, and critics on both sides were gaining ground. In the next three years Roosevelt passed the laws that most changed American life: the right to join a union, old-age pensions and unemployment insurance, a minimum wage. He then won the biggest election victory since 1820, overreached by attacking the Supreme Court, and watched a new slump undo some of his progress. To answer questions on this period you need to explain why he changed course, judge what the new laws achieved and left undone, and weigh the New Deal's record on recovery, on power and on fairness against the claims made for it.

## Roosevelt's political skills and the election of 1936

### The popularity of the New Deal and the Democratic landslide

The Democrats won big in the midterm elections of 6 November 1934, which is unusual for the party in power: they gained nine seats in the House and nine in the Senate. After 1934 they held about three-quarters of the seats in each house. Voters were rewarding relief, jobs and the end of the banking panic. The Republican candidate in 1936, Alf Landon, Governor of Kansas, accepted some of the New Deal but attacked its waste, its cost and Roosevelt's use of power. Landon had been one of the few Republican governors to survive the 1934 elections.

The result was the biggest landslide in a century:

| | Roosevelt (Democrat) | Landon (Republican) | Lemke (Union Party) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Popular vote | about 27.7 million (60.8%) | about 16.7 million (36.5%) | about 892,000 (2.0%) |
| Electoral votes | 523 | 8 | 0 |
| States won | 46 | 2 (Maine and Vermont) | 0 |

Roosevelt won every state except Maine and Vermont. The Postmaster General and Democratic chairman, James Farley, joked afterwards that the saying "As Maine goes, so goes the nation" should now be "As Maine goes, so goes Vermont." The Democrats also won about three-quarters of the seats in each house.

Why did the Republicans do so badly? The *Literary Digest*, a respected magazine, predicted a Landon victory with 57 per cent of the vote from a postal poll of ten million people, of whom 2.4 million replied. The poll was a famous failure: it drew its names from telephone and car-owner lists and its own subscribers, which over-represented richer people, and those who disliked Roosevelt were more likely to reply. The magazine collapsed within two years. The lesson for historians is the same as for pollsters: whose voices does a source record?

:::key Why Roosevelt won in 1936
1. **The recovery and the relief**: unemployment had fallen from 24.9 per cent in 1933 to 16.9 per cent in 1936 (Lebergott), and millions of families had work-relief wages, a mortgage saved by the HOLC, or farm payments.
2. **The Second New Deal**: Social Security, the Wagner Act and the WPA gave voters concrete benefits and tied particular groups to the Democrats.
3. **The coalition**: workers, Catholics, Jews, Black voters in northern cities, Southern whites, many farmers and reformers voted Democratic (see the section below).
4. **The weak opposition**: Landon was an honest but dull candidate, the Republicans were identified with the Depression, and the Union Party of Lemke, Townsend's followers and Coughlin's split the protest vote and got only 2 per cent.
5. **Roosevelt's skills**: his radio voice, his campaign speeches and his ability to turn the attacks of the rich into an advantage.
**Judgement:** the results of the New Deal and the strength of the coalition won the election; Roosevelt's skill and the weakness of his opponents made it a landslide.
:::

### Roosevelt's communication skills and use of patronage

Roosevelt used the new media better than any earlier president.

- **Radio.** The **fireside chats** (about thirty in all) brought policy into American homes in simple language. They bypassed the press, most of which was hostile, and built a bond with ordinary people. After the first chats he received huge mail from ordinary Americans, many of whom wrote to him personally as if to a friend, which gave him a sense of public opinion and gave voters a sense of access.
- **Press conferences.** He held them twice a week, in his study with reporters standing round the desk; over his time as president he held nearly a thousand. He joked, gave "off the record" background and chose the stories, so most reporters, even those on hostile papers, liked and respected him personally.
- **Campaign speeches.** He turned hostility into strength. At Philadelphia in June 1936 he told the Democratic convention that the country had "a rendezvous with destiny" and attacked the "economic royalists" who he said wanted to take back power. At Madison Square Garden on 31 October 1936 he said that the old enemies of peace were "business and financial monopoly, speculation, reckless banking, class antagonism, sectionalism, war profiteering", that "Government by organized money is just as dangerous as Government by organized mob" and, of his opponents, "They are unanimous in their hate for me, and I welcome their hatred."
- **His wife.** Eleanor Roosevelt travelled the country, wrote a newspaper column and acted as his eyes and ears among the poor, women, African Americans and the young.

:::def Patronage
The use of government jobs, contracts and money to reward supporters and build political loyalty. Under the New Deal the federal government had thousands of new posts and billions to spend, so the party in power could use patronage on an unprecedented scale.
:::

**Patronage** was the other tool. James Farley, as Postmaster General and chairman of the Democratic National Committee, controlled thousands of local appointments and used them to reward loyal state organisations. The new agencies employed millions on relief, and relief money followed political as well as economic needs: the economist Gavin Wright (1974) found that New Deal spending per head was higher in states that were politically close or pivotal in elections than in some poorer states, and in several states in 1938 Senate investigators found relief officials pressing WPA workers to support particular candidates. The scandal led Congress to pass the **Hatch Act** (1939), which forbade federal employees paid from relief funds to take part in election campaigns. Historians differ on how much the votes were "bought". The honest answer is that relief did tie millions to the Democrats, but that millions also voted for Roosevelt because they believed the New Deal had helped them, and because they trusted him.

### Maintaining the New Deal coalition

The New Deal coalition (see [Crash, Depression and New Deal (2)](#hist.us.depression.2)) took firm shape in 1934–36. Each group had a reason:

- **Organised labour.** The Wagner Act (1935) and the CIO's unions tied labour to Roosevelt. Labor's Non-Partisan League, formed by CIO leaders in 1936, backed his campaign, and John L. Lewis's United Mine Workers gave about $500,000.
- **Urban ethnic voters.** Relief, WPA jobs and recognition of Catholics, Jews and the children of immigrants (Roosevelt appointed an unusual number of Catholics and Jews to office) won them over.
- **African Americans.** In 1932 most Black voters still voted Republican, as the party of Lincoln. By 1936 most Black voters in the northern cities voted Democratic. They had benefited from relief and WPA jobs, though less than white Americans did, and they admired Eleanor Roosevelt, the "Black Cabinet" of advisers around Mary McLeod Bethune, and the president's openness. But Roosevelt would not support a federal anti-lynching law, because he needed the votes of Southern Democrats who controlled Congress's committees.
- **Southern whites.** The "Solid South" voted overwhelmingly for Roosevelt in 1936, because of farm payments, the TVA and the party label, and because he did not challenge segregation.
- **Women and reformers.** Perkins, Eleanor, and a host of women appointees, with social-welfare reformers like Hopkins.

The coalition was fragile because these groups wanted incompatible things. Black Americans and white Southerners; unions and employers; farmers who wanted high food prices and workers who wanted low ones. After his landslide Roosevelt made two mistakes that put the coalition under strain: the court plan of 1937, which divided the Democrats, and the "purge" of 1938, in which he tried to defeat conservative Democrats in the primaries and mostly failed. Even so, he held enough of it together to win a third term in 1940.

## The reasons for a Second New Deal

### Roosevelt's motives, 1935–37

Historians call the legislation of 1935–38 the **Second New Deal**. Roosevelt did not use the name, and some of its policies had been planned in 1934, but there was a change of emphasis from recovery by cooperation with business to reform through welfare, labour rights and a heavier attack on wealth.

:::def The Second New Deal
The burst of legislation of 1935–38, much of it passed in the summer of 1935 (sometimes called the "Second Hundred Days"), which concentrated on social security, labour rights, work relief on a larger scale and taxation of the rich, in contrast with the First New Deal's emergency measures for banks, industry and farming.

**Pressure from the Left.** Long, Coughlin and Townsend had mass followings in 1934–35. Roosevelt told advisers he wanted to take the wind out of Long's sails, and the Social Security Act, the "Wealth Tax" and the WPA all took up demands that the Left had made: pensions, taxing the rich, and jobs.

**The Supreme Court.** The *Schechter* decision removed the NRA, the heart of the First New Deal, and Roosevelt had to find another way to protect labour and regulate industry. The Wagner Act was ready to pass in the Senate when the Court acted; Roosevelt, who had been lukewarm about it, now supported it.

**The 1934 midterms.** The result gave him a bigger and more liberal Democratic Congress, with many northern urban members who wanted more action. It was also a message that the voters liked what he had done.

**The economy.** Recovery was slow. Unemployment was still 20.1 per cent in 1935 on Lebergott's figures, and about 10 million Americans were on relief of some kind. Work relief by the CWA and FERA was a patchwork that Hopkins wanted to replace with something steadier, and the old and the poor had no permanent protection.

**Business hostility.** By 1934–35 Roosevelt had decided that business leaders, led by the Liberty League, would never support him. If he could not win their cooperation, he could win their rivals' gratitude: workers, farmers, the old. In 1935 he became more critical of "economic royalists" and of the concentration of wealth, and listened more to Brandeis's followers such as Felix Frankfurter, Thomas Corcoran and Benjamin Cohen, who wanted to break up big concentrations of power.

**Roosevelt's own beliefs and plans.** He, Frances Perkins and Hopkins believed that Americans should be protected against the risks of old age, unemployment and sickness. Roosevelt had set up the Committee on Economic Security under Perkins in June 1934, before Long was a serious threat, and it had been drafting the Social Security Act for months. So the Left's pressure accelerated plans already under way; it did not create them.

**The 1936 election.** An election was coming, and Roosevelt needed to hold the votes of workers, the old and the poor, whom Long and Townsend were courting.

:::key Why Roosevelt introduced a Second New Deal in 1935
1. **Political pressure** from Long, Coughlin and Townsend, who threatened to split his supporters.
2. **The Supreme Court**: *Schechter* removed the NRA and its labour provisions.
3. **A mandate**: the 1934 midterms gave him a larger, more liberal Congress.
4. **Slow recovery**: unemployment of 20 per cent and a patchwork of relief.
5. **The break with business**: the Liberty League and business hostility freed him to attack concentrated wealth.
6. **His own convictions and planning**: the Committee on Economic Security of 1934; Perkins's and Hopkins's belief in social insurance.
7. **The 1936 election**: the need to bind labour, the old and the poor to the Democrats.
**Judgement:** the plans were Roosevelt's own and began in 1934; the Court's decision and pressure from the Left turned them into law in the summer of 1935.
:::

### The impact and importance of the Second New Deal

**Work relief: the WPA.** The Works Progress Administration, under Harry Hopkins, replaced the CWA and FERA. Between 1935 and 1943 it employed about 8.5 million people, at a peak of about 3.3 million in November 1938. They built roads, bridges, airports, schools and hospitals, and the WPA's arts projects (the Federal Writers', Art, Theatre and Music Projects) paid writers, painters, actors and musicians; the writers' project recorded the memories of many formerly enslaved people. The "security wage" was set above relief payments but below private wages, so that workers would move into private jobs when they could. Critics called the WPA "boondoggling" (wasteful make-work), and Republicans accused it of being a vote-buying machine. Hopkins replied that it kept skills and self-respect alive. Its limits: one worker per family, wages that were low, projects that were cut when budgets were cut (as in 1937), and a share of the unemployed who never got work.

**Labour: the Wagner Act.** The National Labor Relations Act of 5 July 1935 gave private-sector workers the legal right to form unions and bargain collectively, banned employers from firing union members or running company unions, and set up the National Labor Relations Board to hold elections and punish unfair practices. It was written by Senator Robert Wagner of New York. Roosevelt, who had doubted it, came out in support only when it was clear that it would pass and when *Schechter* removed Section 7(a). The Act changed American labour. John L. Lewis and others had already split from the cautious American Federation of Labor to form the **Committee for Industrial Organization** (CIO, 1935; renamed the Congress of Industrial Organizations in 1938) to organise whole industries, skilled or not. Strikes and union drives followed: the sit-down strike at General Motors in Flint, Michigan, from 30 December 1936 to 11 February 1937, ended with GM recognising the United Auto Workers; US Steel agreed to deal with the steelworkers' union in March 1937. But employers fought back, and when the "Little Steel" firms resisted in 1937 police shot ten strikers in the "Memorial Day Massacre" at Chicago (30 May 1937). The Supreme Court upheld the Act in *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel* on 12 April 1937 (5 to 4). Union membership grew from under three million in 1933 to about nine million by 1940 (estimates vary). The Act's limits: it excluded agricultural and domestic workers and did not ban discrimination by unions, and some unions kept Black workers out.

:::def The Wagner Act
The National Labor Relations Act of 5 July 1935, which guaranteed private-sector workers the right to organise and bargain collectively, banned unfair labour practices by employers, and set up the National Labor Relations Board.
:::

**Welfare: the Social Security Act.** The Act of 14 August 1935, "to provide for the general welfare", was the most lasting law of the whole New Deal. It had several parts:
- **Old-age insurance.** A federal scheme paid for by a payroll tax on workers and employers (1 per cent each on the first $3,000 of wages from 1937, rising later), giving monthly pensions from age 65. Taxes began in January 1937; the first monthly benefits were due in 1942 and were brought forward to 1940.
- **Unemployment insurance.** A federal tax on employers that was refunded if the state set up an approved scheme, so the states ran the benefits.
- **Assistance** (paid from general taxes with federal matching grants) for the poor elderly and the blind, and **Aid to Dependent Children** (mostly children whose fathers had died or left).
It was a cautious design. Roosevelt wanted it to be "insurance" paid for by contributions so that no later Congress could scrap it, and he is said to have told Perkins that Congress could not resist the Townsend Plan unless it passed a real old-age insurance system. National health insurance was dropped after lobbying by the American Medical Association. The payroll tax was regressive (it took a larger share of income from low earners than from high earners) and drew money out of the economy at the wrong moment (see the recession below). Benefits were low and state schemes unequal, with Southern states paying least. Above all, the Act left out **agricultural labourers and domestic servants**, and also teachers, nurses, hospital workers and the self-employed. Black workers were over-represented among those excluded: the NAACP's lawyer Charles Houston told Congress in February 1935 that the bill would exclude about 490,000 Black sharecroppers and cash tenants and most domestic servants. Ira Katznelson (*When Affirmative Action Was White*, 2005) argues that Southern Democrats, who chaired the key committees, insisted on these exclusions to keep control of Black labour. Larry DeWitt (a Social Security Administration historian) has argued in 2010 that the evidence points rather to the difficulty of collecting taxes from farms and households. Both agree on the effect: a large share of Black workers were outside the scheme until the law was extended in 1950 and 1954.

:::def The Social Security Act
The law of 14 August 1935 that created old-age insurance funded by payroll taxes, a federal-state scheme of unemployment insurance, and federal grants to the states for the poor elderly, the blind, and dependent children.
:::

**Taxation: the Wealth Tax.** The Revenue Act of 30 August 1935, nicknamed the "Wealth Tax" or "soak the rich" tax, raised taxes on large incomes, estates and gifts and introduced graduated corporation taxes, with a top rate on the very largest incomes of roughly three-quarters. It raised little (about $250 million a year) and hit very few people. It was a political gesture to the Left and against concentrated wealth more than a redistribution. The historian Barton Bernstein (1968) used such facts to argue that the New Deal did little to change the distribution of wealth.

**Finance and power.** The Banking Act of 1935 gave the Federal Reserve Board in Washington, under Marriner Eccles, more control over interest rates and bank reserves. The Public Utility Holding Company Act broke up the giant electricity holding companies, with a "death sentence" clause, after a bitter lobbying fight.

**Farms and housing.** The Rural Electrification Administration lent money to co-operatives to run power lines to farms; in 1934 fewer than 11 per cent of American farms had electricity. The Resettlement Administration (Tugwell) and from 1937 the Farm Security Administration helped poor tenants and migrants, and recorded the poverty of the Dust Bowl (explained below) in photographs. The US Housing Act (1937) began federal public housing.

**The Dust Bowl.** This was the name given to the southern Great Plains (parts of Oklahoma, Texas, Kansas, Colorado and New Mexico) in the 1930s. High wheat prices and tractors had led farmers in the First World War and the 1920s to plough up millions of acres of prairie grass, whose roots had held the soil together. When a long drought began in the early 1930s the crops failed, the bare soil dried out and the wind blew it away in "black blizzards"; the worst, on 14 April 1935 ("Black Sunday"), blotted out the sun across the Plains. About 2.5 million people left the worst-hit states during the 1930s. Many families, ruined and in debt, drove west to California to work as migrant labourers picking fruit and cotton, and were called "Okies" (though many came from other states). The government answered in two main ways: the Resettlement Administration moved some poor farmers to better land, and the Soil Conservation Service, set up in April 1935, taught farmers to plough along the contours of the land, to rotate crops and to plant grass and trees as windbreaks.

**Minimum standards: the Fair Labor Standards Act.** Signed on 25 June 1938, it set a national minimum wage of 25 cents an hour (to rise to 40 cents), a 44-hour week (to fall to 40), and banned most child labour. It covered about a fifth of the workforce at first, and left out farm workers, domestic workers and many others. The law passed only after a long fight with Southern Democrats, who feared a rise in Southern wages.

:::key The importance of the Second New Deal
1. **It created the permanent American welfare state in outline**: pensions, unemployment insurance, aid to children.
2. **It changed labour relations**: the Wagner Act and the CIO made unions a national force.
3. **It made the federal government responsible for jobs** (the WPA) and for minimum standards (the FLSA).
4. **It was less radical than it looked**: the Wealth Tax was small; the benefits left out the poorest workers; recovery was not complete.
5. **It tied groups to the Democrats**: the New Deal coalition of 1936.
**Judgement:** the Second New Deal did more than the First to change American society for the long term, but its protections reached the better-organised and the better-paid first.
:::

## The Supreme Court and the court plan of 1937

### Roosevelt's plan

The 1936 landslide encouraged Roosevelt to act against the Court. He had said nothing about it in the campaign. On **5 February 1937** he sent Congress a bill to "reorganize the judiciary". For every federal judge who did not retire within six months of reaching seventy, the president could appoint an additional judge, up to six extra Supreme Court justices (a Court of fifteen) and fifty extra lower-court judges. Six of the nine justices were over seventy.

The way Roosevelt presented it was part of the problem. His message to Congress did not say that he wanted a Court that would uphold the New Deal. Instead it spoke of the overload on elderly judges and the need for "younger blood" and efficiency. Everyone knew the real aim, and critics said he was dishonest. On 9 March, in a fireside chat, he dropped the pretence and attacked the Court directly. The Court had been acting, he said, "not as a judicial body but as a policy-making body", and "we have, therefore, reached the point as a nation where we must take action to save the Constitution from the Court and the Court from itself." To the charge of "packing the Court" he answered that if it meant appointing justices who "will act as justices and not as legislators", then "I and with me the vast majority of the American people favor doing just that thing, now".

:::def Court packing
The name given by critics to Roosevelt's plan of February 1937 to add up to six justices to the Supreme Court, which they said would make the Court obey the president. Roosevelt said the aim was to bring younger judges onto an over-aged bench.
:::

### Why the plan failed

- **The principle.** Many Americans, including Democrats who supported the New Deal, thought the independence of the judiciary mattered more than any single law. Opponents argued that a president who could add judges to get the rulings he wanted would set a precedent for any later president. Gallup polls between February and May 1937 showed public opinion opposed to the bill.
- **Roosevelt's handling.** He had not consulted Congressional leaders in advance, he had used the wrong argument at the start, and his party was split. Vice-President John Nance Garner and the chairman of the Senate Judiciary Committee, Henry Ashurst, did not support it, and Senator Burton Wheeler of Montana, a Democrat who supported the New Deal, led the opposition.
- **Chief Justice Hughes.** On 22 March 1937 Wheeler read to the Judiciary Committee a letter from Hughes (dated 21 March), approved by Justices Van Devanter and Brandeis, which said that the Court was abreast of its work and that more justices would not make it more efficient but less. It destroyed the claim that the Court was overburdened.
- **The Court changed course.** On 29 March, in *West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish*, the Court upheld Washington State's minimum-wage law for women by five votes to four, reversing its decision in *Morehead* the year before. Justice Roberts, who had voted against the New York law, now joined Hughes and the three liberals. On 12 April it upheld the Wagner Act, and on 24 May the Social Security Act. A popular quip called this "the switch in time that saved nine". Later research, notably Barry Cushman's *Rethinking the New Deal Court* (1998), has shown that the Court voted in *Parrish* in December 1936, before the plan was announced, so the story that Roberts changed his vote because of the plan is doubtful.
- **Retirement.** On 18 May Justice Van Devanter, one of the Four Horsemen, announced he would retire. Roosevelt had now a vacancy, and a justification for the plan weakened.
- **Robinson's death.** The Senate Majority Leader, Joseph Robinson of Arkansas, had been promised the first vacancy and was leading the fight for a compromise. He died of a heart attack on 14 July 1937. On 22 July the Senate voted 70 to 20 to send the bill back to committee, which killed it. The Senate Judiciary Committee had already issued its report on 14 June, with seven Democrats and three Republicans signing, calling the plan "a needless, futile, and utterly dangerous abandonment of constitutional principle" and "a proposal without precedent and without justification". Congress did pass a smaller bill reforming the lower courts.

### The new appointments and the results

Roosevelt lost the battle but, in a sense, won the war. Hugo Black, a New Deal Senator from Alabama, was nominated in August 1937 to take Van Devanter's seat. Over the next three years, as the older justices retired or died, Roosevelt named five more:

| Justice | Replaced | Year |
|---|---|---|
| Hugo Black | Van Devanter | 1937 |
| Stanley Reed | Sutherland | 1938 |
| Felix Frankfurter | Cardozo | 1939 |
| William O. Douglas | Brandeis | 1939 |
| Frank Murphy | Butler | 1940 |

By 1941 the Court was largely his. It upheld the Fair Labor Standards Act (*United States v. Darby*, 1941) and gave the commerce clause a broad meaning that has lasted. The constitutional barriers to the New Deal had gone. The costs of the plan, however, were serious.

:::key The court plan: how it should be judged
1. **Aim**: to remove the Court's veto over the New Deal. It was a response to real obstruction (*Schechter*, *Butler*, *Carter*, *Morehead*).
2. **Method**: a mistake. It was presented dishonestly, made enemies in his own party and looked like an attack on the Constitution.
3. **Outcome for the law**: the Court accepted the New Deal in 1937 and Roosevelt's appointments entrenched that.
4. **Outcome for politics**: the plan split the Democrats, wasted the capital of the 1936 landslide, gave the Southern Democrats and Republicans a common cause (the **conservative coalition**) and made Congress reluctant to pass more reform.
**Judgement:** a political defeat that coincided with a constitutional victory.
:::

:::def The conservative coalition
The informal alliance of Republicans and conservative (mostly Southern) Democrats in Congress from about 1937–38, which could outvote the liberal Democrats and block new New Deal measures on labour, welfare and the executive's power. It was a long-term effect of the court plan and the recession.
:::

## The recession of 1937–38

### The causes

Production, profits and wages had regained their 1929 levels by the spring of 1937, though unemployment was still 14.3 per cent (Lebergott). Then the economy turned down sharply. The NBER dates the recession from May 1937 to June 1938, and the fall in output was very fast. The causes are debated, but most historians point to a mixture of policy mistakes.

- **Fiscal tightening.** Roosevelt and his Treasury Secretary, Henry Morgenthau, wanted to balance the budget now that the economy seemed to be recovering. Spending on the WPA and PWA was cut in 1937, and the veterans' bonus, paid in June 1936, had been a one-off stimulus that did not recur. At the same time taxes rose: the Social Security payroll tax started in January 1937, taking money from workers' pay packets, while no benefits were being paid out.
- **Monetary tightening.** The Federal Reserve doubled the banks' reserve requirements (the share of deposits that banks must hold in reserve instead of lending) in three steps between August 1936 and May 1937 to soak up what it thought were dangerous excess reserves (reserves above the legal minimum, which banks held as a safety margin instead of lending), and the Treasury "sterilised" gold inflows from December 1936 (it kept the imported gold from adding to the money supply by paying for it with borrowed money and not with newly created money). Together these cut the supply of credit. (Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz, *A Monetary History of the United States*, 1963, stressed these monetary factors.)
- **Business behaviour.** Firms had built up large stocks in 1936–37 in expectation of price rises and strikes, and then cut orders and laid workers off when demand weakened. Roosevelt himself blamed a "capital strike" by business and monopoly pricing. The new unions and the Wagner Act, which businessmen blamed for higher costs, were part of the story they told.
- **Weak foundations.** The recovery had been uneven, with large unemployment and weak investment.

### The impact

Industrial production fell by about 32 per cent between 1937 and 1938, and real GDP (the nation's total output adjusted for price changes, the "national output" of earlier chapters) by about 10 per cent. Stock prices roughly halved. Unemployment, which Lebergott puts at 14.3 per cent in 1937, rose to 19.0 per cent in 1938, a rise of 4.7 percentage points that put millions of workers out of a job within months. Business profits fell, and the weakest groups, such as Black and unskilled workers, were the first to be laid off. The WPA rolls had to be raised again. For many families the recession felt like a return to 1932; the Republicans called it the "Roosevelt recession", which hurt, since Roosevelt had claimed credit for recovery.

### The recovery from the recession

The recession ended in 1938 for three reasons. Roosevelt reversed course: in April 1938 he asked Congress for a new programme of spending and lending (the sums quoted for the spring of 1938 range from about $3.75 billion to $5 billion, depending on what is counted), which raised WPA employment and public works. The Federal Reserve cut reserve requirements and the Treasury released the gold it had sterilised, so credit expanded again. And gold flowed into the United States from Europe as war threatened, which also lifted the money supply. Output grew by around half between 1938 and 1942 (see the Federal Reserve's account of the episode), helped by a defence build-up.

### Political consequences

The recession, coming on top of the court plan, was a political disaster. In the November 1938 midterms the Democrats lost about 70 seats in the House and 8 in the Senate, and the conservative coalition took control of Congress's agenda. Roosevelt's attempt to "purge" conservative Democrats in the primaries failed: in Georgia, Senator Walter George, whom he had attacked in person, won easily. In December 1937 a group of conservative senators had circulated what became known as the "Conservative Manifesto", calling for lower spending and less regulation of business. Little major reform legislation passed after the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938, and Congress cut the funds for the WPA and refused Roosevelt's plan to reorganise the executive branch in 1938.

The recession also changed economic thinking. Roosevelt's advisers, including Eccles and Hopkins, concluded that the cuts in spending had caused the slump, and moved towards a Keynesian policy of using government spending to sustain demand (after the economist John Maynard Keynes, who had argued that governments should spend more in a slump). The historian Alan Brinkley (*The End of Reform*, 1995) argued that after 1937–38 New Deal liberalism turned away from attacking monopoly and reforming business structures and towards managing demand through spending and taxation.

:::key Did the New Deal help African Americans? Both sides
- **Gains**: relief and WPA jobs, which went to Black Americans in larger proportion than their share of the population because they were poorer; Black officials in the "Black Cabinet" (Mary McLeod Bethune ran the NYA's Division of Negro Affairs); Eleanor Roosevelt's public support (in 1939 she resigned from the Daughters of the American Revolution after it barred the singer Marian Anderson from its hall); Black voters had a place in the Democratic coalition.
- **Exclusions and losses**: the farm programmes displaced tenants; domestic and agricultural workers were left out of Social Security and the Wagner Act; the CCC was segregated; the NRA and the AAA treated Black workers worse; Roosevelt would not back a federal anti-lynching law because Southern senators filibustered it in 1938 (talked it to death with endless speeches); federal housing agencies (the HOLC's maps and the FHA's rules) encouraged segregation in housing.
**Judgement:** the New Deal gave many Black Americans a federal government they had never had on their side, and relief that kept them alive, but it did not challenge segregation and its biggest programmes passed over the Black poor of the South.

### Changes to the role of the American government

- **The federal government became responsible for economic security.** Social Security, work relief and the minimum wage meant that Washington, not local charity, was expected to help the old, the unemployed and the poor.
- **It regulated the economy.** The SEC, FDIC, NLRB, the Federal Reserve Board, the utility law and the FLSA made the federal government the regulator of finance, labour and utilities.
- **It grew.** Federal spending was about 3 per cent of national output in 1929 and nearly 10 per cent in 1940 (roughly), and the number of federal employees rose sharply.
- **The presidency grew.** Roosevelt used executive orders, direct appeals to the public and a White House staff. The Executive Reorganization Act of 1939 gave him an Executive Office of the President.
- **The Constitution was reinterpreted.** After 1937 the Court accepted a wide federal power over the economy.
- **A new idea of the economy.** After 1937–38 the idea that government spending could sustain demand entered American policy.

Historians disagree about whether this was a revolution. Barton Bernstein (1968) saw the New Deal as conservative, because it saved capitalism and left wealth where it was. Others, including Leuchtenburg, saw a lasting change in what Americans expected of government. David Kennedy (*Freedom from Fear*, 1999) stresses how much Americans' sense of security and of government's role changed even though many problems remained.

*Source: as the chart above.*

In 1940, after seven years of the New Deal, unemployment was still 14.6 per cent on Lebergott's figures, or 9.5 per cent on Darby's. It fell below 10 per cent only in 1941, as defence orders and conscription (the first peacetime draft, September 1940) took up the slack; by 1943 it was below 2 per cent. Federal spending rose from about $9 billion in 1939 to about $14 billion in 1941 and over $30 billion in 1942.

So the evidence supports three judgements. First, the New Deal brought **partial recovery**: output, profits and wages had returned to 1929 levels by 1937 and unemployment had fallen by about ten percentage points. Second, it did not bring **full recovery**: a recession in 1937–38 and unemployment of 14.6 per cent in 1940 show how incomplete the recovery was. Third, **full employment came with rearmament and war**, which raised government spending far more than the New Deal ever had. The usual summary is that the New Deal ended the collapse, relieved suffering and reformed institutions, but it was the war economy that ended mass unemployment. Economists disagree about why: the Keynesian view says that spending was too small and too cautious; Christina Romer (1992) argues that gold inflows and monetary expansion explain most of the recovery; others stress the effects of the end of the banking panic, and the weakness of investment in the face of business distrust.

## Weighing the evidence: the main questions

**Why was there a Second New Deal?** The strongest answers separate the *causes of the change of direction* (the Court, the Left, the mandate, business hostility) from Roosevelt's own *long-standing beliefs and plans* (the Committee on Economic Security of 1934). Neither alone explains the timing.

**Was the Second New Deal more radical than the First?** In what it promised, yes: it created new rights (to organise, to a pension, to a minimum wage). In what it did, it was careful: the Wealth Tax was small, Social Security excluded many, and the First New Deal's gold and banking decisions had been just as large a break with the past.

**How successful was the New Deal by 1941?** Use a criterion. *Recovery*: partial, completed by the war. *Relief*: large, but unequal. *Reform*: lasting, and the most important achievement. *Fairness*: limited, because of the exclusions and the politics of the South. *Politics*: it made the Democrats the majority party until the 1960s.

The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (return to full employment) and weighs the New Deal's contribution against other causes. With an opening paragraph on the case for recovery, this is Level 5 (18–20). Note that "recovery from the collapse" and "full recovery" are different claims: separating them is a mark of a strong answer.
:::

It speaks of liberty and prohibits the deprivation of liberty without due process of law. … Liberty implies the absence of arbitrary restraint, not immunity from reasonable regulations and prohibitions imposed in the interests of the community.

*Adapted from the opinion of Chief Justice Hughes for the Supreme Court in West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish, 29 March 1937, upholding Washington State's minimum-wage law for women. The last sentence is Hughes's quotation of an earlier judgment (Chicago, Burlington and Quincy Railroad Co. v. McGuire, 1911). Shortened.*

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that Roosevelt's plan to reform the Supreme Court in 1937 was a mistake?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support (it was a mistake): B (the Chief Justice says more justices would make the Court less efficient, and answers the claim of overwork); C (the Senate committee, with Democrats among the signatories, calls it dangerous, without precedent and disguised). Challenge (it was not a mistake): A (Roosevelt's case that the Court had been acting as a policy-making body); D (within eight weeks the Court itself accepted that liberty of contract was not absolute, and upheld a minimum wage, so the pressure seemed to work). Qualify: A (a constitutional argument, not a defence of the method); D (it may show that the plan was unnecessary, because the Court was already changing).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a public speech by the author of the plan, made when he was losing the argument in Congress, to appeal over the heads of the legislators. It shows his case at its strongest, including the claim that the Court had blocked laws passed by the people's representatives (*Schechter*, *Butler*, *Carter Coal*, *Morehead*). But it is propaganda, and it avoids the questions about method that his critics raised.
- **B**: a letter from the head of the Court, and so highly authoritative on the Court's workload, and it contradicted the facts presented by Roosevelt's administration. But Hughes was not neutral: he had a strong interest in defending the Court's independence, the letter was approved by two other justices (Van Devanter, a conservative, and Brandeis), and it spoke only of efficiency and said nothing on whether the Court was blocking the New Deal. Still, its effect was great because of who wrote it.
- **C**: an official report, signed by seven Democrats and three Republicans, which shows that opposition came from within Roosevelt's party. But it was written by those who opposed the plan, in strong language ("obscured its real purpose" accuses Roosevelt of disguising his aim), and is evidence of the strength of the opposition, not necessarily of the plan's merits.
- **D**: the opinion of the Court itself, written by the same Chief Justice, delivered seven weeks after the plan was announced, and it reverses the reasoning of *Morehead* (1936). It is strong evidence that the Court's attitude to regulation changed in spring 1937. But it does not say why. Later research shows that the Court had voted on *Parrish* in December 1936, before the plan, so D cannot show that the plan caused the change; on the other hand it does show that the main aim of the plan, a Court that would accept the New Deal, was met without it being passed.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources B and C most strongly support the view that the plan was a mistake. Hughes's letter (B) answered Roosevelt's argument that the Court was overburdened with a direct statement from the man who ran it, and the committee report (C), signed by seven Democrats, shows that the plan lost the support of Roosevelt's own party; its language, 'without precedent and without justification', is the language of principle, not just of party, and its charge that the plan's 'real purpose' was obscured goes to the heart of Roosevelt's handling. My own knowledge supports this: the Gallup polls showed opposition, Vice-President Garner and Senator Wheeler opposed the bill, and the plan split the Democrats and helped to create the conservative coalition that blocked reform after 1937. Source A, however, shows what the plan was for, and Roosevelt's complaint was well founded: the Court had struck down the NRA, the AAA, the Guffey Coal Act and a minimum-wage law in 1935–36. Source D shows that by 29 March 1937 the Court itself had changed, and the Wagner Act and Social Security were upheld within weeks, followed by Roosevelt's five appointments by 1940, so he got the Court he wanted. Both D and the later outcome qualify the view that the plan was a mistake, because the Court of the 1940s accepted the New Deal, although D, since the vote on *Parrish* came before the plan, does not prove that the plan was the cause. On balance the sources support the view to a large extent: the plan was a mistake of method and of politics, which cost him his party's unity and the momentum of the 1936 landslide. But the aim was reasonable and the result for the constitution favoured Roosevelt; so it is more accurate to say that the plan was a political error that coincided with a constitutional victory than that it was simply a mistake."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1937; the answer explains where sources support, challenge and qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far did the New Deal benefit African Americans?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The New Deal helped African Americans in many ways. Eleanor Roosevelt supported civil rights and Mary McLeod Bethune was in the Black Cabinet. African Americans got jobs from the WPA and the CCC. Many Black voters moved from the Republicans to the Democrats in the 1930s and voted for Roosevelt in 1936. On the other hand, there was segregation in the CCC camps and Roosevelt did not support an anti-lynching law. In conclusion, the New Deal benefited African Americans a lot."

**The level.** It has accurate points on both sides, but it only lists them, the "on the other hand" sentence does not weigh them, and the conclusion contradicts the evidence it has just given without explaining why. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Choose a criterion, weigh the sides, and give a judgement that follows. "The New Deal benefited African Americans most through relief: Black Americans, hit hardest by unemployment, received a larger share of WPA and relief jobs than their share of the population, and the Black Cabinet gave them a voice in Washington. However, the benefits were limited by the way the programmes were run. Domestic and agricultural workers, most of them Black in the South, were excluded from Social Security and the Wagner Act; the AAA's payments went to landlords and led to the eviction of tenants; the CCC was segregated; and Roosevelt refused to support an anti-lynching law because he needed Southern votes. Overall, the New Deal helped African Americans survive and gave them a political voice, but it did not challenge segregation or help the poorest in the South, so the benefit was real but limited." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

Q: Give the 1936 election result and three reasons for it.
A: Roosevelt won 523 electoral votes to 8 and about 60.8 per cent of the popular vote (Landon 36.5, Lemke 2.0). Reasons: recovery and relief (unemployment 24.9 to 16.9 per cent), the Second New Deal and the coalition, the weak opposition (and the Union Party's split), and Roosevelt's skills.
Q: Name three of Roosevelt's communication skills or political tools.
A: Fireside chats; twice-weekly press conferences; campaign speeches ("economic royalists", "I welcome their hatred"); patronage through Farley and the relief agencies; Eleanor Roosevelt's travels.
Q: Explain why the Wagner Act was passed in 1935.
A: Schechter removed Section 7(a) of the NIRA; unions wanted a firm legal right to organise; Senator Wagner and the Democratic Congress wanted it; Roosevelt, once doubtful, supported it when it was clear it would pass; it also tied labour to the Democrats.
Q: What were the main parts of the Social Security Act, and who was left out?
A: Old-age insurance (payroll tax), unemployment insurance (federal-state), aid to dependent children, the blind and the poor elderly. Left out: agricultural and domestic workers (many of them Black), the self-employed and some public employees.
Q: Give three reasons why the court plan of 1937 failed.
A: It looked like an attack on judicial independence and it was presented dishonestly (overwork rather than the real aim); the Democrats split (Garner, Ashurst, Wheeler); Hughes's letter; the Court's switch (*Parrish*, *Jones & Laughlin*); Van Devanter's retirement and Robinson's death.
Q: Give three causes of the recession of 1937–38 and say how it ended.
A: Cuts in federal spending and the start of Social Security payroll taxes; the Fed's doubling of reserve requirements and the Treasury's gold sterilisation; business stock-building and mistrust. It ended with new spending (April 1938), the Fed cutting reserve requirements and desterilised gold, and gold inflows from Europe.
Q: Give two groups who benefited from the New Deal and two who did not, with a reason each.
A: Benefited: organised labour (Wagner Act), the elderly (Social Security). Did not: sharecroppers and tenants (AAA evictions, no coverage), domestic servants (excluded from the main laws).
Q: What were unemployment rates in 1937, 1938 and 1940 (Lebergott), and what do they show?
A: 14.3, 19.0 and 14.6 per cent. Recovery was partial, set back by the recession, and still incomplete in 1940; full employment came with rearmament and war.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the National Labor Relations (Wagner) Act was passed in 1935.

Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the economy went into recession in 1937.
A: Indicative content: the federal budget was cut and taxes raised (the Social Security payroll tax from January 1937; cuts in WPA and PWA spending); the Fed doubled reserve requirements between August 1936 and May 1937 and the Treasury sterilised gold, reducing credit; firms built up stocks and then cut orders; recovery was still fragile (14.3 per cent unemployment in 1937); business mistrust of the New Deal. Level 4 connects the policy mistakes with the weakness of the recovery, and judges which mattered most (the combined effect of fiscal and monetary tightening on a recovery that depended on government spending).
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Second New Deal was more radical than the First.' How far do you agree?

Against: the First New Deal's break with the gold standard, the bank holiday and the NRA/AAA were big breaks in method; the Second was cautious (the Wealth Tax raised little; Social Security excluded many and the payroll tax was regressive); neither challenged the structure of capitalism (Bernstein); the Second New Deal followed pressure rather than a new philosophy. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "change to the role of government or to the distribution of power", and judges, e.g., that the Second New Deal was more radical in creating permanent rights and obligations, but less so in its economic effect on wealth.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Ira Katznelson argues that the New Deal's laws were shaped by Southern Democrats so as to exclude most Black workers from their benefits. How far does the evidence support this view?

A: Support: Social Security and the Wagner Act excluded agricultural and domestic workers (the occupations of most Southern Black workers); Southern Democrats chaired key committees and could block bills; the FLSA was passed only after concessions on wages; Roosevelt would not press the anti-lynching bill; state control of relief and assistance let the South pay less. :::recap
- **1936 election**: Roosevelt 523 electoral votes to 8, about 60.8 per cent of the popular vote; the coalition of labour, ethnic city voters, Black voters in the North, Southern whites, farmers and reformers; skills: radio, press conferences, speeches; patronage (Farley, relief; Hatch Act 1939).
- **Why a Second New Deal (1935)**: pressure from Long, Coughlin and Townsend; *Schechter*; the 1934 midterms; slow recovery (20.1 per cent); business hostility (Liberty League); Roosevelt's own plans (Committee on Economic Security, June 1934); the 1936 election.
- **Laws**: WPA (May 1935; about 8.5 million employed); Wagner Act (5 July 1935); Social Security Act (14 August 1935; agricultural and domestic workers excluded); Wealth Tax (August 1935); Banking Act of 1935; REA; US Housing Act (1937); Fair Labor Standards Act (25 June 1938).
- **Court plan**: 5 February 1937; up to six extra justices; failed (Hughes's letter, divided Democrats, *Parrish* on 29 March, *Jones & Laughlin* on 12 April, Van Devanter's retirement, Robinson's death on 14 July, recommitted 70 to 20 on 22 July); five appointments by 1940; the conservative coalition.
- **Recession 1937–38**: causes were spending cuts, payroll tax, Fed and Treasury tightening, business stock-building; unemployment 14.3 to 19.0 per cent; industrial production down about 32 per cent; ended with new spending and monetary easing; Democrats lost about 70 House seats in 1938.
- **Who gained**: organised labour, the elderly, relief workers, landowning farmers, home-owners, the young. **Who did not**: sharecroppers and tenants, domestic and farm workers, Black Southerners, women, Mexican Americans, migrants, the long-term unemployed.
- **Role of government**: welfare, regulation, bigger federal spending and presidency; the Court accepted wider federal power; Keynesian ideas after 1938.
- **Recovery**: partial by 1937; unemployment 14.6 per cent in 1940 (Lebergott); 9.9 per cent in 1941; full employment came with rearmament and war.
- **Judgement**: the New Deal ended the collapse, relieved suffering and reformed institutions, but did not achieve full recovery by 1941; its benefits were unequal.
