## The Republican years, 1921–29

### Normalcy: the Harding administration

The First World War had been fought by a government that took control of railroads, set prices and taxed incomes at up to 77 per cent. When the war ended Americans were tired of it all. The 1920 election was won by a Republican senator from Ohio, **Warren G. Harding**, who promised "normalcy": a return to the quiet, business-friendly government of before 1914. He won about 60 per cent of the popular vote, the biggest share to that date.

:::def Normalcy
Harding's word for a return to the peacetime politics of before 1914: low taxes, a small federal government, high tariffs to protect American industry, and a government that helped business rather than regulated it.
:::

:::def Laissez-faire
The belief that government should interfere as little as possible in the economy and leave businesses and markets free. Most Republican leaders of the 1920s held it, but they did not apply it to tariffs, which they raised.
:::

Harding appointed some able men (Charles Evans Hughes as Secretary of State, Herbert Hoover as Secretary of Commerce) and some corrupt friends. The **Teapot Dome** scandal, in which his Secretary of the Interior Albert Fall took bribes in return for oil leases on government land, came out only after Harding died on 2 August 1923. It did not damage the party's standing for long, because the economy was growing. Harding was succeeded by Vice President **Calvin Coolidge**, the former governor of Massachusetts, who was elected in his own right in 1924. Coolidge was a man of few words, nicknamed "Silent Cal", who believed that the less a president did the better. Critics called his policy "active inactivity".

### Economic policy under Harding and Coolidge

The key figure in economic policy was the Secretary of the Treasury, **Andrew Mellon**, a banker and one of the richest men in the country, who served from 1921 to 1932. His policies had four parts.

1. **Tax cuts.** Mellon argued that high wartime taxes on large incomes discouraged investment, and that lower rates would bring more money into the economy. Congress passed tax cuts in 1921, 1924 and 1926 (the last for the incomes of 1925). The top rate of income tax fell from 73 per cent to 58 per cent, then to 46 per cent, and by 1925 to 25 per cent. The cuts also helped the lower paid, many of whom stopped paying income tax altogether, but the benefit in money terms went mainly to the rich.
2. **Spending cuts and a balanced budget.** The Budget and Accounting Act of 1921 created the Bureau of the Budget, so the president presented one annual budget to Congress. Charles Dawes was its first director. Federal spending fell and the government ran a surplus every year of the decade. By the end of 1928, as Coolidge said in his last message to Congress, about a third of the national debt left by the war had been paid.
3. **High tariffs.** The **Fordney–McCumber Tariff** of 21 September 1922 raised import duties on many goods to protect American farmers and manufacturers from European competition. Many duties were between 60 and 100 per cent of the price of the import.
4. **Little regulation.** Harding and Coolidge appointed people friendly to business to bodies such as the Federal Trade Commission, so that the trust-busting of the Progressive Era ([Gilded Age and Progressive Era (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3)) faded away. The federal government did not regulate the stock market at all.

Immigration was restricted too. Quota acts of 1921 and 1924 limited arrivals to a small percentage of each nationality already in the United States in 1910 and then 1890, ending the mass immigration described in [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (1)](#hist.us.gilded.1). This cut off a supply of cheap labour.

:::key What the Republicans' policies meant for the economy
1. **Business confidence**: low taxes and a friendly government encouraged investment and fed the boom.
2. **Inequality**: tax cuts and weak unions left wealth to flow to the top.
3. **Trade**: high tariffs shut out imports, which made it harder for other countries to repay their debts to the United States (see "The impact of the First World War" below).
4. **Regulation**: none of the 1920s governments tried to supervise banks' securities business, the stock exchange or the Federal Reserve's handling of speculation.
Each of these helped cause the Crash or the Depression, which is why Paper 2 asks "to what extent were government policies responsible?"
:::

:::warn Do not call the 1920s governments "do-nothing" governments
They did a great deal: they cut taxes, raised tariffs, restricted immigration, balanced the budget and (under Hoover as Commerce Secretary) encouraged trade associations and standard sizes for goods. The accurate claim is that they chose **not to regulate or spend** to manage the economy, and that this left the system with no shock absorbers when it was hit.
:::

## The post-war economic boom

### Mass production and the car

After a sharp slump in 1920–21, the economy grew fast. Real national output rose by about 4 per cent a year between 1920 and 1929, and output per worker in manufacturing grew much faster than before the war. Unemployment was low: the rate for 1929 is put at about 3 per cent in the series used in *Historical Statistics of the United States* and about 4.6 per cent in another, but in either case it was low.

The symbol of the boom was the car. **Henry Ford** had built his Model T on a moving assembly line (introduced in 1913), which cut the time needed to build a chassis from about twelve and a half hours to about ninety-three minutes by early 1914. The cheapest Model T cost $825 in 1908 and only $260 by 1925. By 1925 Ford's factories turned out a car about every ten seconds. The number of registered motor vehicles rose from about 9 million in 1920 to nearly 27 million in 1929.

:::def Mass production
Making large numbers of identical goods cheaply, using standard parts, specialised machines and a moving assembly line, so that each worker repeats one task.
:::

One industry drove many others. Cars used steel, glass, rubber, leather and oil. They needed petrol stations, garages and roads. They allowed people to live in suburbs, and they created hotels and roadside diners. Millions of jobs depended on the car. This is called a **multiplier** effect: one industry's spending creates work in many others, and it worked in reverse in 1930.

### Electricity, radio and consumer goods

In 1920 about a third of American homes had electricity; by 1930 about two thirds did. This created a market for **consumer durables**: vacuum cleaners, irons, refrigerators, washing machines and radios. Radio began commercial broadcasting in 1920 (KDKA, Pittsburgh), and by 1930 about two in five households had a set. Spending on household appliances rose by more than 120 per cent between 1919 and 1929. In industry electricity replaced steam and belts, and by 1929 about 70 per cent of manufacturing relied on electric power, against roughly 30 per cent in 1914.

### Credit and advertising

How could ordinary people afford all this? Partly through **hire purchase** (also called instalment buying): a family paid a deposit and the rest in monthly payments, taking the goods home at once. By 1927 more than 60 per cent of cars were bought on credit. Advertising, now on the radio as well as in magazines, taught people to want things they had never known they needed. The advertising man Bruce Barton, in *The Man Nobody Knows* (1925), even presented Jesus as a model businessman. The boom was also a **construction** boom: new homes, offices and, in 1925–26, a speculative boom in Florida land that ended in a crash of its own.

:::def Consumer durables
Goods bought by households that last for years, such as cars, refrigerators and radios. Because buyers can delay buying them, spending on them falls quickly when confidence falls.
:::

:::def Hire purchase (instalment buying)
Buying goods by paying a deposit and then regular payments over months or years. It raised spending in the 1920s, but it meant families had debts that they could not repay when they lost their jobs.
:::

:::key Why the boom happened
1. **New technology and mass production** cut costs and prices (Ford, electricity).
2. **New industries** (cars, electrical goods, radio, chemicals) created jobs and demand for steel, oil and rubber.
3. **Credit** let families buy goods before they could pay for them.
4. **Business-friendly government**: low taxes, no regulation, restricted foreign competition.
5. **Confidence**: after 1921 almost everyone believed that prosperity would continue.
:::

But the boom was not as solid as it looked. Look at who shared in it, and who did not.

## Unequal distribution of wealth

Wages did rise, but slowly. A study of twenty-five manufacturing industries finds that real weekly earnings between 1923 and 1929 rose by about 5 per cent for skilled and semi-skilled men and about 9 per cent for unskilled men, much less than output per worker. Trade unions were weak: employers broke strikes, offered company unions and "welfare capitalism", and the courts were hostile. Immigration quotas and cheap labour from the South kept wages down.

At the top, incomes soared. The economists Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, using tax records, estimate that the richest 1 per cent of Americans took about 19.6 per cent of all income in 1928 if capital gains are left out, and about 23.9 per cent if they are counted: the highest share in their series until after 2000. Estimates differ with the method used, but all show that the top took roughly a fifth to a quarter of the national income. Mellon's tax cuts helped the very rich keep more, and rich households saved and invested a large share of it, often in shares.

:::key Why unequal wealth mattered
1. **Underconsumption**: mass production needed mass buying, but most families could not buy enough to keep up with what factories could make. Hire purchase postponed the problem; it did not solve it.
2. **Speculation**: the rich had money to invest, and much of it went into shares, pushing prices up faster than profits.
3. **Weak safety net**: the poorest had no savings, so a fall in jobs meant a fall in spending at once.
:::

:::note The 1920s as the "consumer society": how far did it reach?
Buying a car or radio was possible for a middle-class family in a city. For a Black sharecropper in Mississippi, a miner in West Virginia or a farmer in Kansas it was not. When historians say "the 1920s boom", ask: for whom? In 1920 the census recorded that more Americans lived in towns than in the countryside for the first time (about 51 per cent urban), but a large rural population, and many poor industrial workers, were left outside the boom.
:::

## The urban–rural divide

### The farm depression

The countryside did not share in the boom. During the First World War European demand and high prices had encouraged American farmers to plough more land and to borrow to buy land and machinery. When the war ended, European farms recovered and prices collapsed. Between 1920 and 1921 the wholesale prices of farm products fell by more than half (about 53 per cent), and farm incomes fell with them. Farmers' debts were fixed in dollars, so as prices fell their debts became harder to pay. Foreclosures stayed at high levels all through the decade, and cultivated acreage fell for the first time in American history.

Several things made things worse. Tractors let farmers grow more with fewer workers and freed land that had grown feed for horses. Overseas markets were hurt by the high tariffs, because countries that could not sell goods to the United States could not buy American wheat and cotton. Cotton farmers also faced competition and the boll weevil in the South.

The government did little. The **McNary–Haugen Bills** proposed that the government buy surplus crops at a fair price and sell them abroad. Congress passed versions in 1927 and 1928, but Coolidge vetoed both, saying the plan was not sound public policy and was unconstitutional. Hoover, then Commerce Secretary, advised against it. The farm states reacted with anger, and rural banks, which depended on farmers repaying loans, began to fail. Between 1921 and 1929 more than 5,700 banks in the United States suspended business, most of them small banks in farming states, and the number of banks fell from about 28,900 in 1920 to about 23,600 by the start of 1930 (see the chart below).

### Town against country

The divide was cultural as well as economic. The cities were growing, more tolerant and more modern, with jazz, cinema, women's changing fashion and a more relaxed attitude to alcohol. Rural America felt threatened. It supported Prohibition (the ban on alcohol, 1920–33), the revived **Ku Klux Klan** (which was at its peak in the mid-1920s and attacked Black Americans, Catholics, Jews and immigrants), and Protestant fundamentalism, which was in the news at the **Scopes "Monkey" Trial** in Tennessee in July 1925. Politically this meant that the 1920s boom depended on a Republican party that was strong in the Midwest and the small towns and had little to offer farmers. Only about 11 per cent of farm dwellings had electricity even by 1932, against about two thirds of homes overall.

2. **Rural banks failed** in large numbers, weakening the whole banking system before 1929.
3. **Farm debt** meant that falling prices after 1929 would be catastrophic.
4. The government's refusal to help farmers (the McNary–Haugen vetoes) left them without protection.
:::

## The causes of the Great Crash

### A decline in confidence, and the signs of weakness

Before September 1929 some things had already started to go wrong in the real economy.
- **Housing.** The building of new homes had peaked around 1925–26 and fell in the later 1920s.
- **Car sales.** The market was beginning to fill up, since by the late 1920s about 60 per cent of families already owned one. Car output fell in the summer of 1929.
- **Industrial production** peaked in the middle of 1929. The National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) dates the start of the recession to **August 1929**, two months before the Crash.
- **Stocks of unsold goods** were rising in warehouses and shops.

The point is that the Crash fell on an economy that was already slowing. Shares were priced as if profits would grow for ever. When people began to doubt that, they began to sell, and a fall in confidence turned into a rush.

:::def Confidence
In economics and history, people's belief that the economy will keep growing and that banks and companies are safe. When confidence falls, people save instead of spending and sell shares instead of buying them. Because confidence feeds on itself, it can collapse quickly.
:::

### The stock market boom

A **share** (or stock) is a part-ownership of a company. Share prices rise when people expect profits to rise. Between August 1921 and September 1929 the **Dow Jones Industrial Average**, which tracks thirty leading companies, rose from about 63 to 381.17, six times higher. At first this was justified by profits. From about 1927 prices rose faster than profits, and more and more people bought shares for the sole reason that prices were going up.

:::def Speculation
Buying something (shares, land) not for its use or income but to sell it later at a higher price. Speculation is risky because the buyer's profit depends on other people paying more.
:::

:::def Buying on the margin
Buying shares by paying only part of the price (the margin) and borrowing the rest from a broker, with the shares as security. Brokers in turn borrowed from banks on "call", meaning the loan could be demanded back at once.
:::

How did it work? Suppose a share cost $100 and you paid $20 of your own money and borrowed $80 from your broker. If the price rose to $120 you sold, repaid $80 and kept $40: a 100 per cent profit on your $20. If it fell to $80, your broker demanded more money (a **margin call**), and if you could not pay, he sold your shares and you lost everything. Margins varied, and historians' estimates of the average differ, but many investors put down only a fraction of the price. Brokers' loans rose to more than $8.5 billion by August 1929. In March 1929 the rate on call money reached 20 per cent, as lenders scrambled for funds, and on 26 March the National City Bank, led by Charles Mitchell, announced that it would lend about $25 million to the market and cut the rate despite the Federal Reserve Board's warnings against speculative lending.

**Investment trusts** made things worse. These were companies that bought shares in other companies and sold their own shares to the public. About 265 new trusts were started in 1929 alone, and between them they sold about $3 billion of securities that year, around a third of all new capital raised. Many were **leveraged**: they borrowed money (by issuing bonds) to buy more shares, so that a rise in share prices multiplied their profits, but a fall multiplied their losses. Holding companies in utilities, like Samuel Insull's empire in Chicago, built pyramids of companies each owning the next. There was also open manipulation: "pools" of rich traders pushed up a share's price and sold at the top to the public.

### Lack of regulation

In 1929 almost nothing stopped any of this. The Securities and Exchange Commission did not exist (it was created in 1934). Companies did not have to publish reliable accounts. Insider trading and pools were not illegal. The New York Stock Exchange regulated itself, and a few states had "blue sky" laws against fraud, but no federal body supervised the market. Banks could set up affiliates to sell securities to their own customers and lend to brokers. The Federal Reserve Board warned in a letter of 2 February 1929 that the Federal Reserve Act did not contemplate the use of Reserve Bank credit for speculation, but it had no power to stop banks lending to brokers, and its tools were blunt.

The Federal Reserve itself was divided. The Board in Washington wanted to deny credit to banks that lent to speculators ("direct pressure"). The New York Federal Reserve Bank, under George Harrison, wanted to raise interest rates. The Board refused New York's requests for months, then agreed in August 1929 to a rise in New York's discount rate to 6 per cent. That squeezed credit across the economy, but did not stop speculation until the market was already about to fall. The Fed was blamed for doing too little and then for doing too much.

### Overvaluation: was it a bubble?

**Overvaluation** means share prices are higher than a company's profits justify. By September 1929 the average share on the Standard and Poor's index was priced at about 33 times its annual earnings, far higher than earlier in the decade. Some experts thought prices were reasonable. The Yale economist **Irving Fisher** said in October 1929 that prices had reached "what looks like a permanently high plateau". Others disagreed: on 5 September 1929 the business forecaster **Roger Babson** warned that "sooner or later a crash is coming". His speech started a sharp fall, called the "Babson Break". In London, the collapse of the financier Clarence Hatry's companies on 20 September 1929 shook confidence in London and added to the selling of shares.

:::note Was 1929 really a bubble? (stretch)
Historians disagree. **J.K. Galbraith**, in *The Great Crash 1929* (1954), saw a speculative mania fed by easy credit and a belief that prices could only rise: the Crash was a collapse of fantasy. Other economists have asked whether prices were justified by the high profits and low taxes of the late 1920s. Eugene White, in an article of 1990 (*The Stock Market Boom and Crash of 1929 Revisited*), argued that part of the rise could be explained by fundamentals but that the last stage looked like a bubble. In an AS answer you do not need the debate; you need to be able to say what the evidence of speculation is (margin, trusts, call money at 20 per cent) and what it is not (proof that the whole market was worthless).
:::

### The Crash

The market had already fallen about 10 per cent by the end of September 1929 and was unsteady in October. Then came three days.

In two days the Dow lost nearly a quarter of its value, and by 13 November it had lost almost half of its September peak. The ticker, which printed the prices, ran hours behind, so that investors did not know what they owned or had lost. The sudden drops, and the margin calls that followed each one, forced selling from people who did not want to sell.

:::key The causes of the Crash: five points to learn
1. **Easy credit and margin buying** let people speculate with borrowed money.
2. **Speculation and overvaluation**: prices rose faster than profits from about 1927.
3. **Investment trusts and holding companies** magnified gains and losses.
4. **No regulation** of the stock market, banks' securities business or credit.
5. **A fall in confidence** after warning signs in 1929 (the slowdown, the Fed's rate rise, Babson, Hatry), which turned buying into selling; margin calls and falling prices then fed each other.
:::

:::warn The Crash was not the Depression
The Crash was a stock-market collapse in October and November 1929. The Depression was a fall in output and jobs that deepened for more than three years. The Dow even recovered about half of its fall by April 1930 (to 294.07). It reached its low on 8 July 1932 (41.22), 89 per cent below the peak. Only a small minority of Americans owned shares, so the Crash by itself ruined few families. The question for the next section is how a stock-market collapse became a collapse of the whole economy.
:::

### The impact of the First World War on the United States and on international trade

The war made the United States the world's leading creditor. Before 1914 it owed money to Europe. By 1919 the Allied governments owed the US government more than $10 billion, and the American government refused to cancel the debts. The Allies, in turn, demanded **reparations** from Germany, fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks (about $31.5 billion). Germany could pay only if it earned money by exporting, but the high American tariffs of 1922 and 1930 made that harder.

The system was held together by a circle of American loans. Under the **Dawes Plan** of 1924 Germany's payments were reduced and a loan of $200 million, sold in New York, was lent to Germany. After that, American banks lent Germany large sums. Germany used the money to pay reparations to Britain and France, which used the money to repay their war debts to the United States. The same dollars went round and round.

When American investors put their money into the stock market in 1928–29, the loans to Germany and Latin America dried up. The **Young Plan** of 1929 cut Germany's total reparations to 121 billion gold marks (about $29 billion), but by then the German economy was weak, and when the Depression came the system collapsed. In 1931 Hoover announced a one-year moratorium on all war debts and reparations, and by mid-1933 every European debtor except Finland had defaulted on its American loans.

**Tariffs and trade.** America's high tariffs (Fordney–McCumber, 1922) made it harder for debtors to earn the dollars they needed. In 1930 the **Smoot–Hawley Tariff**, signed by Hoover on 17 June 1930, raised duties on over 20,000 goods. More than 1,000 economists petitioned Hoover to veto it. Other countries raised their tariffs in reply, and world trade fell by about two thirds in value between 1929 and 1933. Historians disagree about how much of the fall the tariff caused, because the Depression itself shrank demand and prices, but few think it helped, and it damaged international co-operation just when it was most needed. The economic historian Douglas Irwin (*Peddling Protectionism*, 2011) argues that the tariff did harm but was not the main cause of the slump.

:::def Reparations
Payments that Germany was forced to make to the Allies after the First World War to compensate for the damage caused by the war, fixed at 132 billion gold marks in 1921 and reduced by the Dawes (1924) and Young (1929) Plans.
:::

:::def Tariff
A tax on imported goods. It makes them dearer than home-produced goods and protects domestic producers, but it can lead other countries to raise their own tariffs.
:::

### Structural weaknesses in the American economy

**The old industries.** The boom of the 1920s was concentrated in the new industries. The old ones were already sick.
- **Coal** was hit by competition from oil, natural gas and hydroelectric power, and the mines of Appalachia had too many workers.
- **Textiles** faced competition from artificial fibres (rayon) and the shift of mills to the South, where wages were lower.
- **Railroads** were losing passengers and freight to cars, buses and trucks.
These industries employed millions of workers whose wages and spending fell in the 1920s, and there was no federal system to retrain or support them.

**Overproduction and underconsumption.** Mass production could make more goods than Americans could buy. Factories and farms produced more than markets absorbed, and stocks of unsold goods built up by 1929. The reason, as we have seen, was uneven incomes: hire purchase could delay the problem, but not solve it.

:::def Overproduction
Producing more goods than customers can afford to buy at profitable prices. It leads to unsold stocks, falling prices, cuts in output and job losses.
:::

**Agriculture.** The farm depression described above meant that a quarter of the population had little money to spend, and farm debts and bank failures spread into the wider economy.

**The banks.** The American banking system was unusually fragile. In 1920 there were nearly 29,000 banks, and most were small **unit banks** with only one office, because laws such as the McFadden Act (1927) limited branch banking. A single-branch bank in a farming town had all its loans in one area and one crop: when farmers could not repay, it failed. There was no federal deposit insurance, so depositors who heard of a failure rushed to take their savings out, and even a sound bank could be ruined by a **bank run**. Banks had also made loans to brokers and bought securities, and when share prices fell their assets lost value.

:::def Unit bank
A bank with only a single office and no branches, typical of the United States in the 1920s. Unit banks could not spread their risks across regions, so a local slump could close them.
:::

The bank failures that began in November 1930 turned the recession into a banking crisis. The Bank of United States in New York, with about 400,000 depositors, collapsed on 11 December 1930. In 1930 1,350 banks suspended business and in 1931 2,293, taking depositors' savings with them (see the chart above). Each failure destroyed money, so spending and lending fell further.

### The role of the Federal Reserve

The **Federal Reserve System**, created in 1913, was the United States' central bank, made up of twelve regional Reserve Banks and a Board in Washington. Its job was to control the money supply and act as a "lender of last resort" to banks in trouble.

It made two kinds of mistake. In 1928–29 it raised interest rates to try to damp down speculation, which slowed the real economy and, through the gold standard, caused recessions abroad. Then, between 1930 and 1933, it failed to rescue the banks. Banks that could not meet withdrawals needed the Fed to lend to them or to put more money into the system, but the Fed refused or hesitated. Some officials believed that weak banks should be allowed to fail, and under the **gold standard** others raised interest rates in 1931 to protect the dollar after Britain left gold. The money supply, the amount of money in the economy, fell by nearly 30 per cent between the autumn of 1930 and the winter of 1933, and average prices fell by a similar amount.

:::note Historians on the Federal Reserve and the Depression (stretch)
**Milton Friedman** and **Anna Schwartz**, in *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963), argued that the Great Depression was so deep because the Federal Reserve allowed the money supply to shrink by about a third: a small recession became a Great Contraction through its mistakes. **Charles Kindleberger**, in *The World in Depression 1929–1939* (1973), argued that the world economy fell into depression because Britain could no longer lead and the United States would not. **Peter Temin**, in *Did Monetary Forces Cause the Great Depression?* (1976), argued that the fall in spending came first, before the fall in money. **J.K. Galbraith** stressed the speculative mania and the weak structure of the economy. For AS you need only the idea: economists disagree about which cause was most important, and your job is to weigh them with evidence.
:::

:::key The causes of the Great Depression: what to learn
1. **Trigger**: the Crash destroyed wealth and confidence, and hit spending on durable goods and investment.
2. **International**: war debts, reparations, American tariffs and the end of American lending broke the system of trade and payments (the shock travelled round the world through the gold standard).
3. **Structure**: sick old industries, farm depression, overproduction and underconsumption caused by unequal incomes.
4. **Banks**: thousands of unit banks, no deposit insurance, bank runs.
5. **Policy**: the Federal Reserve let the money supply shrink; Smoot–Hawley; government non-intervention; the orthodoxy of balancing the budget.
:::

:::warn Keep the Crash and the Depression apart
A common slip is to write "the Crash caused the Depression" as if one thing led directly to the other. Better: "The Crash was the trigger. It turned a slowdown that had already started in August 1929 into a crisis of confidence, but the Depression lasted so long, and was so deep, because of weaknesses in banking, agriculture and international trade and because policy failed to prevent a collapse in the money supply."
:::

## Why did the Great Depression follow the Crash? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then ask which was necessary, which was sufficient and which was a mistake that could have been avoided.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term weaknesses | Unequal incomes; the farm depression; sick old industries; unit banks | Left the economy with no reserves and no demand to fall back on |
| International | War debts and reparations; the American tariffs; the end of loans; the gold standard | Spread the crisis worldwide and cut export markets |
| Triggers | Housing and car slowdown; Fed rate rises (1928–29); the Crash of October 1929 | Ended the boom and hit confidence |
| Policy mistakes | Fed inaction (1930–33); Smoot–Hawley; no deposit insurance; no federal relief | Turned a recession into the Great Depression |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The Crash was the **trigger** but not the main cause of the Depression. Without the weaknesses of the 1920s the shock would have caused a sharp recession like that of 1920–21. What made it a Depression was the failure of the banking system and the fall in the money supply, which a better-designed system, or a Fed that acted, might have prevented. Government policy was responsible both by what it did (high tariffs, tax cuts that widened inequality) and by what it failed to do (regulate banks and markets, help farmers).
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why there was a stock market boom in the late 1920s.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "There was a boom in the stock market because the economy was doing well. Lots of people bought shares. Prices went up very quickly. Coolidge was president and he did not interfere. Many people borrowed money to buy shares and they hoped to get rich."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it lists things that happened and does not explain how any of them made share prices rise. There are no names, dates or figures. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor with detail.** "One reason was the success of the real economy. New industries such as cars, electrical goods and radio made large profits, and output per worker was rising, so companies' shares looked a good investment. Another reason was credit. Many people bought shares on the margin, paying only part of the price and borrowing the rest from brokers, who borrowed from banks. Brokers' loans reached more than $8.5 billion by August 1929. This pushed up demand for shares without any need for savings."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained and supported by precise evidence. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add a third factor and link them:

"A third reason was the government's attitude. Mellon's tax cuts had left the rich with more money to invest, and nothing in the law stopped pools, insider dealing or banks lending to brokers, since the Securities and Exchange Commission did not exist until 1934. Even the Federal Reserve Board's letter of February 1929 warning against speculative credit had no power behind it.

These factors reinforced each other. Genuine profits in the new industries started the rise; easy credit let ordinary investors join it; and the absence of regulation meant that no one stopped it when prices ran ahead of profits. The Dow rose from about 63 in August 1921 to 381 in September 1929, and the most important reason for the late stage of the boom was credit, because prices rose fastest after 1927, when real profits were growing more slowly and brokers' loans were increasing. Confidence fed on itself: since prices had risen for years, more people believed they would carry on rising."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (profits started it, credit sustained it, the lack of regulation allowed it), with a supported conclusion about the main factor (credit). Level 4, 10 marks. About 350 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

Sooner or later a crash is coming which will take in the leading stocks, and he forecast a fall of 60 to 80 points in the Dow Jones average.

*Adapted from a report of a speech by Roger Babson, a business forecaster, at the National Business Conference, Wellesley, Massachusetts, 5 September 1929. The first part is quoted; the rest is a reporter's summary.*

**Source B**

Stock prices have reached what looks like a permanently high plateau. I do not feel that there will be soon, if ever, a fifty or sixty point break from present levels, such as Mr. Babson has predicted.

*Adapted from a statement by Irving Fisher, professor of economics at Yale University, to a dinner of purchasing agents in New York on 15 October 1929, reported in The New York Times on 16 October 1929. The first sentence and the phrases "fifty or sixty point break" and "such as Mr. Babson has predicted" are quoted.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about confidence in the stock market in autumn 1929.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 5 marks).** "Source A says a crash is coming and Source B says it is not. So they disagree about the stock market. Babson is a forecaster and Fisher is a professor, so they might be biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity and the comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources have one thing in common: both treat the level of share prices as the central question and both take a public risk by making a numerical forecast. Source A predicts a fall of 60 to 80 points, and Source B refers to the 'fifty or sixty point break' that 'Mr. Babson has predicted', so Source B is in fact replying to Source A. Both therefore show that in autumn 1929 the future of the market was a matter of public argument, and not a secret fear.

They differ on what would happen. Source A is sure that 'a crash is coming', while Source B believes the market has reached a 'permanently high plateau' and that a big break is unlikely 'soon, if ever'. Source A sees prices as too high; Source B treats them as a stable new level, justified by the economy.

The context explains the difference in the date and in the provenance. Babson spoke on 5 September 1929, two days after the Dow's peak of 381.17, to a conference of businessmen. He was a professional forecaster who had been warning for a year or more, and his warning started a sharp sell-off that afternoon, so his words were reported widely. A forecaster's reputation depends on dramatic predictions, so the source may exaggerate the certainty and the size of the fall; but he spoke before the fall. Fisher spoke on 15 October, after six weeks in which the market had been shaky, and after the Dow had already fallen from its peak. He was a respected economist and a believer in the new economy of mass production, and as the source says, he was answering Babson directly, which may explain the confident tone. His statement has the weakness of any prediction made nine days before the Crash of 24 October, but the strength that he was an expert who knew the evidence of high corporate profits. Neither source is evidence of what ordinary investors felt, but together they show that expert opinion was split and that even in mid-October many informed people were confident. For the question of confidence, Source B is the stronger evidence of the mood of the market's supporters, and Source A the stronger evidence of an early warning that was dismissed."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through the dates and the nature and purpose of each source; a final comparison of their value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The Wall Street Crash was the main cause of the Great Depression.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which side is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the Crash destroyed wealth and confidence (Dow down almost half by 13 November 1929; about $30 billion lost in the week to 29 October); spending on durable goods and investment fell in 1930; credit tightened; the link to bank failures (banks held securities and made loans to brokers); the timing (the Depression followed it).
- Against 1, the economy was already weak: the recession began in August 1929, two months before; the farm depression, old industries, unequal incomes, small banks; the Dow recovered a third of its fall by April 1930; few families owned shares.
- Against 2, policy mistakes: the Fed allowed the money supply to fall by nearly 30 per cent; Smoot–Hawley (June 1930); no rescue of banks (1,350 suspended in 1930, 2,293 in 1931); the international collapse of war debts and the gold standard.
- Judgement: the Crash was the trigger, but not the main cause. Criterion: which cause explains the depth and the length of the slump, not just its start.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The Wall Street Crash was an important cause of the Great Depression. On Black Tuesday, 29 October 1929, 16.4 million shares were sold and the Dow fell nearly 12 per cent in a single day. Billions of dollars were lost, and many people were ruined. Banks that had lent to brokers lost money and some failed. People stopped buying cars and other goods, so factories laid off workers and unemployment rose. This shows that the Crash was the main cause of the Depression."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the final sentence asserts rather than explains: a stock market collapse is not the same as the loss of a quarter of all jobs. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the Crash alone cannot explain the Depression, because the weaknesses it exposed were already in place. The National Bureau of Economic Research dates the start of the recession to August 1929, two months before Black Thursday, and house-building and car sales had been slowing for some time. Farmers had been in depression since 1920–21, when farm prices fell by over half, and more than 5,700 banks, mostly small rural ones, had failed between 1921 and 1929. Incomes were unequal: the richest 1 per cent took roughly a fifth of national income, so the mass of families could not buy the goods that mass production made. These weaknesses meant that any shock would hit hard. They also explain why the Crash was not by itself enough: only a minority of households owned shares, and the Dow actually recovered from 198.60 in November 1929 to 294.07 in April 1930. A Crash that was followed by a recovery in the market would not have caused a depression unless something else made it worse.

That something else was policy, and here the case against the statement is strongest. Between the autumn of 1930 and the winter of 1933 the Federal Reserve allowed the money supply to fall by nearly 30 per cent, and did not act as a lender of last resort while 1,350 banks suspended business in 1930, 2,293 in 1931 and about 4,000 in 1933. Each failure destroyed savings, so spending and lending fell further, and the deflation made debts heavier. The Smoot–Hawley Tariff of June 1930 raised duties on over 20,000 goods and led other countries to retaliate, so world trade fell by about two thirds, and the war debts and reparations that had been paid with American loans could no longer be paid. Friedman and Schwartz argued that a Fed which had expanded the money supply could have turned the slump into an ordinary recession.

Overall, the Crash was the trigger, and without it the 1920s boom might have lasted a little longer. But it was not the main cause of the Great Depression. The Depression was deep because the economy was structurally weak, and long because policy failed to stop the collapse of banks and the money supply. The statement is therefore true of the timing of the Depression, but not of its cause. If I had to choose one cause, it would be the failure of the banking system and the Fed, because that explains why a downturn became a catastrophe."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (what explains the depth and length of the slump, not just its start), and distinguishes trigger from cause. With an opening paragraph on the case for the Crash, this is Level 5 (19–20). Note that "the Crash" and "the weaknesses of the 1920s economy" are different factors; separating them is itself a mark of a strong answer.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

America's present need is not heroics, but healing; not nostrums, but normalcy; not revolution, but restoration.

*From a speech by Warren Harding, Republican candidate for president, in Boston, 14 May 1920.*

**Source B**

Coolidge said that the chief business of the American people is business. They are profoundly concerned with producing, buying, selling, investing and prospering in the world.

*Adapted from an address by President Calvin Coolidge to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, Washington, 17 January 1925. The first sentence is a paraphrase; the second is quoted.*

**Source C**

No Congress of the United States ever assembled, on surveying the state of the Union, has met with a more pleasing prospect than that which appears at the present time. About a third of the national debt has been paid.

*Adapted from the annual message of President Calvin Coolidge to Congress, 4 December 1928. The first sentence is quoted; the second is a paraphrase.*

**Source D**

The Federal Reserve Act does not contemplate the use of the resources of the Federal Reserve banks for the creation or extension of speculative credit.

*Adapted from a letter from the Federal Reserve Board to the Federal Reserve banks, 2 February 1929.*

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that the policies of the Republican governments of the 1920s helped to cause the Great Crash?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A and B show a government philosophy of leaving business free (normalcy, "the chief business … is business"), and C shows complacency (the "pleasing prospect"). Challenge or qualify: D shows that a part of the government, the Federal Reserve Board, saw speculation as a danger and tried to discourage it. B is about the press as well, not about the economy; none of the sources mentions the causes of the Crash itself (margin buying, investment trusts, overvaluation).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a campaign speech in 1920 by a candidate who wanted votes; it is slogans, not policy. Its value is that it states a philosophy that the administration then followed: tax cuts, high tariffs, a small federal government. Weak evidence about causes of the Crash, strong evidence for the climate of 1921.
- **B**: Coolidge was speaking to newspaper editors about the press and business, so the sentence is partly a compliment to his audience. But it is a statement by the president himself of a view that shaped policy, and the surrounding policy (Mellon's cuts, weak regulators) bears it out. It was often misquoted as "the business of America is business", which suggests it became a symbol of the 1920s.
- **C**: the president's last message, a valedictory with a motive to claim success; published weeks after Hoover's election and eleven months before the Crash. The debt claim is accurate, but "pleasing prospect" shows the complacency that allowed the speculative boom to go unchecked. It does not mention farmers or the slowdown in housing.
- **D**: a private letter between banking authorities, addressed to the Reserve Banks. It is not propaganda, and it is strong evidence that the Fed saw a problem. But the same Board did not have the power to stop banks lending to brokers, and the New York Fed disagreed about rate rises; the letter shows the government was divided, not that it was determined.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A, B and C support the view that the philosophy of the Republican governments helped create the conditions for the Crash, though each does so in a different way. Source A shows the intention to return to a business-friendly government; Source B shows a president who regards business success as the nation's main purpose; Source C, from December 1928, shows a government that believed the prosperity would last, when the housing market was already weakening and farmers were still in depression. Policy did follow these ideas: Mellon cut the top tax rate from 73 per cent to 25 per cent, the Securities and Exchange Commission did not exist, and the stock exchange regulated itself. However, Source D challenges the view that the government simply encouraged speculation. The Federal Reserve Board's letter of 2 February 1929 shows it recognised the danger and urged the Reserve Banks to discourage speculative lending, and my own knowledge confirms that the Fed raised the discount rate to 6 per cent in August 1929. Yet the Board and the New York Fed disagreed, and it is arguable that their late and confused action made the fall worse, so even D does not clear government of responsibility. Moreover, the sources do not mention causes that were outside government's control, such as the optimism of investors, margin buying and the mania for investment trusts. On balance the sources agree only to a large extent: they show a climate of complacency created by the government's attitude, which allowed speculation to develop, but the Crash itself was the work of the market, and D shows that the regulators knew it."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of the 1920s; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; and the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "To what extent were government policies responsible for the Great Depression?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Wall Street Crash happened on 29 October 1929, which was Black Tuesday. On that day 16 million shares were sold and people lost all their money. Many banks failed and many people lost their jobs. President Hoover did not do anything about it, so the Depression got worse. Banks failed because people took their money out. This led to the Great Depression, so government policies were responsible."

**The level.** There is some knowledge, but the paragraph is a **narrative** of the Crash with an assertion on the end. It runs the Crash and the Depression together, offers no policy that caused the Depression and says "Hoover did not do anything" without evidence. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description, few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument about policy, name the policies, separate the Crash from the Depression and use evidence to link them. "Government policies did contribute to the Depression, but the most damaging were actions of the Federal Reserve and the tariff. Between 1930 and 1933 the Fed allowed the money supply to fall by nearly 30 per cent and did not lend to failing banks, so that 1,350 banks closed in 1930 and 2,293 in 1931, destroying savings and credit. In June 1930 Hoover signed the Smoot–Hawley Tariff, raising duties on over 20,000 goods, which led to retaliation by other countries and a fall in world trade of about two thirds. These were choices of policy, not consequences of the Crash." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement and the Crash is not confused with the Depression.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What did Harding mean by "normalcy"?
A: A return to peacetime politics before 1914: low taxes, a small federal government, high tariffs and a government friendly to business rather than regulating it.
Q: Give three parts of Mellon's economic policy and one effect of each.
A: Tax cuts (top rate from 73 to 25 per cent, which left the rich with more to invest and increased inequality); a balanced budget and spending cuts (a third of the debt paid by 1928, and no government spending to cushion a slump); high tariffs, the Fordney–McCumber Tariff of 1922 (which protected farmers and manufacturers but made it harder for foreign debtors to pay).
Q: Why did farmers not share in the prosperity of the 1920s?
A: Farm prices fell by more than half in 1920–21 after wartime demand ended; farmers' debts stayed the same in dollars; overproduction and mechanisation kept prices low, tariffs reduced exports; Coolidge vetoed the McNary–Haugen Bills; rural banks failed.
Q: What is "buying on the margin", and why was it dangerous?
A: Buying shares by paying only part of the price and borrowing the rest from a broker. It magnified profits when prices rose, but when prices fell the broker demanded more money (margin calls), forcing investors to sell, which pushed prices down further.
Q: Give four causes of the Wall Street Crash.
A: Any four of: easy credit and margin buying; speculation and overvaluation; investment trusts; lack of regulation of the stock market; a fall in confidence after the slowdown and warnings in 1929; the Fed's rate rise in August 1929.
Q: Why did war debts and reparations matter to the Depression?
A: The US was owed more than $10 billion by the Allies, who relied on German reparations to pay it; Germany relied on American loans (Dawes Plan) to pay reparations. When US lending dried up after 1928 the circle broke, and American tariffs made it harder for debtors to earn dollars.
Q: Why were the small banks of the 1920s a weakness?
A: Most were single-office unit banks with loans concentrated in one area; farm failures closed them (5,712 suspended 1921–29); there was no deposit insurance, so depositors' fear of a failure caused runs that closed sound banks too.
Q: What mistakes did the Federal Reserve make between 1928 and 1933?
A: In 1928–29 it raised interest rates to curb speculation, slowing the economy; from 1930 to 1933 it failed to act as lender of last resort to failing banks and allowed the money supply to fall by nearly 30 per cent.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Republican governments of 1921–29 cut taxes and raised tariffs.
A: Indicative content: Mellon's belief that high wartime taxes discouraged saving and investment, and that low taxes would encourage growth (top rate from 73 to 25 per cent by 1925); the wish to reduce the government's wartime role (normalcy, laissez-faire); high tariffs (Fordney–McCumber 1922) to protect farmers and manufacturers hurt by a post-war slump and by cheap European imports; the Republican base in business and the Midwest; a balanced budget. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects the factors, e.g. by showing that low taxes and high tariffs came from the same philosophy of helping business, and judges which mattered more (for example, that the tax cuts mattered more to the boom and tariffs to the later trade problem).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the farming communities did not share in the prosperity of the 1920s.
A: Indicative content: farm prices fell by over half in 1920–21 after the war ended; debts taken on in the war boom could not be repaid; overproduction and mechanisation kept prices down; tariffs cut foreign markets for farm goods; foreclosures; the vetoes of the McNary–Haugen Bills (1927, 1928); rural banks failed (most of the 5,712 suspensions of 1921–29); lack of electricity (about 11 per cent of farm dwellings in 1932). Level 4: connect the factors (low prices and debts led to bank failures and falling farm spending, and government policy did not help) and judge which was most important, probably the post-war fall in prices combined with fixed debts.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The stock market boom of the late 1920s was caused by speculation.' How far do you agree?
A: For: margin buying and call loans (over $8.5 billion in August 1929); investment trusts (265 new in 1929); prices rose faster than profits after 1927; the Dow from about 63 to 381; call money at 20 per cent in March 1929; pools. Against: the boom began with real profits in the new industries and rising output; low taxes and rising productivity; confidence in a new era (Fisher's "permanently high plateau"); a lack of regulation allowed it to continue; the Fed's low rates in 1927. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that profits explain the start of the boom and speculation its last stage, so the statement is true of the extremes but not of the whole boom, with the criterion of what drove prices above company profits.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Unequal distribution of wealth was the main reason for the Great Depression.' How far do you agree?
A: For: the top 1 per cent took about a fifth to a quarter of the income in 1928; wages rose by only 5–9 per cent in 1923–29 while output per worker rose faster; overproduction and underconsumption; hire purchase postponed the problem; the rich saved and speculated. Against: the Crash; the banking collapse and the Fed's failure to stop the money supply falling; Smoot–Hawley and war debts; the farm depression and sick old industries; historians (Friedman and Schwartz, Kindleberger) who stress money and international causes. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "which cause explains the depth and length of the slump", and judges, e.g., that inequality was a long-term weakness that made the economy vulnerable, but the banks and the Fed explain why the vulnerability became a catastrophe.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the American economy.
A: Similarities: both give a view of the American economy by someone with an interest in how it was described; both are concerned with the position of ordinary Americans or farmers (A: poverty; B: farm prices). Differences: A is optimistic and general (poverty is nearly beaten), B is bleak and precise (36¼ cents wheat, 20 cents corn); A is a political speech from August 1928, at the peak of the boom; B is an official bank report written four years later at the bottom of the Depression. Explanation: the dates explain the difference (before and after the Crash); A's purpose was to win votes and had to be positive; B's purpose was to record, though a regional bank might stress farm distress. Evidence value: A is strong evidence of the mood of 1928 but weak about farmers, who were not prospering; B is strong evidence for prices in 1932, weak on the causes. A Level 4 answer explains the differences through the context and judges which is better evidence of the economy as a whole.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources support the view that the American economy was healthy in the late 1920s?
H: Decide which sources describe 1928 (A, D) and which describe 1929–32 (B, C). What can the later sources show about the earlier years?
H: Use your own knowledge: how did farm incomes in 1929 compare with the rest of the economy?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature, date and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: A (Hoover in August 1928, "nearer to the final triumph over poverty") claims prosperity; D (rugged individualism) shows the confidence of the system in its own strength. Challenge: B and C show the farm collapse (grain at its lowest level in the history of the trade; farm cash income down from $4.89 billion to $1.47 billion), but they are from 1932 and so describe the result of the Depression rather than the 1920s. With knowledge: the farm depression began in 1920–21, so even in 1929 farm income was already low and the countryside was not healthy; the figure for 1929 in C is the boom-year baseline. Evaluation: A and D are campaign speeches by a candidate who wanted to win, and so weak evidence about the real state of the economy; B and C are official records and strong evidence for the figures but not for the cause. Level 5 judges how far: the sources support the view for the cities and industries of 1928, but own knowledge (farmers, old industries, banks, inequality) shows that the prosperity was narrower than A suggests.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The Federal Reserve, not the stock market, caused the Great Depression.' How far does the evidence support this view?
H: Separate the Crash (a shock to wealth and confidence) from the banking collapse and the fall in the money supply.
H: Use Friedman and Schwartz (1963) and Kindleberger (1973) as competing explanations, and ask what each leaves out.
A: Support: the money supply fell by nearly 30 per cent between 1930 and 1933; the Fed did not act as lender of last resort while thousands of banks closed (1,350 in 1930, 2,293 in 1931, about 4,000 in 1933); it raised rates in 1928–29 and again in 1931; the gold standard transmitted the shock. Challenge: the Crash and the structural weaknesses (farm debt, unit banks, inequality) created the crisis; Smoot–Hawley and the war-debt system were not the Fed's doing; Kindleberger blamed the lack of an international leader; Temin said spending fell first; the Fed was divided and its mistakes were shared with Congress, the Treasury and Hoover. A* judgement: the Fed's failure explains the depth, the weaknesses explain the vulnerability and the Crash the timing, so the statement is partly right but the Depression needed all three.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Peter Temin, J.K. Galbraith and Friedman and Schwartz explain the Depression in different ways. Which of the three explanations does the evidence of 1929–33 best support?
H: State each argument in one sentence.
H: Choose a criterion, such as "explains the timing, the depth and the length".
A: Galbraith: speculation and a weak economy; explains the Crash and the timing but not the length. Friedman and Schwartz: monetary contraction; explains the depth and length (money supply down by nearly 30 per cent; bank failures), but not what started the fall. Temin: a fall in spending before the money supply fell (the recession began in August 1929, and consumer durables fell); explains the start. A* answers rank them by the criterion and recognise that they can be combined: the recession and the Crash (Galbraith, Temin) started the fall and the Fed (Friedman and Schwartz) made it a depression.

:::recap
- **Republican policy 1921–29**: normalcy; Mellon's tax cuts (top rate 73 to 25 per cent), balanced budgets, Fordney–McCumber (1922), immigration quotas, no regulation.
- **Boom**: mass production (Ford), cars (9 to 27 million vehicles, 1920–29), electricity (about a third to two thirds of homes), radio, credit, advertising; growth of about 4 per cent a year.
- **Weaknesses**: unequal wealth (top 1 per cent about a fifth to a quarter of income), weak wages and unions, the farm slump (prices down by over half in 1920–21), sick old industries (coal, textiles, railroads), 5,712 bank suspensions in 1921–29.
- **Crash**: margin buying, brokers' loans over $8.5 billion, 265 new trusts in 1929, no regulation, overvaluation; Dow peak 381.17 (3 September 1929); 24, 28 and 29 October 1929; 198.60 on 13 November; low 41.22 on 8 July 1932.
- **Depression causes**: war debts and reparations, tariffs (Smoot–Hawley, June 1930), unit banks and runs, the Fed's mistakes (money supply down by nearly 30 per cent), overproduction and underconsumption.
- **Judgement**: the Crash was the trigger; the weaknesses made it hurt; policy mistakes made it last.
