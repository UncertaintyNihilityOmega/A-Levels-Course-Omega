:::try Warm-up
Q: Two newspapers print different numbers for how many people attended the same protest. What would you want to know before deciding which to believe?
A: Who wrote each report and why (one may support the protesters, the other the government), how they counted, and whether either writer was there. These are the questions of provenance, which this chapter teaches.
Q: A friend says "The war started because of one shooting." Why might that be an incomplete explanation?
A: One event can set a war off without being the only cause. Long-standing rivalries, alliances and decisions made over years make a shooting powerful enough to start a war. This chapter separates long-term causes, short-term causes and triggers.
Q: Is "the 1900s" the same as "the twentieth century"?
A: Not quite. "The 1900s" can mean the decade 1900–09 or the whole hundred years; the twentieth century is 1901–2000. The chapter explains why the century number is always one more than the hundreds figure.
:::

## What history is

The **past** is everything that ever happened. **History** is something smaller and more human: the attempt to explain parts of the past, using the evidence that has survived.

:::def History
The study of the past through evidence, in which historians select, interpret and explain what has survived in order to answer questions about what happened, why, and how much it mattered.
:::

:::def Evidence
Anything that survives from the past and can be used to answer a question about it: a letter, a law, a budget, a cartoon, a photograph, a building, an interview. The same item can be evidence for one question and useless for another.
:::

Three facts about the past explain why history is an argument and not a list.

1. **The evidence is incomplete.** Most of what people said, did and thought left no trace. Ordinary peasants and workers wrote little; many documents were lost, burned or never made. A historian works like a detective who has some of the clues.
2. **The evidence is made by people.** A minister writes a report to defend his policy; a newspaper prints what will sell; a pamphleteer wants to persuade. Nothing comes to us as a neutral recording.
3. **Historians choose.** No book can tell everything. Each historian selects the facts that seem important to the question being asked, and different historians, writing in different times and places, ask different questions.

The British historian E.H. Carr, in *What Is History?* (1961), made this point by calling history "an unending dialogue between the present and the past": the historian's questions come from the present, and the answers come from evidence that is always partial. You do not need to agree with Carr. You do need to see that every statement in a history book is the result of a choice.

Take the storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789. The basic event is not in doubt: a crowd attacked the fortress, the garrison surrendered, the governor was killed. But France has celebrated 14 July as its national day since 1880. That is a decision made by a later generation about which day of the French Revolution should stand for the whole. A historian studying 1789 asks different questions: how many people were there, what did they want, and how much did the capture change? The event is the same; the history differs because the question differs.

This is the reason the exam is built the way it is. Paper 1 asks you to judge evidence (AO3). Paper 2 and Paper 4 ask you to explain and weigh causes and consequences (AO2) with accurate knowledge (AO1). Paper 3 asks you to explain why historians differ (AO4). Nobody expects you to memorise "what happened" as an end in itself; the knowledge is the raw material for an argument.

:::tip The one-sentence summary of A Level history
Answer the question you are asked, with accurate evidence, and **weigh** one explanation against another. A pile of facts is Level 2. A weighed argument is Level 5.
:::

## Sources: primary and secondary

A **source** is anything from which historians learn about the past. Sources come in two families.

:::def Primary source
A source made at the time being studied, by someone who lived through it or who recorded it then: a law, a letter, a diary, a speech, a newspaper article, a cartoon, a photograph, a budget.
:::

:::def Secondary source
A source made later, by someone who was not there, which uses primary sources (and other secondary sources) to explain or describe the past: a textbook, a history book, an article, a documentary.
:::

A primary source is **not** automatically true, and a secondary source is **not** automatically weaker. A primary source is close in time, but its author may have been wrong, badly informed or trying to persuade. A good secondary source has the advantage of hindsight: the author has read thousands of documents that no single eyewitness could see. The useful question is not "is it primary?" but "what is it good evidence of?"

Whether a source counts as primary or secondary also depends on the question. Georges Lefebvre's *The Coming of the French Revolution* (1939) is a **secondary** source for the events of 1789, because he was writing 150 years afterwards. But it is a **primary** source for the question "What did a Marxist historian (a follower of Karl Marx's ideas about class struggle) in the 1930s believe about the French Revolution?" Almost every source can be used in both ways.

Sources also come in **types**. Knowing the type tells you what to expect from it.

| Type | Examples from this course | Usually good for | Beware |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laws, treaties, decrees | The Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789); Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (1919) | What the authorities decided and wanted to be official | Words may differ from what happened; laws can be ignored |
| Private writings | A diary, a letter, a journal such as Arthur Young's travels in France (1787–89) | Individual experience, opinions, details | One person's view; may be written for later publication |
| Public speeches and pamphlets | Sieyès, *What is the Third Estate?* (1789); Lincoln's First Inaugural Address (1861) | What leaders wanted people to think; persuasion | Written to win support, so selective |
| Statistics and accounts | The royal budget of France in 1788 | Scale: how much, how many | Who collected the figures, and how? Compare like with like |
| Visual sources | A 1789 cartoon of a peasant carrying a clergyman and a noble; a photograph; a poster | Public feeling; what the maker wanted viewers to feel | Cartoons exaggerate; photographs are framed and can be posed |
| Memoirs and oral history | Napoleon's dictated recollections on St Helena (recorded by Las Cases, 1823) | Later reflection, motive | Memory is selective; the author is justifying his life |

:::eg Classify five sources
Question: Say whether each item is a primary or secondary source for the French Revolution of 1789, name its type, and give one question it would be good evidence for.

1. **Article 1 of the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (26 August 1789).** Primary; an official statement of principle voted by the National Assembly. Good evidence for what the deputies wanted the new France to stand for. Not good evidence for what ordinary people thought, or for what really happened in the villages.
2. **William Doyle, *Origins of the French Revolution* (1980).** Secondary; a history book written 190 years later by a professional historian. Good evidence for how historians in 1980 explained the causes. For the causes of 1789 itself it is an argument built from primary sources, which you can test against them.
3. **A French cartoon of 1789 showing an old peasant carrying a clergyman and a noble.** Primary; a visual source. Good evidence for the propaganda message that people wanted spread in 1789; weak evidence for how heavy the real burdens were, because cartoons simplify.
4. **A lawyer's memoir written in 1830 about being a deputy in 1789.** Primary, because it is a participant's account, but written forty-one years later: memory has faded and the author knows how the story ended. Good for his later attitudes and some detail; risky for exact dates and motives.
5. **A history textbook published in 2015.** Secondary; a summary aimed at students. Useful for an overview; for an exam answer you must go to the facts and arguments it summarises.

*Lesson.* The label (primary or secondary) is only the first step. The second is always "evidence of what?"
:::

## Provenance: judging a source

**Provenance** is where a source comes from, and it is the key to AO3. You ask four questions of every source, and a fifth about the world around it.

:::def Provenance
The origin and background of a source: what kind of source it is (nature), who made it, when and where (origin), why and for whom it was made (purpose), and the circumstances of the time that explain what it says (context).
:::

:::steps
1. **Nature.** What kind of source is it: a law, a diary, a speech, a cartoon? Each type has strengths and weaknesses (see the table above).
2. **Origin.** Who made it? When, and where? How close was the author to the events, and what did he or she know?
3. **Purpose.** Why was it made, and for whom? A private diary and a public speech are not made for the same reasons. Did the maker want to persuade, defend, warn, report or impress?
4. **Context.** What was happening when it was made that helps you read it? A speech made in the middle of a crisis says different things from one made in calm.
5. **Content, read against the question.** What does it actually say, and what is it evidence of for the question you have been asked? Use its own words.
:::

Use these questions to make a judgement about the source's value as evidence **for the question**. Notice three things.

- **Bias is not the end of the discussion.** Saying "this source is biased" earns almost nothing. A biased source can be very good evidence of what its author wanted people to believe. A political leaflet is poor evidence of what happened, but excellent evidence of what a party was trying to make people feel.
- **Reliable and useful are different.** A source may be unreliable on one point and valuable on another. Ask: useful for what?
- **Cross-check.** Compare a source with another source and with what you know. If a diary, a law and a cartoon point the same way, the case is stronger.

:::eg Evaluate two sources against two different questions
**Source A**

I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. I believe I have no lawful right to do so, and I have no inclination to do so.

*From the First Inaugural Address of Abraham Lincoln, President of the United States, 4 March 1861. Lincoln introduced these words by saying they came from an earlier speech of his.*

**Source B**

The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies.

*Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919.*

*Background for this example: in 1860–61 the Southern slave states left, or **seceded** from, the **Union** (the states that stayed under the national government in Washington). Source A is from the new President's first speech, just after seven of them had gone.*

**Question 1.** How useful is Source A as evidence of why the Southern states left the Union in 1860–61?

*Answer.* The source is a presidential speech (nature), made by the new President on the day he took office, when seven Southern states had already seceded (origin and context), to reassure the South and hold the border states (slave states between North and South that had not yet left) in the Union (purpose). Because it is Lincoln's promise, it is strong evidence of what the Republican President said he would do: he would not attack slavery where it already existed. It is much weaker evidence of why the South seceded, because it is not a Southern voice. In fact it hints at the problem: the Southern states left because they feared what Lincoln's election meant for the future of slavery, in spite of this promise. For that question you would need Southern sources, such as the states' own declarations. The source is useful, but for a different question than the one asked.

**Question 2.** How useful is Source B as evidence of Allied attitudes towards Germany in 1919?

*Answer.* The source is a clause of a treaty (nature), drawn up by the Allied governments and presented to Germany in the Treaty of Versailles (origin) in 1919, after four years of war that had killed millions and devastated parts of France and Belgium (context). Its purpose, as the Allies explained it, was to give a legal basis for Germany's liability to pay reparations. It is therefore strong evidence that the Allies held Germany responsible and intended to make it pay. But it cannot tell us what every Allied government or citizen felt, and it says nothing about how Germans received it. They called it the "war guilt clause" and resented it. For those questions you would need other sources.

*Lesson.* The same method gave "limited" on the first question and "strong" on the second, because the value of a source depends on the question, not on the source alone.
:::

:::note A famous diary entry: do not read more into a source than it contains
For 14 July 1789, the day of the Bastille, the diary of King Louis XVI contains one word: *rien* ("nothing"). It has often been quoted as proof that the king did not know or care that a revolution had begun. But the diary was mainly a record of his hunting and engagements, and historians point out that it contains the word on many other days too. So it may well record only that no game was killed, or that nothing was formally scheduled. Historians still disagree about how much to read into it. Notice what the example teaches: first, work out what the source was **for**; only then decide what it can show.
:::

## Fact, opinion and interpretation

Three kinds of statement appear in a history book, and a good student can tell them apart.

:::def Fact
A statement about the past that can be checked against evidence and shown to be true or false: "The Bastille was stormed on 14 July 1789."
:::

:::def Opinion (judgement)
A statement that depends on someone's values or point of view and cannot simply be checked: "Louis XVI was a weak king." People may agree, but the statement is a judgement, not a fact.
:::

:::def Interpretation
A historian's overall explanation of an event or period, built by selecting facts, weighing them and linking them into an argument: for example, that the French Revolution was above all a revolution of the rising middle class, or above all the result of a political and financial collapse of the state.
:::

| Statement | Type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Louis XVI became king on 10 May 1774." | Fact | A date that can be checked in records |
| "Louis XVI was too indecisive to save the monarchy." | Opinion (judgement) | Depends on what "indecisive" means and on how much was possible |
| "The financial crisis, not the Enlightenment, caused the Revolution." | Interpretation | An argument about cause, built from evidence and open to challenge |
| "The Estates-General met on 5 May 1789." | Fact | Checkable |
| "The Revolution was inevitable." | Opinion or interpretation | An argument; the evidence can be used against it |

Four reminders prevent the commonest mistakes.

1. **A fact can be wrong.** If a source says the Bastille held hundreds of prisoners, it is stating a fact that can be tested, and it fails: only seven prisoners were inside.
2. **An opinion can be good evidence.** A pamphlet of 1789 that says the nobles were "a people apart" is useless as proof that nobles were foreigners, but excellent evidence of what many people thought.
3. **An interpretation is not "just an opinion".** It is an argument supported by evidence. A good interpretation can be wrong, but it can be wrong in ways that teach something. Paper 3 is about this.
4. **Inference** is a reasonable conclusion drawn from a source: "This cartoon was published in 1789, so some people already thought of the privileged orders as a burden on the peasants." A good inference says what the source shows **and** what it does not.

## Causation: why things happened

Most questions in this course begin with "why". Your answers improve once you know the shapes that causes take.

:::def Cause
A factor that helped to bring an event about. Historians almost always speak of **causes** (plural), because big events have several, at different levels, that work together.
:::

Causes are sorted by **when** they acted.

:::def Long-term cause
A condition that built up over decades and made the event possible, but did not make it certain: for example, unfair taxation or a rigid political system.
:::

:::def Short-term cause
A development in the years or months before the event that made it much more likely: a financial crisis, a lost war, a bad harvest.
:::

:::def Trigger
The immediate event that sets the outcome off at that moment: an assassination, a dismissal, a shooting. A trigger would normally have no effect if the earlier causes were absent.
:::

Two further ideas help you **weigh** causes, which is what separates Level 3 from Level 5.

:::def Necessary and sufficient
A **necessary** cause is one without which the event could not have happened. A **sufficient** cause is one that, on its own, was enough to bring the event about. Most causes in history are necessary but not sufficient: they were needed, but they needed company.
:::

For example, the bankruptcy of the French state in 1788 was a **necessary** cause of the calling of the Estates-General (without it the king would not have summoned them), but it was not **sufficient** to explain the Revolution: that needed the struggle over who should speak for the nation, and the hunger of the crowds. You meet this argument in full in [France (1)](#hist.eu.france.1).

:::key Six ways to weigh causes
1. **Necessity.** Could the event have happened without it? If not, it ranks high.
2. **Scale.** How many people did it affect, and how deeply?
3. **Duration.** Did it act for decades or for one afternoon?
4. **Links.** Did it make other causes stronger? A cause that strengthens the others outweighs one that stands alone.
5. **Timing.** Was it the factor that made the difference at the critical moment?
6. **Chance.** Was it contingent: an accident or a personal decision that might have gone differently?
Whichever criterion you choose, say so. "The most important cause was X, because without it the others would not have mattered" is a judgement; "X was important" is a statement.
:::

:::warn Trigger is not the same as main cause
The shooting at Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 triggered the First World War, but no historian argues that one assassination was the main cause: alliances, rivalries, war plans and the July crisis decisions turned it into a continental war. Treat a trigger as the last link in the chain, and ask what made the chain ready to break.
:::

## Consequences, change and continuity, significance

Causation looks backwards from an event; the other concepts look at what followed and how much it mattered.

:::def Consequence
A result of an event or development. Consequences can be **short-term** (within days, months or a few years) or **long-term** (over a generation or more), **intended** or **unintended**, **direct** or **indirect**.
:::

For example, the immediate consequences of the storming of the Bastille included the withdrawal of the royal troops and the creation of the National Guard; its long-term consequence was that 14 July became the symbol of a nation's revolution. A consequence that nobody intended is often the most interesting: Louis XVI called the Estates-General to save the finances, not to start a revolution.

:::def Change and continuity
**Change** is how things became different; **continuity** is how they stayed the same. Almost every period contains both, so a good answer judges their **balance**: what changed, what did not, and which matters more for the question.
:::

To judge change, ask three questions: how **fast** (a sudden turning point or a slow drift), how **far** (did it affect everyone or one group), and in what **direction** (towards more or less freedom, equality, wealth). The August Decrees of 1789 abolished the privileges of the orders overnight (rapid, far-reaching change), but most French people still farmed the land and paid dues until 1793, and bread stayed the main food. A claim that "everything changed in 1789" overlooks the continuities; the claim that "nothing changed" ignores the decrees. The best answer says which is stronger and why.

:::def Significance
How much an event, person or development mattered: to people at the time, to what followed, and to how later generations understand the past. Significance is a judgement made against stated criteria, not a quality that events have by themselves.
:::

Criteria for significance: **scale** (how many people were affected), **depth** (how greatly their lives changed), **duration** (how long the effects lasted), **resonance** (how far later people used the event as a symbol or example), and **revealing** (how much it shows about the society). The Bastille was a small military event but of great resonance; the Great Fear was a panic with a large effect on policy. You will compare such events in exam essays.

## Chronology: dates, centuries and calendars

**Chronology** means putting events in order, and being exact about when. Four small skills avoid most errors.

**1. BC and AD.** Years before the traditional birth date of Jesus are counted back, "BC" (before Christ), and after it forward, "AD" (*anno Domini*). Modern writers often use BCE and CE ("before the common era", "common era"): the numbers are the same. There is no year 0, so the first century AD is 1–100. The AS and A Level topics in this course are all AD, so you will meet the letters only in background reading.

**2. Centuries.** The twentieth century is the years 1901–2000: its number is **one more** than the hundreds figure of the years in it. Therefore:
- 1789 is in the **eighteenth** century (1701–1800);
- 1871 is in the **nineteenth** century (1801–1900);
- 1914 and 1991 are both in the **twentieth** century (1901–2000).

**3. Decades, "c." and ranges.** "The 1920s" means 1920–29. "c.1870" (or "circa") means about 1870. A range such as 1919–29 is written with an en dash; this course keeps two digits after the dash within a century (1789–92) and gives both years across a century (1899–1901).

**4. Old Style and New Style dates.** Until the twentieth century many countries used the **Julian calendar**, introduced by Julius Caesar, which has a slightly too long year and so drifts behind the seasons. Pope Gregory XIII issued a corrected **Gregorian calendar** in 1582, and Catholic Europe moved to it quickly. Britain and its colonies changed in 1752, when Wednesday 2 September was followed by Thursday 14 September. Russia kept the Julian calendar until 1918, when the Bolshevik government decreed that the day after 31 January 1918 would be 14 February.

:::def Old Style (OS) and New Style (NS)
Old Style dates follow the Julian calendar; New Style dates follow the Gregorian calendar used in most of the world. In the nineteenth century the Julian date was 12 days behind the Gregorian; from 1900 to 1918, 13 days behind.
:::

This matters for the Russia topic. The "October Revolution" took place on 25 October 1917 **Old Style**, which is 7 November 1917 New Style, and the "February Revolution" began on 23 February 1917 OS (8 March NS). This course gives Russian dates in Old Style, with the New Style date at first mention, as most textbooks do. Always say which you are using.

:::eg Convert and calculate
Question: Bloody Sunday took place on 9 January 1905 (Old Style). (a) What is the New Style date? (b) How long was it from the start of the Russo-Japanese War on 8 February 1904 NS to Bloody Sunday, counting in years and months? (c) In what century do both fall?

(a) In 1905 the gap was 13 days: 9 January OS is 22 January NS.

(b) The war began on 8 February 1904 (New Style, so the date as the rest of Europe wrote it). Bloody Sunday on 22 January 1905 NS is just under a year later: about eleven and a half months. The slip to avoid is to compare an OS date with an NS date without converting first.

(c) 1904 and 1905 are both in the twentieth century.

*Lesson.* Convert to one calendar before you subtract, and always say which calendar the date uses.
:::

## Taking notes that you can use

You will read a lot of history. Notes that copy the text do nothing; notes that sort it help you write essays. Three tools fit this subject.

The two tables below use a few French terms from 1789, the year the French Revolution began; you will meet them again in [France (1)](#hist.eu.france.1). The **livre** was the French unit of money (like the pound). The *taille* was a land tax from which nobles were exempt. The **Estates-General** was the assembly of the three orders of French society that the king called in 1789: the clergy, the nobles and the **Third Estate**, which was everyone who was neither clergy nor noble. The **Notables** were a hand-picked assembly of leading nobles and clergy that the king called in 1787 to approve reforms. **Necker** was the finance minister the public trusted, and **Sieyès** was a churchman who wrote a pamphlet for the Third Estate.

**1. The timeline.** A table with two columns, Date and Event, in order, with short cells. The chapters of this course open with one. Make your own for each topic from memory, then check it.

## Reading a historian's argument

From AS Level you read secondary sources, and at A Level (Paper 3) your whole answer is about them. Slow down and take an extract in five steps.

:::steps
1. **Who, when, where?** What does the text say about the author and the date? A book of 1939 and a book of 2008 may disagree for reasons that have nothing to do with the evidence.
2. **The main argument.** What is the author trying to prove? Look at the first and last sentences of paragraphs, and at words that signal judgement: "above all", "mainly", "the real reason", "not … but …".
3. **The evidence.** Which facts, examples, quotations does the author choose? Which relevant ones are left out?
4. **The opponent.** Historians write against someone. Signals: "it has been said that", "traditionally", "however", "yet". Who is being answered?
5. **The approach.** What questions does the author ask, and what do those questions suggest about beliefs and interests (political, economic, national)?
:::

:::eg Read an extract
**Extract** (*written for practice in the style of a historian's argument; these are not the words of a real historian*)

It has often been said that the Bastille fell because the people of Paris were angry about a fortress that stood for royal tyranny. This view is hard to sustain. Only seven prisoners were inside, and in the previous years the fortress had held very few. The crowd that gathered on 14 July was looking for gunpowder, having taken muskets that morning from the Invalides. The symbolism was added later, by the deputies and journalists who needed an event around which to organise their story of the Revolution. What mattered on 14 July was the breakdown of the king's authority in Paris: when the army would not fire, the crown had lost control of its capital.

**Question.** What is the author's argument, and with whom does the author disagree?

*Answer.*
1. **Who, when?** Unknown; the style suggests a modern historian interested in power and its collapse.
2. **Main argument.** The Bastille fell not because of what it symbolised but because the crowd needed gunpowder and because the army's loyalty had broken. The sentence that gives it away is "What mattered on 14 July was the breakdown of the king's authority".
3. **Evidence.** The seven prisoners; the raid on the Invalides; the crowd's need for powder; the army's refusal. Left out: the economic crisis of 1789, the role of Necker's dismissal on 11 July and of the district assemblies in Paris.
4. **Opponent.** "It has often been said" signals the traditional view that anger at tyranny, expressed in the fortress, explains the day. The author calls it "hard to sustain".
5. **Approach.** The author is interested in political and military power, and in how a story is built afterwards, not in popular feeling or economic hardship.

*Lesson.* A good reading separates the **whole argument** from the details, and notices what the author left out. At A Level this is the heart of Paper 3.
:::

## Writing a paragraph that argues

The most common weakness in History answers is **narrative**: telling the story in order, accurately, without answering the question. At A Level you must write **analysis**: a series of paragraphs that each make a point, prove it, explain it and tie it to the question.

:::def Analysis (in history)
Explaining why something happened or how much it mattered, by breaking it into causes or aspects, supporting each with evidence and judging how they relate. The opposite of narrative, which only tells what happened in order.
:::

Why "Explain" is the key: **evidence does not argue for itself**. A date or a figure only helps when you say what it shows. "Interest was 318 million livres" is evidence; "so about half of royal spending went on interest before any soldier was paid, which is why the Crown could not borrow its way out" is an explanation. In the exam, the move from describing to explaining is the move from Level 2 to Level 3 (Paper 2 part (a)), and the move from explaining to weighing is the move from Level 3 to Level 4 and 5.

:::eg From narrative to analysis
Question: Why did the Southern states secede in 1860–61?

**A narrative paragraph.** "Abraham Lincoln was elected President in November 1860. He won 180 electoral votes and his name was not on the ballot in many Southern states. South Carolina seceded on 20 December 1860. Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana and Texas then seceded, so that by 1 February 1861 there were seven states out of the Union."

*What is wrong?* Every sentence is true, but the paragraph never says **why**. It reports events in order and leaves the reader to guess the connection (this is Level 2 in a Paper 2 (a) answer).

**An analytical paragraph (PEEL).**

*Point.* "The trigger for secession was Lincoln's election in November 1860, because it showed Southern leaders that they had lost control of the national government.

*Evidence.* Lincoln won 180 of the 303 electoral votes, with about 40 per cent of the popular vote, without carrying a single slave state; the Republican platform opposed the spread of slavery into the western territories.

*Explain.* For leaders who saw the future of slavery as depending on its extension, this meant that the free states could now govern the Union without the South, even though Lincoln promised in March 1861 not to interfere with slavery where it already existed. It was this fear of losing the protection of the national government, not any immediate action against slavery, that made the election result enough to start secession in South Carolina on 20 December 1860.

*Link.* The election was therefore the trigger, but the underlying cause it activated was the defence of slavery, which is what the next paragraph will examine."

*Lesson.* The analytical version has the same facts plus a point, an explanation and a link, and it separates the trigger from the underlying cause.
:::

:::warn Three habits that hold back History answers
1. **Telling the story.** A chronological account is easier to write than an argument, and it feels like knowledge, but the exam rewards the answer to the question.
2. **Dropping in names and dates without saying why.** Evidence needs explanation.
3. **Saying "biased" and stopping.** Say whose view it is, what it is evidence of, and what it leaves out.
:::

## The language of ideas and government

History uses a small group of words over and over. Meet them now; every later chapter assumes you know them. Each is defined here as the syllabus topics use it.

### Ideas (ideologies)

:::def Ideology
A connected set of beliefs about how society should be organised and who should hold power, which guides political action.
:::

:::def Liberalism
In the nineteenth century, the belief in individual freedom, equality before the law, written constitutions, elected assemblies and the protection of property and of freedom of speech and religion. It opposed arbitrary rule by kings and aristocrats. (In this course it does not mean "left-wing" or "tolerant" in the modern everyday sense.)
:::

:::def Conservatism
The belief in preserving established institutions such as monarchy, the Church and a hierarchical society, and in changing them slowly if at all. In 1815 conservatives such as Metternich of Austria wanted to restore the old order after the French Revolution and Napoleon.
:::

:::def Nationalism
The belief that people who share a language, culture and history form a **nation**, and that each nation should govern itself, usually in its own state. It could unify (Germany, Italy) or break up multi-national empires (Austria-Hungary, Ottoman Empire), and it could turn aggressive.
:::

:::def Socialism
The belief that wealth and the means of production (factories, land, banks) should be owned or controlled by the community, not by a few private owners, in order to reduce inequality.
:::

:::def Communism
The form of socialism based on the ideas of Karl Marx: history is a struggle between classes, the workers will overthrow the owners of capital, and private ownership of the means of production will be abolished. In practice, in Soviet Russia (the Bolsheviks, later the Communist Party), it meant a one-party state that controlled the economy.
:::

:::def Fascism
An extreme nationalist, authoritarian ideology of the early twentieth century that glorified the nation and its leader, suppressed opposition and individual freedom, rejected both liberalism and communism and praised violence and military strength. Mussolini's Italy was the first fascist regime.
:::

:::def Imperialism
The policy or practice of extending a state's power over other territories and peoples, by conquest, settlement or economic control, to create or run an empire.
:::

### Systems of government

:::def Absolute monarchy
Rule by a king or queen who holds all political authority and is not limited by law or an elected body (Louis XVI before 1789).
:::

:::def Autocracy
Rule by a single person with unlimited power. The Tsars of Russia were autocrats; they also used the title *autocrat* officially until 1917.
:::

:::def Constitutional monarchy
A system in which a monarch reigns but a constitution limits the monarch's power, usually by giving much of it to an elected assembly (France in 1791).
:::

:::def Republic
A state without a monarch, in which power is exercised by representatives chosen by or for the people, such as an elected president and assembly (France from 1792; the USA).
:::

:::def Democracy
Rule by the people, usually through elected representatives; a **liberal democracy** adds protected rights and the rule of law. How many people could vote differed hugely across the period: women could not in most countries until the twentieth century.
:::

:::def Dictatorship
A government by one person or one party that holds unlimited power, usually without free elections, and rules by force and propaganda.
:::

:::def Constitution
The set of basic rules, written or unwritten, that says how a state is governed and what rights citizens have.
:::

:::def Sovereignty
The supreme authority in a state: the question of who has the final say. In 1789 France's sovereignty passed in theory from the king to the nation.
:::

:::def Federalism
A system in which power is divided between a central government and regional governments (in the USA, between Washington and the states). Disputes over states' rights and federal power run through the American option.
:::

Learn each term with a **real example** (a country, a date, a leader), because that is what the exam rewards. "Fascism" without an example is a label; "fascism, as in Mussolini's Italy from 1922" is knowledge.

## Worked examples collected: three skills in the exam

You have met the moves one at a time. Here are three short exam-style exercises showing how they combine.

:::eg Build a causation diagram
Question: Draw a diagram of the causes of the Russian Revolution of 1905, labelling each cause as long-term, short-term or trigger, and add one link between two causes.

*Answer.* Using the figure above:
- **Long-term:** autocracy (the Tsar held all power, there was no elected parliament); peasant land hunger after the emancipation of 1861; fast industrialisation that created a discontented working class in the cities.
- **Short-term:** the Russo-Japanese War from February 1904, in which Russia suffered defeats, including the surrender of Port Arthur at the start of January 1905 (New Style); an economic downturn at the start of the century; and the sense, spread by liberal reformers in 1904, that change was needed.
- **Trigger:** Bloody Sunday, 9 January 1905 (Old Style; 22 January New Style), when troops fired on a peaceful workers' march to the Winter Palace led by the priest Father Gapon.
- **A link:** the war made the long-term weakness of the autocracy visible, because military defeat showed that the Tsar's government could not deliver; the trigger then turned discontent into mass action.

*Lesson.* A causation diagram has three layers and at least one **link** between them. The link is the analysis.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Why did the 1905 Revolution break out?" [10]

A student wrote:

"The 1905 Revolution broke out in January 1905. Russia was at war with Japan and losing. The workers marched to the Winter Palace with a petition led by Father Gapon. The soldiers shot at them and many people were killed. The workers were very angry and there were strikes all over Russia. In October there was a general strike and the Tsar issued the October Manifesto."

*Assessment.* This reaches the bottom of Level 2 (3–5 marks). It is accurate and relevant, but it is a narrative: it describes what happened in order without explaining why it caused the revolution. The words "very angry" are an assertion, not an explanation. There is nothing before January 1905, so no long-term causes, and the paragraph does not choose between causes.

*Fix.* Begin with a point that answers "why": "The revolution broke out because long-standing discontent with autocracy was turned into mass action by military defeat and by the shooting of peaceful marchers." Add evidence for each part (the lack of any elected parliament; the loss of Port Arthur; Bloody Sunday on 9 January 1905), explain how each led to the next, and conclude which was most important. That one change moves the answer into Level 3 or 4.
:::

## Practice

:::try Recall
Q: What is the difference between a primary and a secondary source? Give an example of each from the French Revolution.
A: A primary source comes from the time studied, such as the Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) or a 1789 cartoon. A secondary source was made later by someone who was not there, using other sources, such as a history book written in 1980.
Q: List the four things you ask about a source's provenance, and add the fifth step.
A: Nature, origin, purpose, context (and then content read against the question).
Q: Why is "this source is biased" a weak comment?
A: It does not say whose view the source expresses, what it is evidence of, or what it leaves out. A biased source can be excellent evidence of what its author wanted people to believe.
Q: Classify as fact, opinion or interpretation: "The Estates-General met on 5 May 1789." "Louis XVI was weak." "The Revolution was above all caused by the financial crisis."
A: Fact; opinion (a judgement); interpretation.
Q: What is the difference between a long-term cause, a short-term cause and a trigger?
A: A long-term cause is a condition built up over decades. A short-term cause is a development in the years or months before. A trigger is the event that sets the outcome off at that moment.
Q: What does "necessary but not sufficient" mean?
A: Without it the event could not have happened, but it was not enough on its own.
Q: In which century is 1914? What is the New Style date for 25 October 1917 (Old Style)?
A: The twentieth century (1901–2000). 7 November 1917.
Q: What does the E in PEEL (the second one) stand for, and why does it matter most?
A: Explain: it says how the evidence proves the point. Without it a paragraph is only a list of facts.
:::

:::try Exam-style practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [4 marks] ★ A student says: "Source A is a speech by a politician, so it is biased and useless." Give two reasons why this comment would not earn high credit, and say what the student should write instead.
A: Indicative content: (1) "biased" is a label that does not say whose view it is or what it shows; (2) a biased source can still be good evidence, for example of what the politician wanted the public to believe or of how a policy was defended; (3) "useless" ignores the question: a source is useful or not for a particular enquiry. A better answer says who made the speech, when and why, uses its content, and explains what it shows (and cannot show) about the question. A top answer ends with a judgement of how strong the evidence is.
Q: [6 marks] ★★ Read this extract and answer the question. "The people of Paris stormed the Bastille because it was a hated symbol of royal tyranny." (an unsupported statement written for practice). Is this a fact, an opinion or an interpretation? Explain, and give one piece of evidence that complicates it.
A: It combines a fact (a crowd stormed the Bastille on 14 July 1789) with an interpretation of why (hatred of the fortress as a symbol of tyranny). The interpretation can be challenged: only seven prisoners were inside and the crowd went there for gunpowder after taking muskets at the Invalides; the fortress's symbolism was added in the telling. A good answer says the whole sentence is an interpretation, supports its judgement with at least one piece of evidence and notes that evidence can be read more than one way.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Russia lost the war with Japan, 1904–05, in no more than three causes, linking them. Facts you may use: Russia's only rail link to the Far East was the single-track Trans-Siberian Railway, thousands of miles from Moscow; Russia's Baltic fleet sailed round the world and was destroyed by Japan at Tsushima in May 1905; Japan had a modernised army and navy. (This question practises the structure, not a topic; you can try it again after [Russia (2)](#hist.eu.russia.2).)
A: Indicative content: any three linked causes with evidence, for example the distance from the centre of Russia and the single Trans-Siberian line for supplies, the destruction of the Baltic fleet at Tsushima in May 1905 after a voyage of about seven months (and the loss of the Far East fleet at Port Arthur), and Japan's modernised army and navy. Top answer: causes are connected (the supply problem made the Russian fleet's late arrival worse) and the answer ends with a reasoned view about which mattered most. A list of events with no linking words stays in Level 2.
Q: [12 marks] ★★★ "The Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789) shows what the Revolution was about." Use your knowledge of provenance to explain what the Declaration is good evidence of, and what it is not good evidence of.
H: Think about nature (an official statement of principle), origin (the National Assembly, August 1789), and purpose.
H: What does a statement of principles tell you about what people did?
H: Who is not represented in it?
A: Indicative content: nature, an official statement of principle, adopted 26 August 1789; origin, deputies of the National Assembly, mostly lawyers and other educated men, influenced by Enlightenment writers and the American declarations; purpose, to set out the principles of the new constitution and to justify the break with the Ancien Régime. It is good evidence of the aims of the deputies: Article 1 ("Men are born and remain free and equal in rights") and the sovereignty of the nation. It is weak evidence of what peasants wanted (dues and bread), or of what happened (the August Decrees left most dues to be redeemed) and it says nothing about women or slaves. A Level 5-style answer weighs both and judges that it is strong evidence of the Assembly's aims and weak evidence of the Revolution as a whole.
Q: [8 marks] ★★★ Convert 25 October 1917 (Old Style) to New Style, and explain in two sentences why a student must state which calendar a Russian date uses.
H: The gap was 13 days.
H: Think about a date given in a British book of 1917 and in a Soviet book of 1930.
A: 7 November 1917 (New Style). Russia used the Julian calendar until February 1918, 13 days behind the Gregorian calendar used elsewhere; a Russian date is therefore not the same day as the same-numbered date in a Western source, and failing to say which is used makes events seem to fall in the wrong month (the "October" Revolution happened in November New Style).
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ "Primary sources are always more useful than secondary sources." How far do you agree with this view? Use examples from the sources and historians named in this chapter.
H: Think about what a primary source is good evidence of, and what a secondary source can do that a primary source cannot.
H: Use the Lefebvre example, and the diary entry for 14 July 1789.
A: Indicative content. For the claim: primary sources are made at the time, show what people said and believed, and cannot be influenced by later events (for example, Lincoln's 1861 speech shows what he promised). Against: primary sources are partial, written for a purpose and by people who did not see the whole (the "rien" of Louis XVI's diary can mislead if read as political comment); secondary sources can combine thousands of primary sources, have hindsight and can test one source against another; Lefebvre's book is secondary for 1789 and primary for 1930s Marxism. Judgement: neither kind is better in itself; usefulness depends on the question, and the best historical work uses both. A top answer states that criterion and applies it.
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ Draft one PEEL paragraph answering: "Why did Louis XVI call the Estates-General in 1788?" using only these facts: the 1788 budget showed spending of 629 million livres and revenue of 503 million; the Notables (1787) and the Parlement of Paris (1787) refused new taxes; the Estates-General was called on 8 August 1788.
H: Calculate the deficit.
H: Say why refusal by the Notables and the Parlement made the Estates-General the only route.

Link, "Bankruptcy therefore forced the decision, but the question of how the Estates-General would vote created the next crisis."

:::recap
- **History** is an argument about the past made from incomplete evidence, and the exam rewards a weighed argument, not a list of facts.
- **Primary** sources are from the time; **secondary** sources are later. Neither is automatically reliable. Ask "evidence of what?"
- **Provenance**: nature, origin, purpose, context, then read the content against the question. "Biased" is the start of a judgement, not the end.
- **Fact** (checkable), **opinion** (judgement), **interpretation** (an argued explanation).
- **Causes**: long-term, short-term, trigger; **necessary** versus **sufficient**; weigh them by necessity, scale, duration, links, timing and chance.
- **Consequences** (short and long term), **change and continuity** (speed, extent, direction), **significance** (scale, depth, duration, resonance).
- **Chronology**: the n-th century is the years (n−1)00 + 1 to n00; Old Style dates were 12 days behind in the nineteenth century and 13 days behind from 1900 to 1918; 25 October 1917 OS is 7 November NS.
- **Notes**: timeline, key-people table, factor grid. **Reading** a historian: who, argument, evidence, opponent, approach.
- **Writing**: PEEL, with the Explain step doing the work. **Key terms**: liberalism, conservatism, nationalism, socialism, communism, fascism, imperialism, absolute monarchy, autocracy, republic, democracy, dictatorship, constitution, sovereignty, federalism.
