:::why
In 1945 the United States was the strongest nation on earth and had no tradition of peacetime alliances. Within five years it had joined a permanent military alliance in Europe, rebuilt its former enemies, fought a war in Korea and tripled its defence budget. Under Eisenhower it then tried to hold the same line more cheaply, by nuclear threats, covert action and summit meetings. The question of how different the two presidents were asks you to separate what stayed the same (the aim of containing Communism) from what changed (the means, the risks and the places). It is a classic comparison question, and it rewards students who can use both men's records as evidence.

## What "different" means: four tests

To compare two presidents you need a method. This chapter uses four tests, and you can use them in an exam.
1. **Aims:** what did each president want to achieve?
2. **Means:** how did he try to do it (troops, money, nuclear weapons, covert action, diplomacy)?
3. **Places and risks:** where did he act, and how close did he come to war?
4. **Results:** how far did each succeed on his own terms?

The key point is that **the aim stayed the same and the means changed**. Both presidents wanted to contain Soviet and Communist power without fighting a world war. Truman built the structure (alliances, aid, a larger military) and fought one limited war. Eisenhower inherited the structure and tried to run it more cheaply, relying on nuclear deterrence, covert action and talks with the Soviet leaders. The historian **John Lewis Gaddis**, in *Strategies of Containment* (1982), gave a useful framework. He argued that containment took two forms: **asymmetric** (answer a threat selectively, where and how the USA chooses, using its strengths) and **symmetric** (answer every challenge wherever it arises, at the same level). In his account, Truman's policy moved from the first to the second after NSC-68 and Korea, and Eisenhower returned to the first. Use this framework with care, because you must test it against facts, as the essays below do.

## Truman and the policy of containment

:::def Containment
The American policy of preventing the spread of Soviet power and Communism beyond the areas the USSR already controlled, by economic aid, alliances, military strength and diplomatic pressure, without direct war. The word comes from George Kennan's article of 1947.
:::

### Truman's concerns about Soviet expansion

Harry Truman became president on 12 April 1945, when Franklin Roosevelt died, with little experience of foreign affairs. In his first months he was advised that Stalin was breaking the promises made at Yalta about free elections in Eastern Europe. On 23 April 1945 he spoke sharply to the Soviet foreign minister, Vyacheslav Molotov, about Poland. Over the next two years his worries grew for five reasons.
- **Eastern Europe.** The Red Army controlled Poland, Romania, Bulgaria and Hungary, and Soviet-backed governments were being installed.
- **Iran and Turkey, 1946.** Soviet troops stayed in northern Iran after the agreed withdrawal date of 2 March 1946, and left only in May under American and United Nations pressure. In August 1946 the USSR asked Turkey to share control of the Straits.
- **Ideas and information.** On 22 February 1946 George Kennan, the American chargé d'affaires in Moscow, sent the 8,000-word "Long Telegram" to Washington. He argued that Soviet leaders saw the outside world as hostile because of their own insecurity and ideology, and that Soviet power would respond to firmness but not to concessions. On 5 March 1946 Winston Churchill, in Fulton, Missouri, spoke of an "iron curtain" across Europe.
- **Weakness in Western Europe.** See below.
- **Atomic weapons.** The USA tested the bomb on 16 July 1945 and used it on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August, but its monopoly ended when the USSR tested its own on 29 August 1949.

Historians disagree about whether these fears were justified (that debate is the heart of the Paper 3 topic), but for this chapter the point is that Truman and his advisers *believed* that the Soviet Union was a threat that had to be contained. Melvyn Leffler, in *A Preponderance of Power* (1992), argued that American leaders were driven less by ideology than by a fear that the Soviet Union might take control of the industrial regions of Europe and Asia, and so shift the balance of power in the world.

### The Potsdam Conference, 17 July to 2 August 1945

Potsdam, near Berlin, was Truman's first meeting with Stalin. On 16 July, the day before it opened, he learned that the first atomic bomb had been tested successfully in New Mexico, and on 24 July he told Stalin only that the USA had a "new weapon of unusual destructive force". Winston Churchill attended until the British election result brought in Clement Attlee, who became Prime Minister on 26 July and took Churchill's place at the table.

The conference agreed that:
- Germany would be disarmed and denazified, and run by an Allied Control Council;
- it would be divided into four **zones of occupation** (American, British, French and Soviet), and Berlin, although deep inside the Soviet zone, into four sectors;
- each power would take **reparations** mainly from its own zone, with some extra goods for the USSR from the western zones;
- Poland's western frontier would follow the Oder and Neisse rivers, provisionally until a final peace treaty;
- a Council of Foreign Ministers would prepare the peace treaties.

The importance of Potsdam for this chapter is what it left open. The Allies treated Germany as one economic unit in theory, but the reparations arrangement let each zone go its own way in practice, and no peace treaty was ever agreed. Relations were already cooler than at Yalta: Truman was blunter than Roosevelt, the atomic bomb changed the balance of confidence, and the Soviet-controlled governments in Eastern Europe were already a quarrel.

### The state of post-war Western Europe

Europe in 1945–47 was devastated: cities in ruins, railways and bridges destroyed, farms short of fertiliser and machines. The winter of 1946–47 was one of the worst on record, and coal, food and transport broke down in Britain and Germany. European countries needed American goods but could not pay for them, a "dollar gap". In France and Italy the Communist parties were among the largest, and Americans feared that hunger and unemployment would carry them to power. Western Europe was therefore both a **market** the American economy needed and a **front line** in the new struggle. Truman's advisers concluded that Europe would be lost to Communism unless the USA acted.

### Greece and the Truman Doctrine

Greece was in civil war from 1946, between the government and a Communist-led army. Britain had supported the Greek government since 1944, but on 21 February 1947 it told the USA that it could no longer afford to give aid to Greece and Turkey. Truman and his Under-Secretary of State, **Dean Acheson**, decided that the United States must take over. Acheson later recalled that the Republican Senator Arthur Vandenberg advised Truman to put the case to Congress in the starkest terms, and on 12 March 1947 Truman asked Congress for 400 million dollars of aid (about 300 million for Greece and 100 million for Turkey).

:::note Evidence: the Truman Doctrine
"I believe that it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures."

*From President Truman's address to a joint session of Congress, 12 March 1947 (Avalon Project, Yale Law School).*
:::

The speech was far wider than the problem. It presented the world as a choice between two ways of life, and it committed the USA, in principle, to help any people resisting Communism. The aid was approved in May 1947. The Greek government, helped by American money and advisers, won the civil war in 1949, aided by the quarrel between Stalin and the Yugoslav leader Tito in 1948, which cut the rebels' supplies. The Truman Doctrine is therefore the first great statement of containment in practice, and the model for what later presidents would do. It also began the habit of treating very different local conflicts as parts of one global struggle, which would cost the USA dearly in Vietnam.

:::def Truman Doctrine
President Truman's declaration of 12 March 1947 that the USA would support free peoples resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressure. It was made to win 400 million dollars of aid for Greece and Turkey, and it committed the USA to opposing Communist expansion in the world.
:::

### The Marshall Plan

Aid to Greece and Turkey was a small sum. Western Europe needed much more. On 5 June 1947, at Harvard, the Secretary of State **George Marshall** proposed that the USA help Europe to rebuild if the European countries drew up a joint plan. The offer was open to all Europe. Britain and France called a conference in Paris, but Molotov left on 2 July, and Stalin forced Poland and Czechoslovakia to refuse the offer. Sixteen countries drew up a plan, and Congress passed the **Economic Cooperation Act** on 3 April 1948, soon after the Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February, which helped to win votes. Over four years (1948–52) the USA gave about 13 billion dollars (the Office of the Historian says "over $12 billion", and other sources give 13.3 billion, depending on what is counted), mostly as grants, to sixteen countries and West Germany, administered by the Economic Cooperation Administration under Paul Hoffman.

The plan mattered in four ways.
- **Economic recovery:** industrial production in Western Europe was well above pre-war levels by 1951, though other factors (the end of the winter shortages, the countries' own efforts) also contributed.
- **Containment by prosperity:** it was meant to deprive the Communist parties of the poverty on which they fed, and the Communists lost ground in France and Italy.
- **A divided Europe:** the USSR set up the Cominform (September 1947) and a rival Molotov Plan and Comecon (January 1949), and Eastern Europe was tied more tightly to Moscow.
- **A new relationship:** it bound Western Europe, and the USA, into closer economic and political cooperation, and led to the creation of the Organisation for European Economic Cooperation in 1948.

Kennan, who headed the State Department's Policy Planning Staff, described the idea of containment in an article signed "X" in *Foreign Affairs* in July 1947.

:::note Evidence: Kennan on containment
"…a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies…"

*From George Kennan ("X"), "The Sources of Soviet Conduct", Foreign Affairs, July 1947. Shortened; the phrases are the document's own. Kennan later said that he had meant mainly a political and economic containment, not a military one.*
:::

### From economic to military containment: NATO and NSC-68

The coup in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 and the Berlin Blockade (below) persuaded Western governments that a military alliance was needed. On 4 April 1949 **twelve** countries signed the North Atlantic Treaty in Washington: the USA, Canada, Britain, France, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Norway, Denmark, Iceland and Portugal. It was the first peacetime military alliance the USA had joined outside the Americas since 1778. Its key article said that an attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all.

In 1949 two shocks followed: the Soviet atomic bomb on 29 August and the Communist victory in China (the People's Republic was proclaimed on 1 October). In January 1950 Truman approved work on a hydrogen bomb. In April 1950 a top-secret study, **NSC-68**, drafted under Paul Nitze, argued that the Soviet Union was driven to expand and that the USA needed a large rearmament, in both nuclear and conventional forces. Truman did not accept its cost until the Korean War broke out in June; he approved it in September 1950. Defence spending then rose from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to over 50 billion in 1953.

:::key Judging Truman's containment in Europe
**Successes:** Greece saved (1949); Western Europe's recovery under Marshall Aid; the Communist parties weakened in France and Italy; NATO (1949); Berlin held (below); the creation of a stable West Germany.
**Costs and limits:** it hardened the division of Europe; the Soviet bloc tightened its control; the USA took on global commitments; the rearmament of NSC-68 tied the American economy to defence spending.
**Judgement to defend:** Truman's policy in Europe was **a success in its own terms**, because Western Europe was held and rebuilt, but it **confirmed the division of the continent**.
:::

## The issue of Germany

### The post-war division and the tension between the victors

Germany was the centre of the quarrel. The Allies had agreed to treat Germany as an economic unit, but they disagreed about how. The USSR wanted heavy reparations and a weak Germany; the USA and Britain, as they saw the cost of feeding their own zones, wanted a revived German economy. In May 1946 the American commander, General Lucius Clay, halted reparations deliveries from the US zone to the USSR. On 6 September 1946 in Stuttgart the American Secretary of State, James Byrnes, promised that the USA would stay in Germany and help it to recover. On 1 January 1947 the American and British zones were merged as "Bizonia", and later in 1948 the French zone was added to form a "Trizone". Conferences of the foreign ministers in Moscow (1947) and London (1947) failed to agree a joint policy, and in 1948 the western powers prepared a West German state.

### The Berlin Blockade, 1948–49: causes

Berlin was a vulnerable point: a city about 100 miles inside the Soviet zone, divided into four sectors, with the three western sectors reached by road, rail and canal across Soviet-held land. The immediate **causes** of the Blockade were:
- **The western plan for a West German state**, agreed in London in 1948, which Stalin saw as the end of any hope of controlling Germany or of reparations;
- **The currency reform:** on 20 June 1948 the western powers introduced a new currency, the Deutschmark, in their zones. On 23 June the Soviets issued their own currency and tried to extend it to all of Berlin;
- **Stalin's wish to force the West out of Berlin**, or at least to halt the creation of a West German state, and to remove what he saw as a Western base inside his zone.

### Course

On 24 June 1948 the Soviet authorities stopped all road, rail and barge traffic to West Berlin and cut electricity from the east. About two million people lived in the western sectors, with food and coal for a few weeks. The Western options were to withdraw, to force a convoy through, or to supply the city by air. Clay and Truman rejected withdrawal, and rejected a convoy as too likely to start a war. The **Berlin Airlift** began on 26 June 1948 with the first flights of transport aircraft; American and British planes flew in food, coal and other supplies, landing at Tempelhof and other airports at intervals of minutes. By spring 1949 the airlift was carrying more than the city needed, and on one day in April 1949 over 1,300 flights landed in 24 hours. Between 26 June 1948 and 30 September 1949 it delivered more than 2.3 million tons of cargo, about three-quarters of it in American aircraft. The Americans also moved B-29 bombers to Britain as a signal, though the aircraft were not armed with atomic bombs. Stalin did not shoot at the planes, because that would have meant war. On 12 May 1949, after secret talks, the USSR lifted the blockade.

### Consequences

- **A Western victory, and a Soviet defeat.** The Blockade failed to expel the West, and West Berlin survived and became a symbol of freedom.
- **NATO.** The crisis led directly to the signing of the North Atlantic Treaty on 4 April 1949.
- **A permanent American commitment to Europe.** Troops stayed in Germany; the USA had shown that it would defend its position.
- **Two Germanies.** The Blockade completed the split.

### The creation of two Germanies

On 23 May 1949 the western powers' Basic Law created the **Federal Republic of Germany** (West Germany), with its capital in Bonn; the first elections in August brought Konrad Adenauer to power as Chancellor in September. On 7 October 1949 the Soviet zone became the **German Democratic Republic** (East Germany). Each claimed to speak for all Germany. In 1952 Stalin offered a unified, neutral Germany (the note of 10 March 1952), and historians still debate whether it was a serious offer; the West rejected it. West Germany's place in the Western camp was confirmed in 1955 when it joined NATO (9 May); the USSR responded with the Warsaw Pact on 14 May 1955.

### The Berlin Crisis, 1958–59

The division of Germany left West Berlin as an irritant for the Soviet Union: a showcase of Western prosperity inside East Germany, through which well over two million East Germans had left for the West by the end of the 1950s, many of them skilled workers. Khrushchev is often quoted as calling Berlin a bone in his throat. He was also alarmed by the prospect of West German armed forces having nuclear weapons. On 27 November 1958 he sent a note to the Western powers demanding that they leave Berlin within six months and that it become a demilitarised "free city". Otherwise the USSR would sign a separate peace treaty with East Germany and give it control of the access routes.

Eisenhower and Dulles refused to be pushed (Dulles was ill and resigned in April 1959; he died on 24 May). The Western position was that the USA would stay in Berlin and would not negotiate under a deadline, but it was willing to talk. A conference of foreign ministers met in Geneva from May to August 1959, and agreed nothing, but the ultimatum quietly lapsed. Khrushchev then visited the United States for twelve days from 15 September 1959 and talked with Eisenhower at **Camp David**, where he agreed to drop the deadline, in exchange for a summit meeting. The crisis was therefore left unresolved: the summit planned for Paris in May 1960 was wrecked by the U-2 affair (below), and the problem returned under Kennedy and led to the Berlin Wall in August 1961.

:::key Judging American policy on Germany
**Truman:** firm and successful in 1948–49: the airlift, NATO, the creation of the Federal Republic; but it hardened the division.
**Eisenhower:** held the line without concessions, took the Soviet ultimatum calmly and replaced confrontation with talks (Geneva, Camp David); but he did not solve the problem and the question returned in 1961.
**Judgement to defend:** the two presidents kept **the same policy on Germany** (West Germany in the Western system, the defence of West Berlin, no recognition of East Germany), and differed only in style: **confrontation and airlift under Truman, patience and negotiation under Eisenhower**.
:::

## The significance of US involvement in Korea

### The division of Korea and US policy towards South Korea

Korea had been a Japanese colony since 1910. In August 1945, as Soviet troops entered the north, two young American officers, including Dean Rusk, were told to find a line that would keep the capital, Seoul, in the American zone: they chose the **38th parallel**. The USSR accepted it, and Soviet troops occupied the north and American troops the south. The two powers could not agree on how to hold elections for a united Korea. In the south, elections under United Nations supervision created the **Republic of Korea** on 15 August 1948 under the anti-Communist Syngman Rhee; in the north the Soviets installed **Kim Il Sung**, and the **Democratic People's Republic of Korea** was proclaimed on 9 September 1948. Soviet troops left the north at the end of 1948, and American combat troops left the south in June 1949, leaving a small advisory group.

American policy towards South Korea was cautious. The USA gave it economic aid and some arms, but not the tanks, aircraft and heavy guns that Rhee wanted, partly because it feared he would use them to attack the north. The Joint Chiefs of Staff saw Korea as of little strategic value. On 12 January 1950, in a speech at the National Press Club, Dean Acheson, now Secretary of State, described the American defence perimeter in the Pacific as running along the Aleutians, Japan, the Ryukyus and the Philippines. He did not mention South Korea or Taiwan, which would have to rely on their own resources and on the United Nations. Critics later said that this invited an attack. Records opened in Russia in the 1990s show that Stalin approved Kim Il Sung's plan in the spring of 1950, subject to Mao's agreement, and that Soviet and Chinese decisions mattered more than any American speech, though the perimeter speech may have encouraged him to think the risk was low.

### The invasion and the US reaction

On 25 June 1950 (24 June in Washington) the North Korean army attacked across the parallel with tanks and artillery, took Seoul in three days and drove the South Korean army back. Truman, who was at home in Missouri, returned to Washington and, in meetings at Blair House on 25–26 June, decided to act. He saw the invasion as a test of containment and drew a lesson from the 1930s: that appeasement of aggressors led to wider war. The decisions followed quickly.
- **The United Nations.** The Security Council, which the USSR was boycotting over the question of China's seat, passed Resolution 82 on 25 June, condemning the attack, and Resolution 83 on 27 June, recommending that members help South Korea.
- **American forces.** On 27 June Truman ordered air and naval support to South Korea and sent the Seventh Fleet into the Taiwan Strait to prevent a Communist attack on Taiwan (and any Nationalist attack on the mainland). On 30 June he authorised American ground troops. General Douglas MacArthur, in Tokyo, was named commander of the UN forces.
- **No declaration of war.** Truman called it a "police action", and did not ask Congress for a declaration of war. This avoided a debate at the time, but it gave his critics a line of attack later.

Sixteen UN members eventually sent fighting forces, but the USA supplied most of the troops, aircraft and ships; South Korean forces were also large. The first American troops were beaten back, and by August the UN forces held only the **Pusan Perimeter** in the south-east corner.

### The decision to invade North Korea

On 15 September 1950 MacArthur's landing at **Inchon**, behind the North Korean lines, was a brilliant success: Seoul was retaken by the end of the month, and the North Korean army collapsed. The question was then whether to stop at the 38th parallel, which restored the situation of June, or to cross and unify Korea by force. Truman's advisers, MacArthur and the UN General Assembly (by a resolution of 7 October 1950) favoured crossing. The aim of containment had changed to **rollback**, the liberation of territory held by Communists. The reasons were the chance to end the threat at its source, the political pressure on Truman from Republicans, who were attacking his policy over the "loss" of China, the belief that China would not intervene, and the momentum of a victory. South Korean forces crossed on 1 October, and US and UN troops followed from 7 October. Truman met MacArthur on Wake Island on 15 October; MacArthur assured him that Chinese intervention was unlikely, and that if it came, it would be met with great slaughter. China had in fact warned through India on 3 October that it would enter the war if American forces crossed the parallel. Pyongyang was taken on 19 October, and American troops approached the Yalu River, the border with China.

### The Chinese intervention and the limited war

Chinese "volunteers" had crossed the Yalu in secret from 19 October, and on 25 November 1950 they attacked in force. The UN forces were driven back in the longest retreat in American military history, including from the Chosin Reservoir, and Seoul fell again on 4 January 1951 (it was to change hands four times in the war). On 30 November Truman said at a press conference that the use of the atomic bomb had always been under active consideration, which alarmed the allies, and the British prime minister, Clement Attlee, flew to Washington to urge restraint. In spring 1951 the lines settled near the parallel.

**MacArthur and Truman.** MacArthur wanted to bomb China, blockade its coast and use Nationalist troops from Taiwan. Truman and his Joint Chiefs wanted to keep the war limited, in case it brought in the Soviet Union and became a world war. When MacArthur criticised the policy publicly, and a letter of his was read in Congress, Truman **dismissed him on 11 April 1951**. MacArthur returned to a hero's welcome and addressed Congress, but the Joint Chiefs backed Truman, and General Omar Bradley told a Senate committee that a wider war would be "the wrong war, at the wrong place, at the wrong time, and with the wrong enemy". The principle that the president controls the military was upheld, but Truman's popularity fell sharply.

**The end of the war.** Truce talks began in July 1951 and dragged on for two years, mainly over the exchange of prisoners. The war was a stalemate with heavy losses. About 36,500 American service members died in the theatre of war, and many more Koreans and Chinese. An **armistice** was signed at Panmunjom on **27 July 1953**, under Eisenhower, leaving a divided Korea and no peace treaty.

### The impact of the Korean War on US policy in Asia

The war changed American policy in Asia and in the world.
- **Rearmament.** NSC-68 was put into effect. Defence spending quadrupled in three years, and the USA rebuilt its forces and stationed more troops in Europe.
- **Taiwan and China.** The Seventh Fleet's move protected the Nationalist government on Taiwan, which the USA had been ready to abandon. The USA refused to recognise the People's Republic and treated China as an enemy for twenty years.
- **Japan.** The USA turned Japan from a conquered country into an ally. The San Francisco Peace Treaty of 8 September 1951 ended the occupation (in force from April 1952), and a security treaty let American forces stay. Japan's economy gained from American orders during the war.
- **A ring of alliances.** The USA signed a defence treaty with the Philippines (30 August 1951), the ANZUS pact with Australia and New Zealand (1 September 1951), and, under Eisenhower, with South Korea (1953), SEATO (1954) and Taiwan (1954).
- **Indochina.** The USA had begun to help France in Vietnam in 1950; after Korea, it saw the war there as part of the same struggle, and its aid grew until it paid most of the cost of the French war.
- **A "limited war" with a political price.** Truman's war was unpopular, as the stalemate dragged on, and helped Eisenhower, who promised in the 1952 campaign to go to Korea, to win.

Historians differ about the war's origins. **Bruce Cumings**, in *The Origins of the Korean War* (1981 and 1990), argued that the conflict was in large part a civil war inside Korea, with roots in the divisions of the 1940s, and that the American role in creating the division and backing Rhee made the war more likely. Other historians stress Stalin's approval and Kim's plan as the immediate cause.

:::key Judging the importance of Korea
**Significance for US policy:** it turned containment from a mainly political and economic policy into a military one (NSC-68 put into effect); it led to a rearmed NATO; it tied the USA to Taiwan, Japan and South-East Asia; it began the American presence in Asia that led to Vietnam.
**Limits:** the war ended where it began; MacArthur's rollback and its disaster showed the limits of American power; Truman was damaged politically.
**Judgement to defend:** Korea was **the most significant event of Truman's foreign policy** because it militarised and globalised containment.
:::

## Eisenhower's foreign policy: a change in direction?

### Aims and methods: the New Look

Dwight Eisenhower, the supreme commander in Europe in 1944–45, was elected in November 1952 after criticising Truman's policy. The Republican platform, written with the help of his future Secretary of State **John Foster Dulles**, called containment "negative, futile and immoral" and promised the "liberation" of Eastern Europe. In office, Eisenhower and Dulles were more cautious. They shared three beliefs: that the Cold War would be long, so the USA must not ruin its economy by paying for a huge army (Eisenhower wanted a balanced budget); that the USA's great advantage was its nuclear weapons and its industry; and that a President should avoid committing American troops to wars on the ground, such as Korea.

The result was the **New Look**, approved in NSC 162/2 on 30 October 1953. It had four parts: reliance on nuclear weapons and the Strategic Air Command, which was meant to give, as the saying of the time went, a bigger bang for the buck; a smaller army and navy; a network of alliances, with the allies supplying the ground troops; and covert action by the Central Intelligence Agency (run by Allen Dulles, the Secretary of State's brother) and psychological warfare.

**Massive retaliation.** On 12 January 1954 Dulles explained the policy to the Council on Foreign Relations in New York.

:::note Evidence: Dulles announces massive retaliation
"…depend primarily upon a great capacity to retaliate, instantly, by means and at places of our choosing."

*Adapted from John Foster Dulles, "The Evolution of Foreign Policy", speech of 12 January 1954, printed in the Department of State Bulletin, 25 January 1954. Shortened; the words are the Secretary of State's own.*
:::

In an interview with *Life* magazine on 16 January 1956 Dulles went further, saying that "the ability to get to the verge without getting into the war is the necessary art." Critics called this **brinkmanship**: bringing the USA to the edge of nuclear war in order to frighten an opponent into backing down. The American nuclear stockpile grew from under 1,500 weapons in 1953 to roughly 20,000 by 1960 (Federation of American Scientists estimates). Defence spending, which had reached over 50 billion dollars in 1953, stayed at about 40 to 50 billion dollars a year, a little below the Korean peak.

### Policy in Asia: Korea and Vietnam

(The syllabus heading says "South Asia"; its examples, Korea and Vietnam, are in East and South-East Asia.)

**Korea.** Eisenhower visited Korea as president-elect in December 1952 and, with Stalin dead (5 March 1953), found the Communist side ready to compromise on prisoners. The armistice of 27 July 1953 left Korea divided. Historians debate whether hints that the USA might use nuclear weapons if the war continued played a part. A mutual defence treaty with South Korea followed in October 1953. In effect Eisenhower accepted the stalemate that Truman had reached, and ended the war.

**Vietnam.** The USA had been giving aid to France since 1950, and by 1954 paid most of the cost of the war against Ho Chi Minh's Viet Minh. In March 1954 the Viet Minh surrounded the French garrison at **Dien Bien Phu**. The French asked for American air strikes, and Admiral Arthur Radford, the chairman of the Joint Chiefs, supported them. Eisenhower refused to act alone: he told congressional leaders that he would need their approval and the participation of allies, and Britain would not take part. At a press conference on 7 April 1954 he explained why the fall of Indochina mattered.

:::note Evidence: the domino theory
"You have a row of dominoes set up, you knock over the first one…"

*Adapted from President Eisenhower's news conference, 7 April 1954, Public Papers of the Presidents. Shortened, with the rest of the sentence left out; the words are the President's own.*
:::

Dien Bien Phu fell on 7 May 1954. The Geneva conference on Indochina agreed on 21 July 1954 to divide Vietnam temporarily at the 17th parallel and to hold elections in 1956. The USA did not sign the agreement. Instead Eisenhower built up the south as a separate anti-Communist state. **SEATO**, the South-East Asia Treaty Organization, was set up by the Manila Pact on 8 September 1954, with eight members (the USA, Britain, France, Australia, New Zealand, the Philippines, Thailand and Pakistan) and a protocol covering South Vietnam, Laos and Cambodia. The USA backed **Ngo Dinh Diem** as ruler of the south, took over the training of his army from the French, and supported his refusal to hold the elections of 1956, because Ho Chi Minh was expected to win them. So Eisenhower avoided war in 1954, but he deepened the American commitment: he left Kennedy a South Vietnam that depended on American money and advisers.

**Taiwan.** In 1954–55 and again in 1958 China shelled the offshore islands of Quemoy and Matsu. Eisenhower signed a defence treaty with Taiwan in December 1954, and Congress passed the Formosa Resolution in January 1955 allowing him to defend Taiwan, and left it unclear whether he would defend the islands. Nuclear weapons were discussed, and no war followed. Supporters call this brinkmanship succeeding, and critics call it dangerous.

### Policy towards the USSR

**Defence policy.** The New Look's reliance on nuclear weapons gave the USA a large lead, but the USSR tested its own hydrogen bomb in 1953 and launched the first satellite, Sputnik, on 4 October 1957. Americans feared a "missile gap", and a secret report, the Gaither Report of November 1957, urged heavy spending on defence. Eisenhower resisted: he knew from U-2 flights that the Soviet missile force was much smaller than critics claimed, but he could not reveal the source. He accepted more missiles and the Polaris submarine, while refusing to increase the budget greatly. The unease also led him to give his warning in 1961 about the "military-industrial complex".

**Relations after 1953.** Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 opened a period of change. On 16 April 1953 Eisenhower made his "Chance for Peace" speech.

:::note Evidence: Eisenhower on the cost of arms
"Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed…"

*Adapted from President Eisenhower's address to the American Society of Newspaper Editors, 16 April 1953, "The Chance for Peace". Shortened; the words are the President's own.*
:::

**Peaceful coexistence.** After a struggle for power, Nikita Khrushchev became the dominant Soviet leader, and in February 1956 he spoke of "peaceful coexistence", the idea that the two systems could compete without war. The **Geneva Summit** (18–23 July 1955) was the first meeting of the leaders of the four wartime powers since Potsdam: Eisenhower, the Soviet premier Nikolai Bulganin (with Khrushchev in the background), and the British and French leaders. It agreed nothing on Germany or arms control. Eisenhower proposed **Open Skies**, mutual aerial inspection, which the USSR rejected, but the talks produced a "spirit of Geneva". The Austrian State Treaty of May 1955, which ended the occupation of Austria and made it neutral, showed that agreement was possible.

**Hungary.** On 23 October 1956 a rising began in Budapest against Soviet control, and the reformist communist Imre Nagy formed a government that promised to leave the Warsaw Pact. On 4 November Soviet tanks crushed it. The USA, whose Radio Free Europe broadcasts had encouraged hope, did nothing military. Eisenhower believed that intervention would risk war with the USSR and had no way to reach Hungary, and the Suez Crisis was dividing the West at the same moment. The episode exposed the gap between the rhetoric of "liberation" and the reality of containment: Eisenhower accepted Soviet control of Eastern Europe, as Truman had.

**Khrushchev's visit, 1959.** In September 1959 Khrushchev toured the USA and met Eisenhower at Camp David (the "spirit of Camp David"). Eisenhower was to visit Moscow, and a summit was arranged for Paris.

**The U-2 affair.** The CIA had flown high-altitude U-2 spy planes over the USSR since 1956. On 1 May 1960 a U-2 flown by Francis Gary Powers was shot down near Sverdlovsk. Washington said it was a weather plane that had strayed, but Khrushchev, who had kept silent about the pilot, produced Powers and the wreckage. On 11 May Eisenhower took public responsibility for the flights. At the Paris summit on 16 May Khrushchev demanded an apology and the end of the flights, and when Eisenhower refused, walked out; the visit to Moscow was cancelled. The affair ended the period of improving relations. Eisenhower's critics say that he took an unnecessary risk before a summit; his defenders say that the flights gave the USA vital knowledge of Soviet strength.

### Policy towards the Middle East

**Iran, 1953.** In 1951 Iran's prime minister, **Mohammad Mossadegh**, nationalised the British-owned Anglo-Iranian Oil Company. Britain organised a boycott and asked the USA for help. Truman had refused, but Eisenhower and the Dulles brothers agreed, fearing that Iran might turn to the Soviet Union. In August 1953 a CIA and British Secret Intelligence Service operation (called Ajax by the Americans) helped Iranian army officers to overthrow Mossadegh and bring back the Shah, **Mohammad Reza Pahlavi**. A new international oil consortium gave American companies 40 per cent of Iran's oil. The coup was cheap and succeeded for twenty-five years, but it left a legacy of anti-American feeling that surfaced in the revolution of 1979. The CIA officially acknowledged its role in 2013.

**Suez, 1956.** On 26 July 1956 Egypt's president **Gamal Abdel Nasser** nationalised the Suez Canal Company, a week after the USA withdrew its offer to help pay for the Aswan High Dam. Britain and France, in secret agreement with Israel (the Sèvres protocol), planned to take back the canal. Israel invaded Sinai on 29 October; Britain and France sent an ultimatum and bombed Egypt, landing troops on 5–6 November. Eisenhower, who had not been told, was furious. He opposed the use of force against a country whose cause was seen as anti-colonial, feared that it would push Arab states towards the USSR, wished to condemn Soviet action in Hungary from a position of principle, and was about to face an election (6 November). The USA backed a UN ceasefire (6 November) and refused to support the pound until Britain and France withdrew. **Consequences:** Anglo-French influence in the Middle East collapsed; Nasser's prestige soared; the USSR gained a foothold in Egypt.

**The Eisenhower Doctrine.** On 5 January 1957 Eisenhower asked Congress for authority to use force, if asked, to help any Middle Eastern country threatened by "armed aggression from any country controlled by international communism", with economic aid. Congress agreed in March. It was a Truman Doctrine for the Middle East, and in July 1958, after a revolution in Iraq, 14,000 American troops landed in Lebanon at the request of its president. The Baghdad Pact of 1955 (Britain, Turkey, Iraq, Iran and Pakistan, with the USA as an associate) was an effort to build a barrier along the Soviet border.

### Policy towards Latin America

**Guatemala, 1954.** President **Jacobo Árbenz**, elected in 1950, took over uncultivated land under an agrarian reform law of June 1952, including land of the American-owned United Fruit Company. Washington saw Árbenz as under Communist influence, especially after a shipment of Czech arms in May 1954, and some officials had links with the company. The CIA's covert operation trained and armed a small force under Colonel Carlos Castillo Armas, which invaded on 18 June 1954, backed by radio propaganda and air attacks; Árbenz resigned on 27 June. The operation succeeded cheaply, but it installed a military government and began decades of violence, and it taught Latin Americans that the USA would remove governments it disliked.

**Cuba, 1959–60.** On 1 January 1959 Fidel Castro's guerrillas took power after the flight of the dictator Fulgencio Batista. The USA recognised the new government on 7 January, and Castro visited Washington in April. But when he nationalised land, signed a trade deal with the Soviet Union in February 1960, and seized American property, Eisenhower cut Cuba's sugar quota (July 1960), banned most exports to Cuba (October 1960) and broke off diplomatic relations on 3 January 1961. In March 1960 he had approved a CIA plan to train Cuban exiles to overthrow Castro, which Kennedy carried out in April 1961 (the Bay of Pigs). Eisenhower's handling of Cuba is seen as a failure, because the USA lost an ally ninety miles from Florida and drove Castro towards Moscow.

## How different were the two presidents?

### The case for continuity

- **The same aim.** Both sought to contain Communism, and neither tried to roll it back by force: Truman after the Chinese intervention, Eisenhower in Hungary.
- **The same structure.** NATO, the German settlement, the alliances with Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, the aid programmes and the CIA were Truman's creations (the CIA was set up in 1947), and Eisenhower kept and added to them (West Germany in NATO, SEATO, the Baghdad Pact).
- **Nuclear weapons.** Truman had approved the hydrogen bomb and based the defence of Europe on atomic strength; Eisenhower made nuclear weapons the centre of strategy but did not invent the reliance.
- **Covert action.** Truman had authorised covert operations in 1948, and Eisenhower expanded them.
- **Korea and Vietnam.** Eisenhower ended the Korean War on terms that Truman had been ready to accept, and he inherited Truman's support for the French in Vietnam.
- **Anti-communism at home.** Both presidents fought the Cold War in a country of Red Scare politics (see [Post-war USA (1)](#hist.d.usa45.1)).

### The case for difference

- **Means and cost.** Truman after 1950 spent heavily on conventional as well as nuclear forces. Eisenhower cut costs by relying on nuclear weapons and on covert action. In Gaddis's terms, Truman moved to symmetric response, Eisenhower returned to asymmetric.
- **Use of force.** Truman fought a major war. Eisenhower made no war after 1953, and refused to enter Vietnam alone in 1954 or to fight for Hungary or Suez, though he came close to war over Taiwan.
- **Diplomacy with the USSR.** Truman never held a summit after Potsdam; Eisenhower held three meetings with Soviet leaders (Geneva 1955, Camp David 1959, Paris 1960, though the last failed).
- **The Third World.** Truman's attention was on Europe. Eisenhower's covert overthrow of governments in Iran and Guatemala, and his doctrines for the Middle East, extended the Cold War into Asia, Africa and Latin America in a new way.
- **Rhetoric.** Eisenhower and Dulles condemned containment as passive, and used moral language and "brinkmanship". But in practice they were as cautious as Truman.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **John Lewis Gaddis**, *Strategies of Containment* (1982), distinguishes symmetric and asymmetric containment, and sees Eisenhower's New Look as a return to Kennan's selective approach after Truman's global one.
- **Melvyn Leffler**, *A Preponderance of Power* (1992), argues that the Truman administration's aim was to prevent Soviet control of the industrial centres of Eurasia, which means that many of the policies Gaddis sees as ideological had a strategic logic.
- **Bruce Cumings** (1981, 1990) sees the Korean War as rooted in Korea's own divisions, and criticises the American role.
- **Fred Greenstein**, *The Hidden-Hand Presidency* (1982), argues that Eisenhower was a skilful, active leader who often concealed his role, not the passive golfer his critics portrayed. This affects how you judge the New Look and the covert operations.
Use these to show that you know there is a debate, but your marks come from your own argument backed by evidence.
:::

The most defensible overall verdict is that **the strategic aim and the structure of the Cold War stayed the same, but the means, the style and the places of action changed**. Eisenhower's policy was *continuity in strategy and change in method*, which is why a question that asks whether it was "a continuation" or "a change in direction" always needs a criterion: aims, means, risks or results.

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, so Table B Level 3. But the paragraph lists similarities and differences without a criterion, and the conclusion ("some continuity and some change") judges nothing. Table A Level 2–3.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and compare.** "In aims, Eisenhower's policy was clearly a continuation of Truman's. Both sought to contain Soviet power without general war, and neither tried to push it back by force: Truman halted at the Yalu after the Chinese attack of 25 November 1950, and Eisenhower, who had campaigned on 'liberation', did nothing militarily when the Hungarians rose in October 1956. Where the two presidents differed was in their *means*. Truman, after NSC-68, raised defence spending from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to over 50 billion in 1953, and fought a conventional war in Korea. Eisenhower's New Look (October 1953) relied on nuclear weapons, covert action and allies, to keep spending down."

*Commentary.* Two criteria (aims and means) are used and tested with knowledge, and the paragraph reaches a view on each. This is Level 4. It needs more weighing (risk, results), the evidence that complicates the picture, and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The statement is partly justified. On the aims and the structure of American policy Eisenhower continued Truman's work, but on the means he used, and on the places where he used them, he changed direction. To judge the view I will use four criteria: aims, means, risk and the use of force, and results. The conclusion is that the continuity was in strategy and the change in method, which is why 'a continuation' is a fair description of the first and a poor one of the second.

On aims, the continuity is strong. Truman's policy, announced in the Truman Doctrine on 12 March 1947 and given a theory by Kennan, was to contain Soviet power. Eisenhower's Republican platform of 1952 called containment 'negative, futile and immoral' and promised 'liberation', but when the test came, in Hungary in October and November 1956, the USA did nothing to stop Soviet tanks, because intervention meant war with the USSR. Eisenhower accepted the Soviet sphere as Truman had. The same is true of the structure. NATO (4 April 1949), the Federal Republic of Germany (23 May 1949), the alliances with Japan, the Philippines and Australia and New Zealand in 1951, and the Central Intelligence Agency (1947) were Truman's, and Eisenhower kept them and extended them: West Germany joined NATO in May 1955, SEATO was created on 8 September 1954 and a defence treaty was signed with Taiwan in December 1954. In Korea Eisenhower signed the armistice of 27 July 1953 on terms that left the peninsula divided, close to the settlement that Truman had been ready to accept after the Chinese intervention, and in Vietnam he inherited, and increased, the aid that Truman had begun in 1950.

On the means, the change is real. NSC-68 had led Truman to rearm on all fronts: defence spending rose from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to over 50 billion in 1953, and he fought a three-year conventional war in Korea. Eisenhower, who wanted a balanced budget and feared a 'garrison state', adopted the New Look on 30 October 1953. It relied on the threat of nuclear retaliation, which Dulles described on 12 January 1954 as 'a great capacity to retaliate, instantly, by means and at places of our choosing', on the CIA and on allied troops. This is what Gaddis called a return to an asymmetric strategy, in contrast to the symmetric response to every challenge to which Truman had moved after 1950. It also changed the risk. Eisenhower fought no war after 1953, and refused to send air power alone to Dien Bien Phu in 1954 without Congress and allies, but he also came closer to nuclear confrontation than Truman, in the Taiwan Strait crises of 1954–55 and 1958, and his 'brinkmanship' depended on the willingness to bluff. He also met the Soviet leaders, at Geneva in 1955 and at Camp David in 1959, which Truman never did after Potsdam.

The weakest part of the continuity argument is the Third World. Truman's main concern was Europe, with a war in Korea; Eisenhower extended the Cold War to Iran (August 1953), Guatemala (June 1954), Suez and Lebanon (1956 and 1958) and Cuba, using covert action to overthrow governments and doctrines such as that of 5 January 1957 to commit the USA in the Middle East. These were new in scale and in method, though they applied Truman's logic that Communist influence anywhere was a threat. On results, the picture is mixed on both sides: Truman held Western Europe and failed to win in Korea, and Eisenhower kept the peace and left a bad inheritance in Iran, Cuba and Vietnam.

So Eisenhower's foreign policy was a continuation of Truman's in its aim (containment without general war), its structure (alliances, aid, nuclear deterrence) and its limits (no rollback). It differed in method, cost and, above all, in the range of places where the USA acted. The view is most defensible if 'foreign policy' means strategy, and least if it means the daily practice of policy."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): four explicit criteria; a balanced argument that tests continuity and change; the Third World as the weakest point of the continuity argument; a historian's framework used to analyse, not decorate; the conclusion is qualified and sustained. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates, figures and terms (12 March 1947; 4 April 1949; 14 billion and over 50 billion; 30 October 1953; 12 January 1954; 8 September 1954; 5 January 1957), selected for the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 850 words, 40 minutes.

A: Soviet-backed governments in Eastern Europe; Soviet troops staying in Iran after 2 March 1946 and pressure on Turkey; Kennan's Long Telegram (22 February 1946) and Churchill's "iron curtain" speech (5 March 1946); the weakness of Western Europe; and the atomic bomb.
Q: What did the Potsdam Conference decide about Germany?
A: It was to be disarmed and denazified; divided into four zones (US, British, French, Soviet), with Berlin in four sectors; reparations were to come mainly from each power's own zone, with some extra for the USSR; Poland's western frontier was set provisionally on the Oder-Neisse line; a Council of Foreign Ministers would prepare peace treaties.
Q: Why did Truman announce the Truman Doctrine on 12 March 1947?
A: Britain told the USA on 21 February 1947 that it could no longer support Greece and Turkey. Truman asked Congress for 400 million dollars (about 300 million for Greece and 100 million for Turkey) and said the USA must support free peoples resisting subjugation.
Q: What was the Marshall Plan and how did the USSR react?
A: A programme of American aid proposed on 5 June 1947 and enacted on 3 April 1948: about 13 billion dollars from 1948 to 1952 to sixteen countries and West Germany. Molotov left the Paris conference on 2 July 1947, the Soviet bloc refused, and the USSR created the Cominform (1947) and Comecon (1949).
Q: Give the causes, course and consequences of the Berlin Blockade.
A: Causes: the plan for West Germany and the new currency of 20 June 1948. Course: from 24 June 1948 the USSR cut all land links; the airlift began on 26 June and delivered more than 2.3 million tons by 30 September 1949; the blockade ended on 12 May 1949. Consequences: NATO (4 April 1949), the Federal Republic (23 May 1949) and the German Democratic Republic (7 October 1949), and a lasting American commitment.
Q: What happened in Korea between June 1950 and April 1951?
A: North Korea invaded on 25 June 1950; the UN and the USA responded; Inchon on 15 September turned the war; UN forces crossed the parallel from 7 October; China attacked on 25 November and forced a retreat; Seoul fell on 4 January 1951; MacArthur was dismissed on 11 April 1951.
Q: How did the Korean War change US policy in Asia?
A: NSC-68 was put into effect; the Seventh Fleet protected Taiwan; Japan became an ally (treaty of 8 September 1951); alliances with the Philippines, Australia and New Zealand, then South Korea, SEATO and Taiwan; and rising aid to France in Indochina.
Q: What was the New Look and what is "massive retaliation"?
A: Approved on 30 October 1953 (NSC 162/2): reliance on nuclear weapons and a smaller conventional force, with allies, covert action and psychological warfare. "Massive retaliation" is Dulles's speech of 12 January 1954: the USA would depend on a great capacity to retaliate, instantly, by means and at places of its choosing.
Q: Why did Eisenhower not use American air power at Dien Bien Phu, and what followed?
A: He needed approval from Congress and the participation of allies, and Britain refused. The fortress fell on 7 May 1954; the Geneva Accords of 21 July divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel; SEATO was set up on 8 September 1954 and the USA backed Diem.
Q: What were the U-2 affair and its results?
A: A U-2 spy plane flown by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the USSR on 1 May 1960. Eisenhower admitted responsibility on 11 May. At the Paris summit on 16 May Khrushchev demanded an apology and walked out, and the improving relations of 1959 ended.
Q: What did the USA do in Iran (1953), Guatemala (1954) and Cuba (1959–60)?
A: Iran: a CIA and British operation overthrew Mossadegh (19 August 1953) and restored the Shah. Guatemala: a CIA-backed force under Castillo Armas led Árbenz to resign (27 June 1954). Cuba: relations worsened after Castro's rise on 1 January 1959; Eisenhower cut the sugar quota, banned most exports, broke off relations on 3 January 1961 and approved a CIA plan for an exile force.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the success of the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan in containing Communism in Europe in the years 1947–49.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: did they stop Communist expansion, did they restore economies, what did they cost. Success: Greece saved (civil war won in 1949, helped by the Tito-Stalin split); the Marshall Plan's 13 billion dollars and recovery by 1951; Communist parties weakened in France and Italy; NATO (4 April 1949) followed. Limits: the plan did not stop the coup in Czechoslovakia (February 1948); the Soviet bloc tightened (Cominform, Comecon); Europe was divided; the Soviet threat was partly the product of American actions according to some historians. Level 5 judges, for example, that they succeeded in containing Communism in the West at the price of confirming the division.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the Berlin Blockade was a victory for the United States.
A: Indicative content. Victory: the blockade failed and was lifted on 12 May 1949; the airlift delivered more than 2.3 million tons; West Berlin held; it led to NATO (4 April 1949) and the Federal Republic (23 May 1949); American prestige rose. Not a victory: it completed the division of Germany; hardened the Cold War and the Soviet bloc; Berlin remained vulnerable (1958–61); the USA had risked war. Level 5 defines "victory" (aims, costs, long-term) and judges.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why the USA decided to cross the 38th parallel in 1950.
A: Indicative content. Reasons: the success at Inchon (15 September) and the collapse of the North Korean army; the chance to unify Korea and end the threat; MacArthur's confidence and influence; the UN General Assembly resolution of 7 October; domestic pressure after the "loss" of China; the belief that China would not intervene (despite its warning of 3 October); the shift from containment to rollback. Level 5 ranks the reasons, shows how they linked and judges the decision.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Eisenhower's New Look made the United States safer.' Evaluate this view.
H: Define "safer": from attack, from war, or from the cost of defence.
H: Remember the evidence on both sides: cost, the stockpile, the crises.
A: Indicative content. Support: no war after 1953; the nuclear lead and the stockpile (under 1,500 to about 20,000 weapons, 1953–60); lower defence spending (about 40 to 50 billion dollars) and a balanced economy; deterrence worked in Berlin 1958–59. Challenge: brinkmanship (Taiwan Strait 1954–55 and 1958); the Soviet bomb and Sputnik (4 October 1957) ended American invulnerability; the "missile gap" fear; nuclear weapons were a poor answer to local challenges such as Cuba, Vietnam and Hungary; the Gaither Report; Eisenhower's own warning in 1961. Level 5 judges safety by several criteria and weighs them.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Eisenhower's policy towards Latin America and the Middle East was more aggressive than Truman's.
H: List the covert operations and the doctrines.
H: What did Truman do in Greece, Turkey and Iran?
A: Indicative content. More aggressive: Iran (1953), Guatemala (1954), the plan against Castro (March 1960), the Eisenhower Doctrine (5 January 1957), Lebanon (1958); the CIA as an instrument; Dulles's language. Not more aggressive: Truman's Greece and Turkey, the pressure on Iran in 1946, covert operations from 1948, and the war in Korea; Eisenhower refused force at Suez and at Dien Bien Phu. Level 5 separates method (covert, cheap) from scale, and judges that Eisenhower acted in more places, with less open force.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which relations between the USA and the USSR improved in the years 1953–60.
H: Divide the period into 1953–56, 1957–58 and 1959–60.
H: Decide what counts as improvement: talks, agreements, or lower risk.
A: Indicative content. Improvement: Stalin's death and the Chance for Peace speech (16 April 1953); the Austrian State Treaty (May 1955); the Geneva Summit (July 1955) and the "spirit of Geneva"; peaceful coexistence; Khrushchev's visit and Camp David (September 1959). Limits: no agreement on Germany or arms control; Hungary (4 November 1956); Sputnik; the Berlin ultimatum of 27 November 1958; the U-2 and the collapse of the Paris summit (1 and 16 May 1960); the nuclear arms race. Level 5 judges that the tone improved far more than the substance.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Korean War was a more important turning point in American foreign policy than the Truman Doctrine.' Evaluate this view.
H: Choose criteria: scale, permanence and what each made possible.
H: What would the USA have done without each?
A: Indicative content. For Korea: it militarised containment (NSC-68, spending from about 14 billion to over 50 billion dollars); extended it to Asia; created alliances; led to Vietnam. For the Truman Doctrine: it committed the USA to a global role, set the pattern for Marshall Aid and NATO, and made Korea thinkable. Level 5 argues, for example, that the Truman Doctrine set the aim and Korea the means, and judges which was the greater turning point.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'In foreign policy, Eisenhower and Truman differed more in what they said than in what they did.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare rhetoric and action: liberation, brinkmanship, containment.
H: Think of cases where the action differed from the words in either direction.
A: Support: "liberation" and Hungary; massive retaliation and the use of limited covert methods; the Truman Doctrine's universal language and the limited action actually taken (Greece and Turkey, not China); both presidents halted at a limit (the Yalu and Hungary). Challenge: the New Look really cut conventional forces; Eisenhower refused war in Vietnam, Suez and Hungary where Truman might have acted; summits; covert action in Iran and Guatemala was a real difference; Truman fought a war. A* judgement distinguishes rhetoric from action in each president and concludes, for example, that rhetoric overstated the difference in strategy while the real differences were in cost, method and range.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Truman moved from asymmetric to symmetric containment, and Eisenhower returned to asymmetric containment.' Evaluate this view with reference to the years 1947–60. (The argument is John Lewis Gaddis's.)
H: Take 1947–49, 1950–53 and 1953–60 separately.
H: Test "asymmetric" against cases such as Taiwan, Hungary and Lebanon.
A: Support: before 1950 Truman was selective (Greece, Turkey, Western Europe, not China); NSC-68 and Korea were a general response; the New Look chose nuclear threat and covert action, and spending fell. Challenge: Eisenhower also spread commitments widely (SEATO, Baghdad Pact, the Eisenhower Doctrine, Lebanon); Truman's early policy had a global language; Leffler's emphasis on the balance of power across Eurasia; the continuity in commitments. A* judgement says that the framework fits the means well and the commitments badly, because both presidents expanded the number of places the USA defended.
:::

- **Four tests for a comparison**: aims, means, places and risks, results.
