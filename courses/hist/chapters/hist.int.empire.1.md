## Africa before the Scramble

In 1870 Africa was not an empty map waiting to be filled. It held a great variety of states: the Sokoto Caliphate (a Muslim state, ruled by a caliph with emirs, regional rulers, under him) in what is now northern Nigeria, the Asante kingdom on the Gold Coast, the Zulu kingdom in south-east Africa, the kingdom of Buganda by Lake Victoria, the Christian empire of Ethiopia, the Sultanate of Zanzibar and the Khedivate of Egypt (nominally Ottoman). Most of the continent, away from the coast, was run by African rulers, and European travellers needed their permission to cross it.

Europeans were present but weak. They held:
- **Algeria**, conquered by France from 1830;
- the **Cape Colony** and **Natal** in the south, with the Boer republics (the Transvaal and the Orange Free State, states of the Boers, the Dutch-descended settlers) beside them;
- **coastal forts and trading posts** in West Africa (French Senegal, British Sierra Leone, Gold Coast and Lagos, Portuguese Angola and Mozambique).

That is why historians usually say Europeans controlled about a tenth of Africa in 1870 (a rough figure, since "control" is hard to measure). Europeans did not go inland for three practical reasons: disease killed most of those who tried, there was no cheap way to carry goods or soldiers, and there was little profit to be made. In West Africa, firms traded with African merchants at the coast. Britain had abolished the slave trade in 1807 and used its navy against it; trade in palm oil, the ingredient of soap and a lubricant for machinery, replaced it, and was called "legitimate commerce".

Then, in about thirty years, the position was reversed.

:::def Scramble for Africa
The rapid conquest and partition of Africa by European states between about 1880 and 1914, in which most of the continent changed from African to European rule, with Ethiopia and Liberia the only states to remain independent.
:::

:::def Imperialism
A policy by which a state extends its power over other peoples or territories, by conquest, treaty or economic control, in order to gain wealth, security or prestige. In this chapter it means European rule over Africa; *colonialism* means the settlement and government of the territory that results.
:::

Historians also speak of the **New Imperialism**: the burst of empire-building by industrial powers from about the 1870s, in Africa, South-East Asia and the Pacific, which was different from the older trading empires. The rest of this chapter asks why it happened in Africa (motives), how it happened (process), and what it did (impact and resistance).

## Why Europe wanted Africa: the motives

The syllabus names three sets of motives. Examiners reward answers that keep them apart, show how they overlap, and weigh them for a particular power and a particular moment.

### Economic motives

By the 1870s Britain was the leading industrial power, and Germany, France and the United States were catching up. A long depression, running from 1873 to the mid-1890s, brought falling prices and fierce competition. Governments put up tariffs to protect their industries (Germany in 1879, France with the Méline tariff of 1892), so merchants feared being shut out of markets. Africa promised three things:
- **Raw materials**: palm oil, groundnuts and cocoa; rubber (in great demand after John Boyd Dunlop's pneumatic tyre of 1888 and the growth of cycles and cars); ivory; cotton; and, once found, minerals: diamonds at Kimberley (1871), gold on the Witwatersrand (1886), copper in Katanga.
- **Markets**: manufacturers hoped that millions of Africans would buy cloth, tools and spirits.
- **Investment**: capital seeking a good return could build railways and mines.

Two thinkers turned this into a theory. The British economist **J.A. Hobson**, in *Imperialism: A Study* (1902), argued that rich investors had more capital than could be invested profitably at home, so they pressed governments to take colonies. The gains went to a few, he said, while the whole nation paid the bill, and he described a country that would "squander its pecuniary and military resources in forcing bad markets". **Vladimir Lenin**, in *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (written in 1916, published in 1917), took the idea further: monopoly capitalism had to export capital, and the great powers had divided the world among themselves.

:::key The case for and against economic motives
**For:** firms and individuals did make fortunes (Cecil Rhodes in diamonds and gold; Leopold II and the concession companies in Congo rubber); the depression made merchants and governments anxious about markets; chartered companies (the Royal Niger Company from 1886, the British South Africa Company from 1889) were set up to make profits.
**Against:** tropical Africa was a small part of European trade and most European capital went elsewhere, to the United States, Latin America, Russia and the settler colonies; Hobson's argument fits South Africa better than most of Africa; many colonies, such as Germany's, cost the state more than they earned; and the great expectations of the 1880s (the "vast market") were mostly not met. Economic hope explains the *wish* for colonies more than the actual results.
:::

### Religious and cultural motives: "the civilising mission"

Many Europeans believed that they had a duty to bring Christianity, commerce and "civilisation" to Africa. David Livingstone summed it up in a lecture at Cambridge on 4 December 1857, where he said he was going back to Africa "to try to make an open path for commerce and Christianity"; "Christianity, commerce and civilisation" became the usual short summary of his aims. Missionary societies grew strongly in the nineteenth century (the Church Missionary Society, the London Missionary Society, the Scottish missions on Lake Nyasa from 1875, and the French Catholic White Fathers, founded by Cardinal Lavigerie in 1868). A powerful humanitarian cause linked them: ending the slave trade that still ran across East Africa and the Sahara. The Sultan of Zanzibar closed his slave market in 1873 under British pressure, partly because of the outcry Livingstone's writings had stirred, and Lavigerie toured Europe in 1888 calling for a crusade against slavery.

:::def The civilising mission
The belief, common in nineteenth-century Europe, that Europeans had a duty to spread their religion, laws, education and way of life to peoples they thought "backward". It was used to justify conquest, and it rested on ideas of racial superiority that are now rejected.
:::

The ideas had a pseudo-scientific side. After Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859), writers such as Herbert Spencer applied "survival of the fittest" to nations and "races", claiming that stronger peoples were destined to rule weaker ones. The French politician **Jules Ferry**, twice Prime Minister of France, put the argument to the Chamber of Deputies on 28 July 1885: "the superior races have a right because they have a duty. They have the duty to civilize the inferior races". Ferry (in the same speech) also spoke of "the need for outlets" for French exports, which shows how easily moral and economic arguments were mixed. **Rudyard Kipling**'s poem *The White Man's Burden* (1899) made the idea famous: his first line is "Take up the White Man's burden". In 1877, a young Cecil Rhodes wrote that he believed "we are the finest race in the world", and he spent his life turning that belief into the British South Africa Company.

How seriously should you take these arguments? Historians usually say both too little and too much of them. They were sincerely held by many missionaries and by some officials, and public opinion, especially the anti-slavery cause, did support intervention. But "civilisation" also gave respectable language to conquest decided for other reasons. A Paper 1 answer should ask who is speaking, to whom, and what they gain from the argument. A key test: whenever moral and material interests clashed, as in the Congo, the material interests usually won.

### National and strategic rivalry

The third motive was the competition between the states themselves. After the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71, France wanted to restore its prestige. A united Germany, the strongest power in Europe, was a latecomer to empire. Britain wanted to protect its position as the world's greatest naval and imperial power. Colonies were also counters in a diplomatic game, and the worry that if you did not take a territory your rival would made each claim urgent.

**The Suez Canal and Egypt** were the most important single case. The canal, opened in 1869, shortened the route from Britain to India, and the British government bought the Khedive's shares in 1875 (see below). When Egypt's finances collapsed and an army officer, Ahmed Urabi, led a nationalist revolt in 1882, Britain bombarded Alexandria (11 July), defeated Urabi at Tel el-Kebir (13 September) and occupied the country. Britain said the occupation would be temporary. It lasted until 1956 in practice, and it angered France, which had been Egypt's other great influence. **Ronald Robinson and John Gallagher**, in *Africa and the Victorians* (1961), argued that the occupation of Egypt started the Scramble: to guard the route to India, Britain had to hold Egypt, then the upper Nile, then East Africa, and rival powers reacted by seizing land elsewhere. On their view, ministers in London were driven by strategy and by crises "on the periphery", not by the demands of business.

Germany's entry shows national motives of a different kind. The Chancellor, Otto von Bismarck, had long said he did not want colonies. But in 1884 he proclaimed German protection over land claimed by the Bremen merchant Adolf Lüderitz in South West Africa, and over Togo and Cameroon, partly to win votes at home from supporters of empire, partly to play Britain and France off against each other. The next chapter explains his reasons in full. Italy wanted prestige and land for emigrants and occupied Massawa on the Red Sea (1885), from which it built the colony of Eritrea (1890); Portugal wished to join up Angola and Mozambique across the continent; and **Leopold II**, King of the Belgians, ruled a small country and wanted a colony of his own.

:::key Three kinds of motive, and how they connected
1. **Economic:** raw materials, markets and investment. They explain why governments were willing to back traders, and why companies lobbied.
2. **Religious and cultural:** missions, anti-slavery and the "civilising mission". They supplied the justification and public support, and some of the pioneers.
3. **National and strategic:** prestige, the route to India, and the fear of being shut out. They explain the *speed* of the Scramble, because each seizure made the next one more urgent.
A judgement worth defending: the economic and humanitarian motives created a climate in which empire seemed natural and profitable; strategic rivalry, above all the occupation of Egypt in 1882 and the fear of being forestalled, turned that mood into a scramble.
:::

| Power | Main motives in Africa | Typical early step |
|---|---|---|
| Britain | Protect the route to India (Suez, Egypt, the Cape); keep markets; missionaries and anti-slavery; settlers | Share purchase 1875; occupation of Egypt 1882 |
| France | Restore prestige after 1871; Algeria as a base; link West Africa to the Sahara and Lake Chad | Expansion from Senegal; Tunisia 1881 |
| Germany | Pressure from merchants and colonial societies; domestic politics; later world-power ambitions | Protection of Lüderitz's claim 1884; Togo and Cameroon 1884 |
| Belgium (Leopold II) | A personal colony for profit | International Association of the Congo 1879–84 |
| Portugal | Defend old coastal claims and link Angola with Mozambique | Treaty claims in the 1880s |
| Italy | Prestige; land for emigrants | Occupation of Massawa 1885 |

:::warn Do not write "the Scramble began with the Berlin Conference"
The Berlin Conference (November 1884 to February 1885) set rules for claims on the coasts and the Congo, but it did not divide up Africa, and most of the boundaries were agreed in later treaties between pairs of powers. The rush had begun earlier, in Egypt, the Congo and West Africa. [The next chapter](#hist.int.empire.2) covers the conference in full.
:::

## How Africa was taken: the process of colonisation

### The explorers

Exploration came before conquest. European travellers mapped rivers, made treaties with chiefs, and brought home books that made Africa a subject of public interest. They worked with African guides, porters, interpreters and rulers, whose help was essential and who are too often left out of the story.

| Explorer | What he did | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|
| **Heinrich Barth** (German, 1821–65) | Crossed the Sahara and travelled across the Sahel for five years from 1850, reaching Timbuktu in 1853; published *Travels and Discoveries in North and Central Africa* (1857–58) | Described the states, peoples, languages and trade of West and Central Africa in great detail; showed that these were organised societies with their own history, not "empty" lands |
| **David Livingstone** (Scottish missionary, 1813–73) | Crossed southern Africa from west to east (1853–56); saw the Victoria Falls (1855); led the Zambezi expedition (1858–64); died in 1873 while searching for the source of the Nile | A national hero in Britain; his writing made the slave trade a public scandal and the three Cs (Christianity, commerce, civilisation) a cause; missionary societies set up stations in his name |
| **Henry Morton Stanley** (Welsh-born journalist and explorer, 1841–1904) | Found Livingstone at Ujiji on 10 November 1871; crossed Africa (1874–77) and followed the Congo to the Atlantic; from 1879 to 1884 set up stations and made treaties for Leopold II | His second journey proved the Congo basin was open to steamers; his work for Leopold turned geography into a claim to territory |

Stanley's greeting, "Dr Livingstone, I presume?", is the best-known line of African exploration, but the words come only from Stanley's own published account, and historians note that the page of his diary for that day is missing. Treat it as a famous story, not a verified record. The more important point is that the explorers were *not* neutral. Stanley travelled with guns and fought several battles on his way down the Congo, and his treaties with local chiefs, often written in a language the chiefs could not read, were later used as legal claims. Livingstone, by contrast, taught his readers to think of Africa as a place needing rescue, and so helped to build the support for intervention.

### Technology and science

Why did Europeans suddenly succeed after 1870, when they had been confined to the coast? Part of the answer is that new tools made inland control cheaper and safer.

| Change | What it did | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Quinine** | Taken daily as a preventive, it cut deaths from malaria, the main killer of Europeans in tropical Africa | Expeditions up the Niger from the 1850s used it |
| **Steamships** | Shallow-draught river steamers carried troops, goods and officials far inland, and ocean steamers cut the journey time | Steamers on the Niger and Congo |
| **Railways** | Moved soldiers and heavy goods quickly where there were no roads or pack animals (tsetse flies killed horses and oxen) | The Uganda Railway, built 1896–1901 |
| **Telegraph and cable** | Let governments in Europe give orders in hours rather than months | Cables to the African coasts from the 1880s |
| **Weapons** | Breech-loading and repeating rifles, then the Maxim machine gun (invented 1884), gave small forces an enormous advantage in open battle | Omdurman, 1898 |

The weapons gap is the clearest. At Omdurman, in the Sudan, on 2 September 1898, a British-Egyptian army of about 25,800, with Maxim guns and artillery, faced a Mahdist army that was probably 35,000–50,000 strong. About 12,000 Mahdist fighters were killed; the British and Egyptian dead numbered 47 or 48. In 1893, in the first Matabele War, a few hundred men of the British South Africa Company, with Maxim guns, beat Lobengula's army of thousands. In 1898 the poet Hilaire Belloc summed it up in a couplet that is often quoted: "Whatever happens, we have got the Maxim gun, and they have not". It was meant as satire, but it was true of those battles.

:::warn Technology did not conquer Africa by itself
Many African armies also had modern rifles, and were not beaten in open battle at all. At Isandlwana in 1879 a Zulu army with spears and some old guns destroyed a British column that had breech-loading rifles. At Adwa in 1896 Ethiopia, armed with tens of thousands of modern rifles bought from Europe, destroyed an Italian army. Technology gave Europeans an *edge* where they could choose the time and place of battle and had enough ammunition. It did not make conquest automatic, and Europeans relied heavily on African soldiers, interpreters and local allies.
:::

### The Suez Canal

The Suez Canal, built by a French company under Ferdinand de Lesseps and opened on 17 November 1869, cut the sea distance from Britain to India by thousands of kilometres. Steamships could use the canal, but sailing ships could not easily, so the route favoured the powers with steam fleets. The Khedive Ismail of Egypt, deep in debt, put his 44 per cent of the Suez Canal Company shares up for sale in 1875. The Prime Minister, Benjamin Disraeli, bought them for the British government for about £4 million (borrowed from the bankers Rothschild at short notice), without waiting for Parliament. Britain was now the largest shareholder in a waterway that most of its trade with India and the East depended on.

The canal made Egypt the centre of British strategy and set up the sequence of events that led to the occupation of 1882. The Convention of Constantinople (1888) declared the canal open to all ships in peace and war, but Britain's troops stayed on the spot. Fears for the canal also explain why Britain wanted to control the upper Nile and why it later took the matter of Fashoda so seriously (see [the next chapter](#hist.int.empire.2)).

### How European rule was set up

Conquest took several forms, often in sequence:
1. **Treaties** with African rulers, which offered "protection" in return for trade rights or land. Often the chiefs did not understand what they were giving up.
2. **Chartered companies**, such as the Royal Niger Company (1886), the Imperial British East Africa Company (1888), the British South Africa Company (1889) and several German companies, were given powers to rule and trade, which saved governments the cost.
3. **Military conquest**, once a ruler resisted or the government wanted the land: the Zulu war of 1879, the Sudan in 1896–98, the Sokoto Caliphate in 1903.
4. **Colonial rule**, with a governor, an administration and a tax system.

The new colonies had to pay their own way, so governments imposed taxes, often a "hut tax" or poll tax that had to be paid in cash. This forced Africans to grow crops for sale or to work for wages, and so linked their lives to the colonial economy.

### Direct and indirect rule

:::def Direct rule
A system of colonial government in which European officials rule the territory themselves, through a hierarchy of European-appointed administrators, and African rulers have little or no formal authority.
:::

:::def Indirect rule
A system of colonial government in which the colonial power rules through existing African rulers (emirs, chiefs, kings), who keep some authority under the supervision of European officials.
:::

**Direct rule** is associated with France, Germany and Belgium. In French West Africa, a federation formed in 1895 and run by a Governor-General in Dakar, French officers governed districts (*cercles*) and used chiefs as paid agents. France's ideal was "assimilation", making Africans into French citizens and French speakers, but in practice very few were given citizenship. German East Africa and South West Africa were ruled by governors and military officers, with settlers given the best land. In the Congo, rule was by the state, the army and the companies, then (after 1908) by the Belgian government.

**Indirect rule** is associated with Britain, and above all with **Frederick Lugard**. Lugard had served in Uganda and in the Royal Niger Company's territory, and from 1900 he was High Commissioner of Northern Nigeria. After conquering the Sokoto Caliphate in 1903, he left the emirs in place, with British Residents overseeing them and taxes collected through the emirs' own officials. He explained his ideas in *The Dual Mandate in British Tropical Africa* (1922). The reasons were practical: Britain had few officers and little money, and ruling through existing rulers was cheaper and caused less resistance. Elsewhere, as in Uganda, rulers also made agreements that kept their position (the Buganda Agreement of 1900).

Indirect rule had weaknesses. Where there had been no chiefs, as among the Igbo of south-eastern Nigeria, British officers appointed "warrant chiefs" with no traditional legitimacy, who were often resented. Rulers could become tools of the colonial power. And "direct" and "indirect" were not strict categories: the French also used chiefs, and the British often ruled directly in settler colonies such as Kenya and Southern Rhodesia.

| | Direct rule | Indirect rule |
|---|---|---|
| Associated with | France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal | Britain (Northern Nigeria, Uganda) |
| Who governs locally | European officials | African rulers under European supervision |
| Aim | Control and uniformity; for France, assimilation | Low cost, stability, keep existing authority |
| Weakness | Expensive; provoked resistance | Rulers become agents of the colonial power; invented chiefs |
| Reality | Chiefs used as agents | Direct control in settler colonies |

:::key What the process shows about why imperialism was so strong
European rule grew from a mixture of motives, but it became possible and cheap because of technology and medicine; it was made legal by treaties and, after 1885, by rules agreed between the powers; and it was paid for by taxes on Africans. The colonial state was never very big: it depended on African soldiers, clerks, chiefs and interpreters, which is why resistance and cooperation both matter in Part 3.
:::

## The impact of colonialism on Africa

### Arbitrary boundaries

The borders of the new colonies were agreed in Europe, in treaties between pairs of powers, often using rivers, lakes or straight lines along lines of latitude and longitude, with little knowledge of the people on the ground. The Anglo-German treaty of 1890, the Anglo-French agreements of 1898–99 and many others created colonies that cut across kingdoms and language groups and joined groups that had little in common. The Somali were split between British, French, Italian and Ethiopian rule; the Kongo people lived in the Belgian Congo, French Congo and Portuguese Angola; the Ewe were divided between the Gold Coast and German Togo.

:::def Arbitrary boundary
A border drawn by outside powers for their own convenience, without regard to the political, ethnic, linguistic or economic ties of the people living there.
:::

The effects lasted. Some African peoples became minorities in states they had not chosen; communities lost access to traditional grazing and trading routes; and rival peoples were put under one administration, which colonial officials sometimes encouraged to play off against each other ("divide and rule"). When the African states became independent in the 1960s, the Organisation of African Unity decided in 1964 to keep the colonial borders, because changing them would have started endless conflicts. So the boundaries of 1885–1914 are still the borders of Africa. Be careful with the claim that this was the cause of later wars: boundaries mattered, but so did the way colonial governments ruled and favoured particular groups.

### Human exploitation and abuse: the Belgian Congo

The worst case was the **Congo Free State**. It began as the private project of Leopold II. In 1876 he organised a geographical conference in Brussels and set up the International African Association, then funded Stanley's work (1879–84) and made hundreds of treaties with chiefs. At the Berlin Conference (1884–85) the powers recognised his claim, and in 1885 he became sovereign of the "Congo Free State", an area of over two million square kilometres, about seventy-five times the size of Belgium. It was his personal possession, not a Belgian colony.

Leopold needed money. In 1891–92 decrees claimed all "vacant" land for the state, which meant most of the forest, and gave the state and the concession companies (firms given the right to take a region's rubber and ivory) the right to the rubber and ivory. Rubber was in demand for tyres, hoses and cables. It was taken from wild vines by villagers, who were given quotas to meet and little or no payment. The system rested on force:
- The **Force Publique**, an army of African soldiers commanded by white officers, enforced the quotas.
- Troops and company agents took hostages (often women), burned villages and flogged those who failed to deliver.
- Witnesses, missionaries among them, reported that soldiers were required to show a severed hand as proof of each cartridge used, and that hands were cut from the living and the dead to account for cartridges. Casement himself described a boy of eleven or twelve whose right hand had been cut off.

The scale of the human loss is hard to measure and has been argued about for a century. Casement's own estimates for the districts he visited in 1903 were that the population of Lukolela had fallen from "fully 5,000" in 1887 to under 600, and that of Bolobo from "fully 40,000" to 7,000 or 8,000. Overall, estimates of the fall in the population of the Congo during Leopold's rule range from about one million to ten million or more. The best-known figure, ten million, or about half the population, is the estimate of the journalist **Adam Hochschild** in *King Leopold's Ghost* (1998). Some demographers who have re-examined the evidence suggest a lower total, of between one and five million. The reasons for the disagreement are the lack of any count before the 1920s and the difficulty of separating killing and forced labour from disease and falling birth rates. What no historian disputes is that the system caused terrible suffering and a very large number of deaths.

The abuses were exposed by a handful of people. Missionaries, among them the American Presbyterian **William Sheppard** and the Baptist missionary and photographer **Alice Seeley Harris**, reported and photographed what they saw. **E.D. Morel**, a shipping clerk in Liverpool, noticed that ships brought rubber and ivory from the Congo but carried little but guns and soldiers back, which suggested that Africans were not being paid, and gave up his job to campaign. The British Consul at Boma, **Roger Casement**, travelled up the Congo in 1903 and wrote a report that was published as a parliamentary paper in February 1904. Morel and Casement founded the Congo Reform Association in 1904. Writers such as Joseph Conrad (*Heart of Darkness*, 1899), Mark Twain (*King Leopold's Soliloquy*, 1905) and Arthur Conan Doyle (*The Crime of the Congo*, 1909) kept the issue before the public. Leopold's own commission of inquiry (1904–05) confirmed many of the abuses. In 1908, the Belgian parliament took over the Congo, on 15 November, paying Leopold compensation. Belgian rule ended the worst abuses, but forced labour continued on a smaller scale.

The Congo was the extreme case, but forced labour and violence were not confined to it. The French concession companies in Equatorial Africa, Portuguese contract labour in Angola and São Tomé, and the German war against the Herero all show that the power to compel Africans was widely abused (see "African resistance" below).

### Conversion to Christianity, healthcare and education

Colonial rule opened the way for missionaries. They built churches, schools and clinics in places the state would not have reached. The effects were real but mixed.

- **Christianity.** Mission stations spread from the coasts. Africans themselves were the most effective evangelists: Samuel Ajayi Crowther, a Yoruba former slave, became the first African Anglican bishop in 1864. Conversion was slow before 1914 and much faster later, in the twentieth century, when African-led churches grew. Missionaries often also attacked African customs they disliked, and some were seen as allies of colonial governments.
- **Healthcare.** Mission hospitals, quinine and later vaccination and sleeping-sickness campaigns saved lives. But the colonial period also brought disease: the rinderpest epidemic of the 1890s, which started in the north-east and killed a very large share of the cattle of eastern and southern Africa, destroyed the herders' wealth and food and weakened their ability to resist; forced labour and the movement of workers spread infection.
- **Education.** Mission schools taught reading, writing and trades, and were the only schools most Africans could attend. By 1914 few Africans reached more than primary level. Yet the mission-educated minority was important: its members became the clerks, teachers and, later, the leaders of nationalist movements who used European ideas against European rule.

### Infrastructure development

Colonial governments built railways, ports, roads and telegraph lines, to move troops and take out goods. The Uganda Railway, begun at Mombasa in 1896 and finished at Kisumu on Lake Victoria in 1901, covered over 1,000 kilometres; more than 30,000 labourers were brought from India to build it and several thousand died. The Matadi to Léopoldville railway in the Congo (opened 1898) bypassed the cataracts on the lower river and was built at a terrible cost in lives. In Egypt and Algeria there were larger networks.

The pattern is the key point: lines ran from mines and plantations to ports, not between African regions. They did not form a network for the benefit of Africans, and Africans who needed to travel were seldom the aim. Yet railways, ports, cables and roads were also used by Africans for trade, travel and the movement of ideas, and independent states inherited them. A balanced answer says that infrastructure was built for the colonisers' benefit but had lasting effects that were not all negative.

:::key The balance sheet of colonialism, in a sentence each
1. **Boundaries:** lasting, and a source of tension, though not the only cause of later conflicts.
2. **Exploitation:** extreme in the Congo, widespread in the use of forced labour and taxes.
3. **Missions, health, education:** real benefits to some, but small in scale before 1914, and bound up with the aim of changing African societies.
4. **Infrastructure:** built to take goods out, but it left lasting transport links.
5. **Overall:** whether the gains outweighed the costs is a matter of judgement, and Africans' own voices should be part of it.
:::

## African resistance: causes and impact

Africans did not accept conquest passively. They fought, negotiated, played one European power against another, and adapted. Four cases are named in the syllabus.

### The Anglo-Zulu War, 1879

The Zulu kingdom, under King **Cetshwayo**, was the strongest military power in south-eastern Africa. Britain's High Commissioner, **Sir Henry Bartle Frere**, wanted to unite the Cape, Natal and the Boer republics in a federation, and saw the Zulu army as an obstacle. He sent an ultimatum on 11 December 1878 demanding that Cetshwayo disband it. When it was not met, British forces under **Lord Chelmsford** invaded on 11 January 1879.

On **22 January 1879** a Zulu army of perhaps 20,000 surprised the main British column at **Isandlwana**. Over 1,300 British and colonial troops were killed, including 52 officers: the worst defeat of a modern European army by an African force in the nineteenth century. Zulu losses were also heavy; estimates run from about 1,000 to 3,000. The same day, about 150 British soldiers held the mission station at **Rorke's Drift** against a Zulu force of several thousand, and eleven Victoria Crosses were awarded. Britain sent reinforcements, and on **4 July 1879** Chelmsford's army broke the Zulu at **Ulundi**, the king's capital. Cetshwayo was captured in August. The British split the kingdom into thirteen chiefdoms, and in 1887 annexed Zululand.

### The Ndebele rebellion

The Ndebele (Matabele) kingdom, led by King **Lobengula**, lay in what is now Zimbabwe. Rhodes's British South Africa Company, chartered on 29 October 1889, sent a column into neighbouring Mashonaland in 1890. In 1893 the Company provoked a war, and its forces, using Maxim guns, defeated the Ndebele army; Lobengula fled and died soon after, and the Company took the land and cattle.

In March 1896 the Ndebele rose again. Their reasons were the loss of land and cattle, forced labour, the hut tax, and the rinderpest epidemic of 1896, and a spiritual leader, the **Mlimo**, convinced them that the settlers had caused the drought and the cattle plague. The rising began on 24 March 1896 and in the first week 141 settlers were killed in Matabeleland and 103 in Mashonaland, where the Shona joined in June. The troops that might have defended the colony had been taken away for the Jameson Raid (December 1895, a failed armed raid by Company men into the Boer Transvaal, to overthrow its government; the next chapter explains it). The Ndebele held out in the Matopo Hills until Rhodes himself went unarmed to meet their leaders in a series of *indabas* (councils) in August to October 1896, and promised changes. The Shona fought on into 1897. In the end the rebellion failed and Company rule continued, but it forced the Company to make some concessions, and it left a memory of resistance that later nationalists used.

### The Battle of Adwa, 1896

**Ethiopia** was the great exception. Its emperor, **Menelik II**, had modernised his army by buying modern rifles in large numbers from France and Russia, and had made Addis Ababa his capital. In 1889 Italy and Menelik signed the **Treaty of Wuchale**. Its Italian version, in Article 17, made Ethiopia's foreign relations go through Italy, which would have made it a protectorate (a state that keeps its own ruler but hands its foreign affairs to the protecting power). The Amharic version said Menelik *could* use Italy's services if he wished. When Menelik found the difference in 1890, he rejected the Italian reading. In an April 1891 circular to the European powers he set out the extent of his empire and warned that he would not remain an indifferent spectator if the distant powers divided Africa among themselves.

War followed in 1895. On **1 March 1896** the Italian commander, General **Oreste Baratieri**, advanced on Adwa with an army of roughly 14,500 to 17,800 fighting men (Italians and Eritrean *askari*, African soldiers in Italian service; historians' estimates differ) against an Ethiopian army of 73,000 to over 100,000 under Menelik and his wife, Empress **Taytu Betul**. Baratieri's columns got separated in rough ground, and the Ethiopians destroyed them. Italy lost over 6,000 killed and about 3,800 captured. By the **Treaty of Addis Ababa** (26 October 1896) Italy recognised Ethiopia's independence and Wuchale was annulled. Francesco Crispi's government in Rome fell. Adwa became a symbol across Africa and in the African diaspora that Europeans could be defeated. Ethiopia stayed independent until Mussolini's invasion in 1935.

### The Herero War, 1904–08

German South West Africa (now Namibia) was a settler colony from the 1880s. The Herero, a cattle-herding people led by **Samuel Maharero**, lost land and cattle to settlers and traders, helped by debts and by the rinderpest. On **12 January 1904** the Herero rose and killed over a hundred German settlers. The German government sent General **Lothar von Trotha**, with orders to crush the rising. At the Battle of the Waterberg (11 August 1904) the Herero were defeated and driven into the Omaheke desert, where German troops sealed the waterholes. On **2 October 1904** von Trotha issued a proclamation saying the Herero were no longer German subjects and that any Herero found within the German frontier, armed or not, would be shot. The Kaiser withdrew the order in December 1904, after protests in Germany, but by then the Herero were scattered, and survivors were put in camps and used as forced labour. The Nama, under **Hendrik Witbooi**, rose later in 1904 and met the same treatment. The camps closed in 1908.

Historians' estimates of the deaths range from about 40,000 to 80,000 Herero, perhaps four fifths of the people, and about 10,000 Nama, perhaps half. A German census of about 1911 counted roughly 15,000 Herero and 10,000 Nama. Most historians now call these killings a genocide, and in 2021 the German government formally accepted that description and pledged 1.1 billion euros in development aid over thirty years, though without accepting a legal basis for reparations.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| Anglo-Zulu War | 1879 | Cetshwayo | Zulu victory at Isandlwana; defeat at Ulundi; kingdom divided | A strong state with an army that fought as a unit; Britain could reinforce |

:::key Why most resistance failed, and why Adwa did not
**Why most failed:** the weapons gap in open battle (Maxim guns, artillery); the ability of Europeans to bring reinforcements and supplies by sea and rail; the rivalries within African societies, which Europeans exploited; and the damage done by disease and cattle plague.
**Why Adwa succeeded:** Menelik had bought modern weapons, united the Ethiopian nobility, used the rivalry of the European powers, and fought on ground of his choice against an enemy who was divided and short of supplies.
**Impact:** resistance shaped colonial rule (the British left the emirs in Nigeria in part to avoid more war), made Europeans more brutal in places, and gave later nationalists a tradition of resistance. Even failed resistance mattered.
:::

## Why was imperialism a significant force in Africa? Weighing it up

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Three kinds of answer are worth practising.

- **Motives:** *which was most important?* Economic hopes and humanitarian ideas created the climate; strategic rivalry (Egypt in 1882, then the fear of being forestalled) made it a race. Different powers had different mixes (Leopold: profit; Britain: strategy; Germany: politics and prestige).
- **Means:** *what made it possible?* Technology and medicine made it cheap; treaties, companies and local allies made it work; African divisions made it easier. Resistance shows the limits.
- **Effects:** *how far did it change Africa?* Boundaries, taxes, forced labour, missions, schools, railways and the destruction of many independent states. How far Africa was changed, and for whom, depends on place: the Congo and South Africa were not the same as Northern Nigeria.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **J.A. Hobson**, *Imperialism* (1902), and **Lenin**, *Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism* (1917), saw imperialism as driven by capitalism's need for investment outlets. Hobson wrote as a radical critic; Lenin as a revolutionary.
- **Ronald Robinson and John Gallagher**, *Africa and the Victorians* (1961), argued that British expansion was driven by strategic concerns, above all the route to India, and that the occupation of Egypt in 1882 began the Scramble. They saw an "official mind" reacting to crises, not an economic plan.
- **Thomas Pakenham**, *The Scramble for Africa* (1991), wrote a narrative history in which rivalry, personalities and chance played a large part.
- **Adam Hochschild**, *King Leopold's Ghost* (1998), told the story of the Congo and the campaign against it, and made it widely known.
At AS you do not need to name historians, but you can use "some historians stress economic motives, others strategic ones" to show you know the debate. The mark schemes reward your own argument from evidence.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why European powers wanted colonies in Africa after 1870.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "European powers wanted colonies in Africa for several reasons. They wanted raw materials like rubber and palm oil, and markets for their goods. Missionaries wanted to spread Christianity and end the slave trade. There was also rivalry between the powers, such as Britain and France. Britain took Egypt in 1882 and Germany took colonies in 1884."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it lists. The reasons are named, not explained, and the examples at the end (Egypt, Germany) are not tied to a reason. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor with support.** "One reason was economic. Europe was in a long depression from 1873, and with other countries putting up tariffs, merchants and governments feared losing markets. Africa promised raw materials such as palm oil and, after Dunlop's tyre of 1888, rubber, and companies like the Royal Niger Company (1886) were set up to make a profit. A second reason was the belief in a civilising mission. Livingstone had called in 1857 for 'an open path for commerce and Christianity', and missionary societies and anti-slavery campaigners built public support, so that a speaker such as Jules Ferry could tell the French Chamber in 1885 that the 'superior races' had 'the duty to civilize the inferior races'."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support (dates, names, a quotation). Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was strategic rivalry. When Britain bought the Khedive's Suez Canal shares in 1875 and occupied Egypt in 1882 to protect the route to India, it made Egypt and then the Nile valley essential to its strategy. France, which had lost Egyptian influence and wanted to restore its prestige after 1871, extended its rule from Senegal across the Sahel, and Germany, whose Chancellor Bismarck saw political advantage in empire at home, claimed Togo, Cameroon and South West Africa in 1884. Each claim made rivals fear being shut out, so each had to act.

These reasons were connected. Economic hopes and the missionary cause created a mood in which empire seemed profitable and right; that is why governments were willing to back traders and charter companies. But the speed of the Scramble came from rivalry, because each seizure made the next more urgent. Many of the economic hopes were disappointed, while the strategic fears were real, so the most important reason was the fear of rivals. The powers wanted colonies because they feared the consequences of their rivals having them."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (economic and moral motives created the climate; rivalry made it urgent), with a supported conclusion about the most important reason. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

... the need for outlets ... a navy such as ours cannot do without safe harbors, defenses, supply centers on the high seas ... the superior races have a right because they have a duty. They have the duty to civilize the inferior races ...

*Extracts, in an English translation, from a speech by Jules Ferry, a former Prime Minister of France, to the Chamber of Deputies, 28 July 1885 (the sourcebook that prints the translation gives the date as 1884). Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Source B**

... I contend that we are the finest race in the world ... the bringing of the whole uncivilized world under British rule ... the absorption of the greater portion of the world under our rule simply means the end of all wars ...

*Extracts from the "Confession of Faith" written in 1877 by Cecil Rhodes, a young man in his twenties, in John E. Flint, Cecil Rhodes (1974). Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the reasons Europeans gave for building empires in the later nineteenth century.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Both sources think Europeans are better than other people. Source A says the superior races have a duty to civilize the inferior races and Source B says the British are the finest race in the world. They are different because Source A is from France and Source B is from Britain. They might be biased because they are both Europeans."

*Commentary.* One similarity, supported; one difference that is only the nationality of the author. The comment on bias is generic. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share the belief in the superiority of Europeans, and both link it to a duty or destiny. In Source A Ferry says 'the superior races have a right because they have a duty', and in Source B Rhodes says that 'we are the finest race in the world' and speaks of 'bringing the whole uncivilized world under British rule'. Both treat expansion as good for the people who are ruled, as well as for the rulers: Ferry speaks of a 'duty to civilize', and Rhodes imagines that British rule 'simply means the end of all wars'.

They differ in what else they say. Ferry mixes moral argument with practical points about trade and naval power: 'the need for outlets' for exports and 'safe harbors' and 'supply centers' for the fleet. Rhodes, in these extracts, offers a more ambitious and racial vision, of British rule over 'the greater portion of the world', and does not mention economics or ports. Ferry's argument is a duty of the strong towards the weak; Rhodes's is the destiny of one 'race'.

The context and provenance explain these differences. Ferry spoke in the Chamber of Deputies in 1885 to win votes for a colonial policy that many French politicians opposed, since colonial expansion was costly and was seen by critics as a distraction from the loss of Alsace-Lorraine. As a politician he needed arguments for several audiences at once: businessmen (outlets), the navy (harbours) and idealists (civilisation). That explains why his speech is a mixture. Rhodes, by contrast, wrote a private statement of beliefs as a young man in 1877, not meant for publication (it appeared only after his death in 1902). It has the freedom, and the extravagance, of a private manifesto, and it comes from a man who later acted on it, as head of the British South Africa Company and Prime Minister of the Cape. The sources therefore show two different kinds of evidence: Source A is evidence of what a politician thought would persuade a French audience in 1885; Source B is evidence of the private beliefs of one of imperialism's leading figures. Together they show that the language of superiority and duty ran across national lines, but Source A better shows how this language was used to persuade a public, and Source B the extreme form the beliefs could take."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through each author's purpose and audience (a politician in a parliament, a private young man); a final comparison of what each is evidence of. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Technology made the Scramble for Africa possible.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: quinine and medicine (Europeans could survive); steamers, railways and telegraph (movement and control); the Maxim gun and rifles (Omdurman, 1898: about 12,000 Mahdist dead to fewer than 50; the Matabele War of 1893).
- Against 1, timing: steamships, quinine and rifles existed from the 1830s–1860s, yet the Scramble came after 1880, so something else changed.
- Against 2, politics: Suez (1875), Egypt (1882), Leopold, Bismarck's turn in 1884 and the Berlin rules were decisions, not machines.
- Against 3, limits and African agency: Isandlwana (1879) and Adwa (1896); European rule relied on African soldiers, allies and divisions.
- Judgement: technology was an enabling condition (a necessary one in most of the interior) but not the cause; criterion: the question says "possible", so ask whether conquest would have been possible without it.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Technology was very important. Quinine meant Europeans could survive malaria, so they could go inland. Steamships and railways moved soldiers, and the Maxim gun gave them a huge advantage. At Omdurman in 1898 about 12,000 Mahdist fighters were killed and fewer than fifty British and Egyptian soldiers. This shows that technology made the Scramble possible."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than argues: one battle shows technology helped win a battle, not that it made the whole Scramble possible. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet technology does not explain *when* the Scramble happened, or why. Steamships had gone up the Niger since the 1830s, quinine was being used in expeditions by the 1850s, and breech-loading rifles were common by the 1860s, but Europeans controlled only about a tenth of Africa in 1870. The change came after 1875 and was political. Britain's purchase of Suez Canal shares in 1875 and occupation of Egypt in 1882 made the Nile a strategic concern; Leopold II's claim to the Congo and Bismarck's decision in 1884 to claim colonies made rivals fear being shut out; and the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 set rules for staking claims. The Maxim gun, invented in 1884, came after these decisions. Technology made conquest cheaper, but it did not make governments want it.

Nor was technology decisive in every case. At Isandlwana in 1879 a Zulu army with spears destroyed a column of breech-loading rifles, and in 1896 Ethiopia, with modern rifles of its own, destroyed an Italian army at Adwa and remained independent. The weapons gap mattered in open battle, but Europeans also relied on African soldiers (the Force Publique, the Senegalese riflemen), on local allies, and on the divisions among African states, which made conquest possible with small forces. In this sense the key condition was not machinery but the political fragmentation of the continent.

Overall, technology was a necessary condition in the interior: without quinine and steamers, Europeans could not have survived or supplied themselves, and without repeating weapons they could not have won so cheaply. But it was not sufficient, and it did not decide where or when. The statement is true of how conquest was achieved, but not of why it happened. Judged by whether the Scramble would have been *possible* without technology, the answer is that it would have been slower and more expensive but not impossible, as the French conquest of Algeria from 1830 shows; judged by what actually explains the Scramble, politics and motive matter more."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (necessary versus sufficient, possible versus caused). With an opening paragraph on the case for technology, this is Level 5 (18–19).

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: D (the powers promised, in 1885, to care for the "well-being" of the people), B (the State's own claim that the labour required was limited and paid). Challenge: A (a mutilated child; a population falling by 60–70 per cent), C (Leopold as a snake crushing a Congolese man). Qualify: B also admits that the "sentry" system was criticised "not without reason"; D is a promise, not evidence of what happened.

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: written by an official of the British government sent to investigate, after a journey of several months, who named places and gave figures; it was a confidential report to the Foreign Secretary, so it was written to inform, not to campaign. But it is the view of one man, who had his own views on the Congo, and the figures come from conversations with villagers and missionaries; it describes the upper Congo he visited, not the whole State. Its findings were largely confirmed by Leopold's own commission of inquiry (1904–05). A strong source on the districts he visited.
- **B**: a government defending itself to a foreign government under pressure from Britain; it speaks to the question of the "benefit" of the Congolese only in a limited sense. Its claim of forty hours a month is the State's own figure and cannot be checked; the same document concedes that the British Consul's criticism of the sentries was "not without reason", which makes it a reluctant admission. It is weak evidence that the system was fair, but good evidence of the arguments the State used.
- **C**: a cartoon in a British magazine in 1906, after Casement's report and the Congo Reform Association's campaign. As propaganda it simplifies, and it is a British view of a Belgian king, which shows that British opinion was hostile, not what Africans experienced. But its date and wide circulation show that by 1906 the picture of a state that was "free" in name only was widely believed.
- **D**: part of an international treaty of 1885, when the powers were trying to give a high moral gloss to their claims. The signatories included Leopold's own International Association. It tells us what the powers promised, not what was done, but it is useful as a standard against which the later evidence can be judged.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources B and D support the view in different ways. Source D shows that the powers promised in 1885 to look after the 'well-being' of the people, and Source B shows that the State claimed in 1903 that its demands on labour were small and paid. But neither is evidence of what happened. D is a statement of intention made by powers that were recognising Leopold's claim, and B is a government's defence under pressure, which also admits that the British Consul's criticism of the 'sentry' system was made 'not without reason'. Against them stand A and C. Casement's report is the strongest source in the set: it is a confidential report by an official who visited the districts and gave places and figures, and it reports a mutilated child and a fall in population of 60 or 70 per cent in ten years on the lake. Its picture was confirmed by Leopold's own commission of inquiry in 1904–05 and by the Belgian parliament's takeover in 1908. Source C, though propaganda, shows how widely that conclusion was accepted by 1906. On balance, the sources give little support to the view. The only sources that support it are promises and a defence by the accused, and the sources that challenge it are an independent official report and a hostile but well-informed cartoon, which the later history confirms. The claim is true only in the limited sense that the powers said they intended it."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1903–08; the answer explains where sources support and where they challenge; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: How far did African resistance limit European control?
**What the levels want.** The same Level 5 descriptor as above. The difficulty is the word "limit": define what the limits could be (stopping conquest, slowing it, shaping the terms of rule) before you argue.

**Plan.**
- Define "limit": prevent conquest; delay it; shape how Europeans ruled.
- For: Adwa kept Ethiopia independent; Isandlwana and Rorke's Drift cost Britain a war and a reinforcement of thousands; the Ndebele rising of 1896–97 forced Rhodes to negotiate and make concessions; resistance in Nigeria shaped indirect rule.
- Against: of all the cases only Ethiopia stayed independent (and Liberia, which was never taken); the Zulu, Ndebele and Herero were beaten; the Herero were almost destroyed; the Maxim gun, reinforcements and divisions among Africans decided the outcome.
- Judgement: resistance rarely *prevented* conquest but often shaped its terms and its cost; the exception shows what was possible with modern arms and unity.

**A Level 5 paragraph (the alternative perspective and the judgement).** "Resistance did limit European control, but mostly in how Europeans ruled, not in whether they ruled. Only Ethiopia, which had used the years after 1889 to buy modern rifles and unite its nobles under Menelik II, stopped conquest outright, at Adwa on 1 March 1896. Everywhere else the outcome was conquest: the Zulu won at Isandlwana but lost at Ulundi within six months, the Ndebele rising of 1896–97 ended in defeat, and the Herero were crushed in 1904. In each case the weapons gap and the power to reinforce decided the result. Even so, resistance had effects. The Anglo-Zulu War cost the British their reputation for invincibility and ended with Zululand divided rather than directly run; the Ndebele, though beaten, forced Rhodes to meet their leaders unarmed in the Matopos in 1896 and promise changes; and the fear of further risings was one reason for Lugard's decision to rule Northern Nigeria through the emirs. Resistance therefore limited European control in that it raised its cost and altered its form, but it did not change who held power. The judgement is that the limits were real but narrow."

*Commentary.* The paragraph defines the word that matters, uses precise evidence for both sides, and ends with a comparative judgement. In a full answer this would follow a paragraph on the case for resistance and one on the case against. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the civilising mission the main reason for European expansion in Africa after 1870?" [20]

A student wrote:

"David Livingstone was born in Scotland in 1813 and worked in a cotton mill as a boy. He became a missionary and went to Africa in 1841. He crossed Africa from west to east and saw the Victoria Falls in 1855. He wrote books about the slave trade, and he died in 1873. His body was carried to the coast by his friends Susi and Chuma. Many missionaries followed him to Africa and built schools and churches. Therefore the civilising mission was important."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story of one man without saying how it answers the question. The last sentence asserts a link ("therefore") without evidence. There is no comparison with other motives and no judgement about "main reason". Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The civilising mission was important as a source of public support rather than as the main reason for expansion. Livingstone's writing made the slave trade a scandal in Britain and led to missionary stations in East Africa, and politicians such as Jules Ferry used the language of duty in 1885. However, the powers acted when strategic interests were at stake, as with Egypt in 1882, and the humanitarian promises of the Berlin Act of 1885 were ignored in the Congo where profit was at stake. So the civilising mission justified expansion and won it popular support, but strategic rivalry and economic hopes determined where and when it happened." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: About what proportion of Africa did Europeans control in 1870, and what in 1914? Which two states were independent in 1914?
A: Roughly a tenth in 1870 (mostly coastal strips, Algeria and the Cape), and roughly nine tenths by 1914. Ethiopia and Liberia were independent.
Q: Give two examples each of an economic, a religious or cultural, and a strategic motive.
A: Economic: raw materials such as rubber and palm oil; markets for manufactured goods (also investment, Hobson's argument). Religious and cultural: spreading Christianity; ending the slave trade; the civilising mission. Strategic: protecting the Suez route to India (Egypt in 1882); fear of rivals taking territory; national prestige.
Q: What did Livingstone, Stanley and Barth each contribute to European interest in Africa?
A: Barth: detailed knowledge of West and Central Africa (journey 1850–55, reaching Timbuktu in 1853). Livingstone: made the slave trade a public cause and the "three Cs" a programme (died 1873). Stanley: found Livingstone in 1871, crossed Africa 1874–77, and in 1879–84 made treaties for Leopold, which became the basis of the Congo Free State.
Q: Name three technologies that helped Europeans take inland Africa and say what each did.
A: Quinine (protected against malaria); steamers and railways (moved troops and goods); telegraph (communication); repeating rifles and the Maxim gun (an edge in battle, as at Omdurman).
Q: When did the Suez Canal open, and how did Britain become involved in Egypt?
A: 17 November 1869. Britain bought the Khedive's 44 per cent of the shares in 1875 for about £4 million and, to protect the canal and route to India, occupied Egypt in 1882 after Urabi's revolt (Tel el-Kebir, 13 September).
Q: Distinguish direct from indirect rule, with one example each.
A: Direct rule: European officials govern (French West Africa, German colonies, Belgian Congo). Indirect rule: Europeans govern through African rulers (Lugard in Northern Nigeria, from 1900).
Q: Why is it misleading to say the Berlin Conference drew the borders of Africa?
A: It set rules for claims on the coasts and the Congo, but most boundaries were agreed in later treaties between pairs of powers, often after years of negotiation. (Many borders are still those drawn between 1885 and 1914.)
Q: Describe the system of rubber collection in the Congo Free State and how it was exposed.
A: Villagers had to supply quotas of rubber for little or no pay, enforced by the Force Publique and company agents through hostage-taking, burnt villages and flogging. It was exposed by missionaries, E.D. Morel and Roger Casement, whose report was published in February 1904; Belgium annexed the Congo on 15 November 1908.
Q: Why did the Ethiopians win at Adwa, and why did the Herero lose in 1904?
A: Ethiopia had modern rifles, a united army of tens of thousands under Menelik II and Taytu, and faced a divided, outnumbered Italian army. The Herero faced a well-supplied German army with machine guns that could reinforce by sea and rail; they lost land and cattle, and after Waterberg were driven into the desert.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Suez Canal was important to Britain in the years 1869–82.
A: Indicative content: it shortened the sea route to India and the East by thousands of kilometres, so most of Britain's trade and its military links with India depended on it; Britain's government bought the Khedive's 44 per cent share in 1875 (about £4 million), giving it a financial stake; when Egypt's finances collapsed and Urabi's revolt threatened the canal and British investors, Britain occupied Egypt in 1882 (Tel el-Kebir, 13 September). Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors (the canal's value led to the share purchase, which gave a stake that the 1882 crisis put at risk) and ends with a judgement on the main reason (strategic, the route to India, rather than the profit from the shares).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why King Leopold II was able to set up the Congo Free State.
A: Indicative content: Leopold's money and determination (the 1876 conference, the International African Association, Stanley's treaties 1879–84); the rivalries among the great powers, which each preferred Leopold's weak state to a rival's colony (France and Germany for the Congo to be open; Britain wanted to block Portugal); recognition by the United States (April 1884), France (with a first option to buy) and then the Berlin Conference (1884–85), which promised free trade on the Congo; the weakness of African states in the Congo basin; public belief in his humanitarian purpose (the anti-slavery campaign). Level 4: connect Leopold's own actions (the base) with the powers' rivalries (the opportunity) and judge that the rivalries were decisive, since he could not have succeeded against a determined opponent.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The main reason for the Scramble for Africa was economic.' How far do you agree?
A: For: raw materials (rubber, palm oil, minerals), markets in the depression of 1873–96, companies (Royal Niger Company, BSAC), Hobson and Lenin; Leopold's Congo. Against: the economic returns of most colonies were small and most European investment went elsewhere; strategic motives (Suez and Egypt 1882, the Cape route, fear of rivals), national prestige (France after 1871, Italy), and the civilising mission and anti-slavery cause. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "what best explains the timing and speed of the Scramble", and judges that economic hopes created the conditions while strategic rivalry decided the timing; a differentiated judgement by power (Leopold: economic; Britain: strategic) is stronger still.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent did European explorers make the Scramble for Africa possible?
A: For: Barth, Livingstone and Stanley opened the interior to knowledge, mapped rivers, made treaties (Stanley for Leopold) and created public interest and missionary support. Against: explorers depended on African guides, porters and rulers; the interior was already known to Africans and Arab traders; the Scramble needed medicine and arms (quinine, steamers, the Maxim gun), political decisions (Egypt 1882, Berlin) and motives; many explorers did not intend conquest (Livingstone wanted trade and the end of slavery). Level 5: judge that exploration was a preparation, providing knowledge and claims, but that the decisions were made by governments and companies, so it contributed without being the main cause.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and C (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about European attitudes to non-European peoples around 1900.
A: Similarities: both treat the peoples concerned as inferior subjects of a European power (A: "your captives' need"; C: "German subjects no longer"); both set out what Europeans see as their right to act. Differences: A frames empire as a duty and sacrifice ("burden", "exile", "serve"), presenting the empire as service; C is a threat of violence by a military commander ("will be shot"), with no pretence of service. A is a poem to persuade the American public to take the Philippines (1899); C is an order to an enemy in the field (1904). Explanation: A is the public ideal; C shows what happened when a settler colony was challenged, so the contrast between the ideal and the practice is the point. Provenance: A is Kipling, a poet who was a popular supporter of empire, written for the American debate; C is a military proclamation, translated, with the Kaiser later withdrawing the order. Level 4 explains the differences through these contexts and judges which is better evidence of attitudes.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). 'Greed rather than idealism drove European imperialism in Africa.' How far do these sources support this view?
H: Decide what each source says about *why* (A: idealism; B: profit; C: what happened when Africans resisted; D: ambition).
H: Evaluate each by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries (A is a poem, B a critic's argument).
H: Use your own knowledge: where did money and idealism each decide events? (Congo; Ferry's mixed argument; Egypt in 1882.)
A: Support: B (surplus capital seeking investment; forcing bad markets); D (Rhodes striding along Africa with the telegraph: ambition and an empire for the sake of reach and, as Rhodes's career shows, for profit); C (brutality when the colonised resisted). Challenge: A (the language of duty and sacrifice, which many missionaries and officials believed), and C and D also show strategic ambition, not only greed. Evaluation: A is a poem for the American debate about the Philippines (so it shows an ideal, written to persuade, and its "burden" is ironic to later readers); B is a radical critic of 1902 who attacked imperialism, so he selects evidence for his case, and his argument fits South Africa better than most of Africa; D is a cartoon in a magazine, supportive or satirical depending on the reading, and represents the telegraph and railway ambitions of 1892; C is a military order from a commander, and shows the brutality of one colony, not of all. Level 5 judges: the sources show both idealism and greed, but greed (B, and the behaviour in C and D) explains what happened in practice; "greed" is too narrow, though, because strategic rivalry and prestige are missing from the sources and were central. The support is therefore partial.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was the Congo Free State typical of European rule in Africa?
H: Define "typical": the aims, the methods, or the results.
H: Compare the Congo with Northern Nigeria and German South West Africa.
A: For: forced labour and taxation, the use of violence, concession companies and the pursuit of profit were found elsewhere (German South West Africa, Portuguese Angola, French concession companies in Equatorial Africa). Against: the Congo was the personal property of one king, not a colony of a state, until 1908; the scale of the abuses and the rubber system was exceptional; Britain's indirect rule in Northern Nigeria used existing rulers; missions, schools and railways existed in other colonies; the international outrage and annexation of 1908 show that the Congo was seen as exceptional at the time. Level 5: judges that the Congo was the extreme case on a spectrum rather than a different kind of rule, as all colonies were founded on force and taxes, but that its personal ownership and rubber quotas made it exceptional in degree.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'African resistance failed because of European technology.' How far do you agree?
H: Consider Adwa, where there was a technology gap in Europe's favour at most in artillery.
H: What else decided the outcome in the Zulu, Ndebele and Herero cases?
A: For: Maxim guns and rifles (Omdurman, the Matabele War of 1893) and the effects of ammunition supply and artillery. Against: Ethiopia's modern rifles at Adwa; Isandlwana, where Zulu tactics beat rifles; the importance of reinforcements, supply, disease and cattle plague (rinderpest 1896), divisions among Africans, and the African soldiers in European armies; and Europe's determination (Ulundi, the Herero war). Level 5 judges: technology was important in the *outcome of battles*, but the reasons resistance failed overall were organisation, supply, division and the willingness of Europeans to reinforce, which technology (railways, steamers) also helped.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Robinson and Gallagher argued that the British occupation of Egypt in 1882 began the Scramble. How far does the evidence support this view?
H: List what other powers did in 1882–85 and ask whether they reacted to Egypt or acted for their own reasons.
H: Consider the economic and humanitarian explanations as alternatives, and the timing of the Congo and German claims.
A: Support: Egypt was the first major seizure by a great power in the period; it angered France, which looked for compensation (Tunisia was taken in 1881, then expansion in West Africa and the Congo), and encouraged Bismarck to side with France against Britain in 1884; the Anglo-German and Berlin negotiations came after it. Challenge: Leopold's Congo project began in 1876–79, before Egypt; France's expansion in Senegal and Tunisia (1881) began earlier; Bismarck's turn in 1884 had German domestic causes (trade, elections) and an Anglo-Portuguese treaty of February 1884 that prompted the conference; economic and missionary motives were long-term causes; the Scramble was too many local events to be traced to one. Judgement: Egypt was a major trigger and a good explanation for the *timing* of Britain's and France's moves, but not a sufficient single cause; the Scramble is better seen as several processes (Congo, West Africa, East Africa, southern Africa) that came together.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Historians have written about the Congo Free State mainly from the evidence of its critics. How far does this make their conclusions unreliable?
H: Who were the main critics, and what were their purposes?
H: What independent evidence (Leopold's commission, the Belgian takeover, Casement's figures) supports their claims, and what remains uncertain (the numbers)?
A: Strengths of critics' evidence: eyewitnesses, photographs, official reports (Casement was a British consul), and independent confirmation from Leopold's own commission of 1904–05 and the Belgian parliament's takeover; the missionaries lived in the districts. Weaknesses: the critics were campaigners (Morel, the Congo Reform Association) and selected their cases; missionaries had their own rivalries with the State and with Catholic missions; propaganda simplified (the single figure of ten million); villagers' voices are mostly missing. The numbers are the weak point, since there was no census before the 1920s. Judgement: the central conclusion (a system of forced labour and violence that caused very large loss of life) is reliable, because it is confirmed by sources that were hostile to the critics or independent of them; the exact scale is uncertain, and sources from African witnesses, which are now being collected, would improve it.

:::recap
- **Scale:** Europeans held about a tenth of Africa in 1870 and about nine tenths in 1914; only Ethiopia and Liberia stayed independent.
- **Motives:** economic (raw materials, markets, investment; Hobson 1902, Lenin 1917), religious and cultural (the civilising mission, Livingstone's three Cs, anti-slavery), national and strategic (prestige; Suez and Egypt 1882; fear of rivals). Strategic rivalry gave the Scramble its speed.
- **Explorers:** Barth (1850–55), Livingstone (died 1873), Stanley (found Livingstone 10 November 1871; Congo for Leopold 1879–84).
- **Means:** quinine, steamers, railways, telegraph, repeating weapons and the Maxim gun (Omdurman, 2 September 1898); treaties, chartered companies, African soldiers and allies.
- **Suez:** opened 17 November 1869; British share purchase 1875 (44 per cent, about £4 million); occupation of Egypt 1882.
- **Government:** direct rule (France, Germany, Belgium) and indirect rule (Britain; Lugard in Northern Nigeria from 1900).
- **Impact:** arbitrary boundaries still in use; the Congo Free State (1885–1908: rubber quotas, the Force Publique, population loss estimated at one to ten million; Casement report 1904; annexed 15 November 1908); missions, health and education; railways built to take goods out.
- **Resistance:** Zulu (Isandlwana 22 January 1879, Ulundi 4 July 1879); Ndebele (1893, 1896–97); Adwa (1 March 1896; Menelik II); Herero and Nama (1904–08; genocide).
- **Judgement:** economic hope and the civilising mission made empire seem natural; strategic rivalry made it a scramble; technology made it cheap; resistance mostly failed but shaped the terms of rule.
