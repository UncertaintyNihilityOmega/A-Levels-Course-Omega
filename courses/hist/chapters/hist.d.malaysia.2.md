:::why
In 1945 Britain came back to a Malaya that it meant to run more efficiently and more fairly than before. Twelve years later the country was independent, and six years after that it had become Malaysia, a federation larger than Malaya, with Singapore and two territories on the island of Borneo. In between, the Malays had defeated a British plan in a matter of months, a communist rising had been fought for twelve years, and a Malay prince had built a partnership of three communal parties that won an election almost without losing a seat. This chapter asks **what factors led to the formation of Malaysia**, so it is a chapter about causes: which mattered most, and how they fitted together. The answer shows how a country with a divided people, an unwilling imperial power and a violent insurgency still reached independence by negotiation and not by revolution.
:::

## What is the question really asking?

*What factors led to the formation of Malaysia?* is a question about **causes**, and it has a trap in it: the formation of Malaysia in 1963 came **after** independence for Malaya in 1957, which came after an insurgency, which came after the failure of a British plan. A strong answer sees that the chain was long and that each link had its own causes. Keep four questions in mind.
1. **What did Britain want?** Did it plan to leave Malaya and form Malaysia, or was it pushed by events? How far did British policy change after the Malayan Union failed?
2. **What did the Malays do?** How far was the outcome made by the Malays' response to the Malayan Union and the leadership of UMNO and the Tunku?
3. **What did the Emergency change?** Did the communist rising slow independence, speed it up or shape its terms?
4. **Why Malaysia, and why Singapore?** Why did the Tunku propose a bigger federation in 1961, why did Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak agree, and why did Singapore leave after less than two years?

The last section, "Why did Malaysia form, and why did Singapore leave?", comes back to these.

:::def Malayan Union
The British plan, put into effect on 1 April 1946, to join the nine Malay states, Penang and Malacca (Singapore stayed apart as a separate colony) into one unit under a British Governor, with the sultans giving up their sovereignty and citizenship open to all who had a real tie with Malaya whatever their race. Malay opposition forced Britain to abandon it in 1948.
:::

:::def UMNO
The United Malays National Organisation, founded in May 1946 to oppose the Malayan Union. It was led by Dato Onn bin Jaafar until 1951 and then by Tunku Abdul Rahman, and it became the main party of the Malays and the senior partner in the Alliance.
:::

:::def Emergency
The state of emergency declared in June 1948 when the Malayan Communist Party began an armed rising. It lasted until 31 July 1960. The British called it an "emergency" and not a war, partly because planters and insurers could not claim for war damage, and so it is the name used in the syllabus.
:::

:::def Merdeka
The Malay word for "independence" or "freedom". It was the cry of the independence movement, and the name given to the day of independence, 31 August 1957 (and the Merdeka Constitution, which took effect then). The Tunku shouted "Merdeka" seven times when he proclaimed independence in the Stadium Merdeka in Kuala Lumpur.
:::

## Re-establishing British rule after the defeat of Japan

### What Britain wanted

When Japan surrendered in August 1945, the British had spent a decade or more thinking about Malaya. Their plans came from a view, shared by officials and by the Colonial Office, that the **pre-war pattern had failed**: it had left three administrations, a weak central government and a defence that had collapsed. Several things pulled in the same direction. Britain wanted a **single, efficient and defensible Malaya** (Singapore's loss had shown the cost of divided government). Many officials believed that Britain should prepare the colonies for **self-government in the long run**, and they thought the Malay sultans' rule was out of date. And, after the war, Britain was poor and wanted Malaya's **tin and rubber**, which earned dollars that were badly needed.

A **British Military Administration (BMA)**, which began on **12 September 1945**, ran the country until civil government returned in April 1946. Meanwhile Sir Harold MacMichael was sent to Malaya to obtain **new treaties** with the nine rulers, and he got their signatures in the last months of 1945. The rulers signed reluctantly. Some had worked under the Japanese and were open to the charge of collaboration, and they were not in a strong position to refuse.

### The Malayan Union, 1946

The scheme was announced in October 1945 and, after a White Paper in January 1946, the **Malayan Union** began on **1 April 1946** under a Governor, **Sir Edward Gent**. It combined the Federated Malay States, the Unfederated Malay States, Penang and Malacca. **Singapore was left out** as a separate Crown colony, since it was the base of British power in the region and had a large Chinese majority which, it was feared, would alter the balance of the Union if it were added.

Its main terms were these.
- **A strong central government** under the Governor in Kuala Lumpur, with advisory councils in the states.
- **The sultans lost their sovereignty.** Under the MacMichael treaties they gave their powers to the Crown and kept only a role in Islam and Malay custom.
- **Citizenship** was open to people born in Malaya or Singapore and to long-term residents, whatever their race. This was the part that mattered most for the future of the country: it would have given Chinese and Indians who had settled in Malaya equal rights with Malays.

To the British this seemed fair and reasonable. It rewarded the Chinese, who had suffered and resisted under Japan, and it created a basis for a single Malayan nation. To the Malays it seemed a **betrayal**.

### Why the Union failed: the Malay response

The Malay reaction was strong and fast, and it took the British by surprise.
1. **They had not been consulted.** MacMichael had got the rulers' signatures quickly and in secret, and the Malays heard of the terms only after they had been signed. Many felt that they had been tricked.
2. **The sultans were the symbols of Malay identity.** To reduce them to figureheads was to attack the heart of what the Malays understood themselves to be. The Malays had been **protected** under British rule as the people of the land, and now this protection was being removed.
3. **Citizenship was the real fear.** After the occupation Malays knew that the Chinese had suffered most and had the guerrilla army, and that the Chinese controlled much of the economy. Citizenship for all, without any protection of Malay rights, seemed to threaten Malays with becoming a minority in their own country.
4. **Malay opinion was now organised.** The associations of the 1930s and the experience of the occupation had given it a basis. In 1946 mass meetings were held across the peninsula, Malays wore white cloths as a sign of mourning, and the sultans, encouraged by the protests, refused to attend the installation of the Governor.

The leader of the movement was **Dato Onn bin Jaafar**, a Johor official and writer who was a gifted speaker and organiser. In March 1946 the Malay associations held a congress, and on **11 May 1946** (some accounts say 10 May) they formed the **United Malays National Organisation (UMNO)** with Onn as president. Its slogan was *Hidup Melayu* ("Long live the Malays"). UMNO did not ask for independence. It asked Britain not to change the position of the sultans and the Malays, and it was supported by the rulers.

The British gave in. Gent's government set up a **Working Committee** with representatives of the rulers, UMNO and the British, and later a consultative committee of other communities, and it drew up a replacement plan.

:::key Why the Malayan Union failed
1. **Methods**: the sultans' consent was got quickly and under pressure, and the Malays were not consulted.
2. **The sultans**: the Union reduced the rulers, who were the symbol of Malay identity and the foundation of British indirect rule, to a minor role.
3. **Citizenship**: equal citizenship for non-Malays seemed to put the Malays' position in their own country at risk.
4. **Organisation**: the Malays now had UMNO, led by Dato Onn, which was able to unite the sultans and the people in the first mass Malay political movement.
5. **British weakness**: Britain needed Malay cooperation to restore order and defend the country, and it gave way within two years.
:::

### Dato Onn and UMNO

Dato Onn is one of the main figures of the story, and the **role of Dato Onn and UMNO** is named in the syllabus. In 1946 he turned a set of local associations into a nation-wide party in a few months, he persuaded the sultans to stand with the people, and he forced a great power to change its mind. His achievement was to show that the Malays could act together, and that the British, who had treated Malays as loyal subjects, would have to treat them as partners.

But his later career shows the limits of his vision. In 1951 he proposed that UMNO should open its membership to **all races** and change its name, as the only way to build a Malayan nation that Britain could hand power to. UMNO rejected the idea, and Onn left the party and formed the **Independence of Malaya Party (IMP)** in September 1951. The IMP was unable to win a following, and the party that he had made went on to lead Malaya to independence under another leader. Historians usually conclude that Onn was ahead of his time, or at least ahead of Malay opinion.

### The Federation of Malaya, 1948

The Working Committee's plan became the **Federation of Malaya Agreement**, signed in January 1948 and in force from **1 February 1948**. It gave the Malays most of what they had asked for.
- The **sultans' sovereignty was restored** and each state kept its ruler and its government.
- A **High Commissioner** (Sir Edward Gent first, and from late 1948 Sir Henry Gurney) and a Federal Executive and Legislative Council ran the central government.
- **Citizenship was made much harder** to get for non-Malays, who had to show long residence and some knowledge of Malay or English.
- **Singapore stayed separate.**
- The Malay **special position** was recognised, as part of the High Commissioner's duty to protect the rights of the Malays.

The Federation was a victory for UMNO. It was also an unequal compromise, in which the non-Malay communities, which made up about half of the population, had been consulted far less than the Malays.

### Non-Malay opposition: the PMCJA and PUTERA

The Chinese and Indians, and many Malays of the left, were unhappy with the plan. The opposition took the form of a joint movement. In December 1946 the **All-Malaya Council of Joint Action (AMCJA)**, later known as the **Pan-Malayan Council of Joint Action (PMCJA)**, was formed under the Straits-born Chinese leader **Tan Cheng Lock** and joined by Chinese and Indian groups, trade unions and left-wing parties. It worked with a Malay left-wing coalition, **Pusat Tenaga Rakyat (PUTERA)**, the "Centre of People's Power", whose parties included the Malay Nationalist Party. The two bodies drew up the **People's Constitutional Proposals** of 1947, which asked for
- a **united Malaya including Singapore**,
- a **fully elected legislature**,
- **equal citizenship** for all who made Malaya their home, and
- Malay as the official language, with the position of the sultans and Islam respected.

They were rejected by the British, who had given the Malays the greater voice, and on **20 October 1947** the groups organised a **hartal**, a general strike in which shops closed and workers stayed at home across the country. It was a strong show of feeling, particularly among the Chinese, and it was ignored. The movement showed that non-Malays wanted a say in the future of the country, and it **left many Chinese and Indians with a sense that they had been excluded**, which the MCP would use.

**What did it all mean?** The failure of the Malayan Union and the setting up of the Federation showed that **no settlement could be made without the Malays**, and that it could not be made without the others either. The question of how to bring them together was to occupy the rest of the story. It is also worth noting that the Federation of 1948 returned Malaya to a pattern close to what had been there before the war, a federation of sultanates under British guidance, and that the British learned how little they could do without allies.

## The Emergency

### Why the Emergency began

The **Malayan Communist Party (MCP)** had come out of the war with an army, prestige and a plan. It first tried legal methods: it ran trade unions and Chinese associations, and for a time cooperated with the British. In **1947** its leadership changed. **Lai Teck**, the general secretary since the 1930s, was exposed in 1946–47 as a man who had worked for the British and then for the Japanese, and he disappeared with much of the party's money before he could be questioned (March 1947). The young **Chin Peng** became the leader, and the party moved to a harder line. In 1947 there were about 300 strikes, and the British, alarmed, passed laws that restricted trade unions and began to arrest and deport communist organisers.

Historians disagree about **what triggered** the revolt. One view is that the MCP, influenced by communist movements elsewhere in Asia, had decided on an **armed struggle** and began it in 1948. Another view, which Chin Peng himself later gave, is that the British crackdown **forced** the party into the jungle. Both views agree that by mid-1948 the party was preparing for violence. On **16 June 1948** three European planters were killed on estates at **Sungai Siput** in Perak. A state of emergency was declared in Perak and then, within days, across Malaya, the MCP was banned, and its leaders and fighters went to the jungle. They formed an army, which from February 1949 was called the **Malayan Races Liberation Army (MRLA)**.

### The communist terror, 1948–49

The first years were hard on the British. The guerrillas attacked **rubber estates and tin mines**, the sources of the colony's wealth, and killed European managers, Asian staff, police officers and Chinese and Malay civilians they accused of helping the government. They cut roads and railways. The aim was to force the British off the estates and to build **liberated areas** in the jungle where the party could rule, on the model of the Chinese communists, who were winning their civil war in China in these years (the People's Republic was declared on 1 October 1949).

The rising had three main sources of strength.
- **The jungle.** Most of Malaya was covered with forest, and the guerrillas could hide, move and strike.
- **The squatters.** About half a million rural people, mostly Chinese, had moved to the jungle edge during the occupation to grow food. They were poor, they had no legal title to the land and many had worked with the MPAJA. They fed the guerrillas, hid them and gave them information, in an organisation called the **Min Yuen** ("People's Movement").
- **Experience.** Many of the fighters had fought the Japanese and had weapons hidden in 1945.

But it had weaknesses too. Around **90 per cent of the guerrillas were Chinese**, and by 1951 the MRLA had about 7,000 men at most. It had almost no Malay support, and the Malays, who formed the greater part of the police and the army, saw it as a Chinese force. It was also dependent on the squatters for food, and this was its weak point, as the Briggs Plan would show.

**British methods.** The government's first response was to use **tough emergency regulations**: detention without trial, deportation, curfews and collective punishment of villages that helped the guerrillas. Some actions were harsh, and some were crimes: in December 1948 soldiers of the Scots Guards killed 24 unarmed villagers at Batang Kali, an event that remains disputed and for which no one was ever convicted. The first years also showed that the British forces and police were too few and untrained in the jungle, and by 1950 the guerrillas had killed hundreds of civilians and security men and were gaining ground.

### The role of Lai Teck and Chin Peng

The syllabus names the **role of Lai Teck and Chin Peng**. Lai Teck was a mystery figure: a Vietnamese-born agent who worked for the British before the war, who was arrested by the Japanese in 1942 and who, to save his life, worked for them as well. His information led to the Batu Caves ambush of 1 September 1942, which killed many of the MCP's leaders. After the war he led the party on a policy of cooperation with the British, and the party's own investigation of his past in 1946–47 ended with his flight. His fall opened the way to **Chin Peng**, born in 1924 in Perak and a leader of the MPAJA who had worked with the British, who led the MCP and the armed struggle from 1947 until the end. Lai Teck's years show the **weakness of the party**, since a double agent led it, and its change to a more violent policy under Chin Peng shows the **turn** from legal struggle to war.

### Britain's reaction and the reasons for its success

The Emergency lasted until **31 July 1960**, though the real fighting was over by about 1957. The insurgents numbered at most about 7,000 at any time, and the British and Commonwealth forces and the Malayan forces were much larger. How was it won? Two names stand out, and the syllabus names both.

**The Briggs Plan (1950).** In April 1950 **Lieutenant-General Sir Harold Briggs** was appointed Director of Operations. He saw that the guerrillas could not be beaten in the jungle while the **squatters** supplied them, so his plan was to separate the guerrillas from the population. It had four parts.
1. **Resettlement.** Squatters were moved into guarded settlements called **New Villages**, with fences, police posts, floodlights and a curfew. By the time it was done about **500,000 people** (estimates from about 470,000 to 573,000, and over a million if all regrouping of plantation and mining workers is counted) had been moved into about 450 villages, most of them Chinese. It was a harsh policy, since families lost their homes and farms, but it cut the Min Yuen from the guerrillas.
2. **Food control.** Rice and other food were rationed and checked, and communities were searched at the gates, so that supplies could not be smuggled out to the jungle.
3. **Coordination.** Civil, police and military leaders were brought together in committees at state and district level, and the war effort was run by a single director.
4. **Clearing by stages.** The country was cleared state by state, beginning in the south and moving north, with "white areas" declared free of communists.

**General Sir Gerald Templer (1952–54).** In October 1951 the guerrillas killed the High Commissioner, **Sir Henry Gurney**, in an ambush near Fraser's Hill (6 October 1951), and the government seemed to be losing. In response Churchill appointed **General Sir Gerald Templer**, announced on 22 January 1952, as both High Commissioner and Director of Operations, with the powers of both civil and military head. Templer was an energetic and forceful soldier, and he did three things.
- He made **intelligence** the centre of the campaign, building up the Special Branch of the police, so that patrols could be directed to the places where the guerrillas actually were, and he used rewards for surrender.
- He **extended the Briggs Plan** and combined it with a political effort: New Villages were given schools, clinics and elected councils, and "white areas" had their curfews and food controls lifted as a reward. He is credited with the saying that the answer lies "not in pouring more troops into the jungle, but in the hearts and minds of the people." Some historians dispute how much of this was his idea.
- He used **collective punishment** where villages were thought to be helping the guerrillas, with long curfews and reduced rations, and he used troops, Special Branch and a large Home Guard of local volunteers.

By the time he left in 1954 the number of guerrilla attacks had fallen sharply, the MRLA had lost most of its areas, and Chin Peng's forces were retreating to the Thai border.

**Other reasons for success.** The Briggs Plan and Templer's leadership are only part of the story.
- **Military forces.** The British brought in the Gurkhas and other Commonwealth troops, the air force, specialist jungle units, and Iban trackers from Sarawak. The Malayan forces, particularly the Malay-dominated police and army, grew to a large size, and the Home Guard reached a quarter of a million.
- **The insurgents' weaknesses.** Their base was one community, the Chinese, and the Malay majority did not support them. From about 1951 the MCP, having lost support by killing civilians, changed its policy to reduce attacks on civilians, but by then it was losing the war.
- **Politics.** Britain made clear that independence would follow when the country was ready, and the Malayan Chinese Association (below) gave Chinese a legal political channel. The communists lost their strongest argument, that independence could only be won by war. The **Baling Talks** of December 1955, in which the Tunku met Chin Peng, failed because Chin Peng would not accept less than recognition of the MCP as a legal party, which the government refused.

:::key Why the Emergency was won
1. **Population control**: the Briggs Plan (1950) resettled about half a million squatters in New Villages, with food control, and cut the guerrillas off from supplies.
2. **Leadership and intelligence**: Templer (1952–54), as High Commissioner and Director of Operations, made Special Branch and intelligence the centre of the war and combined the military campaign with a political one.
3. **Force**: Gurkhas and Commonwealth troops, the air force, a large police force and Home Guard.
4. **The insurgents' weakness**: they were mainly from one community (about 90 per cent Chinese), they killed civilians and they lost support.
5. **Political change**: a promise of independence and a Chinese party, the MCA, that offered a legal path.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: military or political?)
Richard Stubbs, in *Hearts and Minds in Guerrilla Warfare* (1989), argues that Britain won the Emergency by combining military force with political measures, and he stresses that the "hearts and minds" part came after the military and coercive part had gained the upper hand and was a means of consolidating it. Karl Hack has questioned the phrase "hearts and minds", arguing that it hides the role of resettlement, food control and coercion in the success. Both historians therefore doubt the idea that kindness alone won the war. For an essay, test the question "mainly military" by asking what the soldiers did that the police and the administration could not, and whether population control counts as "military", a "political" or a "civil" method. The best answers say that it was all three and that the choice of label decides the answer.
:::

## The growth of political parties and the election of 1955

While the soldiers fought the guerrillas, the British were also trying to build a political alternative. They knew that the communists' best argument was that the only way to independence was war, so they needed to show that there was another way. The answer came from the communities themselves, not from Britain.

### The parties

**UMNO** (1946) was the party of the Malays and the country's biggest political organisation. After Dato Onn left in 1951 it chose **Tunku Abdul Rahman** as president (25 or 26 August 1951, sources differ). The Tunku was a prince of the royal house of Kedah, who had studied at Cambridge and trained as a lawyer, and was a relaxed, popular man, more of a conciliator than a politician of the platform. Unlike Onn, he kept UMNO a **Malay party**, but he was ready to **cooperate** with others.

**The Malayan Chinese Association (MCA)** was founded on **27 February 1949** under Sir **Tan Cheng Lock** (its president until 1958), a Straits-born Chinese leader and the head of the AMCJA, who now chose another path. The British helped to encourage it. They wanted a respectable Chinese party that could draw Chinese away from the communists, and many Chinese businessmen wanted a voice in government and protection of their interests. The MCA also helped the resettlement of Chinese squatters in the New Villages.

**The Malayan Indian Congress (MIC)** had been founded in 1946, with the aim of working for the Indian community and, originally, for Indian independence. It was smaller, and it joined the Alliance in 1954.

**The IMP**, Onn's non-communal party (1951), and his later **Party Negara** (1954) tried to build a party across the communities, but each lost to the communal parties.

### The Alliance

The **UMNO–MCA Alliance** started as a local arrangement. In the Kuala Lumpur municipal election of **February 1952** the two parties, which had separate reasons to fear the IMP, agreed to put up joint candidates and won **9 of the 12 seats**. It was so successful that they made it permanent, and the MIC joined in 1954. The Alliance was a **partnership of three communal parties**, each of which kept its own base and leaders but agreed a common programme and worked out its policy by bargaining behind the scenes: UMNO would speak for the Malays, the MCA for the Chinese and the MIC for the Indians. It asked for **independence**, a united Malaya with **one citizenship** open to all communities, a place for English and Malay and a fair deal for each community.

**Why was it so successful?**
1. It solved the problem that had defeated the IMP: how to unite the communities without asking any of them to give up its identity. Each community voted for its own party and the parties worked together.
2. It had resources. UMNO had the Malay villages and the support of the sultans and civil servants; the MCA had Chinese money and organisation.
3. It was led by men who could **compromise**, and the Tunku's style, not confrontation but patient bargaining, suited it.
4. It had the **British**, who saw the Alliance as the leaders they could hand over to, and who gave it the chance to take power in stages.

### The election of 1955

The British allowed elections to the Federal Legislative Council in **July 1955**, as a step toward self-government. Fifty-two of its 98 seats were elected (the others were filled by nomination or from the government and the states), and the first election was held on **27 July 1955**. It was a landslide. The Alliance won **51 of the 52 seats** on **81.7 per cent of the votes**. The only other winner was the Pan-Malayan Islamic Party (PMIP), which took one seat in Perak. The Tunku became **Chief Minister**.

2. **Resources and organisation**: UMNO's Malay base, the MCA's money and Chinese support, the MIC's Indian voters.
3. **Leadership**: the Tunku's gift for compromise.
4. **Policy**: independence by negotiation, with one citizenship open to all.

## The formation of Malaysia

### Britain's role in granting independence to Malaya

The Tunku's victory made independence a question of when, not whether. Britain had several reasons to agree.
- **The Emergency was being won**, and Britain now had in the Alliance a government that was anti-communist, friendly to the Commonwealth and likely to protect British interests.
- **Decolonisation was the general trend.** India and Pakistan had become independent in 1947, Burma in 1948, and Ghana, the first sub-Saharan colony, would do so in 1957. A Britain that was short of money was less willing to hold down peoples who did not want to be held.
- **British interests could be protected by agreement.** An Anglo-Malayan defence agreement (1957) allowed British and Commonwealth forces to use bases in Malaya, and the large British investments in tin and rubber were safe under a friendly government.
- **The Alliance had proved itself.** The Tunku's government had shown that it could win elections and hold together three communities.

In **January–February 1956** the Tunku led a delegation to London with representatives of the Malay Rulers and the High Commissioner. The talks (18 January to 6 February 1956) agreed that Malaya would become independent by **August 1957**, and that an independent commission should draw up a constitution.

### The Reid Commission and the Merdeka Constitution

The commission, appointed in March 1956, was led by **Lord Reid**, a British judge, and had four other members from Australia, India, Pakistan and Britain (Sir Ivor Jennings, Sir William McKell, Justice B. Malik and Justice Abdul Hamid). It was deliberately made up of outsiders, who could not be said to favour any of the communities. It took evidence in 1956 and made its report in early 1957. A working committee then reviewed it, and the Federal Legislative Council passed it on 15 August 1957.

The **Merdeka Constitution** set up the following.
- **A federation** of eleven states, with a strong central government (a Parliament with an elected lower house and a Senate) and the states keeping some powers.
- **A constitutional monarchy**, with a **Yang di-Pertuan Agong** (a paramount ruler chosen from the nine sultans in turn for a fixed term).
- **Islam** as the religion of the Federation (with freedom of worship for others) and **Malay** as the national language (with English allowed for a period, and Chinese and Tamil schools left in place).
- **Citizenship** for non-Malays, with the principle that people born in the Federation after independence were citizens, and an easier route for those already there.
- **The special position of the Malays**, protected by Article 153: quotas in the civil service, in education places and in some licences. The Reid Commission recommended that this should be reviewed after fifteen years. The Alliance leaders (the working committee) did not accept a time limit, and the review clause was dropped.

Historians and Malaysians describe the package as a **bargain** or "social contract": the Chinese and Indians got citizenship and the vote, the Malays kept their special position, their language, their rulers and Islam. It was a solution that worked in 1957, because the leaders of the three communities negotiated it, and it later became a source of argument about whether the special position was temporary or permanent.

**Independence** came on **31 August 1957**. In the Stadium Merdeka in Kuala Lumpur the Tunku proclaimed it and shouted "Merdeka" seven times, and he became the first Prime Minister. It was a rare case in which a colony reached independence **peacefully and by negotiation** with the colonial power, while fighting a communist insurgency at the same time.

### The support of Tunku Abdul Rahman

The role of the Tunku (syllabus: "the support of Tunku Abdul Rahman") runs through the whole story. He led the Alliance to its victory in 1955, he persuaded Britain to give a date in the 1956 talks, he held the constitutional bargain together and, in **1961**, he proposed the larger federation. He did so for reasons of his own: as a Malay leader he was cautious about adding millions of Chinese, and he needed to be reassured that Malay leadership would remain safe. His influence came from his style, which was relaxed, personal and trusted by the rulers, and from the trust of Malays in his party. It also had limits: he disliked conflict, and in 1965 he chose to end the union with Singapore rather than fight it out.

### The proposal of 1961

For several years the Tunku had refused to consider merger with Singapore, because the extra Chinese would upset the balance of the electorate. What changed his mind in **1961** was **fear of what might happen in Singapore**. Singapore had gained internal self-government in 1959 under the **People's Action Party (PAP)** of **Lee Kuan Yew**. In July 1961 the party split and 13 PAP members were expelled, forming the left-wing **Barisan Sosialis**, which threatened to take power. The Tunku and his government were alarmed by the prospect of an independent Singapore, a Chinese city with a strong left, on the border of Malaya, a "Cuba of the East" at a time when Fidel Castro had come to power in Cuba (1959). It would be a base for communism, or for Chinese influence, that Malaya could not control.

On **27 May 1961**, in a speech to the Foreign Correspondents' Association in Singapore, the Tunku said that Malaya should look to the future, and proposed a plan by which Malaya, Singapore, North Borneo (Sabah), Sarawak and Brunei should be brought closer together in a political and economic association, that is, a **Greater Malaysia**. This answered the problem. If Singapore came in, it would be under a government which controlled internal security, and its Chinese would be balanced by the **Malays and the indigenous peoples of the Borneo territories**, many of whom could be counted with the Malays. Britain, which wanted to leave Singapore and Borneo and to keep its bases, was glad to agree.

### Political, economic and cultural reasons for Singapore's merger

The syllabus asks for three kinds of reason on Singapore's side.

**Political.**
- **Independence.** Britain would not give independence to Singapore on its own, since it kept the base and was afraid of a left-wing government. Merger with Malaya was the route out of colonial rule that Britain would accept, and the PAP's leaders also believed that Singapore could not survive as a separate state.
- **The communist threat.** Lee Kuan Yew and the moderate PAP wanted to defeat the Barisan Sosialis. Merger would put the security forces of the Federation behind them, and the Malayan government would take responsibility for internal security. Critics said that it was also a way of controlling the left.
- **The PAP's survival.** After the split of 1961 the party's majority in the Legislative Assembly fell to one seat. A successful merger would restore its authority.

**Economic.**
- Singapore had no resources except its port and its people, and its prosperity depended on the **entrepot trade**, the import and re-export of the goods of its neighbours (Malayan rubber and tin were shipped through Singapore). It needed a **hinterland**, a larger home market and a **common market** to attract industry and create jobs for a population that was growing fast and had many young unemployed.
- The Federation had the same interest in Singapore's trade and wealth.

**Cultural and social.**
- Singapore and Malaya had been part of the same British Malaya, with ties of family, language and trade. Many Singaporeans had relatives in Malaya.
- The Malays were a minority of about 14 per cent in Singapore, but a Malaya that included Singapore might not be a Malay country, and Singapore's Chinese would be a large new population in it. These were also the grounds of later difficulty.

**The referendum.** To win support, the PAP held a **referendum on 1 September 1962** with three options, none of which was independence on its own. **Option A**, the PAP's, was merger with autonomy for Singapore in labour and education and automatic Malaysian citizenship for Singaporeans. Option B was complete merger as an ordinary state, and Option C was terms no less favourable than those for the Borneo territories. About **71 per cent of all the votes** (397,626 of 561,559, or 95.8 per cent of those which were not blank) were for Option A, in a vote where the opposition urged people to cast blank votes (25.7 per cent did so).

### Sabah and Sarawak

The two territories of north Borneo, North Borneo (Sabah) and Sarawak, were British colonies with small, scattered populations, many of them indigenous peoples, and little political life. Britain and Malaya persuaded them that a union would protect them from outside pressure (Indonesia and the Philippines both had claims or fears) and give them development. A British commission under **Lord Cobbold** (January–April 1962) toured both territories and reported on **1 August 1962**. It found that about **one third** of the people in each territory strongly favoured early Malaysia, another third favoured it with safeguards, and the rest wanted independence first or British rule to continue. It advised that Malaysia should go ahead, with safeguards. These were set out in the **20 points** (Sabah) and **18 points** (Sarawak): protection for religion, language, native rights, immigration control and the use of English.

**Brunei** did not join: its Sultan disagreed with the terms, particularly over the oil revenue and his place among the rulers, and a revolt against its government (December 1962) was put down with British help.

### The Constitution of Malaysia

The Malaysia Agreement was signed in London on **9 July 1963**. After a delay (the date of 31 August was moved to **16 September 1963**, because a United Nations team had first to check opinion in the Borneo territories, and because of the protests of Indonesia and the Philippines), **Malaysia** was formed on 16 September 1963. It was a federation of **fourteen states**: the eleven states of Malaya, Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak.

Look at the figure. Malaya, with 104 of the 159 seats, kept an overwhelming majority, and the three new members together had 55. The constitution had these features.
- **Federal powers**: defence, foreign affairs, internal security and most finance were in the hands of the central government. The Tunku's government in Kuala Lumpur was in control.
- **Singapore's special terms**: it had **15 seats**, fewer than its population would have given, but kept **control of education and labour**, and agreed to pay about **40 per cent of its revenue** to the federal government and to lend 150 million dollars for the development of Borneo. A **common market** was to be created over twelve years. Its citizens could vote only in Singapore.
- **Borneo safeguards**: Sabah and Sarawak kept control over immigration, and many powers over religion, language and the rights of native peoples, with English as an official language in them for a period. The special position of Malays was extended to the natives of Borneo.

It was thus a **compromise** between a unitary state and a loose federation, designed to give the Malay-led government in Kuala Lumpur control of the whole, while giving Singapore and Borneo enough self-government to accept it. It tried to join four societies that had been separate under British rule.

### Singapore's separation, 1965

The union lasted **693 days**, less than two years. Its breakdown is a favourite examination topic, and the syllabus names "political and economic differences".

**Political differences.**
- **PAP against UMNO.** The two parties were rivals for the same position as the leaders of Malaysia. UMNO regarded the Malay peninsula as its own territory, and the PAP, which had been in the Singapore Alliance's way, took a larger part in Malayan politics. In the **April 1964** federal election the PAP, against what UMNO believed to be an understanding, put up candidates in nine seats in the peninsula, and won one (Bangsar, Devan Nair). UMNO's leaders were angry, and the MCA, which had been the Alliance's Chinese party, felt betrayed.
- **"Malaysian Malaysia".** The PAP campaigned for a Malaysia in which all citizens would have the same rights, whatever their race. UMNO read this as an attack on the **special position of the Malays**, which was the basis of its power and of the 1957 bargain. On **9 May 1965** the PAP and other opposition parties formed the Malaysian Solidarity Convention. Some UMNO leaders and the Malay press began a campaign against Lee, whom they accused of arousing the Chinese.
- **Communal tension.** Race riots broke out in Singapore on **21 July 1964**, during a procession for the Prophet Muhammad's birthday: 23 people were killed and 454 hurt, and a second outbreak on 2 September killed 13. Each side blamed the other, and the riots showed how far the communities were from trust.

**Economic differences.**
- The **common market** was not carried out as planned, and Singapore complained that the central government held up its industrial plans.
- **Money.** The federal government asked Singapore to pay 40 per cent of its revenue and also to provide a loan for Borneo, and the two sides disagreed over the amounts and the terms.

**The decision.** The attempt to keep the two sides together by a truce failed in 1964–65. By mid-1965 the Tunku, who had hoped to avoid separation, came to see it as the only way to avoid violence. Goh Keng Swee, one of Singapore's ministers, put the idea of separation to the Malaysian Deputy Prime Minister, Tun Razak, on 15 July 1965, and the agreement was drawn up in secret. On **9 August 1965** the Malaysian Parliament passed the separation Bill by 126 votes to 0, with no PAP member present, and **Singapore became an independent republic** the same day. It was a negotiated parting, and not a war, but it ended the main attempt to bring the Chinese-majority city and the Malay-led federation into one country.

:::key Why Singapore left Malaysia in 1965
1. **Political rivalry**: the PAP's entry into Malayan politics in 1964 (nine seats, one won) angered UMNO and the MCA.
2. **Malaysian Malaysia against Malay special rights**: a clash over the principle of the 1957 bargain.
3. **Communal violence**: the riots of 21 July and 2 September 1964 (23 and 13 deaths).
4. **Economic disputes**: the delayed common market, the 40 per cent revenue payment and the loan for Borneo.
5. **Leaders' judgement**: the Tunku and Lee each concluded by mid-1965 that a clean break was safer than a continuing quarrel.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: who made Malaysia?)
Tan Tai Yong, in *Creating 'Greater Malaysia': Decolonization and the Politics of Merger* (2008), presents the merger as the result of the converging interests of London, Kuala Lumpur and Singapore, each with its own reason for wanting it, in the context of decolonisation and the Cold War. Barbara Watson Andaya and Leonard Andaya, in *A History of Malaysia* (1982; later editions), set the same events in the long story of the country's communities. For an essay on "factors", you can sort the causes by whose they were: **British** (leave with bases and without costs), **Malayan** (control of Singapore and a balance of races), **Singapore's** (independence and a hinterland) and **Borneo's** (development and safeguards). The question is which was decisive, and the best answers add that the factors were linked, since the same fear of a left-wing Singapore moved all three governments.
:::

## Why did Malaysia form, and why did Singapore leave?

Use the four questions at the start of the chapter.

**What did Britain want?** After the failure of the Malayan Union in 1946–48 Britain changed from imposing a plan to **negotiating one**, and it came to see that it could leave Malaya safely if the Alliance took over. In the early 1960s it wanted to give up its colonies in South-East Asia and keep its bases, and Malaysia seemed to do both.

**What did the Malays do?** The Malay response to the Malayan Union gave the country UMNO and the principle that **no settlement could ignore the Malays**. Under the Tunku, UMNO then chose partnership and not confrontation, and this made the Alliance and the Merdeka bargain possible.

**What did the Emergency change?** It did not delay independence so much as shape it. It made the communists' appeal weaker (their army was almost wholly Chinese and was beaten), it made the Chinese leaders of the MCA join the system, and it gave Britain a government it could trust. It also gave the Tunku his fear of communism in Singapore.

**Why Malaysia, and why Singapore?** The proposal of 1961 grew from **fear of a left-wing, Chinese-majority Singapore** and from the wish of Britain to leave. The merger went ahead because each government gained something: Singapore's leaders got independence and a hinterland, Malaya's got control of Singapore's security and a balance of races, and Britain got a withdrawal that protected its bases. It failed in 1965 because the same communal and political questions that had made the Malayan Union fail, **who counts as a citizen and what is the place of the Malays**, were now put in a state where the Chinese had a majority.

:::key A judgement you can defend
Malaysia was formed by **a series of linked causes**: **British** decolonisation and the wish to keep bases; **Malay** political organisation, which forced Britain to negotiate and produced UMNO; the **Emergency** and the fear of communism, which pushed Britain and the Tunku towards a moderate Alliance and later towards controlling Singapore; the **Alliance** and its constitutional bargain; and the interests of the **Singapore and Borneo** leaders. The most important cause was probably the **fear of a left-wing Singapore**, since it moved the Tunku to propose the union and Britain to support it, and the main cause of its failure was the unresolved **question of the Malays' special position** against equal citizenship.
:::

But they were not the main cause, because the shift came from the cutting of the supply line and the removal of the cause of the war, and both were done by civil and political means. Historians such as Richard Stubbs make the same point when they argue that the success lay in combining the methods. The claim that the Emergency was won 'mainly by military methods' is therefore too simple. The fair verdict is that it was won by population control and political change, with force as the means."

It took away the power of the sultans and gave citizenship to the Chinese and Indians, so the Malays set up UMNO under Dato Onn and had meetings. The British then gave up and made the Federation in 1948. So the Malayan Union failed mainly because of the Malays and the view is correct."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): one claim, supported by a description of Malay anger, with no criteria and no test of "mainly". It does not consider other causes of the Union's failure, and the conclusion repeats the question. Table B Level 2 (4–6): accurate but vague: no dates (the Union began on 1 April 1946, UMNO was founded in May 1946, the Federation began on 1 February 1948), nothing on the MacMichael treaties, and no mention of the non-Malay opposition or of British motives.

**The fix.** Choose a criterion and test it. "Malay opposition was the main cause if we ask what **forced** the British to change course. The MacMichael treaties, signed under pressure in late 1945, were seen as a trick, and the loss of the sultans' sovereignty and the offer of equal citizenship brought mass protests, the formation of UMNO under Dato Onn in May 1946, and a boycott by the rulers of the installation of the Governor. Britain, which needed Malay cooperation to restore order, set up a Working Committee and produced a new plan, which came into force on 1 February 1948. But the Union also failed **in its own terms**: it was meant to be a Malayan nation and it excluded Singapore, it was resented by the Chinese left (AMCJA–PUTERA) for different reasons, and it had little time. The Malays decided the outcome, but the British had made the Union's weaknesses." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement and the paragraph is on its way to Level 4 or 5.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was the Malayan Union, and when did it begin?
A: The British plan to join the nine Malay states, Penang and Malacca into one unit under a British Governor, with the sultans giving up their sovereignty and citizenship open to all with a real tie to Malaya, whatever their race. It began on 1 April 1946, with Singapore left as a separate colony.
Q: Why did the Malays oppose it, and what did they do?
A: The sultans lost their sovereignty (the MacMichael treaties were obtained under pressure and in secret), the Malays were not consulted, and equal citizenship seemed to threaten their position in their own country. They held mass meetings, the sultans boycotted the Governor's installation, and in May 1946 they formed UMNO under Dato Onn bin Jaafar.
Q: What were the main terms of the Federation of Malaya of 1 February 1948?
A: The sultans' sovereignty was restored, a High Commissioner and a stronger central government ran the Federation, citizenship was made harder for non-Malays, the special position of the Malays was recognised and Singapore stayed separate.
Q: What were the PMCJA and PUTERA, and what did they ask for?
A: The Pan-Malayan (All-Malaya) Council of Joint Action, led by Tan Cheng Lock, and the Malay left-wing coalition Pusat Tenaga Rakyat. Their People's Constitutional Proposals of 1947 asked for a united Malaya including Singapore, an elected legislature and equal citizenship, and they organised the hartal of 20 October 1947. The British ignored them.
Q: Why did the Malayan Communist Party begin the Emergency in 1948?
A: After the war it had an army and prestige; Lai Teck was exposed and fled in March 1947 and Chin Peng took a harder line; British restrictions on unions in 1948 and the arrest of organisers coincided with the murder of three planters at Sungai Siput on 16 June 1948 and the declaration of the Emergency. Historians disagree whether the party chose war or was pushed.
Q: What was the Briggs Plan?
A: A plan of 1950 by Lieutenant-General Sir Harold Briggs, Director of Operations, to cut the guerrillas off from supplies by moving about half a million squatters, mostly Chinese, into about 450 guarded New Villages, controlling food and clearing the country state by state.
Q: What did General Templer do in Malaya?
A: Appointed High Commissioner and Director of Operations in January 1952, he put intelligence and Special Branch first, built on the Briggs Plan, gave the New Villages schools, clinics and councils, lifted controls in "white areas", used curfews and collective punishment, and is credited with the "hearts and minds" approach.
Q: How did the Alliance win the 1955 election?
A: UMNO and the MCA had been a successful partnership since the Kuala Lumpur municipal election of 1952 (9 of 12 seats), and the MIC joined in 1954; the Alliance won 51 of the 52 elected seats on 81.7 per cent of the votes on 27 July 1955, and the Tunku became Chief Minister.
Q: What were the main terms of the Merdeka Constitution?
A: A federation under a Yang di-Pertuan Agong chosen from the nine rulers, Islam as the religion of the Federation, Malay as the national language, citizenship for non-Malays, and the special position of the Malays (Article 153). Independence came on 31 August 1957.
Q: What was the Tunku's proposal of 27 May 1961, and why did Singapore agree to it?
A: A plan to bring Malaya, Singapore, Sabah, Sarawak and Brunei into a Greater Malaysia. Singapore's leaders saw it as the route to independence from Britain, a defence against the Barisan Sosialis and a way to get a hinterland and a common market. The referendum of 1 September 1962 gave about 71 per cent of all votes to the PAP's option.
Q: Why did Singapore separate from Malaysia on 9 August 1965?
A: The PAP's entry into the 1964 election and its "Malaysian Malaysia" campaign clashed with UMNO and the special position of the Malays; there were race riots on 21 July and 2 September 1964; there were disputes over the common market and revenue; and the Tunku and Lee concluded that separation was safer. Parliament voted 126 to 0.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why the Malayan Union failed.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: which reasons forced the change, and which were underlying. Malay opposition: the MacMichael treaties, the loss of the sultans' sovereignty, citizenship, UMNO under Dato Onn (May 1946), the boycott; British weakness and the need for Malay cooperation; non-Malay left-wing opposition (AMCJA–PUTERA) for different reasons; its leaving out of Singapore and its short life. Level 5 ranks the Malay response as decisive and explains why the British gave way.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The Federation of Malaya of 1948 was a victory for the Malays.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: the sultans' sovereignty restored; harder citizenship; the special position recognised; UMNO consulted. Challenge: the British kept control through the High Commissioner; the Federation was a compromise on administrative unity; non-Malay opposition (hartal, 20 October 1947) was ignored but would return; it kept the central principle of a strong government. Level 5 judges that it was a victory on citizenship and sovereignty but not on power.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the role of Dato Onn bin Jaafar in Malay politics to 1951.
A: Indicative content. For: he formed UMNO (May 1946) and forced Britain to abandon the Malayan Union; he united the rulers and the people. Against: he lost UMNO when he proposed to open it to all races in 1951 and his IMP (September 1951) failed; others (the Tunku, the sultans) shaped the outcome. Level 5 judges him as crucial in 1946 and wrong, or too early, by 1951.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons for the success of the British in the Emergency.
A: Indicative content. The Briggs Plan; Templer; intelligence; force; the insurgents' weakness; the political promise of independence and the MCA. Level 5 ranks the factors and explains how they linked, with a judgement on whether the Briggs Plan or political change was the main cause.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Emergency delayed independence for Malaya.' Evaluate this view.
H: When was independence promised and when did it come?
H: Did the Emergency also speed up the process by strengthening the Alliance?
A: Indicative content. Delay: the British said that independence could not be given until the communists were defeated; the need for British troops and administrators; the New Villages and emergency powers; the 1948 Federation was kept strongly centralised. Speed: the Emergency produced the MCA and the Alliance, and the need to offer an alternative to the communists led the British to talk about independence by 1952–55, and the 1955 election; the Alliance's triumph; London talks in 1956. Level 5 judges that the Emergency shaped independence more than it delayed it.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the Alliance was the main reason why Malaya reached independence peacefully in 1957.
H: What did the Alliance do that earlier parties had not?
H: What did Britain and the Emergency contribute?
A: Indicative content. Support: the partnership of three communal parties; 51 of 52 seats on 81.7 per cent in 1955; the Merdeka bargain; the Tunku's compromise. Challenge: British readiness to go; the Emergency's effect on British policy and on the MCP; the Reid Commission; the Rulers' agreement. Level 5 judges the Alliance as the main force for peaceful transfer but dependent on British willingness and the defeat of the MCP.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons for the merger of Singapore with Malaya in 1963.
H: Give political, economic and cultural reasons from each side.
H: Which side wanted it more?
A: Indicative content. Singapore: independence, the defeat of the Barisan Sosialis, the common market and a hinterland, family ties. Malaya: fear of a left-wing Singapore, the wish to control internal security, the balance of races with Sabah and Sarawak. Britain: decolonisation and bases. Referendum of 1 September 1962 (about 71 per cent of all votes for Option A). Level 5 weighs the reasons and judges that political fear on both sides was decisive.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that economic differences were the main reason for Singapore's separation from Malaysia.
A: Indicative content. Support: the delayed common market; the 40 per cent revenue payment and the loan for Borneo. Challenge: communal tension (riots, 1964); political rivalry (nine seats, 1964); the Malaysian Malaysia dispute. Level 5 judges that economic differences were real but secondary to the communal and political ones.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Malaysia was formed more to suit British interests than those of the peoples of the region.' Evaluate this view.
H: What did Britain gain from the merger, and what did each other government gain?
H: Did the peoples of Sabah and Sarawak have a real say?
A: Support: Britain wanted to leave Singapore and Borneo and keep its bases; the Cobbold Commission found only about a third of the people strongly in favour; Brunei did not join; the merger was decided by governments. Challenge: the Tunku and Lee each had their own strong reasons; the Singapore referendum (about 71 per cent for the PAP's option); the 20 and 18 points; the peoples had safeguards. A* judgement: British interests were important but they were one of several converging interests, and Tan Tai Yong's view of a three-way convergence is the best framework; the voice of the Borneo peoples was weak.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The communal bargain of 1957 made the formation of Malaysia possible and also made its first form impossible.' Evaluate this view.
H: What was the bargain, and what did Malaysia add to it?
H: What did "Malaysian Malaysia" challenge?
A: Support: the bargain (citizenship in return for the Malays' special position) gave stability and the Alliance; it made the Tunku willing to add Borneo's peoples to balance Singapore's Chinese; it was challenged by the PAP's equal-rights campaign, which led to separation in 1965. Challenge: other causes (the PAP–UMNO rivalry, the riots, economic disputes, personalities); the bargain survived the separation. A* judgement: the bargain was central, since it set the terms on which Malaysia could be formed and the point at which Singapore could not fit, but the personalities and the economics were part of how the break happened.

:::recap
- **Malayan Union (1 April 1946)**: one unit under a Governor, sultans' sovereignty given up (MacMichael treaties, late 1945), citizenship for all with a tie to Malaya; Singapore apart. Malay opposition: UMNO (May 1946, Dato Onn); the British gave in.
- **Federation of Malaya (1 February 1948)**: sultans restored, a High Commissioner, harder citizenship, Malay special position. Non-Malay opposition: AMCJA/PMCJA (Tan Cheng Lock) and PUTERA; People's Constitutional Proposals 1947; hartal 20 October 1947.
- **Emergency (June 1948 to 31 July 1960)**: Lai Teck fled in March 1947, Chin Peng led the MCP; Sungai Siput, 16 June 1948; MRLA about 7,000 at most, about 90 per cent Chinese; communist terror 1948–49.
- **British success**: Briggs Plan (1950; about 500,000 resettled in about 450 New Villages; food control); Gurney killed 6 October 1951; Templer (from 22 January 1952; intelligence, "hearts and minds", white areas); force; the insurgents' weakness; the promise of independence.
- **Parties**: UMNO (1946; Tunku from 1951), MCA (27 February 1949; Tan Cheng Lock), MIC (1946), Alliance (Kuala Lumpur, February 1952, 9 of 12 seats; MIC 1954); 1955 election 27 July: 51 of 52 seats on 81.7 per cent.
- **Independence**: London talks (18 January to 6 February 1956); Reid Commission; Merdeka Constitution (Agong, Islam, Malay, citizenship, Article 153); Merdeka 31 August 1957.
- **Malaysia**: Tunku's speech 27 May 1961; Barisan Sosialis (July 1961); Singapore referendum 1 September 1962 (about 71 per cent of all votes for Option A); Cobbold Commission (reported 1 August 1962); Malaysia Agreement 9 July 1963; formed 16 September 1963 (159 seats; Singapore 15, 40 per cent revenue, labour and education).
- **Separation (9 August 1965)**: PAP in 1964 election (9 seats, 1 won), Malaysian Malaysia (Convention 9 May 1965), riots (21 July 1964: 23 dead; 2 September: 13 dead), economic disputes; Parliament 126 to 0.
- **Judgement**: Malaysia came from linked causes (British decolonisation, UMNO's organisation, the Emergency and the fear of communism, the Alliance's bargain, Singapore's and Borneo's interests); it broke on the unresolved question of Malay special rights against equal citizenship.
