:::why
In 1961 the United States was the richest country in history and the leader of the "free world", yet one American in five was poor, Black Americans were denied the vote across the South, and many people feared the Cold War would end in nuclear war. Thirteen years later one President had been murdered, another had been driven out of the race by a war he could not win, and a third had resigned to avoid being removed from office. In between, the federal government did more for the poor, the old, the sick, the young and Black citizens than at any time since the New Deal. The key question asks how significant these three presidencies were. To answer it you must weigh what each President meant to do, what Congress and events let him do, and what lasted.
:::

## How to judge a presidency: four criteria

The key question asks how *significant* these presidencies were. Significance is a judgement, so you need a way to measure it. This chapter uses four criteria, and your essays can use them too.
1. **Achievement against intention:** how much of what the President promised did he get passed or done?
2. **Scale of impact:** how many Americans, and which ones, were affected?
3. **Permanence:** did the change last after the President left office, or was it undone?
4. **Constraints and context:** what did Congress, the courts, public opinion, the economy and events let him do? A President can be significant because he overcame obstacles, or because events made him significant.

A fifth question runs under the other four. Presidents are judged by their results, but also by what they *meant*: Kennedy by hope and style, Johnson by ambition, Nixon by the abuse of power that ended his career. You must separate the image from the record.

## Kennedy's election victory in 1960

### The candidates and the campaign

Dwight Eisenhower could not stand again (the Twenty-Second Amendment limits a President to two terms), so the Republican candidate was his Vice-President, **Richard Nixon**, aged 47. The Democrat was **John F. Kennedy**, aged 43, a senator from Massachusetts who had come close to being nominated for Vice-President in 1956. Kennedy was a war hero (he commanded a torpedo boat, PT-109, in the Pacific), the son of a very rich father, and a Roman Catholic. No Catholic had been elected President since the Democrat Al Smith lost in 1928, and many voters, especially Protestants in the South, distrusted him for it. Kennedy met the question head on in a speech to Protestant ministers in Houston on 12 September 1960, saying that his religion should not decide whether he could serve.

:::def The New Frontier
Kennedy's name for his programme and style, taken from his speech accepting the Democratic nomination in Los Angeles on 15 July 1960, in which he said Americans stood "on the edge of a New Frontier". It meant new action at home and abroad: to restart a sluggish economy, help the poor, protect civil rights and win the Cold War, and it asked citizens to give their energy to the country.
:::

### Why Kennedy won

Kennedy won by 303 electoral votes to Nixon's 219, with 15 votes going to Senator Harry Byrd of Virginia (cast by unpledged electors from Mississippi and Alabama and one elector from Oklahoma). The popular vote was one of the closest in American history: 34,220,984 for Kennedy against 34,108,157 for Nixon, a margin of 112,827 votes or about 0.17 per cent. Illinois was won by under 9,000 votes and Texas by about 46,000, and a swing in those two states alone would have changed the result. So no single reason "won" it, and a good answer weighs several.
- **The New Frontier and Kennedy's message.** Kennedy accused the Republicans of drift. The economy had been through recessions in 1957–58 and again from 1960, and in 1960 unemployment was above 5 per cent. Kennedy promised to get the country moving again, and said that the USA was falling behind the USSR in missiles (the "missile gap", which later turned out not to exist). His call for energy and sacrifice appealed to younger voters and to those weary of the Eisenhower years.
- **Kennedy's youth, style and campaigning skill.** Kennedy was a tireless, well-funded campaigner with a brilliant team run by his brother **Robert Kennedy**, and a wide organisation in the states. His wealth paid for polls, staff and advertising. His youth was both a weakness (Nixon stressed his own "experience") and a strength: it made the Eisenhower generation seem old, and Kennedy's wit and good looks suited the new medium of television.
- **The television debates.** The four debates, the first on **26 September 1960** in Chicago, were the first between presidential candidates broadcast on television. About 70 million people watched the first. Nixon, recovering from a knee infection, pale, thin and unshaven, looked tired next to a tanned and composed Kennedy. It is often said that those who listened on the radio thought Nixon won and those who watched on television thought Kennedy did, but the evidence for that is slight. The sure points are that the debates made Kennedy, until then less well known, an equal of the Vice-President, and that the first was the most watched.
- **The importance of Johnson.** Kennedy chose **Lyndon B. Johnson**, the Senate Majority Leader from Texas, as his running-mate. Johnson was a former rival, and many of Kennedy's liberal supporters disliked the choice. But Johnson could hold the South, which the Democrats needed. Kennedy won Texas by about 2 per cent, and North and South Carolina and Louisiana too. Without Johnson's state and the Southern vote the electoral college result would probably have gone the other way.
- **Nixon's campaign.** Nixon made mistakes. He promised to visit all fifty states and so wasted time in small states he had no chance of winning. He wanted to run on the Eisenhower record, but Eisenhower was lukewarm, and when asked for an example of Nixon's contribution to his administration said he would need a week to think of one. Nixon also did little to win the Black vote, fearing to lose white Southern support.
- **The African American vote.** In October 1960 Martin Luther King Jr was arrested at a sit-in at an Atlanta department store and then sentenced to four months of hard labour in a Georgia state prison. Kennedy telephoned his wife, **Coretta Scott King**, on 26 October to express concern, and his brother Robert Kennedy telephoned the judge, helping to get King released on bail. Nixon said nothing publicly. Martin Luther King Sr, who had earlier been a Republican supporter, announced that he would now vote for Kennedy, and the Kennedy campaign circulated pamphlets about the call in Black churches and neighbourhoods. Estimates are that about 70 per cent of Black voters supported Kennedy, more than had voted for the Democrat Adlai Stevenson in 1956, and historians argue that in close states such as Illinois and South Carolina Black votes were large enough to decide the result.

:::key Why Kennedy won, ranked
**Reasons:** the narrow result means *several* factors were needed. **Ranking to defend:** (1) the *Democratic base and Johnson* (the South, Texas and the Catholic and union vote, which gave the structural basis for victory); (2) *the African American vote* (large and decisive in close states, and helped by the call to Coretta Scott King); (3) *the television debates* (which raised Kennedy to equal status and mattered because the race was so close); (4) *Kennedy's campaign skill and the New Frontier* (energy, organisation and money); (5) *Nixon's weaknesses* (mistakes, and the cool backing of Eisenhower).
**Judgement to defend:** in an election this close, *each* of these was needed, but **the Democratic coalition, strengthened by Johnson and the Black vote, was the foundation**; the debates, the campaign and Nixon's mistakes then decided which side of a very even contest Kennedy finished on.
:::

## Kennedy's domestic policies: the New Frontier

### What Kennedy wanted and what he could do

Kennedy took office on 20 January 1961. In his inaugural address he asked Americans to "ask not what your country can do for you", and the style was bold. The substance was more cautious. Kennedy had won by the narrowest of margins and the Democrats lost seats in the House in 1960, and Congress was run by the **conservative coalition** of Southern Democrats and Republicans, who controlled the key committees and blocked bills on health, education and civil rights. The House Rules Committee, which decides what the House may vote on, was at first controlled by a conservative Southerner, Howard Smith of Virginia; in January 1961 Kennedy and Speaker Sam Rayburn had to enlarge the committee from twelve to fifteen members to get it to let bills through, and they won by only five votes. This is the context for every measure below.

### Measures to stimulate the economy

In 1961 the economy was coming out of a recession and unemployment, which averaged 6.7 per cent that year, was high. Kennedy's advisers, led by the economist **Walter Heller**, believed in Keynesian economics: that government should stimulate demand when the economy is slack.
- **Early steps (1961).** The government sped up federal spending, extended unemployment pay, and raised the **minimum wage** from $1.00 to $1.25 an hour (phased in by 1963), extending it to 3.6 million more workers.
- **The tax cut.** In January 1963 Kennedy proposed a large cut in income tax to boost spending and investment, even though it would add to the deficit, to the surprise of many who thought a President should balance the budget. Congress held it up, and it passed only after his death, as the Revenue Act of February 1964, which cut the top personal rate from 91 to 70 per cent (phased in over 1964–65).
- **Keeping prices down.** In April 1962 Kennedy forced US Steel to withdraw a price rise by threatening investigations and the loss of defence contracts. Business leaders were angry, but he also showed that he wished to control inflation.
- **Results.** The economy grew steadily through the 1960s, and unemployment fell to 5.2 per cent in 1964 and 4.5 per cent in 1965. Kennedy's contribution was less the measures he passed than the *change of thinking* he began and Johnson completed.

### Support for the poor, housing and welfare

- **Area Redevelopment Act (May 1961).** It gave loans and grants of nearly $400 million to areas of high unemployment, such as the coalfields of Appalachia, which Kennedy had seen in the West Virginia primary of 1960.
- **Help for the unemployed.** The Manpower Development and Training Act of 1962 paid for retraining of workers whose jobs had gone because of automation.
- **Welfare.** Kennedy extended aid to families with unemployed fathers, started a pilot food stamp scheme in 1961 and, through the Public Welfare Amendments of 1962, paid for social workers and services to help families to leave welfare. The Social Security Amendments of 1961 allowed men to retire on reduced pensions at 62.
- **Housing.** The **Housing Act of 1961** (June) authorised about $4.9 billion for the clearing of slums, the building of housing for middle- and lower-income families and for the old, and for urban transport. In November 1962 he signed an executive order banning racial discrimination in federally supported housing, but only after delaying it for nearly two years, and its reach was narrow.
- **Poverty.** In 1963, in the wake of Michael Harrington's *The Other America* (1962), Kennedy asked Walter Heller and others to plan a more far-reaching attack on poverty. He did not live to see it launched. Johnson did, in 1964.

### Healthcare and education

These were Kennedy's two great failures in Congress.
- **Healthcare.** His plan to provide health insurance for the old through Social Security (the King-Anderson bill) was strongly opposed by the American Medical Association, which called it "socialised medicine", and by conservatives. It was defeated in the Senate on 17 July 1962 by 52 votes to 48. The idea, however, had become a mainstream cause, and it was passed three years later. Kennedy did sign a Community Mental Health Act in October 1963, which paid for local mental health centres.
- **Education.** His bill of 1961 for federal aid to public schools was blocked in the House, mainly because of a quarrel over aid to Catholic schools: Kennedy, himself a Catholic, would not support aid for church schools, and Catholic leaders would not accept a bill that did not. Only the narrower Higher Education Facilities Act, which paid for college buildings, passed (in December 1963, just after his death), and the great school-aid bill had to wait for Johnson.

### Equal rights for women

- **The Commission.** On 14 December 1961 Kennedy set up the **President's Commission on the Status of Women**, chaired until her death in 1962 by Eleanor Roosevelt. Its report, *American Women* (1963), documented discrimination in pay, jobs and the law, and recommended better child care and equal pay.
- **The Equal Pay Act.** Kennedy signed it on 10 June 1963. It required equal pay for men and women doing equal work in the same workplace. It was a landmark, although it had loopholes, covered many jobs only partly, and women still earned about 60 cents for each dollar earned by men.
- **Limits.** Kennedy did not otherwise place women's rights near the centre of his agenda; the Commission was partly a way of avoiding the Equal Rights Amendment, which women in the labour movement feared would remove protective laws. But the commission's findings led to state commissions and to the movement described in [A time of challenges, 1961–74 (4)](#hist.d.usa61.4).

### Support for farmers

Farm incomes were falling and surpluses were piling up in government stores. The **Agricultural Act of 1961** raised price supports and paid farmers to take land out of production to cut surpluses, and the Food for Peace programme, begun under Eisenhower and expanded by Kennedy, sent surplus food abroad. Farm incomes rose a little, but the scheme was costly, and the problem of too many small farmers and too much grain remained.

### Civil rights

Kennedy's record on civil rights was cautious until 1963. He was reluctant to lose the support of Southern Democrats in Congress, and his first aim was to act by **executive order** and by the courts, not by new laws.
- **Executive Order 10925** (6 March 1961) set up the Committee on Equal Employment Opportunity and told federal contractors to take "affirmative action" to ensure that applicants were treated equally regardless of race. This was the first official use of the phrase. The committee had little power to enforce.
- **The Justice Department.** The Attorney General, **Robert Kennedy**, sued to protect Black voters, and he sent 400 federal marshals to Montgomery, Alabama, in May 1961 when the Freedom Riders were attacked by mobs. In the **Ole Miss crisis** (30 September 1962) he and the President sent marshals, and then 23,000 troops, to enforce the admission of James Meredith, the first Black student at the University of Mississippi, after riots in which two people died.
- **The turning point of 1963.** In April and May, in Birmingham, Alabama, Commissioner of Public Safety Eugene "Bull" Connor used police dogs and fire hoses on Black children and young people who were marching with Martin Luther King's Southern Christian Leadership Conference. The pictures, shown around the world, shocked northern opinion. On 11 June, when Governor George Wallace stood in the doorway of the University of Alabama and then backed down, Kennedy made a television address saying that civil rights was "a moral issue". On 19 June he sent Congress a **civil rights bill** to ban segregation in public places and to give the government power to cut off funds from segregated programmes. After the March on Washington on 28 August 1963, about a quarter of a million people gathered at the Lincoln Memorial for the "I have a dream" speech of Martin Luther King. The bill was still stuck in Congress when Kennedy was killed in Dallas on 22 November 1963.

:::key Judging the New Frontier
**Achievements:** the minimum wage rise and wider coverage; the Area Redevelopment, Housing and Manpower Acts; the Equal Pay Act (1963) and the Commission on women; the first affirmative action order; the tax-cut idea that became the Revenue Act of 1964; the move to civil rights legislation in 1963.
**Failures and limits:** Medicare defeated (1962); federal aid to schools blocked (1961); the tax cut and civil rights bill not passed in his lifetime; slow on civil rights until Birmingham; a conservative Congress.
**Judgement to defend:** Kennedy's **legislative record was thin** (most of the great measures became law under Johnson), but the New Frontier was significant as **the agenda-setter**: it put tax cuts, health care, aid for education, civil rights and the war on poverty on the national list, and the shock of his death helped Johnson to pass them.
:::

## Johnson and the Great Society

### Johnson's vision

Lyndon Johnson was sworn in as President on the afternoon of 22 November 1963, on the plane at Dallas. He was a very different man from Kennedy: a Texan who had grown up in poverty in the Hill Country, a New Deal congressman and a master of the Senate who knew how to count votes and when to threaten and when to flatter. He used the grief after Kennedy's death, asking Congress to pass the programme "in his memory", and in May 1964 he gave the programme its name. Speaking at the University of Michigan on 22 May 1964 he said that:

:::note Evidence: Johnson's Great Society speech
"The Great Society rests on abundance and liberty for all. It demands an end to poverty and racial injustice, to which we are totally committed in our time."

*From Lyndon B. Johnson, "Remarks at the University of Michigan", Ann Arbor, 22 May 1964. Two sentences quoted exactly. A speech to students at a graduation, written to inspire, so it shows intention, not what Congress later passed.*
:::

:::def The Great Society
Johnson's name for his domestic programme of 1964–68: an attempt, by federal law and federal money, to end poverty and racial injustice and to widen opportunity in education, health, cities and the countryside. It went beyond the New Deal, which had set out to rescue people in a depression, by aiming to improve life for people in a rich country.
:::

Johnson's strengths were his political skill and his majorities. In the election of **3 November 1964** he beat the Republican Senator **Barry Goldwater**, who opposed the Civil Rights Act and the growth of federal power, with 61.1 per cent of the vote and 486 electoral votes to Goldwater's 52. The Democrats gained some three dozen seats in the House (to 295 against 140) and two in the Senate (68 to 32), a majority big enough to defeat the conservative coalition. The 89th Congress (1965–66) passed more important laws than any since 1933. Johnson pushed them through quickly, aware that a President's strength fades and that the shock of Kennedy's death and the 1964 landslide would not last.

### The war on poverty

In his State of the Union address on 8 January 1964 Johnson declared an "unconditional war on poverty". The **Economic Opportunity Act** (20 August 1964) authorised about $1 billion in the first year and set up the **Office of Economic Opportunity (OEO)**, led by Sargent Shriver. It created:
- the **Job Corps**, residential training for young people from poor homes;
- **VISTA** (Volunteers in Service to America), a domestic Peace Corps;
- **Community Action Programs**, run by local agencies on a principle of "maximum feasible participation" of the poor themselves;
- and from 1965 **Head Start**, pre-school education for poor children (more than half a million children in its first summer).

The **Food Stamp Act** of 1964 made the food stamp scheme permanent, and the Appalachian Regional Development Act of 1965 gave roads and aid to a mountain region of deep poverty.

The thinking was that poverty was caused by lack of opportunity, education and skills, not simply lack of money, so the programmes aimed to give "a hand up, not a hand-out". The Community Action Programs soon caused trouble: they gave money directly to neighbourhood groups and so threatened city mayors, who complained that the government was paying people to organise against them. Johnson, himself a politician, took the mayors' side, and in 1966 and 1967 control of the local agencies was moved back towards city hall.

### Medical care

- **Medicare** and **Medicaid** were created by the Social Security Amendments signed on 30 July 1965. Medicare gave hospital and, for a small premium, doctor insurance to Americans aged 65 and over, whatever their income. Medicaid, paid for jointly by federal and state governments, gave health care to some of the poor. Johnson signed the bill at the Truman Library in Independence, Missouri, with Harry Truman, who had proposed national health insurance in 1945, at his side.
- The effect was large. About 19 million older Americans were enrolled in Medicare in its first year, and for the first time hospitals which wished to be paid had to treat Black patients on equal terms, so Medicare helped to desegregate Southern hospitals.
- Other health measures included funds for heart, cancer and stroke research and for training doctors and nurses.

### Civil rights

Johnson was a Southerner who pushed civil rights through Congress against his own region. He told Congress in his first address that no memorial would honour Kennedy better than the early passage of the civil rights bill.
- **The Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964).** It banned segregation in hotels, restaurants and other public places (Title II) and discrimination in employment on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin (Title VII), set up the **Equal Employment Opportunity Commission**, and allowed federal funds to be cut off from segregated schools and programmes (Title VI). It passed the Senate only after the longest filibuster in its history, ended by a vote of 71 to 29 on 10 June 1964.
- **The Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965).** After the attack by state troopers on marchers at Selma, Alabama, on 7 March 1965 ("Bloody Sunday"), Johnson appeared before Congress on 15 March and used the civil rights slogan "We shall overcome". The Act banned literacy tests, and let federal examiners register voters where fewer than half of those eligible were registered. In Mississippi the proportion of Black adults of voting age on the roll rose from about 7 per cent in 1965 to about 60 per cent by 1967, according to the US Commission on Civil Rights.
- **Other acts.** The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended national-origin quotas, and the **Fair Housing Act** of 11 April 1968, passed a week after the assassination of Martin Luther King, banned discrimination in the sale and rental of most housing. This is covered in depth in [A time of challenges, 1961–74 (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3).

### Education reform

- The **Elementary and Secondary Education Act (ESEA)** of 11 April 1965 was the first big programme of federal aid to schools, with more than a billion dollars a year, most of it for schools with many poor children (Title I). It avoided the old quarrel over church schools by giving help to *children*, not to schools. Johnson signed it at the old schoolhouse in Johnson City, Texas, where he had started school, beside his first teacher.
- The **Higher Education Act** (8 November 1965) provided scholarships, student loans and money for colleges.
- Head Start, described above, extended education to pre-school children.

### Urban renewal, rural development and the environment

- The **Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD)** was created in 1965, and its first head, Robert Weaver, became the first Black Cabinet member. The **Model Cities** programme (1966) aimed to improve all aspects of life, not simply buildings, in chosen poor neighbourhoods. The Housing and Urban Development Act of 1965 included rent supplements for the poor.
- **Rural development** included the Appalachian Regional Development Act of 1965 (roads, health centres and aid for the poorest hill counties) and aid to rural water and sewage systems.
- **Environmental initiatives** were supported by Lady Bird Johnson, who campaigned for "beautification". They included the Wilderness Act (3 September 1964), which protected 9 million acres in a national system, the Water Quality Act of 1965, the Highway Beautification Act of 1965 and the Wild and Scenic Rivers Act of 1968. The Public Broadcasting Act and the National Endowments for the Arts and the Humanities (1965) extended aid to culture.

### Reasons for opposition to the reforms

The Great Society met opposition, which grew after 1966.
- **Conservatives.** Goldwater Republicans and Southern Democrats argued that the programmes cost too much, gave Washington too much power over states and individuals, and encouraged dependency on welfare. Medicare, said the American Medical Association, would bring "socialised medicine".
- **Cost and the Vietnam War.** Johnson tried to pay for guns and butter together, and in 1965 and 1966 did not ask for a tax rise. As the war grew, so did the cost (see [A time of challenges, 1961–74 (2)](#hist.d.usa61.2)), inflation rose, and in 1967 he had to ask for a 10 per cent tax surcharge, passed in June 1968. The war also took Johnson's attention and credibility from the reforms.
- **Disorder in the cities.** Riots broke out in Watts, Los Angeles, in August 1965 (34 dead), and in Newark and Detroit in July 1967 (26 and 43 dead). Many white voters blamed the reforms for raising expectations and rewarding disorder, and Johnson's "law and order" opponents gained from this "white backlash". The Kerner Commission (February 1968) blamed white racism.
- **Problems with the programmes.** The war on poverty promised "victory" and could not deliver it, and the Community Action Programs gave Johnson's enemies a target. Critics on the left said the programmes spent too little on jobs and income, and critics on the right that they spent too much on social workers.
- **Politics.** In the mid-term elections of November 1966 the Republicans gained 47 seats in the House, and the conservative coalition could again block bills. After 1966 the great flow of reforms slowed.

### The impact of the reforms

- **Poverty fell.** The official rate fell from about 19.0 per cent in 1964 to 12.1 per cent in 1969 and 11.1 per cent in 1973, a fall of 7.9 percentage points in nine years. How much of the fall was due to the Great Society and how much to the economic boom of the 1960s is argued: most economists give both a share, and give the credit for the largest fall among the old to Social Security and Medicare.
- **Health.** Medicare and Medicaid gave tens of millions access to care and cut the poverty of the old.
- **Education and civil rights.** Federal money reached poor schools, and the Voting Rights Act transformed Black registration in the South.
- **Cities and the war on poverty.** The programmes failed to end the inner-city crisis, and the riots showed this.
- **Government.** The federal government took on a role in health, education and welfare that no later President could reverse. It also raised expectations that could not be met and created a backlash that shaped politics for a generation.

:::key Judging the Great Society
**Achievements:** the Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965); Medicare and Medicaid; the Elementary and Secondary Education Act and Higher Education Act; the Economic Opportunity Act and Head Start; HUD; the Wilderness Act and other environmental measures; poverty down from 19.0 to 11.1 per cent (1964–73).
**Failures and limits:** the war on poverty never ended poverty; the cities exploded in riots; the Community Action Programs quarrelled with mayors; Vietnam took money and trust; the backlash helped Nixon and Wallace in 1968.
**Judgement to defend:** the Great Society was **the most far-reaching programme of social reform since the New Deal** and, in Medicare, civil rights and education, **changed American life for good**; its weakness was that it promised more than a Congress and a war-straining budget could deliver.
:::

## Nixon: the election of 1968 and the domestic policy

### Why Nixon won in 1968

The year 1968 was one of the most violent in American history. In January the Tet Offensive shook belief in victory in Vietnam; on 31 March Johnson announced that he would not seek re-election; Martin Luther King was murdered on 4 April and Robert Kennedy, who had been the leading Democratic candidate, on 5 June; and at the Democratic convention in Chicago in August police and protesters fought in the streets, on television. The Democratic candidate, Vice-President **Hubert Humphrey**, was tied to the war and to a party in disarray.

Nixon's campaign:
- **Law and order.** He promised to restore order to the cities and respect to the police, and appealed to the "forgotten Americans", later called the "silent majority", who were angry about riots, protests and crime.
- **The Southern strategy.** Republicans had begun to win white Southerners who left the Democrats over civil rights. Nixon, courting Southern Republican leaders such as Senator Strom Thurmond, used careful language on school integration and "states' rights" to take Southern votes without matching the open segregationism of George Wallace.
- **The war.** He said that new leadership would end the war and win the peace, without saying how.
- **Wallace.** The third-party candidate, the Alabama governor George Wallace, won 13.5 per cent of the vote and five Southern states. This took votes from Humphrey in the North and from the Democrats in the South.

The result: Nixon 43.4 per cent and 301 electoral votes; Humphrey 42.7 per cent and 191; Wallace 13.5 per cent and 46. It was a narrow popular win but a clear shift of the white South and the suburbs to the Republicans.

### The New Economic Policy

Nixon inherited inflation, which Vietnam spending had raised, and a worsening balance of trade. By 1971 unemployment was about 6 per cent and inflation was still about 4 per cent, a combination that was thought impossible under Keynesian theory. On **15 August 1971** he announced the **New Economic Policy**, a package of measures that broke with Republican beliefs:
- a **90-day freeze on wages and prices**, followed by controlled increases (Phases II to IV) until 1974;
- a **10 per cent surcharge** on imports;
- an end to the convertibility of the dollar into gold, which began the end of the system of fixed exchange rates set up at Bretton Woods in 1944 (the "Nixon shock").

It worked in the short term: inflation fell, and with tax cuts and a rise in spending the economy boomed in 1972, the year of the election. But the controls caused shortages and distorted markets, and when they ended and the oil price rose after the Arab embargo of October 1973 inflation rose to about 11 per cent in 1974. The NEP therefore helped Nixon to win in 1972 but left a legacy of "stagflation": rising prices and rising unemployment together.

### New Federalism

**New Federalism** was Nixon's idea of returning power from Washington to states and cities, which he thought knew local needs better. Its main measure was **revenue sharing**: the State and Local Fiscal Assistance Act, signed on 20 October 1972, which gave about $30.2 billion to state and local governments over five years with few conditions on how it should be spent. A second part, the **Family Assistance Plan** (1969), would have given every poor family a guaranteed minimum income ($1,600 a year for a family of four), but it was attacked from the right as a "welfare" measure and from the left as too small, and died in the Senate in 1970 and 1972. Nixon did achieve a 20 per cent increase in Social Security pensions in 1972, with future rises linked to inflation, and the Supplemental Security Income programme for the old, the blind and the disabled.

### Environmental reforms

Nixon, no friend of environmentalists, saw that the cause was popular (Earth Day on 22 April 1970 drew about 20 million people) and signed a series of major environmental laws.
- the **National Environmental Policy Act**, signed on 1 January 1970, which requires environmental impact statements for federal projects;
- the **Environmental Protection Agency**, created by reorganisation and opened on 2 December 1970 under William Ruckelshaus;
- the **Clean Air Act of 1970**, which set national air quality standards, and the **Clean Water Act of 1972**, passed by Congress over his veto on the ground of cost;
- the **Occupational Safety and Health Act** (1970) and the **Endangered Species Act** (1973).

### Civil rights

Nixon's record was more liberal than his campaign.
- **Schools.** He disliked busing but, after the Supreme Court ordered at once an end to dual school systems (*Alexander v. Holmes*, October 1969), his administration worked with Southern officials to end segregated schools. The share of Black children in the South attending all-Black schools fell from 68 per cent in 1968 to single figures by the early 1970s. In the North, where segregation came from housing, little changed.
- **The Philadelphia Plan.** In 1969 the Department of Labor required building contractors on federal projects to set goals and timetables for hiring Black workers: the first large "affirmative action" measure with teeth. He also created an Office of Minority Business Enterprise.
- **Other measures.** He signed the 1970 extension of the Voting Rights Act, and Title IX of 1972, which bars sex discrimination in schools that receive federal money. In July 1970 he rejected the policy of "termination" for Native Americans.
- **Limits.** He opposed busing, and the Southern strategy aimed at white voters who did.

### Watergate and Nixon's resignation

On 17 June 1972 five men were arrested while breaking into the offices of the Democratic National Committee at the Watergate building in Washington. They were linked to the **Committee to Re-elect the President (CREEP)**. Nixon was re-elected in November by 520 electoral votes to 17, taking 60.7 per cent of the vote in a landslide over George McGovern. But the case did not go away.
- **The cover-up.** Nixon and his aides tried to stop the FBI investigating, and paid money to the burglars. Reporters on the *Washington Post*, Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, followed the money, and Judge John Sirica pressed the burglars to talk. In March 1973 one of them, James McCord, wrote to Sirica that there had been pressure to plead guilty and to keep quiet.
- **The Senate hearings.** The Senate Select Committee under Sam Ervin held televised hearings from 17 May 1973. Nixon's counsel, John Dean, told the committee that the President had known of the cover-up. On 16 July 1973 an aide, Alexander Butterfield, revealed that Nixon had secretly taped his conversations in the White House.
- **The tapes and the Saturday Night Massacre.** The special prosecutor, Archibald Cox, demanded the tapes. On 20 October 1973 Nixon ordered his dismissal; Attorney General Elliot Richardson and his deputy, William Ruckelshaus, resigned rather than do it, and the Solicitor General, Robert Bork, carried it out. The public reaction was so strong that Nixon had to give up some tapes, one of which had a gap of 18½ minutes. The Vice-President, Spiro Agnew, had already resigned on 10 October 1973 in a separate corruption case, and Gerald Ford was appointed Vice-President in his place.
- **The final stage.** On 24 July 1974 the Supreme Court, in *United States v. Nixon*, ruled by 8 votes to 0 that he had to hand over sixty-four tapes. On 27, 29 and 30 July the House Judiciary Committee voted three articles of impeachment (obstruction of justice by 27 to 11, abuse of power by 28 to 10, and contempt of Congress by 21 to 17). On 5 August Nixon released a tape of 23 June 1972 on which he ordered the CIA to tell the FBI to stop its inquiry, the so-called "smoking gun". His Republican support in Congress collapsed, and on 7 August Senator Barry Goldwater and other Republican leaders told him that he would be impeached and convicted. He announced his resignation in a television address on 8 August 1974, effective at noon on 9 August. A month later, on 8 September 1974, President Ford gave him a full pardon.

:::key Why Nixon resigned
**Reasons:** (1) *the cover-up itself* (his own decisions and the abuse of power by CREEP and the White House); (2) *the tapes* (the proof, once the Supreme Court ordered them to be handed over); (3) *the Senate hearings and the press* (which exposed the story and kept it alive); (4) *Congress and the Judiciary Committee* (the threat of impeachment, backed by Republicans); (5) *the courts* (Sirica, Cox and *United States v. Nixon*); (6) *the climate after Vietnam* (distrust of the "imperial presidency").
**Judgement to defend:** Nixon was brought down by **his own conduct and the tapes that recorded it**; the press, the courts and Congress were the *means* by which the system forced him out. He would not have fallen without the tapes, and the tapes existed because he had made them.
:::

## How significant were the three presidencies?

| President | Achievement against intention | Scale | Permanence | Constraints and context |
|---|---|---|---|---|

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **Robert Dallek**, in his biographies of Johnson (*Flawed Giant*, 1998) and Kennedy (*An Unfinished Life*, 2003), stresses how far Johnson's legislative achievement outstripped Kennedy's, and how much both were shaped by the Cold War and by Vietnam.
- **Joan Hoff**, in *Nixon Reconsidered* (1994), argued that Nixon's domestic record was more liberal and more creative than his image, and that Watergate has hidden this.
- **Stanley Kutler**, in *The Wars of Watergate* (1990), treated Watergate as the product of Nixon's long wars against his opponents and of his abuse of the powers of the presidency.
Use them to show that you know there is a debate, but your marks come from your own argument backed by evidence.
:::

The most defensible overall verdict has three parts. **Kennedy** was significant as a *symbol and agenda-setter*, and as a President whose handling of the Cold War is covered in the next chapter, but his domestic record was modest. **Johnson** was significant as a *legislator*: no other President of the century except Franklin Roosevelt did so much, and he did it by using Kennedy's death and a huge majority, but his own war undid his reputation. **Nixon** was significant in two contradictory ways: as the President who *continued and extended* much of the Great Society state while moving the Republican Party towards the white South, and as the first President to be forced from office for abusing his power.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'Johnson's Great Society was more successful than Kennedy's New Frontier.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** Criteria: (1) laws passed against what was promised; (2) scale (how many Americans); (3) permanence; (4) the obstacles faced. Support: Johnson passed the Civil Rights Act, Voting Rights Act, Medicare and Medicaid, ESEA; poverty fell from 19.0 to 11.1 per cent. Challenge: Kennedy opened the agenda; Johnson had the majorities, sympathy after the assassination and Kennedy's groundwork; the Great Society's failures (riots, Vietnam, backlash). Judgement: Johnson's programme was far more successful in law and impact, but the two cannot be separated.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Johnson's Great Society was more successful than Kennedy's New Frontier. Kennedy wanted to improve civil rights, health and education, but Congress stopped him. Johnson passed the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act and Medicare. Johnson also started a war on poverty. However, Johnson's war in Vietnam hurt his reputation and there were riots in the cities. Kennedy was very popular and was shot, which made people sad. So overall Johnson was more successful but there were some problems."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with a balance of sorts, so Table B Level 3 at best. But it is a **list of facts on each side** with no criteria, and the final "but there were some problems" is the weak balance of a Level 3 answer. There are no dates, no figures and no explanation of *why* Johnson succeeded where Kennedy failed. Table A Level 3 (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply criteria and explain.** "If success is measured by what became law, Johnson was far more successful. Kennedy failed to pass Medicare (defeated 52 to 48 in the Senate on 17 July 1962), federal aid to schools or his civil rights bill, which were all blocked by the conservative coalition. Johnson passed all three: the Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964), Medicare (30 July 1965) and the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (11 April 1965). The reason was not that Johnson's aims were different, but that his *conditions* were: after the assassination he could ask Congress to act in Kennedy's memory, and after the landslide of 3 November 1964 the Democrats held 295 House seats, enough to outvote the conservative coalition. His own skill as a Senate leader did the rest."

*Commentary.* There is now a criterion (what became law) and the reasons for the difference are explained, with dates and figures. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. It still needs a second criterion (impact and permanence), the limits of the Great Society and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The claim is largely valid, but it needs two qualifications. On the criterion of what each President achieved, the gap is very large. On the criterion of what the Great Society shows about the limits of reform, and of how far the two were separate, the picture is more complicated. I shall judge success by four criteria: laws passed against what was promised, the scale of the effect on Americans, permanence, and the obstacles faced.

On laws passed, Johnson was much more successful. Kennedy's programme was ambitious but largely stopped by a Congress controlled by the conservative coalition. His Medicare plan was defeated in the Senate by 52 votes to 48 on 17 July 1962, his schools bill failed in 1961 over the question of aid to Catholic schools, and his tax cut and civil rights bill were still waiting when he died. His real successes were smaller: the Area Redevelopment and Housing Acts of 1961, the minimum wage rise to $1.25 and the Equal Pay Act of 1963. Johnson, in contrast, passed in the 89th Congress (1965–66) the Medicare and Medicaid law of 30 July 1965, the Elementary and Secondary Education Act of 11 April 1965, the Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 and the Higher Education Act of 8 November 1965, after the Civil Rights Act of 2 July 1964 and the Economic Opportunity Act of 20 August 1964.

On scale and permanence, too, the Great Society was more successful. About 19 million older Americans had been enrolled in Medicare in its first year; the Voting Rights Act, with federal examiners, helped to raise Black registration in Mississippi from about 7 per cent in 1965 to about 60 per cent by 1967; and the official poverty rate fell from about 19.0 per cent in 1964 to 11.1 per cent in 1973. Medicare, federal school aid and the right to vote were still in place a half-century later. The New Frontier, by contrast, left few lasting institutions of its own, although the Peace Corps and the Equal Pay Act did survive.

The qualification is that this comparison flatters Johnson by ignoring why he could do it. The New Frontier set the agenda: the tax cut (passed in February 1964 and cutting the top rate from 91 to 70 per cent), Medicare, school aid, the civil rights bill and the planning of the poverty programme were all Kennedy's. Johnson's skill was to turn them into law, using the shock of the assassination and the Democratic landslide of 1964, in which his party gained some three dozen House seats. It may be fairer to see the two as one programme in two stages. And the Great Society had failures of its own: the war on poverty promised more than it delivered, the Community Action Programs quarrelled with city mayors, the riots in Watts (1965), Newark and Detroit (1967) showed that the inner-city problem was untouched, and the cost of the Vietnam War brought inflation and a backlash which in 1966 gave the Republicans 47 House seats and in 1968 gave Nixon the presidency.

Overall, therefore, the Great Society was clearly more successful than the New Frontier in what it passed and in what lasted, and that is the standard by which most historians, among them Robert Dallek, rank the two. But it was not independent of the New Frontier, and its success carried the seeds of the backlash that limited it. The better judgement is that Kennedy was the more significant as an *originator* and Johnson as an *achiever*, and that a President needs both."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): four explicit criteria; the argument is balanced and the qualification (the two as one programme, the limits of the Great Society) is weighed rather than added; a historian is used with care; the conclusion is qualified and sustained. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates, votes and figures (52 to 48; 17 July 1962; 19.0 to 11.1 per cent; 91 to 70 per cent; the gain of some three dozen House seats in 1964; 47 seats lost in 1966), selected for the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 650 words, 35 to 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the reasons for Nixon's resignation in 1974. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. An "Assess the reasons" answer must weigh the causes against each other and judge which mattered most, using criteria such as whether a cause was necessary and whether it was decisive.

**Plan.** Reasons: (1) Nixon's own conduct (the break-in, the cover-up, the abuse of power); (2) the tapes (the existence of a recording system; the "smoking gun" tape); (3) the press and the Senate hearings; (4) the courts (Sirica, Cox, *United States v. Nixon*); (5) Congress and the loss of Republican support; (6) the wider context (Vietnam, distrust of the imperial presidency, Agnew). Criteria: was it necessary (without it he would not have gone), and was it decisive in the last stage? Judgement: his own conduct, proved by the tapes.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "Nixon resigned because of Watergate. Some men broke into the Democratic headquarters and Nixon tried to hide it. The newspapers found out and then there were tapes. Congress was going to impeach him so he resigned."

*Commentary.* True but undeveloped: the causes are named, not explained, and there is no weighing, no dates, no figures and no names. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 1–2.

**Moving to Level 4: explain how each cause worked and compare.** "The most important reason was the tapes. Without them it would have been Nixon's word against that of John Dean, and Dean himself could not be proved right. Alexander Butterfield's revelation on 16 July 1973 that conversations in the Oval Office had been recorded gave the investigators a source the President could not deny, and Nixon's refusal to hand them over led to the Saturday Night Massacre on 20 October 1973, which turned public opinion against him. The Supreme Court's unanimous ruling in *United States v. Nixon* on 24 July 1974 then removed his last defence, and on 5 August the tape of 23 June 1972 showed him ordering the CIA to block the FBI. The press and the Senate committee were important, but they had only made the case; the tapes proved it."

*Commentary.* The cause is explained, with dates and a chain of events, and ranked against others. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs the counter-weight (the press, Congress and the courts made the tapes matter), a clear criterion and a judgement tested against the alternative of Nixon's own conduct.

**A Level 5 conclusion.**

"Overall, Nixon resigned because a chain of causes joined, but they were not equal. I distinguish the *root* cause, the *proof*, and the *means*. The root cause was Nixon's own conduct: the use of CREEP and of White House staff to spy on opponents, the decision to cover up the break-in of 17 June 1972, and the use of the CIA and FBI to block inquiry. Had he made a full disclosure in 1972, as many advisers urged, there would have been no resignation. The proof was the tapes, which made it impossible to dispute the cover-up: the 'smoking gun' tape released on 5 August 1974 destroyed what remained of his Republican support, and Goldwater and other party leaders told him on 7 August that he would be convicted. The means were the press, the Ervin committee, the courts and the House Judiciary Committee, which voted three articles of impeachment between 27 and 30 July 1974. I judge the root cause the most important, because without his decisions there would have been nothing to expose, and the tapes the most important of the other reasons, because without them the press, the Senate and the courts could not have produced proof. The context mattered too: after Vietnam, many Americans distrusted the 'imperial presidency', and that is why a scandal that in an earlier decade might have been tolerated brought down a man who had won 520 electoral votes in 1972. But context made Congress willing, while Nixon's conduct made it necessary."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies a clear scheme (root cause, proof, means), makes an argued judgement, and links the answer to the wider context. With paragraphs developing each cause, this is Level 5.

Question: "Assess the extent to which the Great Society reduced poverty and inequality in the USA." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Great Society was a big success in reducing poverty. Johnson started a war on poverty and the Economic Opportunity Act created Head Start and the Job Corps. Poverty went down a lot after 1964. There were also Medicare and civil rights laws. Some people did not like Johnson because of Vietnam. In conclusion the Great Society reduced poverty and made America more equal."

Equality of *outcome* barely moved, and the riots of 1965 and 1967 showed that the poor of the inner cities were little affected." Now every fact is evidence for a judgement, with the measure and the limits stated.

Support: Executive Order 10925 (March 1961); the Justice Department's support for voters and Freedom Riders; the Ole Miss crisis (September 1962); the television address of 11 June 1963 and the civil rights bill of 19 June. Challenge: before 1963 he was cautious and delayed the housing order; the bill was passed only under Johnson; achievements elsewhere (the Peace Corps, Equal Pay Act, the Cuban crisis, the Test Ban Treaty); his role was agenda-setting. Level 5 defines "greatest" (scale, permanence) and compares at least two other areas.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which the Great Society achieved its aims.
H: Define the aims first: the ending of poverty and racial injustice (Michigan speech, 22 May 1964).
H: Consider what is the right measure of "achieved": laws passed, or poverty reduced, or lasting change.
A: Indicative content. Achievements: Civil Rights Act (1964) and Voting Rights Act (1965); Medicare and Medicaid; ESEA; poverty from 19.0 per cent (1964) to 11.1 per cent (1973); Head Start; HUD and the environment. Limits: poverty was not ended; inner-city riots; Community Action disputes; the war's cost; the backlash and the 1966 losses; difficulty of separating the Great Society from the boom. Level 5 argues that its aims for opportunity were largely met but that the aim of ending poverty and racial injustice was not, and says why.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Great Society failed because of the Vietnam War.' Evaluate this view.
H: List the other possible causes of failure: opposition, design faults, riots, the economy.
H: Is a cause the same as the only cause?
A: Indicative content. Support: the war took Johnson's attention and money; inflation and the tax surcharge (1967–68); the "credibility gap"; the 1966 mid-term losses; the end of reform after 1966. Challenge: conservative opposition from the start; riots and white backlash (Watts, Newark, Detroit); problems of design in the community programmes; limits of the aims; and the Great Society's real achievements (so "failed" is too strong). Level 5 questions "failed", separates what was achieved from what was lost, and judges the war a major but not the only cause.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which Nixon's domestic policies were a break with the policies of Johnson.
H: Think about what Nixon kept (the Great Society programmes), what he changed (New Federalism) and what he added (environment).
H: Distinguish the style and the party strategy from the policy.
A: Indicative content. Continuity: Social Security increases and indexing; Supplemental Security Income; the Philadelphia Plan and school desegregation; the EPA and environmental law; the Family Assistance Plan idea; the NEP's activism. Break: New Federalism and revenue sharing (20 October 1972); the Southern strategy and opposition to busing; "law and order"; cuts to the Office of Economic Opportunity. Level 5 judges that the substance was more a continuation than the rhetoric, and that the main break was in politics.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the press was the main reason for Nixon's resignation.
H: Consider the roles of the *Washington Post*, the Senate committee, the courts and the tapes.
H: Is the press a cause or a channel?
A: Indicative content. Support: Woodward and Bernstein kept the story alive in 1972–73; the press reported the hush money and the links to CREEP; public opinion shifted. Challenge: the burglars' trial and Sirica; the Ervin hearings and Butterfield (16 July 1973); the tapes; Cox and the Saturday Night Massacre; *United States v. Nixon*; the Judiciary Committee; Nixon's own acts. Level 5 judges that the press was important in keeping the story alive but that the proof came from the tapes, the courts and Congress.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Nixon's domestic achievements were greater than Johnson's.' Evaluate this view.
H: Choose criteria that suit both Presidents: scale, permanence and the conditions each faced.
H: Consider the environment, civil rights enforcement and Social Security, and then Medicare, voting rights and education.
A: Support: the environmental laws (NEPA, EPA, Clean Air Act 1970, Clean Water Act 1972), the Social Security increase and indexing (1972), SSI, revenue sharing, the Philadelphia Plan, school desegregation in the South (68 per cent of Black children in all-Black schools in 1968; single figures by the early 1970s), Title IX; Joan Hoff's argument. Challenge: Medicare, Medicaid, the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts, ESEA and the war on poverty reached more people and changed more; Nixon had a hostile Congress but inherited Johnson's programmes; the NEP's controls and stagflation; Watergate. A* judgement says that Nixon's domestic record was larger than his reputation, but not greater than Johnson's, and that the best case for Nixon is as the President who made the Great Society state permanent and added the environment.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The presidencies of 1961–74 were more significant for what they failed to do than for what they did.' Evaluate this view.
H: Take each President's failures: Kennedy's blocked bills, Johnson's war and the riots, Nixon's resignation.
H: Decide whether significance lies in the reforms or in the damage to trust.
A: Support: Kennedy's programme blocked; the Great Society's limits and the backlash; Johnson's credibility gap; Nixon's abuse of power and the lasting damage to trust in government. Challenge: the Civil Rights and Voting Rights Acts, Medicare, school aid, the EPA and the New Economic Policy's effect on the economy; the poverty rate falling from 19.0 to 11.1 per cent. A* judgement weighs lasting institutions against lasting distrust, and says that the achievements were real and durable while the failures changed politics, so that the period is significant for both.
:::

:::recap
- **Criteria for a presidency**: achievement against intention, scale, permanence, constraints and context.
- **1960 election**: Kennedy 303 to Nixon 219 electoral votes; popular-vote margin 112,827 (about 0.17 per cent); first debate 26 September; Johnson and the South; the call to Coretta Scott King (26 October); Nixon's mistakes.
- **Kennedy**: conservative coalition; minimum wage to $1.25; Area Redevelopment (May 1961) and Housing (June 1961) Acts; Medicare defeated 52 to 48 (17 July 1962); President's Commission on women (14 December 1961); Equal Pay Act (10 June 1963); EO 10925 (6 March 1961); civil rights bill (19 June 1963); assassinated 22 November 1963.
- **Johnson**: Michigan speech (22 May 1964); landslide of 3 November 1964 (61.1 per cent; 486 to 52); Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964); Economic Opportunity Act (20 August 1964); ESEA (11 April 1965); Medicare and Medicaid (30 July 1965); Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965); Higher Education Act (8 November 1965); HUD; Wilderness Act (3 September 1964); Fair Housing Act (11 April 1968).
- **Opposition and impact**: conservatives, cost and Vietnam, riots, backlash, the 47 seats lost in 1966; poverty from 19.0 per cent (1964) to 11.1 per cent (1973).
- **Nixon**: 1968 (43.4 per cent; 301 to 191 to 46; Wallace 13.5 per cent); New Economic Policy (15 August 1971); revenue sharing (20 October 1972; about $30.2 billion); NEPA (1 January 1970), EPA (2 December 1970), Clean Air Act (1970); Philadelphia Plan; Title IX.
- **Watergate**: break-in 17 June 1972; landslide of 520 electoral votes; Butterfield (16 July 1973); Saturday Night Massacre (20 October 1973); *United States v. Nixon* (24 July 1974, 8 to 0); impeachment articles (27–30 July); "smoking gun" (5 August); resignation (9 August 1974); Ford's pardon (8 September 1974).
- **Judgement**: Kennedy the agenda-setter, Johnson the achiever, Nixon both continuer of the Great Society state and the President who abused his power.
