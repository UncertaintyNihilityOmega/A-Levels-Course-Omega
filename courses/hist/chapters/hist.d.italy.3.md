## Aims and development of economic policy

### What Mussolini wanted

Mussolini was not an economist and arrived in office in 1922 with no economic plan. In 1919 the Fascist programme had been radical: an eight-hour day, a minimum wage, a heavy tax on capital and the seizure of church property. By 1922 Mussolini had dropped most of it, because the people who had paid for and backed his rise were landowners and industrialists. What stayed constant were his **political** aims, and they shaped everything that followed.

- **Power and prestige.** A great nation needed a strong currency, a large population, and industry and farms able to feed and arm an army.
- **Self-sufficiency.** Italy had little coal, oil or iron. Mussolini wanted to depend less on imports, especially bread grains.
- **Order.** Strikes and class conflict were to end, replaced by cooperation under the state.
- **Credit for the regime.** Economic success was to be shown off to Italians and to the world as proof that fascism worked.

Historians such as Martin Clark (*Modern Italy, 1871–1995*) and Vera Zamagni (*The Economic History of Italy, 1860–1990*, 1993) agree on one point: the regime's economic policy was repeatedly driven by politics and prestige, not by economic reasoning, and this is why many of the results disappointed. This is a theme you can use in almost any essay on this key question.

### Four phases

:::key Four phases of Fascist economic policy
1. **1922–25: orthodox liberal policy.** De Stefani cuts spending and taxes and trusts private business.
2. **1925–29: the battles.** Under Volpi and then directly under Mussolini, the regime launches campaigns for grain, a strong lira and reclaimed land, and builds the legal framework of the Corporate State.
3. **1929–35: Depression and the state.** The world slump hits Italy; the state rescues banks and industry through the IMI and the IRI, and spends on public works.
4. **1935–41: autarky and war preparation.** Sanctions and the drive for empire push the regime to aim at self-sufficiency, at high cost.
:::

:::def Economic policy under Mussolini
The regime's use of tariffs, campaigns, state spending and state-controlled institutions to pursue political aims (strength, self-sufficiency, order) while leaving most industry and land in private hands.
:::

## Orthodox policy: De Stefani and Volpi

:::def Orthodox economic policy
Policy that follows the established rules of sound finance: a balanced budget, low taxes, little state spending and trade left to private business. In Italy in 1922–25 it is also called a *laissez-faire* approach.
:::

**Alberto De Stefani**, an economics professor, was finance minister from October 1922 to July 1925. He was a liberal in economics and a Fascist in politics, and his policy suited the businessmen and landowners who had welcomed Mussolini:
- He cut government spending and the number of state employees to reduce the large wartime deficit.
- He lowered taxes that fell on business and ended some state monopolies, such as the state life-insurance monopoly, and let private companies take over the telephone service.
- He left trade and industry largely to private firms.

Between 1922 and 1925 output and employment grew quickly, and the budget came close to balance. Part of the credit belongs to the world recovery of those years, but the regime could present De Stefani's years as proof that it was good for business. Many liberal supporters of Mussolini, including senior industrialists, saw this as the true Fascist economy.

The policy did not last. De Stefani's low taxes and cheap credit also fed inflation and a rise in imports in 1925, which weakened the lira. Mussolini, who was working to build a dictatorship after the murder of Matteotti, wanted to end the image of a government run by liberal economists. In July 1925 he replaced De Stefani with **Giuseppe Volpi**, a rich businessman from Venice. Volpi's best-known success was in November 1925, when he negotiated a settlement of Italy's war debt to the United States that spread repayments over 62 years on generous terms. That opened the way to American loans, which Italy was soon to need.

:::key What the orthodox years achieved
1. A balanced budget and lower deficits, which brought business confidence and some foreign credit.
2. Quick growth in 1922–25, helped by the world upturn.
3. But also inflation and a weak lira by 1925–26, which created the pressure that led to the "battle for the lira".
:::

## The "battles"

From 1925 Mussolini began to describe economic policy as a series of **battles**, and the word was chosen on purpose. A battle needed a leader, a front line, heroes and enemies. It turned economic questions into tests of national will, and it let the regime claim a victory by announcing one. Four of them matter for this key question: the lira, grain, marshes and land, and (later) autarky.

:::def Battle for the lira (Quota 90)
Mussolini's campaign, announced in the Pesaro speech on 18 August 1926, to raise the value of the lira to 90 to the pound sterling, the rate of 1922. It was reached, at about 92.46 lire to the pound, by December 1927, through deflation and a return to a form of the gold standard.
:::

### The battle for the lira, 1926–27

By mid-1926 the lira had fallen to about 150 to the pound, from about 90 in 1922. Mussolini took the fall as a national insult. At Pesaro on 18 August 1926 he promised that he would defend the lira "to the last breath, to the last drop of blood". He then set the target of 90 to the pound.

The method was **deflation**: cutting the supply of money and credit, raising interest rates, balancing the budget and cutting wages and prices so that the lira would buy more. The currency was backed by a loan of $100 million from American bankers led by J.P. Morgan, signed in November 1925 as soon as the war-debt settlement was agreed. Wages were cut by decree in 1927, and the cuts were larger than the fall in prices, so real wages and workers' spending power fell.

*Economic effects.* The revaluation raised the lira's value against sterling by about three fifths, from about 150 to about 92.46, and made imports of coal and raw materials cheaper. But it made Italian goods dearer abroad, so exports of silk, cars and textiles suffered. Many firms went out of business or merged (mergers rose sharply in 1928 and 1929), unemployment rose, especially in the agricultural south, and the cuts hit workers hardest. Volpi himself thought 120 to 125 to the pound a more sensible rate and called 90 a drastic overvaluation.

*Political effects.* Quota 90 was a political success: it showed a strong state and a strong currency, and it pleased bankers and people with fixed incomes who held lire. Mussolini presented it as a victory over the speculators. But when the Depression struck Italy had a currency that was too dear for the world market, which made its exports harder to sell.

:::

### The battle for grain, 1925

:::def Battle for Grain
The campaign launched on 20 June 1925 to make Italy self-sufficient in wheat. It combined a high tariff on imported wheat with propaganda, prizes, better seed, chemical fertiliser and machines, and compulsory use of home-grown wheat in milling.
:::

Before the First World War Italy imported a large share of its bread grain, and in the early 1920s wheat made up as much as a quarter of the value of all imports. For a regime that wanted a strong lira and independence in war, that was a weakness. Mussolini launched the battle in 1925 with the slogan that the Italian people must be freed from "the slavery of foreign bread".

*Methods.* The state raised the tariff on imported wheat (by the 1930s it was more than double the world price, according to the economic historian Mario Carillo, *The Economic Journal*, 2021), gave prizes to the best farmers, spread the use of fertiliser and machinery through "travelling chairs of agriculture" (travelling professors who taught farmers new methods), and encouraged better seed varieties developed by the plant breeder Nazareno Strampelli. Millers were required to use home-grown wheat. Fertiliser use rose by more than half in the first four years.

*Results.* The campaign worked on its own terms. Wheat output rose by about a third in the decade after 1925; one commonly quoted set of estimates puts it at about 5.4 million tonnes in 1925 and about 7.3 million tonnes in 1935. The area sown changed little, so the rise came from higher yields per hectare. Wheat imports fell: official figures show them falling from about 2.2 million tonnes in 1925 to about 1.5 million tonnes in 1931, and Italy came close to self-sufficiency in wheat in the 1930s. The regime claimed the battle as one of its greatest successes, and prizes and medals were handed out with great ceremony.

*Costs.* The gains were bought at a price:
- **Dearer bread.** The tariff held up the price of wheat in the shops; the historian Denis Mack Smith (*Mussolini*, 1981) argued that the policy cost consumers heavily and rewarded large landowners.
- **Other crops and livestock.** Land was ploughed up for wheat that suited other uses, particularly in the poorer south and on hill land, where yields were low. Carillo notes that the output of maize stayed at its pre-policy level. Fewer olives, fruit, vegetables and meat meant a less balanced diet, and exports of fruit and vegetables suffered.
- **Inefficiency.** Much of the extra wheat cost more to grow than to import. The policy raised the bills of the whole economy to produce a symbol of self-sufficiency.
- **Long-run effects.** Carillo's study found that the areas where new wheat techniques spread most became richer in later decades, because farmers there learnt skills and used machinery. So the policy had lasting effects, but this was not what Mussolini intended.

### The battle for land: marshes and *bonifica integrale*

Much of Italy's best flat land was marsh, infected with malaria, and its drainage had been talked about for decades. Reclamation of land had begun before the Fascists came to power (a law of 1924 had set up a framework), but the Fascists made it a showpiece. The **Mussolini Law** of December 1928 launched *bonifica integrale* ("complete reclamation"): the state paid for most of the drainage, roads, housing and water supply, and could take over land from owners who would not improve it.

:::def Pontine Marshes
A large area of malarial marsh south-east of Rome, drained under Fascist rule in the 1930s. Mussolini presented the project as the conquest of nature by the new Italy, and it produced new towns such as Littoria (founded 18 December 1932; now Latina) and Sabaudia (1934).
:::

The Pontine Marshes were the best-known project. Once the drainage was completed, the new towns of Littoria, Sabaudia, Pontinia, Aprilia and Pomezia were built. Settlers, mostly poor families from the north-east of Italy such as Veneto and Friuli, were given farms and houses on land that the state owned. The drainage ended malaria on much of the land and provided farms for thousands of families. Newsreels showed Mussolini at the harvest, stripped to the waist beside the farmers.

But there were limits:
- The largest reclamation was done in the Pontine region; elsewhere progress was slower, and in the south the big landowners (*latifundisti*) resisted the loss of their estates and the regime did not force them. The state paid for most of the work, which benefited landowners who received the improved land.
- The scheme provided farms for only a small share of Italy's landless peasants and did little to stop the movement of people to the cities, though the regime also tried to stop this directly by restricting migration (a campaign against "urbanisation").
- Mussolini wanted a "ruralised" population of peasants and large families, partly because he thought towns bred weakness and low birth rates. Yet industrial workers and city dwellers did not return to the land.

Overall it was a real achievement in the places it reached, and a propaganda triumph, but its scale was small compared with the problem of rural poverty.

### Autarky

:::def Autarky
Economic self-sufficiency: a country produces what it needs itself, and imports as little as possible. In Fascist Italy the word is used for the policy, pushed hard from 1935–36, of replacing imports with home products.
:::

Mussolini had talked of self-sufficiency since the Battle for Grain, but autarky became the national policy only after Italy invaded Abyssinia in October 1935. On 18 November 1935 the League of Nations imposed sanctions: its members stopped lending to Italy and banned the import of Italian goods and the export of some goods, such as rubber, to Italy. Oil and coal were not banned, so the practical effect was smaller than the regime pretended, and sanctions ended in July 1936. But Mussolini used them to rally Italians against "the siege" and to justify controls.

The regime:
- campaigned for home production: artificial fibres in place of imported cotton and wool, a milk-based wool called Lanital, and the search for oil and minerals at home;
- asked Italians to give their gold wedding rings to the state, on the "Day of Faith" of 18 December 1935;
- imposed controls on foreign exchange and imports;
- built up the steel, chemical and armaments industries through the IRI and state orders.

*Outcome.* Autarky was **expensive, ineffective and wasteful**. Italy still lacked coal, oil and iron ore, and no amount of propaganda could create them. Substitutes cost more than imports, living standards suffered, and the push towards war industries put heavy strains on the state budget and the lira, which was devalued in October 1936. When Italy entered the Second World War in June 1940 it was far less able to fight than its propaganda had claimed. Autarky shows how a political aim, to look independent and strong, overrode economic common sense.

### Judging the battles

:::key Judging the battles: what to say in an essay
A good essay does not simply list victories. It picks **criteria** and tests each battle against them:
1. **Did it reach its own target?** Grain: yes, largely. Lira: yes, the target was reached. Marshes: partly. Autarky: no.
2. **What did it cost, and who paid?** The lira and grain battles were paid for by workers and consumers.
3. **Did it help the whole economy?** The lira hurt exporters; grain helped large landowners; marshes helped a small number of settlers.
4. **What was it for?** Prestige and political control, more than growth.
Overall judgement: the battles were more successful as **propaganda and politics** than as economics.
:::

|---|---|---|---|
| Lira (1926–27) | 90 to the pound | Yes, about 92 by December 1927 | Dearer exports, wage cuts, unemployment, deflation |
| Grain (1925) | Wheat self-sufficiency | Close by the 1930s | Dearer bread, poorer diet, other crops hit |
| Marshes and land (1928) | Reclaimed land, new farms | Partly; showpiece projects | Small scale; big landowners kept their power |

## The Great Depression

### Impact on Italy

Italy was less industrial than Britain or Germany, so it was hit by the Depression later and in a different way. The effects began to show in 1930 and became severe in 1931 and 1932. World prices fell, which cut the value of farm exports, and exports of silk, wine, fruit and textiles fell. The overvalued lira, held at Quota 90, made matters worse because it kept the price of Italian goods high for foreign buyers; the government held on to it, with tightening exchange controls, until the devaluation of October 1936. Industrial production fell sharply, and it took until the mid- to late 1930s to recover its earlier level. Registered unemployment rose from about 300,000 in 1929 to about one million in 1932–33. The real total was higher, because the official figures left out many agricultural workers and the underemployed, and the regime was keen to hide the problem. Wages were cut again.

The Depression also exposed a weakness in Italian finance. The largest banks (the Banca Commerciale, the Credito Italiano and the Banco di Roma) did not just lend to industry: they owned large blocks of shares in the companies they financed. When firms such as Ansaldo (shipbuilding and engineering) and Ilva (steel) failed, the banks were left holding worthless shares and were in danger of collapse. A bank failure would have wiped out savings and might have brought the regime down.

### Government intervention: the IMI and the IRI

:::def IMI (Istituto Mobiliare Italiano)
A state-backed bank set up in November 1931 to lend money to industry for up to ten years, when the failing banks could no longer do so. It raised funds by issuing state-guaranteed bonds.
:::

:::def IRI (Istituto per la Ricostruzione Industriale)
The Institute for Industrial Reconstruction, founded in January 1933. The state used it to take over the shares in industry that the failing banks held, which in effect nationalised the rescued firms and the banks themselves.
:::

**The IMI (1931).** It was set up with a capital of 500 million lire and lent to private industry for up to ten years, financed by bond issues backed by the state. It gave struggling firms the long-term credit that the banks could no longer provide. An associated body, the Sofindit, was founded in October 1931 to buy the industrial shares the banks held.

**The IRI (1933).** The IMI did not solve the banks' problem, because the banks still held too many shares. In January 1933 the government created the IRI, led by the banker and administrator Alberto Beneduce, to take over those shares, and with them the three largest banks. In effect the state became the owner of a large part of Italian industry and banking. A banking law of 1936 then separated ordinary banking from industrial ownership, so the banks could no longer own firms. The IRI was meant to be temporary, but in June 1937 it was made permanent.

*Scale.* By 1939 the state, through the IRI and other bodies, controlled more than four fifths of Italy's shipping and shipbuilding, about three quarters of pig-iron production and nearly half of steel (Martin Blinkhorn, *Mussolini and Fascist Italy*). In 1934 Mussolini boasted to the Chamber of Deputies that about three quarters of the economy was in the hands of the state, which was an exaggeration. In 1936 the IRI held about an eighth of the capital of all Italian joint-stock companies. Historians often note that Italy ended the 1930s with one of the largest state-owned sectors in Europe outside the Soviet Union.

*Judgement.* The IMI and the IRI were the **most successful** economic measures of the regime. They prevented a collapse of the banks and the industries behind them, saved jobs and left behind modern institutions that lasted long after Mussolini (the IRI survived until the 1990s). But there were limits:
- The rescue was a way of passing private losses to the state, and the cost fell on taxpayers.
- It was a response to crisis, not a plan. Mussolini did not intend to nationalise industry and did not describe the IRI as socialism.
- It gave the state a base for rearmament and autarky, which turned out to be costly.
- Some economists argue that locking up savings in state-controlled banks held back private investment (an argument made in a recent article on the Depression in Italy by Gino Magnini, 2025).

### Public works

The regime used public works for propaganda and to reduce unemployment. They included:
- the first motorways (*autostrade*), beginning with the Milan to Varese road, which opened in September 1924 and was built by a private company, followed by a network in the 1930s;
- electrification of railways and a programme of dams and hydroelectric power;
- land reclamation (*bonifica integrale*), which gave work to tens of thousands of labourers;
- new public buildings, schools, stadiums, post offices and the remodelling of Rome, with new towns and the Foro Mussolini.

They did give work, and some, such as the roads and hydroelectric power, helped economic growth. But the scale was too small to cure mass unemployment, the works were chosen as much for show as need, and the myth that Mussolini "made the trains run on time" has more to do with propaganda than with the railways.

:::key How well did Italy respond to the Depression?
1. **Success:** the IMI and IRI saved the banks and industry from collapse and made the state a major economic player.
2. **Success:** public works created jobs and symbols.
3. **Failure:** unemployment stayed high (about a million registered at the peak) and wages were cut; the overvalued lira was kept too long.
4. **Failure:** policy was improvised and its main goal was political order, not recovery. The Depression ended the idea that Fascist Italy was better placed than the democracies.
:::

## The Corporate State

### Aims: the "Third Way"

:::def Corporate State
The system the Fascists set up to organise the economy through "corporations" in which employers, workers and the state were meant to work together instead of fighting. In theory it replaced both class struggle and competition by cooperation under the state.
:::

Fascism claimed to be a **Third Way** between liberal capitalism, which it said bred selfishness and inequality, and Marxist socialism, which it said bred class war and weakened the nation. In the Corporate State, employers and workers would be grouped by trade in syndicates, and the syndicates of each branch of the economy would meet in "corporations" under state supervision. Strikes and lockouts would be unnecessary because disputes would be settled by the state. Private property and private enterprise stayed.

The Corporate State also served the regime's practical aims:
- to **destroy** independent trade unions and the socialists who ran them, so that workers could not strike;
- to give the Fascist unions, which wanted real power, a role without letting them threaten employers;
- to give the state control over labour relations and, later, a claim to direct the whole economy;
- to give Fascism a distinctive doctrine of its own, in the eyes of Italians and of foreigners.

### How it was built

| Date | Measure | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 2 October 1925 | Palazzo Vidoni Pact | The employers' confederation (Confindustria) and the Fascist unions agreed to recognise each other as the only bargaining partners. The Fascist unions gained a monopoly over representing workers; shop-floor committees (*commissioni interne*) were to be abolished. |
| 3 April 1926 | Rocco Law (named after the Justice Minister Alfredo Rocco) | One legally recognised syndicate (union or employers' body) for each trade and area; all other unions lost their rights; **strikes and lockouts became crimes**; labour courts (*magistratura del lavoro*) settled disputes. |
| July 1926 | Ministry of Corporations | A ministry to supervise the syndicates, headed by Mussolini himself; Giuseppe Bottai, who became minister in 1929, was its leading thinker. |
| 21 April 1927 | Charter of Labour | A declaration of principles approved by the Fascist Grand Council. It recognised private enterprise, said that production was a national interest, and stated that collective contracts would regulate wages and conditions. It was a party statement and had no legal force at first. |
| 20 March 1930 | Law on the National Council of Corporations | The Council, first set up in 1926, was given its form: seven sections, with the head of government in the chair, to advise and coordinate. |
| 5 February 1934 | Law creating the 22 corporations | Each corporation covered a branch of the economy, such as cereals, textiles or the theatre, and was meant to bring together employers, workers and Party officials. |
| 19 January 1939 | Chamber of Fasces and Corporations | The elected Chamber of Deputies was abolished; members of the new Chamber sat because of their Party or corporate posts. |

### Outcomes: how far was it real?

*What changed.* The Rocco Law and the Palazzo Vidoni Pact really did end free trade unions and legal strikes. Collective contracts were negotiated by Fascist union officials and employers, and were binding on all workers in a trade. Wages across a trade and region were set by contract, and the labour courts made employers follow them. The Ministry of Corporations gave the state a say in labour relations that it had never had. By 1939 the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations had replaced the elected Chamber.

*What did not change.* Most historians, including Martin Clark, regard the Corporate State as much more a piece of **propaganda and political control** than a working economic system:
- **Employers kept the upper hand.** Confindustria kept its independence, and in the Palazzo Vidoni Pact it won the abolition of factory committees. The Fascist unions were weak partners: their leader Edmondo Rossoni, who wanted them to have real power, was removed in 1928, and the unions were split into separate bodies under party control.
- **Workers lost their voice.** They could not strike or bargain freely, and union officials were appointed from above, not elected. Wage cuts in 1927 and during the Depression showed which side the system favoured.
- **The corporations came late and had little power.** They were created in 1934, nine years after the Vidoni Pact, and mainly had advisory functions. They did not plan production, set prices or direct investment. The real decisions were taken by Mussolini, the ministries and the big firms and banks, and later by the IRI.
- **The Charter of Labour was a declaration, not law.** It stated principles but, as a party document, it had no legal force when it was issued.
- **Private enterprise survived.** Big business accepted the system because it kept profits and removed the unions; the "Third Way" in practice meant capitalism without free labour.
- **The Chamber of 1939** was less a stage of the economy than the last step in the end of parliamentary government.

*Positive points for the other side.* The system gave the state power to settle disputes, it set up some benefits, and it gave the regime a lasting set of institutions. For some Fascist leaders, such as Bottai, it was a sincere attempt at a new kind of state. But the sincere version never became the real one.

:::key The Corporate State: for and against being "propaganda"
**More propaganda than practice:** unions silenced and strikes banned; employers kept their power; the corporations were created late and had little real power; the Charter of Labour had no legal force; the 1939 Chamber replaced parliament rather than organising the economy.
**Something in practice:** real laws that ended free bargaining and gave the state control of labour relations; a ministry and labour courts that worked; collective contracts that applied to every worker; the corporations and Chamber gave Fascism a formal political structure.
**Judgement to defend:** it was real as a system of **control over workers**, but not as a "Third Way" that shared power between employers, workers and the state.
:::

## Living standards

### The welfare system

The regime built an extensive welfare system, partly to win support, partly to prop up a falling birth rate, and partly to take over what the Church and trade unions had done. The most important parts:
- **Childcare and mothers.** The ONMI (*Opera Nazionale per la Maternità e l'Infanzia*), created by a law of 10 December 1925, ran clinics, nurseries and aid for poor mothers and babies. It was a real service, but it was under-funded in the south and run mainly in towns.
- **Insurance and pensions.** The regime extended compulsory insurance (for example against tuberculosis from 1927) and in 1933 renamed and reorganised the social-insurance fund as the National Fascist Institute for Social Insurance. Pension and sickness-insurance coverage grew over the 1930s.
- **Family allowances.** In 1934, at the time of the 40-hour week, allowances were introduced for industrial workers with children, to make up part of the lost earnings.
- **Employment.** The regime claimed to be fighting unemployment, but official figures were manipulated and many jobs were public works of a temporary kind.
- **Leisure and welfare through the Party.** The Dopolavoro (after-work organisation), covered in [Mussolini's Italy (4)](#hist.d.italy.4), provided cheap holidays, outings and entertainment.

### Pay, hours and consumption

:::def Living standards
How well people live, measured by pay in terms of what it will buy (real wages), hours of work, employment, diet and the goods and services they can afford.
:::

- **Pay.** Real wages fell in the 1920s (by about 15 per cent between 1921 and 1929 in one estimate) because of the deflation of 1926–27. In the early 1930s prices fell faster than wages, so those who kept their jobs did a little better, and by 1938 real wages were close to their 1929 level. Economic historians disagree about how reliable the official wage figures are. Many workers lost hours or jobs, so the total income of working families was lower than wage rates suggest. The regime's own wages policy, with contracts agreed from above, meant workers had no way to ask for more.
- **Hours.** An eight-hour day had been law since 1923. In 1934 the regime introduced a 40-hour week in industry to share work in the Depression. Workers lost pay for the hours they no longer worked, and family allowances were meant to make up some of the loss.
- **Consumption.** Food consumption per head stagnated or fell in the 1930s, with less meat and more cereals, and the diet of the south remained poor. The Battle for Grain kept bread dear, and incomes did not allow the purchase of consumer goods such as cars or electrical appliances, which only a minority could afford.- **Regions.** The south gained least. Wheat replaced other crops, large estates remained, and the population there stayed poor.

:::key Did living standards improve?
**For:** social insurance, maternity and child care, family allowances and cheap leisure were extended; employment in public works; some new housing and schools; falling prices in the early 1930s helped those in work.
**Against:** real wages fell in the 1920s; workers had no unions or strikes to defend pay; unemployment of about a million at the peak; food consumption stagnated; high food prices from the grain tariff; the south stayed poor.
**Judgement to defend:** the regime added welfare benefits but, as it kept wages down and prices high for political and national aims, workers' material living standards **did not improve** and, for much of the period, fell. Welfare was a cushion and a way of control, not a rise in prosperity.
:::

## How successful were the policies overall?

A strong answer gives a judgement on the whole key question as well as on each part. Use these criteria:

| Criterion | Evidence of success | Evidence of failure |
|---|---|---|
| Did policies reach their own targets? | Lira at 90 (1927); wheat output up; IRI saved banks | Autarky not achieved; Corporate State not a working Third Way |
| Did the economy grow? | Growth in 1922–25; modern state industries; hydroelectric power | Deflation 1926–27; Depression recovery slow; low productivity |
| Were ordinary Italians better off? | Welfare, pensions, allowances, leisure | Real wages fell; unemployment; food consumption stagnant |
| Were the aims realistic? | Some, such as land reclamation, were sensible | The lira and autarky put prestige before economics |
| Were the results lasting? | IRI and the state sector lasted into the 1990s | Most of the battles and the Corporate State ended with the regime |

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **Vera Zamagni**, *The Economic History of Italy 1860–1990* (1993), a detailed economic history, shows that the regime's policies were driven by political aims, and that some of them, such as the revaluation of the lira, slowed growth.
- **Martin Clark**, *Modern Italy, 1871–1995*, gives a sceptical account of the Corporate State and the battles as propaganda that did little for ordinary Italians.
- **Denis Mack Smith**, *Mussolini* (1981), criticised the battle for grain as costly to consumers and favourable to large landowners.
- **Mario Carillo**, *The Economic Journal* (2021), a modern economic historian, found that the Battle for Grain had a lasting, unintended benefit in areas where technology spread.
Use these to show that you know the debate, but your marks come from your own argument backed by evidence, not from a list of names.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. A [30] essay gets one mark out of 15 for analysis and judgement (Table A) and one out of 15 for knowledge (Table B). Read the commentary as an examiner would.

:::eg Assess the success of Mussolini's economic "battles" [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 2 (4–6): some understanding, descriptive, criteria implicit. Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, but the argument lacks balance; weak judgement. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** Criteria: (1) did each battle reach its target; (2) what did it cost and who paid; (3) did it help the whole economy; (4) what was it really for. Battles to cover: lira, grain, marshes and land, autarky. Judgement: successful as politics and propaganda, much less as economics.

**A Level 2/3 paragraph (about Table A Level 2, 5 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "Mussolini fought several battles in the 1920s. The Battle for Grain started in 1925 and was successful because Italy grew more wheat and imported less. The Battle for the Lira was announced at Pesaro in 1926 and by 1927 the lira was worth 90 to the pound. The Battle for the Marshes drained the Pontine Marshes and built new towns such as Littoria. The battles were therefore successful."

*Commentary.* Accurate and clear, and each battle is named with a date or result. But it is a **list of descriptions**. The last sentence asserts success without any criteria, and nothing is said about costs or whether the battles helped the economy. Table A Level 2; Table B Level 3 for the accuracy of the detail.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and show the cost.** "The Battle for the Lira met its own target, but at a cost. Mussolini announced the 90-lire target at Pesaro on 18 August 1926, and by December 1927 the lira stood at about 92 to the pound, up about three fifths from the 150 of mid-1926. Yet the method was deflation: wages were cut by decree in 1927 by more than prices fell, and Italian exports of silk and textiles became dearer abroad, so unemployment rose. Volpi, the finance minister, had wanted a rate of 120 or more. The battle therefore succeeded as a show of national strength, but failed the test of helping the economy."

*Commentary.* Now the knowledge is selected to prove a point: the criterion is "did it help the economy", and there is a supported mini-judgement. This is the analytical standard of Level 4, but it is one battle only and the essay still needs balance across the others.

**A Level 5 conclusion (and the paragraph that sets it up).**

"Grain shows the same pattern in a different form. By the 1930s Italy came close to self-sufficiency in wheat, with output rising by about a third between 1925 and 1935 and imports falling, and this was achieved by raising yields through fertiliser, better seed and machinery, not by ploughing more land. That is a genuine success against the target the regime set. Yet the means were a tariff that, by the 1930s, was more than double the world price, so Italians paid more for bread, and other crops such as olives, fruit and livestock gave way to wheat on land unsuited to it. The historian Denis Mack Smith argued that the main winners were large landowners. Carillo's later research adds a twist: in areas where new methods spread, farmers' skills produced lasting economic gains, which Mussolini did not intend. So the Battle for Grain was more successful than the lira battle in economic terms, but still bought a symbol of independence at a high cost.

Overall, the battles were far more successful as politics than as economics. If success is judged by the regime's own targets, the lira and grain battles succeeded, the land battle partly succeeded, and autarky failed. If it is judged by the effect on the economy and on ordinary Italians, the picture is poorer: the lira policy deflated the economy and cut wages, the grain policy raised prices and narrowed the diet, and the land schemes reached too few people. What links the battles is their purpose. Mussolini used the language of war to show a strong state and to rally Italians behind it, and in that sense they worked. But the sharpest test is the Depression, when the overvalued lira and the dear bread made Italy worse prepared than it might have been. The battles were therefore better propaganda than policy."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies **criteria** (the regime's own targets, then the effect on the economy and on Italians), weighs the battles against each other, explains the link between them (the purpose), and reaches a supported judgement. With the earlier paragraphs on the lira and the marshes, this is Level 5: Table A 14, Table B 13–14, about 27–28 out of 30. About 800–900 words in 40 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Evaluate: 'The Corporate State existed more in propaganda than in practice' [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. An "Evaluate" answer must reach a judgement on the quality or extent of something, so it needs a clear view of the claim ("more in propaganda than in practice"), evidence on both sides, and a verdict that compares them.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) did it do what Fascists said it would (a Third Way, cooperation of employers and workers); (2) did it have real power over the economy; (3) did it change life for ordinary workers. For "propaganda": the Charter was not law; the corporations came late and were advisory; employers kept their power; the Third Way was not real. For "practice": the Vidoni Pact and Rocco Law ended free unions and strikes; labour courts and collective contracts worked; the Ministry and the 1939 Chamber changed the state. Judgement: a real system of control, a false Third Way.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The Corporate State was the most distinctive claim of Fascist economic policy. It promised a Third Way between capitalism and socialism, in which employers and workers would cooperate under the state. The statement that it existed more in propaganda than in practice is largely right if the test is whether it organised the economy. It is wrong if the test is whether it changed the position of workers and the shape of the state, because in those respects it was very real. The best judgement divides the Corporate State into two things, a system of control over labour, which was real, and a system of cooperation between classes and joint economic planning, which was largely propaganda.

The case for propaganda begins with the timing and the powers of the institutions that were meant to be the heart of the system. The corporations were created by a law of 5 February 1934, nine years after the Palazzo Vidoni Pact and seven after the Charter of Labour. There were 22 of them, one for each branch of the economy, but they were mostly advisory. They did not plan production, set prices or direct investment, and real decisions were taken by Mussolini, by ministries and by the large firms and banks. When the state did take over economic leadership during the Depression, it did so through bodies that had nothing to do with the Corporate State: the IMI of 1931 and the IRI of 1933. The Charter of Labour of 21 April 1927 was a party statement approved by the Fascist Grand Council, not a law, and it said that private initiative was the most useful instrument of the nation, which tells us that the system was designed to leave business in charge. Employers, in particular, kept their organisation, Confindustria, and in the Vidoni Pact they won the abolition of the factory committees. The Fascist unions did not gain an equal place: their leader, Edmondo Rossoni, who wanted the unions to be strong partners, was removed in 1928. So the claim to be a Third Way, in which workers and employers shared power, was not true. The outcome was capitalism without free labour.

Yet the statement goes too far if it implies that nothing real happened. The Rocco Law of 3 April 1926 was a real law with real effects: it gave one union and one employers' body in each trade a legal monopoly, took away the rights of every other union, made strikes and lockouts crimes, and set up labour courts to decide disputes. This ended the free trade unions that had organised the strikes of 1919–20 (the *Biennio Rosso*), and no mass strike took place in Italy until March 1943. Collective contracts were binding across a trade, so wage rates were fixed from above. The Ministry of Corporations, created in July 1926, put labour relations under state supervision for the first time. In 1939 the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations replaced the elected Chamber of Deputies, a major change to the political system, and members sat because of their posts in the Party or the corporations. These changes were practical, not just propaganda, and they affected millions of workers.

The question is what the changes were for. They were not for cooperation or planning, but for control. The wage cuts of 1927 and the effect of the Depression show that workers bore the cost of the regime's policies and had no means of resisting. Business leaders accepted the system because it removed their main opponent. The corporations, when they came, gave the system a Fascist appearance and allowed Mussolini to tell foreign visitors that Italy had solved the problem of class conflict. In that sense propaganda and practice were joined: the propaganda of a Third Way gave a respectable name to a practice of control.

Overall, the statement is mostly justified. The institutions that were supposed to make the Corporate State a new economic order, the corporations and the Charter, existed on paper and had little power, and the economy was run by employers, bankers and the state in the usual ways, with the IRI as a new state tool. But the part of the system that was a practice, the destruction of free unions and the legal control of labour relations, was real, lasting and important. The Corporate State existed more as propaganda in what it claimed to be, and more in practice in what it did to workers."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): the answer defines the claim, uses valid criteria (what it claimed, what power it had, what it did to workers), argues **both sides in detail**, explains how they connect (propaganda gave a name to control), and ends with a supported, qualified judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates (3 April 1926, 21 April 1927, 5 February 1934), names (Rossoni, Confindustria), laws and institutions, all selected to support the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. Notice that it does not tell the story of the Corporate State; every paragraph argues a point.
:::

:::eg Evaluate: 'The Great Depression showed that Mussolini's economic policies had failed' [30] (plan and Level 4 paragraph)
**Plan.** Criteria: (1) did the policies protect Italy from the Depression; (2) did the response work; (3) did the policies survive it. For "failed": the overvalued lira (Quota 90) hurt exports; unemployment of about a million; banks near collapse; wage cuts; recovery slow until the late 1930s; the Corporate State was no help. Against: the IMI and IRI rescued banks and industry; public works; the regime stayed firmly in power through the crisis; the regime stayed in power. Judgement: the policies of the 1920s left Italy exposed, but the response was effective at saving institutions, not at curing hardship.

**A Level 4 paragraph.** "The Depression exposed the weaknesses of Mussolini's policies of the 1920s more than it showed that they had failed. Quota 90 had fixed the lira at about 92 to the pound in December 1927, and the government held to that rate until the devaluation of October 1936, so Italian exports were dear when world demand collapsed, and registered unemployment rose from about 300,000 in 1929 to about a million in 1932–33. The regime's response was not planned in advance, but it worked in an important way. The IMI (November 1931) gave industry ten-year loans, and the IRI (January 1933) took over the shares of failing banks, which stopped a collapse of the three largest banks and of the firms they owned, such as Ansaldo. By 1939 the IRI controlled more than four fifths of shipping and shipbuilding. So it is fair to say that the economic policy of the 1920s failed to protect Italy, but that the regime was flexible in its response."

*Commentary.* A good paragraph at Level 4: it uses criteria (protect, respond), precise knowledge and a mini-judgement. To reach Level 5, an essay would add a paragraph on the human cost, a comparison with other countries, and an explicit overall judgement about what "failed" means (failure of the policies or of the system?).

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the success of Mussolini's economic policies in improving the living standards of Italian workers." [30]

A student wrote:

"Mussolini introduced many welfare measures to help workers. In 1925 he set up the ONMI to help mothers and babies. He also set up the Dopolavoro, which gave workers cheap holidays and sport. In 1934 there was a 40-hour week and family allowances. There were also pensions and insurance. The Corporate State gave workers a voice through the syndicates. So Mussolini greatly improved the living standards of workers."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the paragraph is accurate and relevant, but it **lists** welfare measures and never tests them. It gives only one side, uses no criteria, and its last two sentences are assertions: the Corporate State did not give workers a voice (unions were banned from bargaining freely), and "greatly improved" has no evidence. Table B Level 3 (7–9): correct detail but narrow, and no figures for pay, hours or prices.

**The fix.** Define "living standards", then weigh the evidence both ways. "Fascist welfare measures such as the ONMI (1925) and family allowances (1934) cushioned workers' lives, but they did not raise their living standards. Real wages fell by about 15 per cent between 1921 and 1929 in one estimate, because the deflation of 1926–27 cut wages by more than prices. Workers could not strike or bargain freely after the Rocco Law of 1926. Registered unemployment reached about a million in 1932–33, and the grain tariff kept the price of bread high. The 40-hour week of 1934 reduced hours but also took away pay, which the allowances only partly replaced. The welfare measures therefore compensated for falling incomes, and helped the regime to control workers, more than they improved their position." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Who was De Stefani, and what was his policy?
A: Alberto De Stefani was finance minister from October 1922 to July 1925. His policy was orthodox and liberal: he cut spending and the number of state employees, lowered business taxes, ended some state monopolies and left industry to private firms. It produced quick growth, but also inflation and a weak lira by 1925–26.
Q: What was Quota 90, and what were its effects?
A: The target of 90 lire to the pound announced at Pesaro on 18 August 1926 and reached (about 92.46) by December 1927. It was achieved by deflation: wages were cut by more than prices, exports of silk and textiles became dearer abroad, and unemployment rose. It was a political success and an economic cost.
Q: What was the Battle for Grain, and what did it achieve?
A: A campaign begun on 20 June 1925 to make Italy self-sufficient in wheat, using tariffs, prizes, fertiliser and machinery. Output rose by about a third and imports fell, so Italy came near to self-sufficiency in wheat. But bread was dearer, other crops and livestock suffered, and large landowners gained.
Q: Name three new towns on the Pontine Marshes and say what the project achieved.
A: Littoria (1932), Sabaudia (1934) and Pontinia (1935); also Aprilia and Pomezia. The drainage ended malaria on much of the land and gave farms to thousands of settlers, mostly from the north-east. But its scale was small compared with rural poverty, and the big landowners of the south were left alone.
Q: What were the IMI and the IRI, and why were they founded?
A: The IMI (November 1931) was a state-backed bank that lent to industry for up to ten years. The IRI (January 1933) took over the industrial shares held by failing banks. Both were founded to prevent the collapse of banks and industry in the Depression. By 1939 the state controlled most of Italy's shipping and shipbuilding and much of its heavy industry.
Q: Give the date and effect of the Rocco Law.
A: 3 April 1926. It gave one union and one employers' body in each trade the sole legal right to bargain, took away the rights of all other unions, made strikes and lockouts illegal and set up labour courts.
Q: Why is the Charter of Labour (1927) a weak piece of evidence that the Corporate State worked?
A: It was a statement of principles approved by the Fascist Grand Council, a party body, so it had no legal force at first. It said that private initiative was the most useful instrument of the nation, which shows that the system was designed to leave business in charge.
Q: When did the 22 corporations come into being, and why does the date matter?
A: By the law of 5 February 1934. This was nine years after the Vidoni Pact, and the corporations were mostly advisory, so the date supports the argument that the Corporate State was more propaganda than practice.
Q: What happened to real wages under Mussolini?
A: They fell in the 1920s, by about 15 per cent between 1921 and 1929 in one estimate, because of the deflation of 1926–27. In the early 1930s prices fell faster than wages, so real wages recovered somewhat, and by 1938 they were about where they had been in 1929. The official wage figures are disputed, and many workers lost hours or jobs.
Q: Why did autarky fail?
A: Italy lacked coal, oil and iron ore, and substitutes cost more than imports. Sanctions (18 November 1935 to July 1936) were not strict enough to force the change, yet the regime kept controls on imports and foreign exchange, and the push for war industries strained the budget and the lira (devalued in October 1936). By 1940 Italy was short of key war materials.

H: Use the criteria: targets, cost, who benefited.
A: Indicative content. Success: De Stefani's balanced budgets and growth; the lira at 90; wheat output rising; the legal framework of the Corporate State; reclamation begun. Limits: inflation and a weak lira in 1925–26; deflation, wage cuts and unemployment in 1926–27; costs of the grain tariff; propaganda outrunning results; workers lost rights. Level 5 judges, for example, that the 1920s gave the regime its biggest political and propaganda successes but left Italy's economy exposed to the Depression.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Corporate State benefited employers more than workers.' Evaluate this view.
H: List what the Vidoni Pact, Rocco Law and wage cuts did to each side.
H: Do not forget what workers gained: contracts, welfare, leisure.
A: Indicative content. For: Vidoni abolished factory committees and Confindustria kept its independence; strikes banned, unions run from above, Rossoni removed (1928); wage cuts of 1927 and in the Depression; profits protected, state rescue of firms through the IRI. Against: employers also lost independence of action (state arbitration, the labour courts, controls from the Ministry); small firms and landowners lost out to large ones; workers gained collective contracts, welfare, family allowances and leisure. Level 5 judges that both were controlled by the state, but workers lost most, and defines "benefit".
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Autarky was the least successful of Mussolini's economic policies.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare it with the lira, grain, the marshes and the IRI.
H: What does "successful" mean: reaching targets, or raising living standards?
A: Indicative content. For: autarky failed to remove dependence on imports of coal, oil and metals; substitutes were costly; shortages in 1940; sanctions were light and the pressure came from the regime's own war aims; it worsened living standards. Against: some artificial fibres and hydroelectric power developed; IRI industries expanded; the corporate state was arguably less real. Level 5 chooses a criterion and justifies the ranking.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Mussolini's economic policies were driven by politics rather than economics.
H: Pick three policies and ask what each was for.
H: Find one policy where economics did drive the decision.
A: Indicative content. Support: Quota 90 (prestige, against Volpi's advice); grain (independence, symbolism); autarky (war aims); battles as propaganda; Corporate State as political control. Challenge: De Stefani's orthodox policy; the IMI and IRI were responses to a banking crisis; Volpi's debt settlement; land reclamation had economic value. Level 5 argues that the aims were political but that economic limits shaped results, and judges the balance with examples.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Fascist economic policy did more to help the powerful than ordinary Italians.' How far do the years 1922–39 support this view?
H: List who gained and who lost from each of: Quota 90, the grain tariff, the Vidoni Pact, the IRI, the 40-hour week.
H: Consider the south, the peasants and the small farmers as a separate group from industrial workers.
A: Support: Quota 90 helped bankers and holders of lire and hurt exporters and workers; the grain tariff helped large landowners and cost consumers; the Vidoni Pact and Rocco Law gave employers control; the IRI rescued banks and big firms with public money; wage cuts fell on workers. Challenge: welfare measures, family allowances, cheap leisure and reclamation benefited ordinary Italians; settlers on the marshes gained farms; many employers also lost independence to the state. A* judgement distinguishes different groups (big industry, large landowners, industrial workers, peasants, small business) and argues that the regime's policies were designed to protect the elites that supported it while giving ordinary Italians benefits that served political control.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Zamagni and Clark both stress that Fascist economic policy was driven by political goals. How far does the evidence of Quota 90 and autarky support this?
H: What would the economic case have been for a different exchange rate in 1926, and for importing materials in 1935?
H: Who gave the advice that was ignored?
A: Support: Quota 90 chosen against Volpi's preference for 120–125 because the lira was a symbol; autarky pursued despite its costs because the regime wanted to look independent and prepare for war. Challenge: both policies had some economic logic (a stable currency to win foreign loans; reducing the need for foreign exchange); in both cases the Depression and sanctions also forced the regime's hand. A* judgement distinguishes political aims from economic means and says that economic constraints shaped what politics could achieve.
:::

:::recap
- **Aims**: power, prestige, self-sufficiency, order. Policy was driven more by politics than by economics.
- **Four phases**: orthodox policy (1922–25); the battles (1925–29); Depression and the state (1929–35); autarky and war preparation (1935–41).
- **De Stefani** (October 1922 to July 1925): cut spending and taxes, left business alone; growth, then inflation. **Volpi** replaced him in July 1925.
- **Lira**: Pesaro speech 18 August 1926; Quota 90 reached by December 1927 (about 92.46); deflation, wage cuts, hurt exports.
- **Grain**: launched 20 June 1925; output up by about a third by 1935, imports down; dearer bread, other crops hurt, large landowners gained.
- **Marshes and land**: Mussolini Law of December 1928; Littoria (1932), Sabaudia (1934); real but small in scale.
- **Autarky**: after the Abyssinian war and sanctions (18 November 1935 to July 1936); costly and ineffective.
- **Depression**: registered unemployment from about 300,000 (1929) to about one million (1932–33); IMI (November 1931) and IRI (January 1933) rescued banks and industry; by 1939 the state controlled most shipping, much of pig iron and nearly half of steel.
- **Corporate State**: Vidoni Pact (2 October 1925), Rocco Law (3 April 1926), Ministry (July 1926), Charter (21 April 1927), National Council (law of 20 March 1930), 22 corporations (5 February 1934), Chamber of Fasces and Corporations (19 January 1939). Real as control of labour; weak as a Third Way.
- **Living standards**: welfare (ONMI 1925, allowances 1934) but real wages fell in the 1920s, unemployment was high, food consumption stagnated.
- **Judgement**: the policies were more successful as politics and propaganda than as economics; the IRI was the most lasting achievement.
:::
