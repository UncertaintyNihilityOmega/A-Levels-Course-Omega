:::why
In May 1948 a party that most of the world had never heard of won an election in South Africa, and within eleven years it had written the separation of the races into the law of the land. Its system, apartheid, decided where a person could live, whom they could marry, what job and school they could have and whether they could vote, and it did so by sorting every South African into a racial group at birth. It lasted until 1994, and it is one of the most studied cases of **minority rule**: government by and for a minority, here the white fifth of the population. Paper 4 asks you to explain how such a system was built, why the voters allowed it and how people lived under it and resisted it. This chapter takes the story from 1948 to the end of the 1950s, when the state had its main laws in place and had shown that it would answer protest with force.

## What is the question really asking?

The key question has two halves. **How was the apartheid state established?** asks for causes and methods: why the National Party won in 1948, and how it turned its ideas into laws and into a machine that enforced them. **What was its impact?** asks for effects: on the economy, on families, on education and above all on the everyday lives of Black South Africans, and on the opposition that grew in reply. A strong answer keeps three questions in mind:
1. **How new was it?** South Africa was already segregated in 1948. Did the National Party change the system or only tighten it?
2. **How was it done?** Which laws mattered most, and what made them work (the register, the pass book, the police, the courts)?
3. **What did it do to people?** Not only what the laws said but what a person could and could not do on a Tuesday morning.

The last section, "How far did apartheid change South Africa by 1959?", comes back to these.

:::def Minority rule
Government in which a minority of the population holds all political power and uses it for its own benefit. In South Africa the white minority (about one in five) ruled the Black majority from the creation of the Union in 1910 until the first non-racial election of April 1994.
:::

:::def Apartheid
An Afrikaans word meaning "apartness". The National Party's policy, from 1948, of classifying people by race and separating them by law in where they lived, worked, studied and voted, and of denying Black South Africans political rights in the white-ruled part of the country. Its supporters called it protection of each group's identity; its opponents called it white supremacy by law.
:::

:::note The official racial terms
The apartheid state sorted people into official groups, and this chapter uses its terms only as historical terms. **White** (or "European"), **Coloured** (people of mixed descent, and others the state placed in the group, mainly in the Cape), **Asian** or **Indian**, and **Native** or **Bantu**, the state's name for the African majority. "Native" and "Bantu" were the official words, but they are now seen as offensive, so this chapter writes **Black South Africans** or **Africans** and keeps "Native" and "Bantu" only inside the names of laws and offices (the Native Affairs Department, the Bantu Education Act). Many historians use "Black" for all the groups that apartheid oppressed and say so when they do; this chapter says when it uses the word in that wider sense.
:::

## South Africa around 1948

### A rich country built on cheap labour

The **Union of South Africa** was formed on 31 May 1910 from the Cape Colony, Natal and the former Boer republics, the Transvaal and the Orange Free State. Its Parliament, the franchise (right to vote) and its government were white. Its wealth came from three sources.

- **Mining.** Gold had been found on the Witwatersrand ("the Rand") in 1886, and Johannesburg grew from nothing around it. Gold dominated the country's exports and paid for much of the government's budget. In the 1940s new goldfields were found in the Orange Free State (the Welkom area), and diamonds, coal and other minerals added to the total. The mines depended on **migrant labour**: hundreds of thousands of African men, from the reserves in South Africa and from Mozambique, Basutoland and other neighbours, signed contracts of several months, lived in all-male compounds, were paid very low wages and went home between contracts. The Chamber of Mines, the owners' organisation, wanted that system to continue because wages were kept down.
- **Manufacturing.** Government help, especially the state Iron and Steel Corporation (ISCOR, founded 1928), had started industries in the 1920s and 1930s, and the Second World War (1939–45) speeded them up. South Africa could not import goods and had to make them: factories produced uniforms, vehicles, munitions and steel for the war effort, and employment in manufacturing rose by about 60 per cent between 1939 and 1945. The extra workers were largely African men and white women.
- **Agriculture.** White commercial farmers, mostly Afrikaans-speaking, grew maize, wheat, wool and fruit on large farms. They relied on African farm labourers and on **labour tenants**, families who lived on the farm and worked for the owner in return for the right to graze a few animals. After the war, farmers complained bitterly that labourers were leaving for the factories and the towns, and asked the government to stop them.

:::def Migrant labour
The system in which African men from the reserves and neighbouring countries worked on the mines, farms and in factories on short contracts, living apart from their families in compounds or hostels, and returning to the reserves between contracts. It kept wages low, because the employer did not have to pay for a family, and it kept workers dependent on the reserves.
:::

### How Black South Africans lived

South Africa was a **segregated** country long before 1948. Segregation grew with the Union, and by 1948 it already shaped almost every part of an African's life.

:::def Segregation
The separation of racial groups by law or custom in land, housing, jobs, schools and public places. In South Africa segregation was built by a series of laws between 1910 and 1948. Apartheid took segregation further and made it systematic, controlled by a central register.
:::

- **Land.** The **Natives Land Act of 1913** banned Africans from buying or renting land outside the "scheduled" reserves, and the **Native Trust and Land Act of 1936** added more land to them. Taken together the reserves covered only about 13 per cent of the country (about 7 per cent in 1913; sources give slightly different figures), although Africans were about two-thirds of the population. In 1951, the first census of the apartheid years, there were 12.67 million South Africans, of whom 8.56 million (about 68 per cent) were African, 1.10 million (about 9 per cent) were Coloured and about one in five were white.
- **The reserves.** The reserves were overcrowded and poor. The land was often too dry or too eroded to feed its people. Families depended on the wages that men sent home, and women, children and the old did the farming. The reserves also supplied the cheap labour that mines and farms wanted.
- **Towns and townships.** More and more Africans lived in the towns by 1948, which the law regarded as white areas where Africans were temporary visitors. By 1946, according to many accounts, there were more Africans than whites living in the towns. Africans lived in segregated **locations** or **townships** such as Orlando, outside Johannesburg, in small, poorly built houses, often with no electricity, and in hostels. There was a serious housing shortage, and families who could not find a house built shacks in squatter camps: in 1944 James Mpanza led a movement of homeless families in Orlando who built their own shanties on empty land.
- **Pass laws and the urban areas.** The **Native (Urban Areas) Act of 1923** let towns control the number of Africans who lived in them, and a person's "pass" showed whether they had permission to be there. Africans could be arrested for being without one, and curfews kept them out of town at night.
- **Work.** Africans were barred from skilled trades in the mines by the "colour bar", and the law did not recognise African trade unions as it did white ones. Wages were a fraction of white wages.
- **Politics.** Africans in the Cape had had a vote on the common roll, but in 1936 the Representation of Natives Act moved them to a separate roll, from which they elected three white members of the House of Assembly. A **Native Representative Council**, set up in 1936, advised the government but had no power.
- **Schools.** Almost all schools for African children were run by Christian missions and churches, which taught many of the future leaders of the ANC: in 1948 about 97 per cent of the schools for African children were run by churches.

Many Africans, Coloured people and Indians also lived under restrictions that were not the same as those on Africans. Indians, who had come to Natal as indentured labourers from the 1860s, had been the target of laws such as the Asiatic Land Tenure Act of 1946 that limited where they could own land. Coloured people in the Cape kept the vote on the common roll, but were poor and discriminated against.

:::warn Do not say that apartheid began segregation
Segregation was much older than 1948, and the idea that the National Party "invented" racial separation is a common mistake that costs marks. Say instead that the National Party took the existing system and made it **more complete, more rigid and more centrally controlled**, and gave it an ideology. Cite 1913, 1923 and 1936, and then say what was new after 1948: the racial register, the Group Areas Act, a systematic plan for the reserves and the state's control of African education.
:::

### The war and the 1946 strike

The war changed South Africa in ways that undermined the old order. Industry grew; the African urban population grew with it; wages in the towns were higher than in the countryside; and African leaders, seeing a war fought against Nazi racism, expected change. In 1944 young ANC members, among them Anton Lembede, Nelson Mandela, Walter Sisulu and Oliver Tambo, founded the **ANC Youth League** to press for a more militant policy. On 12 August 1946 about 60,000 to 76,000 African miners (estimates differ) struck on the Witwatersrand for higher pay, led by the African Mine Workers' Union under J. B. Marks. Police marched the workers back down the shafts and broke the strike within a week; officially nine miners died and more than 1,200 were wounded. The Native Representative Council adjourned in protest and never recovered its authority. White voters saw something else: a frightening display of African organisation, a Communist influence behind it (some strike leaders were Communist Party members) and a government that seemed to have no policy except to wait. The strike belongs to the story of 1948 because it fed white fears, and because it showed Africans that the existing channels of protest were closed.

## The National Party's victory, 1948

### What happened

The general election was held on **26 May 1948**. Most observers expected the United Party of Prime Minister **Jan Smuts** to win, and it did get more votes than any other party. But the seats went the other way.

### Why did the National Party win?

:::key Reasons for the National Party's victory in 1948
1. **The electoral system.** Rural seats were smaller than urban ones, and the National Party was strongest in the countryside.
2. **Afrikaner nationalism.** A fast-growing movement had united most Afrikaans-speaking voters behind the National Party, helped by the Dutch Reformed churches, the Broederbond and Afrikaner business.
3. **The impact of the Second World War.** The war divided white South Africans, brought black and white workers into the cities and left a sense of disorder and fear that the National Party exploited.
4. **The campaign of Smuts and the United Party.** The UP was divided, vague about race, and led by an ageing leader who had been abroad.
5. **Apartheid itself.** For many white voters, from the farmers to the white working class, it offered a clear answer to the question that the UP avoided: what should be done about the African presence in the towns?
:::

#### 1. The electoral system

South Africa used a **first-past-the-post** system of single-member constituencies, and the constituency boundaries were drawn by a commission. The Constitution of 1910 allowed the commission to give rural constituencies up to 30 per cent fewer voters than urban ones, to protect farming interests, and the boundaries drawn before 1948 favoured the rural districts. The result is easy to see in the figures. Divide the votes by the seats in the table: the UP and Labour won about 7,800 votes for each seat they took, while the National Party and Afrikaner Party needed only about 5,600. The UP piled up huge majorities in the cities of the Cape, Natal and the Witwatersrand, where it won seats by many thousands of votes, while the National Party won small rural seats narrowly. A calculation cited by the same source suggests that if every vote had been worth the same, the UP would have won about 80 seats and the HNP and Afrikaner Party about 60.

It is a strong argument that the electoral system was a **necessary** cause: with equal constituencies, the National Party would probably have lost. But it is not a sufficient explanation, because the rural seats were rural precisely because of who lived there, and the National Party also did well among Afrikaans-speaking voters in the towns. The boundaries explain the size of the margin, not the direction of the swing. Notice, too, that the electorate itself was tiny: apart from a few Coloured and Asian voters, only whites could vote, about a fifth of the population. In 1948 no African in the country had a vote that could remove a government.

#### 2. Afrikaner nationalism

:::def Afrikaner nationalism
A movement among Afrikaans-speaking white South Africans, descendants mainly of Dutch, German and French Huguenot settlers, to defend and promote their language, culture, religion and political power, which grew out of resentment of British rule and of the Boer War defeat (1899–1902), and which by the 1940s demanded that Afrikaners govern the country for themselves.
:::

Afrikaner nationalism was the National Party's core. It had been built carefully over three decades.

- **Memory and grievance.** Afrikaners remembered the Boer War, in which about 26,000 Afrikaner women and children are thought to have died in British concentration camps, and the British policy of anglicisation afterwards. In the 1930s the **Voortrekker Centenary of 1938**, when ox-wagons rolled across the country to celebrate the Great Trek, produced an emotional mass movement.
- **Organisation.** The **Afrikaner Broederbond** ("band of brothers"), a secret society founded in 1918, linked teachers, clergy, civil servants, farmers and businessmen and worked to place Afrikaners in positions of power. Churches, newspapers, the Federation of Afrikaans Cultural Societies (1929) and a network of Afrikaner banks and insurance companies (such as Volkskas and Sanlam) formed a whole world of institutions. The Economic Movement launched at a People's Congress in 1939–40 persuaded Afrikaners to save and to buy from each other.
- **Poverty and the city.** Hundreds of thousands of Afrikaners, many of them poor "bywoners" (tenants) ruined by the drought and the Depression, had moved into the cities, where they worked as unskilled labourers alongside Africans and were paid little more. They had reason to fear competition from black workers, and they responded to a party that promised the protection of white labour.
- **Politics.** In 1934 Hertzog and Smuts had joined their parties to form the United Party. Malan refused to join, and his "Purified" National Party became the party of Afrikaner nationalism. In 1939 the UP leaders split over entering the war. On 4 September 1939 (the date most accounts give) Parliament rejected Prime Minister Hertzog's motion for neutrality by 80 votes to 67, Hertzog resigned, and on 6 September Smuts became Prime Minister and South Africa declared war on Germany. Many Afrikaners felt that South Africa had been dragged into a British war, and some sympathised with Germany: the Ossewabrandwag ("ox-wagon sentinel"), a mass movement, opposed the war effort and some of its members committed sabotage.
- **Reunion.** In 1940 Hertzog's supporters joined Malan to form the Reunited National Party (HNP). Hertzog soon left politics, and his follower N. C. Havenga formed the Afrikaner Party in 1941. In 1947 Malan made a pact with Havenga so that the two parties did not stand against each other in the same seats, and together they won 79 seats in 1948.

Afrikaner nationalism gave the party a firm base. Its appeal went beyond identity and language: it offered a feeling of belonging to a people with a mission and a prospect of economic advancement. Without it there would have been no victory. Yet the **limits** are important too. The National Party won only about 41.6 per cent of the total vote in 1948 (with the Afrikaner Party), and the UP's total includes many Afrikaners. Nationalism alone did not explain why a party that won only 37.7 per cent of the votes on its own formed a government.

#### 3. The impact of the Second World War

The war brought the questions on which the election was fought to a head.
- **Division.** The vote on 6 September 1939, and then the war itself, split the white electorate. Opposition to the war was a rallying point for the nationalists.
- **Urbanisation.** African workers moved to the towns to work in the new factories. Housing, transport and food were short, and squatter camps grew. The NP could say that the United Party had lost control of the towns. The 1946 miners' strike and the squatter movements frightened many white voters.
- **War weariness and shortages.** Rationing, price controls and the shortage of consumer goods and housing continued after the war and angered many voters, and farmers resented the loss of workers to the towns.
- **A government abroad.** Smuts spent much of the war overseas as a leader of the Allied cause, and was admired abroad. At home, critics said that he cared more about the Empire and the world than about his own voters.

The war's impact was therefore double: it created the problems (urban Africans, shortages, a divided electorate), and it gave the National Party a language in which to blame the government for them.

#### 4. The campaign of Smuts and the United Party

The UP was an uneasy coalition of English-speaking liberals and businessmen, moderate Afrikaners and some conservative farmers. On "native policy" it gave no clear lead:
- In 1946 the government appointed the **Fagan Commission** (some sources date its appointment to 1947) (the Native Laws Commission, under Judge Henry Fagan), which reported in February 1948, just before the election. Its central finding was that African urbanisation could not be reversed and that the movement of Africans to the towns should be accepted and managed. That was realistic, but it left the UP open to the charge that it would allow Africans to settle in the cities permanently.
- Smuts himself said that segregation had "fallen on evil days", a phrase his opponents quoted, and spoke of **trusteeship** while doing little. The UP did not have a policy that answered the question white voters were asking.
- It had campaigned on its record and on fears of the opposition, but it was divided, and its leader was elderly. It also lost votes among the farmers who wanted tighter control of labour.

The National Party's own **Sauer Report** (drawn up in 1947–48 by a party commission under Paul Sauer) offered what looked like an alternative: **apartheid**, with separate areas for each race as the aim, stronger control of African movement into the towns and an end to African representation in Parliament. Malan's party was not completely united on what it meant. It sometimes meant total separation, which the farmers and the mine owners knew would ruin the economy, and sometimes meant that Africans could work for whites as long as they were controlled. That vagueness helped it, since each group of voters could read in it what they wanted.

#### 5. The voters' fears

The word **apartheid** was used in the 1948 campaign, but the election was mainly fought on the **"swart gevaar"** ("black danger"), the fear among white South Africans that a growing black majority would overwhelm them. The National Party promised to control the movement of Africans to the towns, to protect white jobs, to ensure that farmers had labourers and to end the mixing of the races. The UP's reply seemed weak by comparison.

:::key How far was Afrikaner nationalism the main reason?
**For:** it supplied the party's organisation, its voters (a majority of the Afrikaans-speaking vote) and its sense of mission; the war and the Voortrekker memory had sharpened it; it explains why Afrikaner farmers, workers and professionals voted together.
**Against:** the National Party and its ally won only about 41.6 per cent of the votes; without the **electoral system** it would probably not have won; the **war's** effects and the **UP's weakness** were needed to turn a minority movement into a government; and the party also won votes from English-speaking whites in some areas, who were afraid of African competition.
**Judgement to learn:** Afrikaner nationalism was the necessary foundation, since without it there would have been no National Party of this size. But the party needed the electoral system to turn a minority of votes into a majority of seats, and the war to turn a movement into a government.
:::

:::note Evidence: the three ideas behind apartheid
Historians who study the National Party's own words usually find three ideas in them, and a good answer can name them:
- **Race as the basis of society.** Each racial group was said to have its own character and future, and mixing was said to damage all of them. This idea turned the register of races into the foundation of the state.
- **White survival.** Afrikaners in particular spoke of the survival (*voortbestaan*) of the white, and especially the Afrikaner, people against a black majority. Hermann Giliomee's *The Afrikaners* (2003) puts this fear for the group's survival at the centre of Afrikaner politics.
- **Trusteeship and control.** Africans were said to belong to their own "nations" in the reserves, and the state claimed the right to decide where they could live and work in the white-ruled part of the country.
:::

## Building the apartheid state, 1948–59

### The aim and the method

Between 1948 and 1959 the National Party passed laws that built apartheid. Two prime ministers carried the work: **D. F. Malan** (1948–54), who made the first laws, and **J. G. Strijdom** (1954–58), who tightened them. The man most associated with the system's design, however, was **Hendrik Verwoerd**, a professor of sociology who became Minister of Native Affairs in 1950 and Prime Minister on 2 September 1958. Verwoerd gave the policy its more careful name, "separate development", and argued that Africans should develop in their own areas, the reserves, with their own authorities. The party's first approach, sometimes called *baasskap* ("boss-ship", white domination), openly said that whites would stay in charge. Verwoerd's version said that each group would eventually govern itself in its own territory, which in practice left the white-ruled 87 per cent of the country in white hands.

The method had four parts, and each of them is a good heading for an essay paragraph.
1. **Classify** every person by race (the register).
2. **Separate** the groups: in marriage, housing, public places and schools.
3. **Control** the movement and work of Africans through the pass system and labour law.
4. **Silence** opposition by banning people and organisations and by changing who could vote.

:::def Population Registration Act (1950)
The law that required every South African to be classified as White, Coloured or Native (African), with later subdivisions, and entered on a national register. A person's category decided where they could live, what jobs and schools they could have and whom they could marry, so almost every other apartheid law depended on it. Boards decided doubtful cases, using appearance, descent and reputation in the community.
:::

:::def Group Areas Act (1950)
The law that allowed the government to declare any urban area the residential or business area of one racial group only. People of other groups living or trading there had to leave, and compensation was usually low. It was used to demolish racially mixed areas and to move Black, Coloured and Indian people to new townships far from white areas.
:::

### Classifying people

The Population Registration Act is the foundation. Every South African was registered in one of three main groups, White, Coloured or Native, with subdivisions among the Coloured category (the Cape Malay, the Griqua and others, and Indians) that were added later. The classification was not simple, because many families were of mixed descent, and the boards had to decide each doubtful case. Their tests were arbitrary and humiliating: officials looked at skin colour, hair, speech and the neighbourhood where a family lived, and the "pencil test", in which a pencil was pushed into a person's hair to see whether it fell out, is the best known. Reclassification could split families, with one brother white and another Coloured, and could change a person's whole life. That is why the register mattered: it made a *legal fact* of race.

### Separating people

- **Marriage and sex.** The Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949) and the Immorality Amendment Act (1950) made sexual and marriage relations across the colour line crimes. The police investigated couples, and the state treated love as a threat to the order.
- **Public places.** The Reservation of Separate Amenities Act (1953) allowed park benches, buses, trains, post offices, beaches and public lavatories to be reserved for one group, with signs ("Whites only"), and the Act said that the separate facilities did not have to be equal.
- **Where people lived.** Under the Group Areas Act, areas of towns were assigned to a single group. The first large removal was **Sophiatown**, a crowded and lively suburb west of Johannesburg where Africans owned land, and where writers, musicians and the *Drum* magazine flourished. On 9 February 1955, 2,000 police moved the first families, by lorry, to Meadowlands in the new township of Soweto. About 65,000 people lived there by most accounts, and Sophiatown was levelled and rebuilt as a white suburb that was named Triomf ("triumph"). The Anglican priest **Trevor Huddleston**, who had worked there, protested publicly and described the removal in *Naught for Your Comfort* (1956). Similar removals hit Indian and Coloured communities in many towns.

### Controlling movement and work: the pass system

:::def Pass laws (the pass system)
The laws that required Africans to carry a document, the pass or (after 1952) the **reference book**, showing where they were allowed to live and work, and that allowed police to arrest anyone who could not show it. Together with the labour bureaux, which directed workers to jobs, they were the main means of influx control: keeping Africans out of the "white" towns unless they were needed as workers.
:::

Africans had carried passes before 1948, in the towns and the Cape. What the apartheid state did was to put the whole African population under one system. The **Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents Act of 1952** was misnamed. It "abolished" the many different passes by combining them in a single **reference book**, which every African man of 16 or over had to carry at all times. It held his photograph, his tax record, his employer's signature and the permission, or lack of it, to be in a given area. The 1952 Native Laws Amendment Act then limited the right to remain in a town for more than 72 hours to people who had been born there, or had lived there continuously for 15 years, or had worked for one employer for 10 years (the so-called Section 10 rights). Everyone else needed a permit from the **labour bureau**, which could order them back to the reserve.

The pass laws were extended to **women** in the later 1950s, with the first reference books for women issued from 1956 and in large numbers from 1959. Because they affected women's right to live with their families, they caused a particularly strong protest (see below).

The effects were enormous. Estimates of arrests under the pass laws vary and are unreliable, but one standard estimate puts them at about 500,000 a year in the 1950s, and in the following decades they were higher. A person could be arrested for being in the wrong place, for an out-of-date signature or for having no book, fined or sent to prison, or "endorsed out" of the town and sent to the reserve. Employers could not employ a worker without the right papers, and workers had no right to change jobs freely, and so the pass system worked as a system of labour control as well as of racial separation. The Native Labour (Settlement of Disputes) Act of 1953 denied African workers the right to strike, and the 1956 Industrial Conciliation Act allowed job reservation, so that whites kept the skilled jobs.

### Controlling politics

The Suppression of Communism Act of 1950 was the state's first weapon against opposition. The Communist Party dissolved itself shortly before it passed. The Act's definition of "communism" included any doctrine that aimed at bringing about change by "disturbance or disorder", so that it covered nearly any opponent, and a minister could ban a person from attending meetings, from certain places and from speaking publicly, without any trial. By the end of the decade many of the ANC's leaders were under banning orders.

The state also changed **who could vote**. The 1936 arrangement had left Coloured men in the Cape on the common roll. The National Party wanted them removed, but this needed a two-thirds majority of both houses in joint session, because the Cape franchise was entrenched in the 1909 South Africa Act. When Parliament passed the **Separate Representation of Voters Act in 1951**, the Appeal Court struck it down in *Harris v Minister of the Interior* (1952). The government then declared that Parliament could act as a "High Court" and overrule the court, and again was defeated. In 1955 it enlarged the Senate from 48 to 89 members and the Appeal Court from five to 11 judges, packing both with supporters. In 1956 a joint session finally passed the law, and Coloured voters were placed on a separate roll that elected four white MPs. The **Promotion of Bantu Self-government Act of 1959** then abolished the seats for white representatives of Africans, so that, by the end of the decade, no African in South Africa could vote for any member of Parliament at all. The **Bantu Authorities Act of 1951** abolished the Native Representative Council and substituted a system of chiefs and councils in the reserves, which the government controlled.

### The creation of Bantustans

:::def Bantustan (homeland)
One of the reserves that the apartheid government treated as the "homeland" of an African ethnic group, to which that group's members were said to belong politically whatever the place where they lived and worked. The government later gave four of them nominal "independence". Critics used "Bantustan" to suggest that they were artificial and dependent states.
:::

The idea behind the Bantustans was that Africans were not South Africans but members of separate "nations" (the Zulu, the Xhosa, the Sotho and others), each with a homeland. The reserves covered about 13 per cent of the land, although Africans were about two-thirds of the population, and the soil and rainfall were poor. The **Bantu Authorities Act of 1951** was the first step. It built self-government in the reserves on chiefs and "tribal" councils loyal to the state. Many chiefs gained from it, and many people hated it, and in some areas, such as Sekhukhuneland in the Transvaal and Pondoland in the Transkei, there were rebellions against the chiefs and the new authorities. The government set up the **Tomlinson Commission** (it reported in October 1954; a summary was published in 1955) to study how the reserves could support their people. The Commission recommended large-scale investment and development, but the government rejected its proposals for white capital to be invested in the reserves and for extra land, and accepted only the idea of ethnic self-government. The **Promotion of Bantu Self-government Act of 1959** then turned the reserves into eight (later ten) "national units", each to have its own commissioner-general and in time its own government. Chapter 2 of this topic, [End of minority rule (2): resistance and repression in South Africa, 1960–77](#hist.d.safrica.2), shows what the Bantustans became.

### Changing education for Black South Africans

Education shows the plan most clearly. Before 1953 almost all African schools were run by churches and missions, with state subsidies, and taught the same syllabus as white schools. The **Eiselen Commission** of 1949–51, under the ethnologist W. W. M. Eiselen, recommended that African schooling be placed under state control and adapted to what it called the needs of African society. The **Bantu Education Act of 1953** put this into effect.

:::def Bantu Education
The separate system of education for Africans created by the Bantu Education Act of 1953, under the control of the Native Affairs Department (a separate Department of Bantu Education from 1958). It had its own syllabus, designed, the government said, to prepare Africans for life in their own communities and for work in a society where they had no equal share.
:::

- **What it did.** It took African schools from the provincial authorities and the churches and placed them under the Native Affairs Department, which ran the syllabus, hired the teachers and decided which schools could exist. It made it illegal to run an unregistered school. The church schools were told that they would lose their subsidies from the state step by step (75 per cent of the teachers' salary subsidy in 1955, 50 per cent in 1956, 25 per cent in 1957 and none from 1958). The Anglican Bishop of Johannesburg, Ambrose Reeves, closed his schools instead of handing them over, the Methodists transferred theirs, and the Catholic Church kept its schools running without state help.
- **Why.** The government's reasons were open. Verwoerd's argument, as later histories report it, was that it was pointless to train Africans for a place in the white community that the state would not give them: schooling should prepare Africans to work in the reserves and in low-paid jobs in the white economy. Government spokesmen argued that mission schools had given Africans hopes that South African society would not meet, and that the state should control the curriculum.
- **Effects.** Many more African children went to school, but in poor conditions. By the 1970s about 75 per cent of Africans aged 7 to 15 were in school (about 3.7 million pupils), but only about 9 per cent of them reached secondary school, there were about 60 pupils to each teacher against about 20 in white schools, and the money spent on each pupil was a fraction of that spent on a white child: about 17 rand for an African pupil and about 128 rand for a white pupil in 1953 (a ratio of about 7.5 to 1), and about 42 and 644 rand in 1975–76 (about 15 to 1). Children had to be taught in their home language for their first eight years, and critics said that the syllabus stressed obedience and practical work and prepared pupils for a lower place in society.
- **Universities.** The **Extension of University Education Act of 1959** barred most Black students from the "open" universities (Cape Town and the Witwatersrand) and set up separate "tribal" colleges; the University of Fort Hare, where Mandela and many other leaders had studied, was taken over by the government.

The effect, in the opinion of many historians, was to harm a generation of African schoolchildren, and to turn the schools into places of resentment. Bantu Education later gave students their grievance in the Soweto uprising of 1976 (see [End of minority rule (2)](#hist.d.safrica.2)).

### The impact on everyday lives, 1948–59

Put the laws together and look at the same person through them.

:::key What apartheid meant in daily life by 1959
1. **Where you were.** An African man in Johannesburg needed a reference book, a permit from a labour bureau and a signed contract. If he lost the job he could be sent back to the reserve. His family might not be allowed to join him. If his township was in an area that the government had zoned for whites, he could be moved to a new one, as happened in Sophiatown.
2. **What you could do.** Skilled jobs could be reserved for whites. Africans could not strike. Trade unions that included Africans were not recognised. Wages were low.
3. **What your children learned.** They went to a Bantu Education school, probably crowded, and were taught less than white children, and the way to a university was narrowed.
4. **Whom you loved.** A marriage or a sexual relationship across the colour line was a crime.
5. **How the state saw you.** Your race was a fact on the register, and the state could decide to reclassify you. If you protested, a banning order could be served without trial.
6. **What it did to families.** Migrant labour, the pass laws and the reserves kept husbands in hostels and mothers and children in the countryside for most of the year.
:::

The impact was not the same on every group. Coloured and Indian South Africans were not subject to the pass laws in the same way, but they lost the vote (in the case of Coloured voters), were moved under the Group Areas Act, and were classified. Many white South Africans benefited: the state protected white workers' jobs, the farms got labour, and Black competition for housing and jobs was limited. The state also spent heavily on white schools, hospitals and housing. Some white people, among them members of the Black Sash (founded in 1955 by white women to protest against the removal of Coloured voters), the churches and the Liberal Party (1953), opposed apartheid, but the white electorate returned the National Party with more seats in 1953 and again in 1958.

## Opposition and enforcement, 1948–59

### Opposition: the ANC and the PAC

The **African National Congress** (ANC), founded in 1912 as the South African Native National Congress, was an organisation of educated, mainly mission-schooled Africans who used petitions, delegations and appeals to Britain. By the 1940s its leadership was cautious. The **Youth League** (1944) wanted a mass movement, and its **Programme of Action**, adopted in December 1949, called for boycotts, strikes and civil disobedience. In 1952 Albert Luthuli, a Zulu chief and teacher who was dismissed from his chieftaincy for refusing to leave the ANC, became president-general, and the ANC grew from a small body into a mass organisation, working with Indian, Coloured and white allies in the **Congress Alliance**: the South African Indian Congress, the South African Coloured People's Organisation, the (white) Congress of Democrats and the South African Congress of Trade Unions. The Alliance was committed to a non-racial South Africa. Its women's body, the **Federation of South African Women** (FEDSAW), was formed in 1954.

Not everyone liked this. A group of Africanists in the ANC, among them Robert Sobukwe, a lecturer at the University of the Witwatersrand, wanted a movement of Africans alone, to claim "Africa for the Africans", and believed that the Alliance gave whites and Indians too large a part. They left the ANC after the Freedom Charter and founded the **Pan Africanist Congress** (PAC) on 6 April 1959, with Sobukwe as president. The split between the ANC and the PAC mattered in 1960, as the next chapter shows.

### The Defiance Campaign, 1952

The first large protest was the **Defiance Campaign**, begun on 26 June 1952 by the ANC and the South African Indian Congress. Volunteers broke the laws they considered unjust: they used "whites only" entrances, waiting rooms and railway carriages, entered townships without permits and broke the curfew, and they accepted arrest peacefully. Nelson Mandela, then a young lawyer and a leader of the Transvaal ANC, was the "volunteer-in-chief". Over the next months about 8,500 volunteers went to prison (the South African History Online figure is 8,557), with the peak of more than 2,000 in October 1952.

The campaign was a success as a movement: it made the ANC a mass organisation and brought it national attention. It failed to change any law. The government responded with the **Public Safety Act** and the **Criminal Law Amendment Act of 1953**, which allowed the state to declare a state of emergency and imposed heavy fines, prison and whipping for protest, and it prosecuted leaders under the Suppression of Communism Act. There was also violence in the towns in late 1952, in Port Elizabeth, Kimberley and East London, which the government blamed on the campaign, though the ANC had told volunteers to remain non-violent; police fired on crowds and killed 14 people in Kimberley on 9 November alone. Luthuli was banned in 1953 and the campaign was called off in April 1953. The lesson that many ANC members drew was that non-violent protest was met by laws that made it a crime.

### The pass laws and the women's march, 1956

When the pass laws were extended to women, women's anger was fierce. A woman's pass could end her right to live in town with her husband, and to bring up her children. On **9 August 1956** about 20,000 women of all races marched to the Union Buildings in Pretoria, the seat of government, to deliver petitions to the Prime Minister, J. G. Strijdom, who was not there. They had come by train and bus from all over the country. Led by Lilian Ngoyi, president of the ANC Women's League, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophie Williams, they stood in silence for 30 minutes and then sang the freedom song that has been sung ever since, "Wathint' abafazi, wathint' imbokodo" ("You strike a woman, you strike a rock"). Accounts of the number of signatures on the petitions range from about 14,000 to over 100,000. The march did not stop the extension of the pass laws, which went on, but it became one of the most important symbols of resistance, and 9 August is now Women's Day in South Africa.

### The Freedom Charter, 1955

In 1955 the Congress Alliance organised a **Congress of the People**. Thousands of volunteers collected "freedom demands" from townships and farms, and about 3,000 people met at Kliptown, near Johannesburg, on 26 June 1955 and adopted the **Freedom Charter**. It was drafted by Alliance leaders (Z. K. Matthews is credited with the original idea, and Lionel "Rusty" Bernstein, Ruth First and others with the drafting). Police broke up the meeting on the second day, after the Charter had been read in full.

:::note Evidence: the Freedom Charter, 1955
*From the Freedom Charter, adopted at the Congress of the People, Kliptown, 26 June 1955. Shortened; every cut is marked with dots, and the words are the Charter's own.*

"We, the People of South Africa, declare for all our country and the world to know: that South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white, and that no government can justly claim authority unless it is based on the will of all the people; … that only a democratic state, based on the will of all the people, can secure to all their birthright without distinction of colour, race, sex or belief; …"

Under the heading *The People Shall Govern!*: "Every man and woman shall have the right to vote for and to stand as a candidate for all bodies which make laws; …"

Under *There Shall be Houses, Security and Comfort!*: "All people shall have the right to live where they choose, be decently housed, and to bring up their families in comfort and security; …"

*Use.* Which apartheid measures does each of these clauses answer? The first answers the idea of separate "nations" and the removal of African political rights, the second the loss of the vote, and the third the Group Areas Act and the Native Resettlement Act. As evidence it shows what the Congress Alliance wanted, and that the demand was for a **non-racial** South Africa. It does not show how widely the demands were shared, because the delegates were Alliance supporters.
:::

The Charter was a statement of principle, and not a revolution. Its sections ("The People Shall Govern!", "All National Groups Shall Have Equal Rights!", "The Land Shall be Shared Among Those Who Work It!", "The Doors of Learning and Culture Shall be Opened!") became the programme of the ANC, which adopted it in 1956. It also included a call for the national wealth, including the mines, banks and monopoly industry, to be transferred to the people, which the government used to label it communist, and which some ANC members, and the Africanists, rejected. For the ANC it was the document around which it organised for the next 40 years.

### Enforcement: suppression and the Treason Trials

The state used four main weapons against the opposition.
- **Laws.** The Suppression of Communism Act (1950), the Public Safety Act and Criminal Law Amendment Act (1953) and the Riotous Assemblies Act allowed bans on people, meetings and publications. Leaders such as Luthuli, Mandela and Sisulu were banned for periods from 1952–53 onwards, and could not attend meetings or leave their districts.
- **Police and informers.** The Special Branch of the police watched the leaders and the Alliance, and police broke up meetings.
- **The courts.** The state prosecuted leaders for incitement and for offences under the pass laws and Suppression of Communism Act.
- **Treason.** On 5 December 1956 the police arrested **156 leaders of the Congress Alliance** (105 Africans, 21 Indians, 23 whites and seven Coloured people, including Mandela, Luthuli and Sisulu) and charged them with high treason, on the ground that the Freedom Charter was a plan for a violent overthrow of the state.

The **Treason Trial** lasted from 1956 to 1961. The prosecution case was weakened over the years: charges against most of the accused were dropped by 1958 and 1959, and the last 30, who stood trial in Pretoria before three judges, were found not guilty on **29 March 1961**, because the state had not proved that the ANC had a policy of violent revolution or was a communist organisation. All the accused were therefore acquitted. But the state had achieved much of what it wanted. The leaders had been tied up for more than four years, the organisation had been drained of money and energy by the defence, and the trial had kept Mandela and many others from political work. Yet the trial also gave the accused a platform and widened international attention to the cause, and the accused became better known to each other and to the country.

:::key What the 1950s showed about resistance and repression
1. **Opposition grew in number and in organisation:** the Defiance Campaign, the Congress Alliance, the Freedom Charter, the women's march and the PAC split show a movement that was growing and divided over race and methods.
2. **Peaceful methods did not change the law.** Each campaign met new laws and new penalties.
3. **The state became more repressive each time.** Banning orders, the Public Safety Act and the Treason Trial built up a machinery of control.
4. **The Treason Trial ended in acquittals but achieved much of what the state wanted.** It exhausted the leadership and delayed the next phase of resistance until 1960.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: was apartheid new in 1948?)
Historians disagree about how new apartheid was. Saul Dubow, in *Apartheid, 1948–1994* (2014), stresses both the **continuity** with earlier segregation (the Land Acts, the pass laws and the colour bar all came before 1948) and the **novelty** of 1948: a systematic racial programme, controlled by the state and justified by an Afrikaner nationalist ideology. Hermann Giliomee, in *The Afrikaners* (2003), concentrates on Afrikaner nationalism and on the fear for the survival of the Afrikaners, and so on the National Party's motives. Leonard Thompson (*A History of South Africa*, 1990) and Nigel Worden (*The Making of Modern South Africa*, 1994) put 1948 in the longer history of white power and of African labour. A good essay uses the debate to make one point: to ask whether apartheid was a **break** or a **development** is to ask what you are comparing it to, and the best answer is both.
:::

## How far did apartheid change South Africa by 1959?

Now apply the three questions from the start of the chapter.

| Test | Judgement by 1959 |
|---|---|
| How new was it? | Partly new. The idea of segregation, the reserves and the pass laws were older. New were the register, the Group Areas Act, the systematic plan for the Bantustans, state control of African education, the attack on the Cape franchise, and the sense that the state was building a complete system. |
| How was it done? | By classification (the register), by controlling movement (the reference book, the labour bureaux), by controlling politics (banning, the Suppression of Communism Act, the removal of the voters) and by force (the Public Safety Act, the police, the trials). |
| What did it do to people? | It separated families, moved communities, narrowed schooling and limited jobs for Black South Africans, while giving white South Africans jobs, land and services. |
| How did people respond? | By organising (the ANC, the Alliance, the PAC), by protesting (the Defiance Campaign, the women's march) and by drawing up a programme (the Freedom Charter), against a state that answered each protest with new laws. |

:::key A judgement you can defend
By 1959 the apartheid state had been **established** in law: nearly all its main laws had been passed, the register was in operation, the pass system covered the African population and the reserves were being turned into Bantustans. Its **impact** was greatest on Black South Africans, whose movement, work, education and political rights were controlled more tightly than before 1948. It was a **development of earlier segregation** made far more thorough by a government that had both the electoral power and the ideology to do it. The apartheid state was strong because it controlled the law, the police and the register, and because white voters supported it. It was also weakened by the resistance it had provoked and by its economic dependence on the African labour that it tried to control.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'The National Party won the 1948 election because of Afrikaner nationalism.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained and balanced argument, a clear and supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Because of" needs a test. Criteria: (1) was it necessary (would the party have won without it)? (2) was it sufficient (did it win the seats)? (3) how far did other causes explain the result? Support for the view: the Broederbond (1918), the Voortrekker Centenary (1938), the Afrikaner banks and unions, the HNP–Afrikaner Party pact, the split over the war (4 September 1939, 80–67), a party that took 37.7 per cent alone. Challenge: the electoral system (71 seats on 51.7 per cent of the vote against 79 on 41.6 per cent), the war's effects (the 1946 strike, squatters, shortages), the UP's weakness (Fagan Report, Smuts's age and absence, he lost his own seat), the fear of African urbanisation (the *swart gevaar*). Judgement: the necessary foundation, but not the sufficient cause.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Afrikaner nationalism was the main reason why the National Party won. The Afrikaners felt proud of their culture and had a lot of organisations like the Broederbond. They hated the British because of the Boer War and did not want to fight in the Second World War. Malan said he would bring in apartheid and many Afrikaners voted for him. The United Party also lost because Smuts was old and did not have a clear policy on black people. So the National Party won in 1948 mainly because of Afrikaner nationalism."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, and two other factors are mentioned, so Table B is Level 3. But it is a **list of reasons** with no test of how far each one mattered, and the conclusion repeats the question. There is no use of the electoral system, which is the main challenge to the view. Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If we ask whether Afrikaner nationalism was *necessary*, the answer is yes. Without the Broederbond, the Afrikaans churches and the Afrikaner Party pact, Malan's party would not have had its organisation or its voters, and in 1939 it was the split over entering the war, with Parliament voting 80 to 67, that gave the nationalists their opening. But if we ask whether it was *sufficient*, the figures say no. The National Party and the Afrikaner Party won only about 41.6 per cent of the votes, against about 51.7 per cent for the United Party and Labour, and they won 79 seats only because rural seats were smaller: they needed about 5,600 votes for each seat, and their opponents about 7,800."

*Commentary.* Two criteria (necessary, sufficient) are applied and tested against exact figures. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs the other causes put in order, the link between them, and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is partly valid. Afrikaner nationalism was the base on which the National Party's victory was built, but it was not enough to win an election that the party lost on votes, and it was joined by three other causes that explain why 1948 happened when it did. I shall test the claim with two criteria: whether nationalism was necessary, and whether it was sufficient.

Afrikaner nationalism was necessary. In the thirty years before 1948 Afrikaners had built a world of their own: the secret Broederbond (1918), which put its members into schools, churches and the civil service; the Voortrekker Centenary of 1938, which turned history into a mass movement; Volkskas and Sanlam, which gave Afrikaner business a base; and the Economic Movement of 1939–40, which told Afrikaners to buy from each other. When Hertzog's neutrality motion was defeated by 80 votes to 67 in September 1939, many Afrikaners saw the war as a British one, and the Ossewabrandwag reminded them of the Boer War. In 1947 Malan's pact with Havenga's Afrikaner Party made sure that nationalist candidates did not split the vote. Without this movement, the party would have had neither voters nor workers. On the criterion of necessity the claim is justified.

But it was not sufficient, and the figures show it. The National Party took only 37.7 per cent of the votes on its own, and 41.6 per cent with the Afrikaner Party; the United Party and Labour took 51.7 per cent. The nationalists won 79 seats to 71 because of the **electoral system**: rural seats were allowed to have up to 30 per cent fewer voters than urban ones, so the nationalists needed about 5,600 votes for each seat and their opponents about 7,800, and a calculation cited by historians suggests that with equal seats the United Party would have won about 80. This was at least as important as nationalism in producing the result, because it turned a minority of votes into a majority of seats.

Two further causes gave nationalism its chance. The **war** brought African workers into the towns and filled them with shacks, shortages and, in August 1946, a strike of about 60,000 to 76,000 miners that frightened white voters. And the United Party **was weak**: the Fagan Report of February 1948 accepted that African urbanisation could not be reversed, which Malan could present as softness, and Smuts, 78 and a world figure, seemed remote and lost his own seat. Against this the National Party offered a clear policy, apartheid, which promised that the towns would be controlled and the farms supplied with labour. This appeal to white fear, the *swart gevaar*, won votes from the farmers and the white working class who were not simply Afrikaner nationalists.

On balance, then, Afrikaner nationalism was the necessary foundation but the electoral system was the decisive mechanism, and the war and the United Party's weakness explain the timing. The claim that the National Party won 'because of' nationalism is therefore too simple. The fairest verdict is that nationalism made the National Party a contender, and that the electoral system, the war and Smuts's failure to answer the question that frightened white voters made it the government."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): two explicit criteria, a balanced argument in which the factors are linked, and a sustained judgement that separates the foundation from the mechanism. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise and selected detail (80–67; 37.7 and 41.6 per cent; 79 against 71; 5,600 against 7,800; the strike of August 1946; the Fagan Report). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 650 words, 35 to 40 minutes.

:::eg Assess the impact of the apartheid legislation on Black South Africans by 1959. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the impact" asks how much the laws changed Black South Africans' lives, and so needs **criteria** (for example the areas of life affected, how deep the change was and what could be resisted) and a judgement about the **extent** of the impact.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) where people could live and move; (2) work and wages; (3) education; (4) political rights; (5) how far the impact was new. Evidence: Group Areas and Sophiatown (9 February 1955, 2,000 police); the 1952 reference book and the 72-hour rule; the 1953 strike ban and 1956 job reservation; the Bantu Education Act (1953; subsidies cut to nothing by 1958); the end of African representation (1959) and the Bantu Authorities Act (1951). Limits: segregation and pass laws pre-dated 1948; many laws were not fully enforced by 1959 (the Group Areas Act was applied slowly; women's passes were only just being issued); Coloured and Indian South Africans were affected differently. Judgement: severe and widening, but a development of earlier controls.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "The apartheid laws had a big impact on black South Africans. They had to carry passes and could not live where they wanted. They were moved from places like Sophiatown. They also had worse schools because of Bantu Education. They were not allowed to vote or marry white people. All these things made life very hard for black people, so the apartheid laws had a very big impact."

*Commentary.* True but general. Each law is named in a phrase, none is explained, there are no dates, and "a very big impact" is asserted. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 (4–6).

**Moving to Level 4: judge the effect against a criterion.** "The pass system affected African movement and work more than any other law. After the Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents Act of 1952 every African man of 16 or over had to carry a reference book, and the Native Laws Amendment Act of the same year allowed him to stay in a town for more than 72 hours only if he had been born there, had lived there for 15 years or had worked for one employer for 10. Anyone else needed a permit from a labour bureau and could be sent back to the reserve. By one estimate about 500,000 people were arrested each year under these laws in the 1950s. Yet the pass laws were not new: the Native (Urban Areas) Act of 1923 had begun them, and what 1952 added was a single book for every African man and its extension, from 1956, to women."

*Commentary.* A criterion (movement and work) is applied with accurate detail, and tested against the question of novelty. Level 4. It still needs the other areas of life, the comparison between them and a judgement on the **extent** of the impact.

**A Level 5 conclusion** (after paragraphs on movement, work, education and political rights).

"Overall, the impact of the apartheid laws on Black South Africans by 1959 was severe, but its character was different in different areas. In **movement and housing** the impact was greatest and was new in scale: the reference book, the 72-hour rule and the Group Areas Act made it possible to decide where each person lived, and removals such as Sophiatown in 1955 showed that the state would use force. In **work** the impact was to freeze Africans in a lower place: strikes were illegal (1953), unions were unrecognised and skilled jobs could be reserved (1956). In **education** the effect was slower but perhaps the most damaging in the long term, since the Bantu Education Act of 1953 built a separate and cheaper system, with about 17 rand spent on an African pupil for every 128 on a white one. In **political rights** the impact was to remove the little that remained: by 1959 there were no African seats in Parliament at all. What it did not do in these years was to stop resistance, for the Defiance Campaign, the Freedom Charter and the women's march came from the same decade. The impact was greatest where the state could use the register and the police, and smallest where, as in the Group Areas removals of Coloured and Indian communities, enforcement was only starting. I conclude that apartheid was less a sudden change than a systematic tightening of controls that already existed, and that its impact was to give those controls the force of a single design."

*Commentary.* A clear judgement on the **extent** of the impact, with a distinction between areas (the greatest where the register and the police could be used) that the paragraphs have earned. Table A Level 5; Table B depends on the detail in the paragraphs before it.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Evaluate the view that the Freedom Charter of 1955 was an important step in the resistance to apartheid." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Freedom Charter was very important. It was written at Kliptown in 1955 by the ANC and it said that everyone should be equal. It said that South Africa belonged to everyone and that the land should be shared. The government did not like it and arrested people for treason. So the Freedom Charter was an important step in the resistance to apartheid because it showed what the ANC wanted."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): one claim ("very important"), supported by a description of the Charter, with no criteria, no comparison with other forms of resistance and no judgement beyond the question repeated. Table B Level 2 (4–6): the knowledge is accurate but thin and partly wrong: the Charter was drafted by the whole Congress Alliance (not the ANC alone), about 3,000 people adopted it, nothing is said of the clauses, the nationalisation clause, the split that led to the PAC, or the link to the Treason Trial arrests of 156 people.

**The fix.** Choose a criterion and test it. "The Charter was important as a statement of aims, because it set out, for the first time and in the name of thousands of people, a programme for a non-racial South Africa: 'South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white.' It was also important as a cause of repression, because the state used it as evidence in the Treason Trial of 156 leaders from December 1956. But as a *step in resistance* it had limits. It was a statement and not a campaign, it did not change any law, and it divided the movement, since the Africanists who rejected its non-racialism left the ANC to form the PAC in 1959." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement, and the paragraph is on its way to Level 4 or 5.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give four reasons why the National Party won the 1948 election.
A: The electoral system (rural seats smaller: 79 seats on about 41.6 per cent of the votes); Afrikaner nationalism (Broederbond, churches, the Afrikaner Party pact, the memory of the Boer War and the 1939 split over the war); the effects of the Second World War (urbanisation, shortages, the 1946 miners' strike); and the weakness of Smuts and the United Party (the Fagan Report, no clear race policy, an elderly leader), together with the appeal of apartheid to white fears.
Q: What did the Population Registration Act and the Group Areas Act (both 1950) do?
A: The first required every person to be classified as White, Coloured or Native and entered on a register; the second allowed the government to set aside urban areas for one racial group and to move people of other groups out. The register was the basis of nearly all the other laws.
Q: What was the pass system, and how did it change after 1952?
A: Africans had to carry a document showing that they could be in a given area. The Abolition of Passes and Co-ordination of Documents Act (1952) replaced the many passes with one reference book that every African man of 16 or over had to carry. The Native Laws Amendment Act (1952) limited the right to stay in a town beyond 72 hours (Section 10 rights); labour bureaux decided who could work where. The system was extended to women from 1956.
Q: What was a Bantustan, and which laws began them?
A: A reserve treated as the "homeland" of an African ethnic group. The reserves came from the 1913 and 1936 land laws (about 13 per cent of the land); the Bantu Authorities Act (1951) built self-government there on chiefs; the Promotion of Bantu Self-government Act (1959) made them "national units" and ended African representation in Parliament.
Q: What did the Bantu Education Act of 1953 do, and why did the government pass it?
A: It put African schools under the Native Affairs Department, ended the church and mission schools' independence (subsidies cut to nothing by 1958), and set a separate syllabus. The government said that Africans should be trained for their own communities and for labour; critics said it was designed to prepare them for an inferior place. Spending per pupil was about 17 rand for an African and 128 for a white pupil in 1953.
Q: What happened at Sophiatown in 1955?
A: On 9 February 1955 about 2,000 police began moving residents, by lorry, to Meadowlands in Soweto under the Native Resettlement Act, and Sophiatown was later rebuilt as a white suburb, Triomf. About 65,000 people lived there. Trevor Huddleston protested publicly.
Q: What was the Defiance Campaign, and how did the government respond?
A: A campaign of peaceful law-breaking by the ANC and the South African Indian Congress, begun on 26 June 1952, in which about 8,500 volunteers went to prison. The government passed the Public Safety Act and the Criminal Law Amendment Act (1953), banned leaders and prosecuted others; the campaign was called off in April 1953, but the ANC had become a mass movement.
Q: What was the Freedom Charter and who adopted it?
A: A statement of non-racial principles ("South Africa belongs to all who live in it, black and white") adopted by about 3,000 people at the Congress of the People at Kliptown on 26 June 1955. It was drawn up by the Congress Alliance and adopted by the ANC in 1956; its call for the nationalisation of mines, banks and monopoly industry led some, including the Africanists, to reject it.
Q: What was the women's march of 1956?
A: On 9 August 1956 about 20,000 women marched to the Union Buildings in Pretoria to protest against the extension of the pass laws to women, led by Lilian Ngoyi, Helen Joseph, Rahima Moosa and Sophie Williams. It did not stop the extension but became a lasting symbol (Women's Day).
Q: What was the Treason Trial and how did it end?
A: 156 leaders of the Congress Alliance were arrested on 5 December 1956 and charged with high treason; the trial lasted until 1961, when the last 30 accused were found not guilty (29 March 1961) because the state could not prove that the ANC planned violent revolution. It tied up the leadership for more than four years.
Q: Why did the PAC split from the ANC?
A: Africanists led by Robert Sobukwe objected to the Congress Alliance with Indians, Coloureds and whites and to the Freedom Charter's non-racialism, wanting a movement of Africans alone ("Africa for the Africans"). They founded the PAC on 6 April 1959.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the electoral system in the National Party's victory in 1948.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: was it necessary or sufficient; compared with other causes. For: 79 seats on 41.6 per cent of the votes against 71 seats on 51.7 per cent; about 5,600 votes a seat against 7,800; rural seats could have up to 30 per cent fewer voters; a calculation cited by historians suggests the United Party would have had about 80 seats with equal seats. Against: the electorate was only white; nationalist organisation made the rural base; the war and the UP's weakness; fear of African urbanisation. A Level 5 answer weighs the mechanism against the causes and judges that the system turned a minority into a majority of seats but did not create the movement.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The United Party lost the 1948 election mainly because of its own weaknesses.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: Smuts's age and absence; the divisions in the party; the Fagan Report of February 1948 without a clear policy; Smuts's phrase about segregation having "fallen on evil days" turned against him; he lost his own seat. Challenge: the UP won more votes (51.7 per cent with Labour); the electoral system; the National Party's organisation and the appeal of apartheid; war and the 1946 strike. Level 5 judges, for example, that the UP's weaknesses mattered because they left the question of race to the National Party, but that the system and the organisation of the opposition were what turned a loss of votes into a loss of power.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the impact of the Bantu Education Act of 1953 on Black South Africans.
A: Indicative content. Transfer of control to the Native Affairs Department; church subsidies cut (75 per cent in 1955, 50 per cent in 1956, 25 per cent in 1957, none from 1958) and the different responses of the churches (the Catholic Church kept its schools); spending per pupil (about 17 and 128 rand in 1953); more pupils but crowded schools (about 60 to a teacher) and few reached secondary school; Fort Hare and the 1959 Act; protest and the boycott; long-term effect on 1976. Limits: mission schools had been poor too; enrolment rose. Level 5 uses criteria (access, quality, control, political effect) and judges that the Act widened access but entrenched inequality.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the effectiveness of the Defiance Campaign of 1952.
A: Indicative content. Success: about 8,500 volunteers arrested; the ANC became a mass organisation; Indians and Africans worked together; international attention. Failure: no law was changed; the Public Safety Act and Criminal Law Amendment Act (1953) made protest harder; leaders were banned; violence in Port Elizabeth, Kimberley and East London in late 1952 gave the government a pretext; the campaign was called off in April 1953. Level 5 sets criteria (changing laws, building the movement, provoking repression) and judges that it was effective as organisation but ineffective as pressure.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the pass system was the most important of the apartheid laws for controlling Black South Africans by 1959.
H: List what each law controlled (where, work, schooling, politics), then ask which of those controls the others depended on.
H: Compare the pass system with the register of race and with the Group Areas Act.
A: Indicative content. Support: the reference book (1952) and the 72-hour rule controlled movement and work; labour bureaux; about 500,000 arrests a year in the 1950s; it supplied cheap labour; women's protest in 1956 shows its importance. Challenge: the Population Registration Act (1950) was the basis for all the rest; the Group Areas Act, Bantu Education and the Suppression of Communism Act controlled homes, minds and politics; the Bantustans controlled citizenship. Level 5 argues that the register defined who was controlled but the pass system was the main day-to-day instrument of control, and gives a ranking with reasons.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which apartheid was a continuation of earlier segregation.
H: Compare 1913, 1923 and 1936 with 1950–59: which new things appear?
H: Think about continuity of laws and change in ideology and method.
A: Indicative content. Continuity: the Land Acts of 1913 and 1936 (about 13 per cent of the land); the pass laws and the Native (Urban Areas) Act of 1923; the colour bar; the removal of Cape African voters in 1936; the Native Representative Council. Change: the register of race (1950); Group Areas; the Bantustan plan; Bantu Education (state control of schools); the removal of Coloured voters; the ideology and the Suppression of Communism Act; a state committed to a complete system. Level 5 judges (as Dubow does) that apartheid built on the old foundations, but was a new system in its scale and design.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Women were the most effective opponents of apartheid in the 1950s.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare the women's march with the Defiance Campaign and the Freedom Charter.
H: What did each of them change, and what did the government do in reply?
A: Indicative content. Support: the women's march of 9 August 1956 (about 20,000 women, the Union Buildings, silence and song) and FEDSAW (1954); women made the pass laws a mass issue; the march became a symbol; the pass laws for women were delayed and resisted. Challenge: the march did not stop the extension of passes to women; the Defiance Campaign (about 8,500 arrests) built the ANC; the Freedom Charter set out a programme; the Treason Trial showed the reach of repression; the ANC and the Congress Alliance were led by men. Level 5 sets criteria (changing law, building organisation, lasting symbolic effect) and judges that women's resistance was strongest in the last, and that no campaign changed the law in the 1950s.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the success of the apartheid government's methods of suppressing opposition in the 1950s.
A: Indicative content. Success: the Suppression of Communism Act and banning orders removed leaders from public life; the Public Safety Act and Criminal Law Amendment Act (1953) ended the Defiance Campaign; the Treason Trial exhausted the leadership for more than four years; no change of law. Limits: the Treason Trial ended in acquittals (29 March 1961); the Freedom Charter and the women's march came after the first bans; the PAC was formed in 1959; repression created a new, more radical generation. Level 5 judges the methods successful in the short term, but at the cost of turning moderate opponents into revolutionaries.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Apartheid was a response to white fear more than a plan for white power.' Evaluate this view.
H: What fears did white voters have in 1948, and what did the new laws do about each?
H: Was the system designed in advance (the Sauer Report) or built step by step?
A: Support: the *swart gevaar* and the fear of African urbanisation; the 1946 strike, squatters and shortages; Afrikaner fear for group survival (Giliomee); laws passed as the pressure grew (the pass book in 1952, Group Areas in 1950); the party's vagueness in 1948. Challenge: the laws protected white jobs, land, wages and services; the Sauer Report and Verwoerd's plan show a design; the franchise was removed deliberately; the aim of "white survival" was also an aim of white domination. A* judgement: the two cannot be separated, since fear was the motive and power the means, and historians differ on which to stress; the 1950s laws look like a plan, but the plan was built in response to events.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Freedom Charter was more important as a symbol than as a programme.' Evaluate this view.
H: What did the Charter ask for, and what did the ANC do to bring it about?
H: Consider the PAC split, the Treason Trial and the later history of the Charter.
A: Support: it changed no law; it was a statement, not a plan; the nationalisation clause and the non-racial wording divided the movement; it became the ANC's most quoted document and shaped later debates about the aims of the struggle. Challenge: it was a programme in that it set out specific demands (the vote, land, houses, schools) on which the ANC organised for decades; it was used as evidence in the Treason Trial; it led to the formation of the PAC, so it was important politically. A* judgement: it was both, but its programme was a statement of aims and its power was as a symbol of a non-racial future against the state's policy of separate "nations".
