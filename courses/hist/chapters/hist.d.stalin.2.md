:::why
In 1937 and 1938 the Soviet state arrested about 1.6 million of its own people on political charges and shot about 680,000 of them, among them most of the men who had made the Revolution with Lenin, and the leader who ordered it was celebrated at the same time as a father and a genius. Three years later the same leader, who had signed a pact with Hitler in 1939, was caught by surprise when Hitler's armies invaded. Was this effective government? The question is hard because it can be answered in different ways: Stalin was extremely effective at keeping power, and much less effective at keeping the country safe, and the two are connected. ## Opposition to Stalin and the road to the Terror

### The party in 1933: success and discontent

By 1933 Stalin was the undisputed head of the party (see [Stalin's Russia (1)](#hist.d.stalin.1)). The price of the Great Turn had been high. The Great Turn (1928–29) was Stalin's switch to forced collectivisation of agriculture (peasants made to give up their land and join state-run collective farms) and to the Five-Year Plans for heavy industry; it is taught in full in [Stalin's Russia (3)](#hist.d.stalin.3). Millions of peasants had died in the famine of 1932–33, millions more had been deported as "kulaks" (the better-off peasants, as the regime labelled them), and living standards in the towns had fallen. Inside the party there was real unease. Some leaders thought the pace of change was dangerous, and some wanted to relax it. Stalin never trusted men who criticised his decisions, and his suspicion of former rivals deepened. His wife, Nadezhda Alliluyeva, shot herself on 8 November 1932 after a quarrel, and some biographers think that this loss increased his sense that those closest to him could not be trusted.

### The Ryutin platform, 1932

Opposition to Stalin did not die with the Right. In 1932 a former Moscow party official, **Martemyan Ryutin**, wrote a long document, the **Ryutin platform**, which attacked collectivisation and the pace of industrialisation, called for Stalin's removal and described him as the evil genius of the revolution. A short appeal to party members called on them to save the party from Stalin's leadership. Ryutin and others were arrested in September 1932. According to later accounts, Stalin demanded the death penalty, but the Politburo, which included men who still thought of party members as comrades, refused, and Ryutin was sentenced to ten years in prison (he was shot in January 1937). The episode is important for two reasons. It showed that real opposition existed, if only in small groups. It also showed that members of the Politburo still had a limit that they would not cross, and that Stalin wanted a more obedient leadership.

### The Seventeenth Congress, 1934

The Seventeenth Party Congress (26 January–10 February 1934) was called the **Congress of Victors**, to celebrate the success of the first Five-Year Plan. Old leaders such as Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky, who had opposed Stalin in 1929, made speeches praising him. Sergei Kirov, the head of the Leningrad party and a member of the Politburo, was popular and, according to later accounts, was seen by some delegates as a gentler alternative to Stalin.

Stories were told later, and repeated by Nikita Khrushchev (Stalin's successor as party leader, who denounced him in a secret speech) in his speech to the Twentieth Congress in 1956, that some delegates struck out Stalin's name from the ballot for the Central Committee and that the ballots were destroyed to hide the number. The details are disputed and the evidence is not strong, but the Congress is important for another reason. According to Khrushchev, of the 139 members and candidate members of the Central Committee elected there, 98 were arrested and shot, mostly in 1937–38, and many of the delegates were arrested too. Whatever happened at the voting, Stalin had good reason to see this body as a potential threat.

### Kirov's murder, 1 December 1934

On 1 December 1934, at about 4.30 in the afternoon, Sergei Kirov was shot dead in a corridor of the Smolny Institute in Leningrad by **Leonid Nikolaev**, a young party member who had been dismissed from his job and had a grievance. The murder was a shock, since Kirov was a member of the Politburo and a friend of Stalin's. Within hours Stalin had put his own interpretation on events. That night he signed a decree (the "law of 1 December") that ordered cases of terrorism to be heard in ten days, without lawyers, with no appeal and with immediate execution of death sentences. Stalin went to Leningrad himself and questioned Nikolaev. Kirov's bodyguard, Borisov, died on the way to be questioned, in a lorry accident that has never been explained. Nikolaev was shot on 29 December.

What lay behind the murder has been argued about ever since. Robert Conquest, in *The Great Terror* (1968), thought that Stalin had arranged it so as to have a pretext for destroying his enemies. Some Leningrad NKVD officers had earlier released Nikolaev when he was found carrying a revolver near Kirov, which looked suspicious. But no document has been found that shows that Stalin ordered the killing, and most historians who have worked in the archives, such as Oleg Khlevniuk, conclude that Nikolaev acted alone. What is not disputed is what Stalin did next. He announced that Kirov had been murdered by a conspiracy of former oppositionists, linked to Zinoviev and Kamenev, and he used the murder to start a new wave of arrests.

:::key What the Kirov murder did
1. **Gave a pretext.** It allowed Stalin to say that the oppositions of the 1920s were still active and were murderers.
2. **Changed the law.** The decree of 1 December 1934 let the NKVD try and execute people in secret, with no defence and no appeal.
3. **Targeted the old oppositions.** Zinoviev and Kamenev were arrested within weeks and sentenced in January 1935 to ten and five years in prison (they would be tried again in 1936).
4. **Began the wave of arrests of 1935–36.** Thousands of former oppositionists and Leningrad party members were deported, and the Leningrad NKVD, which had failed to protect Kirov, was purged.
:::

## The reasons for the Terror and Purges

Historians agree about what happened and disagree about why. The best answers to a question on the Terror do not give one cause, but weigh several.

| Reason | The case | The limits of the case |
|---|---|---|
| **Stalin's personality and paranoia** | He was suspicious and vengeful, took the Kirov murder as a conspiracy, approved execution lists himself and wrote on the NKVD's reports; his telegram of 10 January 1939 confirmed that "physical pressure" was allowed | Paranoia alone does not explain why thousands of officials carried the orders out, or why the Terror took the shape it did |
| **Removing opposition and rivals** | The old leaders (Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin, Rykov) were tried and shot; the Central Committee of 1934 was almost wiped out; no one could now oppose Stalin | By 1934 the old opposition had no power, so the great majority of the victims were not a threat to his position |
| **Security and fear of war** | The rise of Hitler and the growth of Japan's power in the east made Stalin fear a "fifth column" (secret supporters of an enemy inside a country) of spies and wreckers; the national operations hit Poles, Germans, Latvians and Koreans | The Terror weakened the army and the economy, so it did not make the USSR safer; many victims had no link to any foreign country |
| **Scapegoats for economic failure** | Failures (the shortages, accidents in mines and the rail system) were blamed on "wreckers", from Shakhty (1928) onwards | The Terror also killed able managers and engineers whom the economy needed |
| **Ideology** | Stalin said at the Central Committee plenum (a full meeting of the Central Committee) of February–March 1937 that as socialism advanced, class enemies would become more desperate; the party's tradition had a place for the idea of the enemy within | Ideology explains the language of the Terror, not its scale or timing |
| **The system itself** | The NKVD received quotas and competed to exceed them; local party bosses used the campaign to settle scores and protect themselves by denouncing others; ordinary people denounced neighbours; careerists used the purge to rise; the machine, once started, was hard to stop | The centre still set the quotas and Stalin could stop it when he wished, as he did in November 1938 |

The debate among historians is largely about how to weigh these.
- **The "totalitarian" or "intentionalist" account** ("intentionalist": Stalin planned the Terror from the start). Robert Conquest, *The Great Terror* (1968), described the Terror as planned and directed by Stalin from the Kirov murder onwards, to destroy the old party and make himself supreme.
- **The "revisionist" account** (here, challenging the older view from the sources; the word changes its meaning from one topic to another, so check each time what is being revised). J. Arch Getty, *Origins of the Great Purges* (1985), argued, from published sources, that Stalin did not control events as fully as the older view supposed. In his account the leadership was divided and hesitant, the regions and the party's own tensions pushed the campaigns forward, and the Terror came from the struggle between the centre and the local bosses and from the rank and file's anger at their officials. He did not deny that Stalin was responsible for the killings.
- **The archive evidence.** After 1991, historians gained access to the Soviet archives. **Oleg Khlevniuk**, in *Master of the House: Stalin and His Inner Circle* (2009), used Stalin's papers and the records of the Politburo to show that Stalin approved the execution lists, set the quotas and directed the NKVD closely. **Stephen Kotkin**, in *Stalin: Waiting for Hitler, 1929–1941* (2017), placed the Terror in the context of Stalin's fear of war and conspiracy and showed him as an active, informed ruler who made the key decisions. **Sheila Fitzpatrick**, in *Everyday Stalinism* (1999), studied how ordinary people lived through the period, with the denunciations, the fear and the opportunities for promotion that the purges offered.

Most historians now accept a mix: Stalin initiated, directed and approved the Terror, but it worked through the NKVD and a society in which many people took part.

## The NKVD and the machinery of terror

The **NKVD** (People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs) was the Soviet state's political police and, from 1934, much more. On 10 July 1934 the OGPU was merged into it, and it controlled the secret police, the regular police, the border guards, the prisons and, through the Gulag, the camps. It was the instrument through which the Terror was carried out.

| Head of the NKVD | Years | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Genrikh Yagoda | 1934–36 | Ran the NKVD during the Kirov investigation and the first show trial; replaced in September 1936, arrested in 1937, tried in March 1938 and shot |
| Nikolai Yezhov | 1936–38 | Ran the mass operations of the Terror ("Yezhovshchina"); removed in November 1938, arrested in April 1939 and shot in February 1940 |
| Lavrentiy Beria | from late 1938 | Ended the mass operations, purged the NKVD of Yezhov's men and later became one of the most powerful men in the USSR |

The NKVD worked through arrest, interrogation, trial and the camps. People were arrested at night. Interrogations went on for days without sleep, with threats against families, and from 1937 with beatings and torture, which Stalin explicitly approved. Many confessed, were sentenced by a "troika" (a panel of three) or by a military court, and were shot or sent to the camps. The great majority of the victims were not party leaders but ordinary people: peasants, workers, clerks, teachers, priests and engineers, whose names were put on lists to fill a quota.

:::def NKVD
The People's Commissariat for Internal Affairs, the Soviet political and regular police from 1934 (when it absorbed the OGPU). It arrested, interrogated and sentenced the victims of the Terror, and ran the Gulag. Its heads were Yagoda (1934–36), Yezhov (1936–38) and Beria (from late 1938).
:::

## The show trials

The three Moscow show trials were staged in the Hall of Columns in Moscow. They were public, with foreign journalists and diplomats present, and the prosecutor was **Andrey Vyshinsky**. The accused confessed to a series of fantastic crimes: murdering Kirov, plotting to kill Stalin and other leaders, sabotaging factories and railways, spying for Germany and Japan, and acting on the orders of the exiled Trotsky. The confessions were obtained by interrogation without sleep, beatings, threats to the families of the accused and promises that they would live if they confessed. Some of the accused were loyal party men who were persuaded that the party needed their confession. The result was a show that was convincing to many, in the USSR and abroad, and it was a tool for blaming the "enemies" for the failures of the regime.

| Date | Trial | Main defendants | Charges | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 19–24 August 1936 | "Trial of the Sixteen" | Zinoviev, Kamenev, Smirnov and 13 others | Organising Kirov's murder and plotting to kill Stalin, on Trotsky's orders | All 16 sentenced to death and shot on 25 August |
| 23–30 January 1937 | "Trial of the Seventeen" | Pyatakov, Radek, Sokolnikov, Serebryakov | Sabotage and treason, links with Trotsky, Germany and Japan | 13 shot; Radek and Sokolnikov got ten years and died in prison |
| 11 June 1937 | Secret military trial | Marshal Tukhachevsky and seven generals | Treason and spying for Germany | All eight shot on 12 June |
| 2–13 March 1938 | "Trial of the Twenty-One" | Bukharin, Rykov, Yagoda, Krestinsky, Rakovsky | Treason, spying, sabotage and the murder of Gorky and others | 18 sentenced to death, including Bukharin, Rykov and Yagoda, who were shot on 15 March; three given prison terms |

The trials had a few moments of drama. On the first day of the 1938 trial Nikolai Krestinsky withdrew his confession in court, and on the next day he reversed himself. Bukharin, who admitted political responsibility but denied the specific charges, tried to use his speeches to say as much as he could, and wrote to Stalin begging for his life from prison. They had no effect.

:::def Show trial
A public trial staged for propaganda, with the result decided in advance, in which defendants confess to crimes, usually invented, so as to blame them for the state's failures and to warn the public. The three Moscow trials of 1936–38 are the best-known examples.
:::

:::key What the show trials achieved
1. **They destroyed the old Bolsheviks.** Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin, Rykov, Pyatakov, Radek and Yagoda: nearly all the leaders of 1917 who might have been alternatives to Stalin were dead or in prison.
2. **They supplied scapegoats.** Problems such as accidents, shortages and delays were presented as the work of conspirators.
3. **They set a script.** The same charges (wrecking, spying, terrorism and Trotskyism) were then used against thousands of lower officials.
4. **They justified the Terror.** If the great Bolsheviks were traitors, no one was safe from suspicion.
:::

## The Yezhovshchina, 1937–38

The **Yezhovshchina** ("the time of Yezhov"), also called the Great Terror, is the name given to the mass terror of 1937–38 when Nikolai Yezhov was head of the NKVD. It was not a purge of the party alone, and it is often misunderstood as such.

### The purge of the party and the elites

From the autumn of 1936 the party's leaders, the central and regional officials, factory directors, army officers and engineers were arrested in waves. Stalin told the Central Committee plenum in February–March 1937 that the party must root out wreckers, and said that enemies would become more desperate as socialism advanced. In the next two years most of the senior people in the party, the government and the army were arrested; the Central Committee elected in 1934 was nearly wiped out. The loss of experience was large: a large part of the leading cadre (trained core of officials) of 1936 was replaced within two years, by younger men, many of whom owed their rise to the purge and were loyal to Stalin.

### The mass operations

The Terror reached ordinary people through the **mass operations** of the NKVD, ordered from the summer of 1937.
- **Order 00447** (30 July 1937) was an operation "against former kulaks, criminals and other anti-Soviet elements". Quotas were set for each region: 75,950 people to be shot ("first category") and 193,000 to be sent to the camps. The quotas were soon raised, and regions asked for more. By November 1938 about 767,000 people had been sentenced under the order, and about 387,000 of them were shot.
- **The national operations** targeted people of ethnic groups thought to be linked to hostile foreign states. The "Polish operation" (Order 00485, August 1937) led to the arrest of about 140,000 people, of whom about 111,000 were shot. Germans, Latvians, Finns, Koreans and others were also targeted, and in 1937 about 170,000 Koreans in the Far East were deported to Central Asia.
- **Family members** of those arrested could be arrested too, and children were put in orphanages.

Behind the numbers were individuals: peasants with a name on a list, a priest who had once been a kulak, a railway clerk who had a German name. At Butovo, a firing range south of Moscow, more than 20,000 people were shot in 1937–38, and the Memorial society in Russia has since recorded their names.

:::key The scale of the Terror, 1937–38
1. **About 1.6 million** people were arrested by the NKVD for political crimes in 1937–38 (NKVD records).
2. **About 680,000** were shot (681,692: 353,074 in 1937 and 328,618 in 1938), and others died in custody. Some historians think the true figure is higher.
3. **Most victims were ordinary people**, not party members: peasants, workers and minorities were the great majority.
4. **Stalin personally approved** lists of names, with his signature or approval on many of the lists, amounting to tens of thousands of names.
:::

### The end of the Yezhovshchina

By 1938 the Terror was out of control: the arrests of managers and officials, and the escalation of quotas, were disrupting the economy and the army. On 17 November 1938 a joint decree of the government and the Central Committee condemned "mistakes" and "excesses" in the NKVD's work, ended the troikas and restored some legal procedure. Yezhov was removed as head of the NKVD (Beria replaced him on 25 November), arrested in April 1939 and shot in February 1940. Many of his officers were shot as well. Stalin said at the Eighteenth Congress in March 1939 that "some mistakes" had been made, but that the purge had been necessary. A few thousand of those who had been arrested were released. The mass terror did not return in the same form, though the repression did not stop.

## The Gulag and forced labour

The **Gulag** (the Main Administration of Camps) was the NKVD's system of forced-labour camps and colonies. It grew from the early 1930s, when it was given the job of building canals, railways and mines and settling remote regions. The first great project was the White Sea–Baltic Canal (1931–33), built by about 100,000 prisoners with hand tools, in which thousands died. The camps in the far north, such as **Kolyma** (gold mining in the north-east of Siberia, with temperatures of 50 degrees below zero) and Vorkuta (coal), were among the deadliest.

Prisoners were of many kinds. Some were "politicals", convicted of counter-revolutionary crimes, and many were ordinary criminals or people convicted under harsh laws (for example, for being late to work, or for taking grain from a collective field). The numbers grew sharply as the Terror pushed people into the camps.

It cannot show the people who came and went in between: millions passed through the camps in these years, and the NKVD's records show about 1 million deaths in the camps between 1934 and 1953, with higher estimates when the colonies and deportations are included. :::def Gulag
The NKVD's Main Administration of Camps, and by extension the whole system of forced-labour camps and colonies of the Soviet Union. It supplied labour for canals, mines, timber and railways, and held about 1.5 million prisoners in its camps by 1941, besides the colonies.
:::

The Gulag had two purposes. It punished and removed people the regime saw as enemies. And it was an economic system, a source of labour for projects in places no one wanted to go, which the NKVD ran like an industrial empire. These two goals pulled in different directions, since camps that killed their workers could not meet their targets, but the system did not stop growing.

## The purge of the armed forces

The Red Army had been Trotsky's creation and had been run in the 1930s by the Commissar for Defence, Kliment Voroshilov, with a group of younger, more professional commanders. The best known was **Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky**, deputy commissar for defence, an advocate of modern, mechanised warfare. On 11 June 1937 Tukhachevsky and seven other senior officers (Yakir, Uborevich, Kork, Eideman, Feldman, Primakov and Putna) were tried in secret on charges of treason and of spying for Germany, and shot the next day. The charges rested on forced confessions and on a file of forged documents; there is no credible evidence that they were guilty. Their deaths began a purge of the officer corps.

The figures usually given for the top ranks are severe: of the five marshals three (Tukhachevsky, Yegorov and Blyukher) were killed; of 15 army commanders, 13; of 57 corps commanders, 50; and of 186 divisional commanders, 154. Almost all army commissars were removed as well. Later research suggests that the share of all officers removed in 1937–39 was smaller than once believed, under one in ten, and that some of those dismissed returned to service. The damage to the leadership, however, was serious, because the best-trained and most independent senior commanders were gone and the men who replaced them were younger and less experienced.

The effects showed in the **Winter War** with Finland (November 1939 to March 1940), when the Red Army struggled against a much smaller force and lost far more men, and in the first months of the German invasion in 1941. Historians argue about how much of the failure of 1941 to blame on the purge, since the Red Army was also the victim of Stalin's mistakes in 1941 and its structural problems, but few doubt that the purge weakened it.

:::key Reasons for the Terror and Purges: summary
1. **Stalin's personality**: suspicion and vengeance, personal approval of lists and torture.
2. **Removing rivals**: the old Bolsheviks and the Central Committee of 1934.
3. **Security**: fear of foreign enemies and a fifth column before a war.
4. **Scapegoats**: blame for economic failure and accidents.
5. **Ideology**: the claim that class struggle intensifies.
6. **The system**: quotas, careerism, denunciation, local bosses.
The best answers rank these and explain how they fit together, and show that the personal and the systemic worked together.
:::

## The cult of personality

### The development of the Stalin cult

A **cult of personality** is the exaltation of a leader by the state so that he appears a superhuman figure to whom all success is due. The Stalin cult grew from the Lenin cult (see [Stalin's Russia (1)](#hist.d.stalin.1)). Its first great moment was the fiftieth birthday on 21 December 1929, when the press was full of tributes. Through the early 1930s the praise grew. Tsaritsyn had been renamed Stalingrad in 1925 and Yuzovka Stalino in 1924, and in the 1930s a mountain peak, a canal (the White Sea–Baltic Stalin Canal), factories, collective farms and streets carried his name. At the Seventeenth Congress in 1934 the delegates' speeches praised him as the party's leader, and after the Kirov murder and the Terror the cult became far more intense, as the leaders who might have been his equals died. By 1936 he was "the Vozhd" (the Leader), "the father of the peoples" and the "best friend" of every group in the country, from children (the slogan "Thank you, Comrade Stalin, for a happy childhood" appeared in 1936) to scientists and soldiers. His sixtieth birthday on 21 December 1939 was marked by huge celebrations and by the founding of the Stalin Prizes.

### Propaganda methods

The party used every medium to spread the cult.
- **Press and posters.** *Pravda* and every newspaper carried his speeches, his picture and tributes; posters showed him as a wise, calm and benevolent leader, often with children, workers or Lenin.
- **Film.** Films such as *Lenin in October* (1937) showed Stalin as Lenin's closest companion and the true organiser of the Revolution, a version of history that had little to do with what had happened.
- **Art, literature and music.** Under **socialist realism**, the official style from 1932–34, writers and painters had to show the Soviet Union's leaders and workers in a heroic and optimistic light. Paintings such as Alexander Gerasimov's *Stalin and Voroshilov in the Kremlin* (1938) presented Stalin as a strong, wise leader. Poets, folk singers and composers wrote odes to him.
- **Rewriting history.** The *Short Course* of the history of the party, published in 1938 and partly written under Stalin's supervision, gave an official account in which Stalin was Lenin's right hand and Trotsky and the other victims of the trials were traitors from the start. It was printed in millions of copies and studied in schools, factories and party circles. Photographs were altered: Trotsky had been removed from pictures of Lenin, and after his fall Yezhov was removed from a photograph of Stalin by the Moscow–Volga Canal.
- **Names and places.** Cities, streets and institutions were renamed for him and his supporters.
- **Mass spectacles.** Parades and meetings in Red Square, with enormous portraits, and the "elections" of 1937, in which crowds cheered the leader.
- **Slogans.** "Life has become better, comrades; life has become more joyous", said at a conference of Stakhanovites (record-breaking model workers) in November 1935, was repeated as proof of the regime's success.

:::def Cult of personality
The deliberate creation by the state of a hero-image of its leader, through propaganda, art, ritual and the control of information, so that he appears wise, powerful and the source of all success. In the USSR it was applied to Lenin after 1924 and to Stalin from 1929, and it was at its height in the 1930s.
:::

### Aims and impact

**The aims** were several.
1. **To legitimise Stalin's rule.** He was not a charismatic figure when he took power, and the cult gave him the glory of the Revolution through his link with Lenin.
2. **To unify a divided country.** In a huge multi-ethnic state undergoing violent change, a single figure could be a focus of loyalty that the party's complex ideas could not.
3. **To justify sacrifice.** Collectivisation, the Five-Year Plans and the Terror could be presented as the work of a wise leader whose judgement was not to be questioned.
4. **To shift blame.** If things went wrong, it was because of wreckers and traitors, never of Stalin.
5. **To protect his position.** An image of omniscience made criticism seem like treason and gave him a weapon against rivals.

**The impact** was mixed, and historians do not agree on how deep it went.
- Many people, particularly the young and those who had gained from the new system (promoted workers, educated women, new officials), believed in the leader sincerely, and some wrote to him as to a father. Fitzpatrick has shown how the language of the regime shaped the way many people described their lives.
- Others went along with the cult out of fear or habit, since to withhold praise could be dangerous, and many spoke with a double voice in public and in private. Jokes and private diaries show doubt, and peasants who had lost their land and relatives had good reason to hate him.
- The cult made the Terror easier, since people were taught to see arrests as the punishment of Stalin's enemies, and made the leader the only person who could correct "mistakes", as in the Yezhov affair, which allowed Stalin to be thanked for ending the terror he had ordered.
- It made the system rely on one man, so that bad news was hidden from him and his errors, as in 1941, were not corrected.

:::key The cult of personality: aims and impact
1. **Aims**: legitimacy, unity, justification of sacrifice, shifting blame, protection against rivals.
2. **Methods**: press and posters, film, socialist realism, rewritten history (the *Short Course*), altered photographs, renamed places, mass spectacle.
3. **Impact**: real belief among many, conformity and fear among others, a Terror easier to carry out, and a system that depended on one man's judgement.
:::

## Stalin's leadership and the Constitution

### The nature of Stalin's leadership

Formally, Stalin held only one post for most of these years: General Secretary of the party. He was not the head of the government, an office held by Molotov as chairman of Sovnarkom from December 1930 to May 1941, when Stalin took it himself. The real power lay in the way he worked.

- **Personal rule.** The Politburo met less often as the 1930s went on, and many decisions were taken by Stalin and a small group (Molotov, Kaganovich, Voroshilov, Ordzhonikidze until his suicide in February 1937, then Mikoyan, Zhdanov and Beria; all members of his inner circle), at meetings in his office and dinners at his dacha. Records of his Kremlin visitors show the time he spent with these men. Decisions were then confirmed by polling the Politburo members, without a meeting. Party congresses became rare: after the Seventeenth in 1934, none met until the Eighteenth in March 1939.
- **Hands-on control.** Stalin read reports, annotated them with instructions and approved lists, decisions on appointments and even details of films and books. Khlevniuk shows that he was closely and personally involved in the Terror and in every important decision.
- **Fear and patronage.** The Terror made officials dependent on Stalin: they could be promoted or shot at his word. Many who rose after 1937 owed their positions to the purges and stayed loyal. Even his closest associates lived with the risk of arrest, and their wives and relatives were arrested.
- **The party reduced to a tool.** The party was no longer a forum for debate. Power had passed to the leader and the NKVD, while the party carried out orders.

This was a personal dictatorship that worked through the party and the police. It was highly effective at keeping Stalin in power and carrying out his decisions, but it depended on fear, it cut him off from honest advice and it was ruinous when he was wrong.

### The Stalin Constitution, 1936

The **Constitution of 1936** was adopted on 5 December 1936 by the Extraordinary Eighth Congress of Soviets and was named after Stalin, who chaired the drafting commission (Bukharin and Radek helped with the drafting before they were arrested). It was announced as the most democratic constitution in the world, and it was a statement that socialism had been built.

| What the Constitution promised | What happened |
|---|---|
| A Supreme Soviet of two chambers elected by **all adults over 18**, by secret ballot and direct vote | In the elections of December 1937 there was one candidate for each seat, chosen under the party's control; the official results gave a turnout of about 97 per cent and about 99 per cent for the single list |
| **Civil rights**: freedom of speech, press, assembly and religion, and the inviolability of the person and the home (Article 125 and others) | The rights were to be used "in accordance with the interests of the working people"; the NKVD arrested people without trial during the very months the Constitution was being applied |
| **Social rights**: the right to work, rest, education and security in old age | These were partly real (for example, free education and health care, and no open unemployment), as a result of the planned economy |
| An end to the **disenfranchisement** (loss of the vote) of "class enemies" (former kulaks, priests, nobles) | The vote was extended in law, but the people concerned were among the main targets of Order 00447 in 1937 |
| A federation of **eleven republics** with national rights | Power stayed with Moscow and the party |
| The **Communist Party as the "leading core"** of all organisations (Article 126) | This confirmed the one-party state |

The Constitution had several purposes. It was a statement to the world, in the context of the Popular Front, that the USSR was a democracy. It was propaganda at home, presenting the new socialist society as one with rights and equality. And it described a state in which the party was already in charge, so that nothing in it limited the leader's power. In practice the rights were on paper, and the Terror began in earnest within months of its adoption. Its real effect was that the Soviet Union's rulers had to speak of rights and democracy, and when the regime later changed, the Constitution gave dissidents something to quote.

:::key How effectively did Stalin govern at home?
1. **Control**: extremely effective. By 1938 there was no organised opposition, the party and the army had been remade, and Stalin's decisions were obeyed.
2. **Methods**: terror, the cult and a personal dictatorship, with a Constitution that gave the regime a democratic front.
3. **Costs**: about 680,000 shot in 1937–38, a Gulag of 1.5 million, the loss of experienced managers and officers, and a system that depended on one man's judgement.
4. **The balance**: the regime was effective in its own terms (power) and damaging in terms of the country's strength, as the army's performance in 1939–41 showed.
:::

## Foreign policy, 1929–41

### The aims of Soviet foreign policy

Stalin's foreign policy had three main aims. The first was **security**. The Soviet leaders believed that the capitalist powers were hostile and that the USSR was in danger of attack, as it had been in the Civil War, when Britain, France, Japan and others had intervened. The second was **time**: the Five-Year Plans were meant to build industry and an army for the war that Stalin thought would come ("fifty or a hundred years behind", as he said in 1931). The third was **influence**: Socialism in One Country put the Soviet state first, and foreign Communist parties, organised in the **Comintern**, were expected to serve the interests of the Soviet Union. Where the aims conflicted, security came first.

### Relations with Germany

After the Treaty of Rapallo (April 1922), in which Germany and the Soviet Union restored relations and renounced claims against each other, the two outcast states cooperated. The Treaty of Berlin (April 1926) confirmed neutrality, and in secret the German army trained and tested weapons on Soviet soil (at Lipetsk for aircraft and Kazan for tanks) in return for technical help. At the same time the Comintern, in its "Third Period" (1928–34), told Communist parties that the main enemy was "social fascism", meaning the Social Democrats, and the German Communists (KPD) refused to cooperate with the Social Democrats (SPD) against the Nazis. Many historians argue that this division helped Hitler to power.

Hitler's appointment as Chancellor on 30 January 1933 changed the situation, because he had called Communism his enemy and had written of winning land in the east (see [Hitler's Germany (1): how Hitler gained control](#hist.d.hitler.1)). The German army's secret cooperation ended in 1933. Stalin at first thought that Hitler would not last, and that a Nazi regime would prepare for revolution in Germany. But in 1934 Germany signed a non-aggression pact with Poland, and Soviet leaders began to look for new partners.

### The search for collective security

From 1933–34, the Commissar for Foreign Affairs, **Maxim Litvinov**, led a policy of **collective security**: agreements with Britain, France and others to deter Germany, within the framework of the League.
- **Recognition.** The USA recognised the Soviet government on 16 November 1933.
- **The League of Nations.** The USSR, which had denounced the League as a capitalist club, joined it on 18 September 1934 and had a permanent seat on its Council.
- **France and Czechoslovakia.** The **Franco-Soviet Treaty of Mutual Assistance** (2 May 1935) and a treaty with Czechoslovakia (16 May 1935) promised help against aggression. The Czech treaty took effect for Soviet aid only if France helped first.
- **The Comintern.** At the **Seventh Comintern Congress** (July–August 1935) the Bulgarian Communist Georgi Dimitrov called on Communists to form **Popular Fronts** with socialists and liberals against fascism. In France and Spain, Popular Front governments were elected in 1936.

:::def Collective security
The policy of the Soviet Union (from 1933–34, led by Litvinov) of making alliances with Britain, France and other states, and working through the League of Nations, to deter Nazi Germany by a united front. It ended with the Munich agreement of 1938 and the Nazi–Soviet Pact of 1939.
:::

:::def Popular Front
The policy of the Comintern, decided at its Seventh Congress in 1935, that Communist parties should join with socialists and liberal parties in alliances against fascism, instead of treating them as enemies. It replaced the "social fascism" line of 1928–34.
:::

### The Spanish Civil War, 1936–39

When General Franco's rising against the Spanish Republic began in July 1936 and Hitler and Mussolini sent him aircraft, troops and supplies, the Soviet Union at first joined the Non-Intervention Committee set up by Britain and France, but it began to aid the Republic from October 1936. Soviet aid included aircraft, tanks, artillery, small arms, pilots, tank crews and advisers, paid for by Spain's gold reserves, about 500 tonnes of which were shipped to Moscow in 1936. The Comintern recruited the **International Brigades**, which brought about 35,000 volunteers from many countries to fight for the Republic. Spain was a test of the Popular Front and a chance for the Soviet Union to show itself as the leading anti-fascist power.

Stalin's aims in Spain were limited and cautious. He wanted the Republic to win, but not to frighten Britain and France, so the Soviet advisers and the Spanish Communists opposed a social revolution and moved against the anarchists and the independent Marxist party (POUM), which the NKVD helped to suppress in Barcelona in 1937. Soviet aid was also affected by the Terror at home: many of the Soviet advisers were recalled and shot. As the Republic weakened, and Stalin turned to other priorities, aid declined in 1938, and Franco won in March 1939. The outcome showed the limits of collective security: the western powers' non-intervention allowed German and Italian help to decide the war.

### Policies towards China and Japan

In the east the USSR faced Japan, whose army had been expanding on the Asian mainland. In 1929 a Soviet–Chinese conflict over the Chinese Eastern Railway in Manchuria ended in a Soviet victory. After Japan occupied Manchuria in 1931–32, the Soviet Union tried to avoid war, offered Japan a non-aggression pact (which Japan refused) and sold its share of the railway to Japan's puppet state of Manchukuo in 1935. It strengthened its Far Eastern army and signed a mutual assistance pact with Mongolia in 1936, and Japan signed the **Anti-Comintern Pact** with Germany in November 1936 (Italy joined in 1937).

After Japan attacked China in July 1937, Stalin supported the Chinese Nationalist government, since a Japan fighting in China would not attack the USSR. The **Sino-Soviet Non-Aggression Pact** (21 August 1937) was followed by credits, aircraft, military advisers and pilots for Chiang Kai-shek's forces. There were serious border clashes with Japan, at Lake Khasan in July–August 1938, and at **Khalkhin Gol** on the Mongolian border in May–September 1939, where General Georgy Zhukov's forces defeated the Japanese Sixth Army and a ceasefire was agreed in September 1939. The victory, with the Nazi–Soviet Pact, helped to turn Japan's attention south, and on 13 April 1941 the two countries signed a **Neutrality Pact**, which protected the Soviet Far East when Germany invaded two months later.

### Munich and the turn to the Pact

Munich was the turning point. In September 1938 Hitler demanded the Sudetenland of Czechoslovakia, and the Soviet government said it would honour its treaty with Czechoslovakia if France did. France did not, Poland and Romania refused to allow Soviet troops to cross their territory, and the Czechs, who did not wish to rely on Soviet help alone, did not ask. On 29–30 September, Britain, France, Germany and Italy agreed to give Hitler the Sudetenland at the Munich conference, to which the USSR was not invited. Stalin concluded that Britain and France were willing to turn Hitler eastward and that collective security was dead. In March 1939, when Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia, Stalin warned at the Eighteenth Congress that the USSR would not pull chestnuts out of the fire for others, and on 3 May 1939 Litvinov, a Jew and the symbol of collective security, was dismissed and replaced by Molotov.

### The Nazi–Soviet Pact, 1939

In the summer of 1939 the USSR negotiated with both sides. Britain and France sent a military mission to Moscow, which arrived by sea on 11 August without authority to sign an agreement, and which could not secure Poland's agreement to let Soviet troops cross its territory. Germany, which wanted to invade Poland without a war with the USSR, offered a deal. On **23 August 1939** Molotov and the German Foreign Minister Ribbentrop signed the **Nazi–Soviet Pact**, a ten-year non-aggression treaty with a **secret protocol** dividing eastern Europe into spheres of influence: Finland, Estonia and Latvia, eastern Poland and Bessarabia fell to the Soviet side, and Lithuania was moved to it in a later agreement.

Germany invaded Poland on 1 September 1939, and Britain and France declared war on 3 September. On 17 September the Red Army entered eastern Poland, and the two states divided the country. In the spring of 1940, the NKVD shot about 22,000 Polish officers and officials held as prisoners, at Katyn and other sites. In the **Winter War** (30 November 1939–13 March 1940) the USSR attacked Finland to gain a buffer to defend Leningrad. It won the Karelian Isthmus and the city of Vyborg after heavy losses (more than 120,000 Soviet dead, against about 25,000 Finnish) and the war damaged its reputation and encouraged German leaders to think the Red Army weak. The USSR was expelled from the League on 14 December 1939. In June 1940 it occupied the Baltic states of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania (annexed in August) and took Bessarabia and northern Bukovina from Romania. Under trade agreements of 1940–41 the USSR supplied Germany with grain, oil and raw materials, and the trains were still crossing the border on the eve of the invasion.

### Barbarossa, 22 June 1941

On 22 June 1941 Germany attacked the Soviet Union, along a front of about 3,000 kilometres, in Operation Barbarossa. Stalin had received many warnings, from British intelligence, from his own agents (including Richard Sorge in Tokyo) and from the German troop build-up on the border, and had dismissed them as provocations, fearing that any preparation would give Hitler an excuse. On 14 June a TASS statement (from the Soviet news agency) had denied rumours of a German attack. In the first hours more than a thousand Soviet aircraft were destroyed on the ground, and in the weeks that followed millions of soldiers were killed or captured as German armies advanced hundreds of kilometres. Stalin, according to some accounts, was shocked and withdrew for a short time, though he returned to lead the war effort.

| Date | Event | Significance |

### How effective was Stalin's foreign policy?

The question of effectiveness depends on the criterion.

| Criterion | The case that the policy was effective | The case that it was ineffective |
|---|---|---|
| **Security and time** | The Pact kept the USSR out of the war for 22 months, and the territory gained (eastern Poland, the Baltic states, Karelia) pushed the frontier westwards | The invasion came anyway, in the most dangerous form, and the buffer was weakly defended; the old frontier defences were partly dismantled before new ones were ready |
| **Alliances** | Collective security got the USSR into the League and a treaty with France; Spain showed Soviet support for anti-fascists | Britain and France distrusted a state that was purging its army and making show trials; Munich excluded the USSR; Spain was lost |
| **Dealing with Germany** | A trade and non-aggression deal bought time and deliveries of machinery | The Pact allowed Hitler to conquer Poland and France without a second front, and Stalin trusted him too far in 1941 |
| **Far East** | Khalkhin Gol and the Neutrality Pact removed a second front | The aid to China ended, and Soviet gains did not stop the war from coming in the west |
| **Judgement of the leader** | Stalin saw that Britain and France would not fight for the USSR, and acted on that | He ignored warnings in 1941; the Terror undermined the army, the officer corps and intelligence |

Historians differ. **Jonathan Haslam**, in *The Soviet Union and the Struggle for Collective Security in Europe, 1933–39* (1984), argued that Moscow sought collective security in earnest, and that its failure was due as much to the western powers' mistrust as to Stalin's own policies. In his later work, *The Soviet Union and the Threat from the East, 1933–41* (1992), he stressed how far the Far Eastern danger shaped Stalin's decisions. **Kotkin**, in *Waiting for Hitler*, presented a Stalin who followed a consistent strategy of keeping out of a war between capitalist powers and expanding Soviet security, and who was caught out by Hitler in 1941. Other historians stress that the 1939 Pact was the reverse of an anti-fascist policy and that it showed that the Soviet Union's aim was power and territory, not collective security.

## Weighing it up: how effectively did Stalin govern?

Questions on this key question ask you to judge Stalin's government in terms of effectiveness, so you must choose your standard. Four are useful.

| Criterion | Judgement |
|---|---|
| **Control**: did he keep and use power? | Extremely effective: no opposition by 1938; the party, the army and the economy obeyed |
| **Efficiency**: did the machinery of government work well? | Mixed: the Terror removed experienced managers and officers, the system depended on fear and on one man |
| **Security**: did it protect the USSR? | Short-term gains (buffer, time) but the invasion of 1941 nearly destroyed the state, and the purges contributed |
| **Human cost and legitimacy**: at what price, and with what consent? | Very high: about 680,000 shot in 1937–38, 1.5 million in the camps, and a regime built on fear as well as belief |

A strong answer separates **effectiveness in Stalin's own terms** (power, control, industrialisation) from **effectiveness for the country** (security, efficiency, lives), and argues that the two diverged. A defensible judgement is that Stalin governed with great effectiveness in securing his own position and that the methods he used, the Terror and the cult, were the same ones that damaged the efficiency and security of the state, so that his strength in one sense was a weakness in another.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
- **Robert Conquest**, *The Great Terror* (1968): the Terror was Stalin's deliberate plan to destroy the old party. His account was based largely on defectors' reports, the trials and the Khrushchev revelations, and is accepted for its basic picture even where its numbers are now revised.
- **J. Arch Getty**, *Origins of the Great Purges* (1985): a revisionist study that stressed the role of struggles between the centre and the regions and the party's own instability. His critics said that he gave too little weight to Stalin's decisions, and the archives have since shown how closely Stalin directed events.
- **Oleg Khlevniuk**, *Master of the House* (2009): used the archives to show that Stalin was the author of the Terror and ruled through a small circle, and that the Politburo was a tool.
- **Sheila Fitzpatrick**, *Everyday Stalinism* (1999): examined the lives of ordinary people, and how denunciation, promotion and fear shaped society.
- **Stephen Kotkin**, *Stalin: Waiting for Hitler, 1929–1941* (2017): a full account of Stalin's ability and ideology, and the pressures of the coming war.
- **Jonathan Haslam** on foreign policy: see above.
:::

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 2.2
1. **Define the criteria first.** For "Assess the effectiveness of Stalin's foreign policy": criteria could be *security* (did it protect the USSR?), *alliances* (did it win allies?) and *time* (did it help the USSR to prepare?). For the Terror: *causes* (personal, systemic, external) or *effects* (on Stalin's power, on the economy, on the army).
2. **Spend a paragraph on each of three or four factors**, each ending with a sentence that says how much it mattered and compares it with another.
3. **Use precise evidence**: 1 December 1934, 75,950 and 193,000 (Order 00447 quotas), about 680,000 shot, 11 June 1937, the 23 August 1939 pact, 22 months, 22 June 1941.
4. **Match the dates.** "1929–41" for foreign policy; "1934–38" for the Terror. Do not give a history of the cult in a question on the purges.
5. **Include the human beings.** Sensitive material is part of the answer, but use it for the argument (the scale and the targets), not for shock.
6. **Judge.** End with a view that says which factor was most important, or how far the policy was effective, and why.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, and the total is out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'The Purges were mainly the result of Stalin's personality.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge that is mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement that is partly substantiated; a good range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criterion: which cause, if removed, would most probably have prevented the Terror as it happened? "Mainly" means more than any other cause.
- For the view (personality): his suspicion and vindictiveness; Ryutin (he wanted death, 1932); the use of the Kirov murder; his approval of lists with his signature; the 10 January 1939 telegram on "physical pressure"; the Terror hit even his own circle (relatives of Molotov and Kalinin arrested); it stopped when he decided (November 1938).
- Against 1 (political calculation): removing the old Bolsheviks and the Central Committee of 1934; no alternative left.
- Against 2 (fear of war): Hitler, Japan and the fifth column; the national operations.
- Against 3 (the system): quotas, competition between NKVD offices, local bosses and denunciation; Getty; Fitzpatrick.
- Against 4 (scapegoating): wreckers, from Shakhty to the show trials.
- Judgement: personality was a necessary condition, but the Terror needed the system and the political situation; "mainly" overstates it.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Purges were mainly the result of Stalin's personality. Stalin was very paranoid and did not trust anybody. He was afraid that people were plotting against him, especially after Kirov was murdered in 1934. He had lots of people arrested and shot, including Bukharin and many generals. Stalin was in charge of the NKVD and the purges happened because he wanted them to. This shows that his personality was the main reason."

*Commentary.* Relevant and broadly accurate, but general ("lots of people", "very paranoid") and with little precise evidence. It describes Stalin's actions and then asserts that this proves the view, without considering any other cause or what "mainly" means. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 2 to 3 (about 6). To move up: give precise evidence, ask what else mattered, and judge.

**A Level 4 paragraph (an alternative cause, but no comparison).** "However, the Terror also had causes beyond Stalin's personality. The NKVD was given quotas for arrests: Order 00447 of July 1937 set quotas of 75,950 people to be shot and 193,000 sent to camps, but regional officials asked for higher numbers and in several regions the quotas were raised. Local party bosses used the campaign to destroy rivals, and ordinary citizens sent in denunciations. J. Arch Getty has argued that these tensions between the centre and the regions helped to drive the purges. This shows that the system helped to shape the Terror."

*Commentary.* Precise and focused, and a real alternative cause, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). But the paragraph never says how important the system was compared with Stalin's personality, and it does not notice that the quotas were set by the centre and that the NKVD was Stalin's instrument. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs a **comparison and a judgement**.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The claim can be tested by asking which cause, if absent, would most probably have prevented the Terror from taking the form it did. On that test Stalin's personality was a necessary condition, but not the main reason in the sense of the one that explains most. The Terror needed a ruler who was suspicious and ruthless, but it also needed a political situation that gave him reasons for it and a system that could carry it out, and the best explanation combines the three.

The case for the view is strong. Stalin's suspicion was long-standing, and he reacted to opposition with hatred. In 1932 he demanded the death penalty for Ryutin, and it was only the reluctance of the Politburo that saved him, and after the murder of Kirov on 1 December 1934 he signed the decree that allowed trials in ten days with no appeal. The archives show how closely he was involved: he approved lists of those to be shot, tens of thousands of names carrying his signature or approval, he set the scope of the operations, and on 10 January 1939 he confirmed in a telegram that physical pressure was permitted in interrogation. The Terror reached into his own circle, in the arrests of the relatives of his closest colleagues, and it stopped when he chose to stop it in November 1938. Historians who have worked in the archives, such as Oleg Khlevniuk, put Stalin at the centre of the story, and it is hard to imagine an equivalent Terror under another leader. Without him, there would have been no Great Terror as it happened.

But personality alone does not explain why it took this form. The first alternative is political calculation. By 1934 the old oppositionists no longer had power, but they remained a symbol of what the party had once been, and Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin and Rykov, and the delegates of the Seventeenth Congress, could be a focus for opposition if Stalin's policies failed. The Terror destroyed them: of the Central Committee elected in 1934, Khrushchev said that 98 of 139 members and candidates were shot. A second is fear of war. The rise of Hitler and Japan's advance in Asia made Stalin look for spies and a fifth column, and the national operations against Poles, Germans, Latvians and Koreans reflected that anxiety. This explains the targets and timing better than a personal trait, though the Terror weakened the army and so made the USSR less safe, which shows that it was not a rational response to the danger.

The most serious challenge to the view is the system. The NKVD worked with quotas, and local officers competed to exceed them. Order 00447 set a target of 75,950 shootings and 193,000 imprisonments, but by November 1938 about 387,000 had been shot under it, five times as many as the first quota, because regional chiefs asked for more and Moscow approved. Local party bosses used the campaign to remove rivals or to prove their loyalty by denouncing others, and ordinary people denounced neighbours for personal reasons. J. Arch Getty and Sheila Fitzpatrick have argued that this made the Terror a social as well as a political event. But the system cannot be separated from Stalin, because it was he who set the quotas and the targets and who ended the operations, so the NKVD's behaviour was a result of his orders.

Overall, Stalin's personality was a necessary condition and the single most important cause of the Terror, but it was not the only one and the word 'mainly' claims too much. His suspicion gave the Terror its initiative and its scale. The political need to remove any alternative to him and the fear of war gave it its targets and its justification, and the NKVD and a society that denounced gave it its reach and its excess. The strongest conclusion is that Stalin was the author and the system the instrument: neither would have produced the Terror without the other."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** (what, if absent, would have prevented the Terror as it happened?), gives the case for the view with precise evidence (Ryutin, the decree of 1 December 1934, the lists, the telegram of 10 January 1939), and tests it against three alternative causes, using figures that prove points (the quotas and the 387,000). It reaches a judgement that explains how the causes fit together (Table A, Level 5: about 14). The evidence is precise and chosen to prove points (Table B, Level 5: about 13). Total about 27/30.

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the effectiveness of Stalin's foreign policy, 1929–41.
**What the levels want.** This is an "Assess" question about *effectiveness*, so Level 5 needs a clear criterion (what was the policy meant to achieve?) and a judgement that distinguishes success in one sense from failure in another.

**Plan.**
- Criteria: security (keeping the USSR safe), time (allowing preparation), allies (winning support), and the cost.
- Effective: the Pact (23 August 1939) kept the USSR out of war for 22 months and moved the frontier west (eastern Poland, the Baltic states, Bessarabia, Karelia); Khalkhin Gol (September 1939) and the Neutrality Pact (13 April 1941) secured the east; the USSR joined the League (1934) and made pacts with France and Czechoslovakia (1935).
- Ineffective: Comintern "social fascism" before 1933 split the German Left and helped Hitler; collective security failed (Munich, 1938; Spain lost, 1939); the Pact helped Germany to conquer Poland and France without a second front; the Winter War revealed weakness; Stalin ignored warnings and was surprised on 22 June 1941; the Terror had weakened the army.
- Judgement: effective as a short-term security policy and ineffective in its main aim of avoiding being attacked unprepared.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"A foreign policy can be judged by what it was meant to achieve. Stalin's aims were the security of the Soviet state, time to build its industry and army, and the avoidance of a war in which the USSR stood alone. On the first two the policy had real successes, but on the third, which mattered most, it failed in 1941, and the best judgement is that it was partly effective, in a way that depended on whether one looks at 1939 or 1941.

In the early 1930s the policy was mostly ineffective. The Comintern's 'Third Period' told the German Communists to treat the Social Democrats as 'social fascists', and they refused to cooperate against the Nazis, so that Hitler was able to take power in January 1933 against a divided Left. The cooperation with the German army that the Treaty of Rapallo had begun ended in 1933. From 1934, however, Stalin changed course, and under Litvinov the USSR joined the League of Nations in September 1934 and signed treaties with France and Czechoslovakia in May 1935, while the Comintern adopted the Popular Front. This was an intelligent response to the new danger, and the USSR was the only great power to give strong help to the Spanish Republic. But collective security was weak. Britain and France distrusted a state that was holding show trials and shooting its generals, Poland and Romania refused to let Soviet troops cross their territory, and the Czech crisis of September 1938 ended in Munich, to which the USSR was not invited. The policy failed because its partners would not commit, and in part because the Terror had undermined Soviet credibility, a cost that Stalin's own domestic policy had imposed on his diplomacy.

The Nazi–Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939 was a change of means, and it was effective in the short term. It kept the USSR out of the war for 22 months, gave it eastern Poland, the Baltic states and Bessarabia and pushed the frontier westwards by hundreds of kilometres, and in the east the victory at Khalkhin Gol, followed by the Neutrality Pact of April 1941, secured the other flank. Stalin had reason to think that the western powers wanted to turn Hitler against him, and he used the time to rebuild an army that had been damaged by the purges. The Pact must be judged against the alternative: a war with Germany in 1939 over Poland, with no ally in the West willing to fight beside him.

But the policy failed on the most important criterion. The Pact allowed Hitler to defeat Poland and France with no war in the east, and then to turn on a USSR that had lost the buffer of allies. It made Stalin trust Hitler too far: he disregarded warnings from British intelligence, from his own agents and from the German build-up, and the TASS statement of 14 June 1941 denied that there would be war. When the attack came on 22 June, more than a thousand Soviet aircraft were destroyed on the ground on the first day, and millions of soldiers were lost in the following weeks. The Winter War of 1939–40, which cost more than 120,000 Soviet dead, had already shown that the army was weaker than it looked, and the purge of the officer corps in 1937–38 was partly responsible. Gains of territory proved less valuable than the price of defences that had been partly dismantled on the old frontier.

Overall, Stalin's foreign policy was effective in gaining time, territory and a safe east, and in avoiding a war in 1939, but ineffective in what it was for: it failed to prevent a surprise attack by a power whose strength it had helped to build and whose intentions Stalin misjudged. Its defects were not accidents. They sprang from a government in which the leader's decisions could not be questioned, so that the strengths and the weaknesses of the policy had the same source."

*Commentary.* The essay defines what the policy was **for** (security, time, avoiding war alone), judges each phase against it (the Third Period, collective security, the Pact, the east, 1941) and reaches a judgement that explains why success and failure coexisted. Precise evidence in each paragraph (the dates, the 22 months, 120,000 dead, a thousand aircraft) gives Table B Level 5 (about 13). The criteria and sustained balance give Table A Level 5 (about 14). Total about 27/30.

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the aims and impact of the Stalin cult of personality. (plan and model paragraphs)
**What the levels want.** An "Assess" question with two parts, *aims* and *impact*. Level 5 treats both, with criteria (for impact: effect on belief, on control and on the working of government) and a judgement.

**Plan.**
- Aims: legitimacy (Lenin's disciple); unity of a divided country; justifying sacrifice; shifting blame; protection from rivals.
- Methods: the fiftieth birthday (1929); press, posters, film (*Lenin in October*, 1937), socialist realism, *Short Course* (1938), altered photographs, renamed places, mass spectacle.
- Impact: sincere belief among many (the young, the promoted), conformity and fear among others; an easier Terror; the cult hid Stalin's errors from him; dependence on one man, with the failure of 1941.
- Judgement: the cult was effective in its aims of legitimacy and control, but the deeper impact was to make a system that could not correct itself.

**A Level 4 paragraph on the aims.** "The cult had several aims. The first was to legitimise Stalin's power, since he lacked the glamour of Lenin or Trotsky. By describing him as Lenin's faithful pupil and then as the leader of the party, and by rewriting history in the *Short Course* of 1938 to make him Lenin's right hand, the party gave him the authority of the Revolution. A second aim was to unify a country that was going through collectivisation and the Five-Year Plans, by providing a figure who stood above all the regions and nationalities. A third was to protect Stalin from blame: when things went wrong, the fault lay with wreckers, and he was the one who found them."

*Commentary.* Precise, clear and explained, with evidence for each aim (Table B about 11), but a list of aims is not an assessment. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). To reach Level 5, rank the aims and test them against the impact.

**A Level 5 conclusion.** "Overall the cult achieved its main aims, since it gave Stalin a legitimacy that his record did not and it made opposition look like treason. Many Soviet citizens, particularly the young and the promoted, sincerely believed in it, and most of the rest conformed. But its deeper impact was to remove the checks on the leader. A system in which every success was Stalin's and every failure was someone else's depended on his judgement and could not correct his mistakes, and the effect was seen in 1941, when no one dared to challenge his belief that Hitler would not attack. The cult was effective as propaganda and damaging as government."

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the role of the NKVD in the Terror." [30]

A student wrote:

"The NKVD was the secret police and it was very important in the Terror. It arrested a lot of people in the middle of the night and took them to prison. They tortured them until they confessed. Yezhov was in charge of it and he killed many people. Later Yezhov was shot himself. The NKVD was therefore the most important part of the Terror."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are broadly accurate but general ("a lot of people", "many people"), and the paragraph **describes** what the NKVD did without analysing its role. It does not say how the NKVD worked (quotas, troikas, mass operations), who controlled it (Stalin, the Politburo and the lists), or how it compares with other causes. "Therefore the most important" is an assertion, and it does not notice that the NKVD was an instrument of Stalin's decisions as much as a cause.

**The fix.** Make a claim, use the detail as proof, and set a limit. "The NKVD was the instrument that carried out the Terror, and its methods gave the Terror its scale. Order 00447 of July 1937 gave each region a quota (75,950 to be shot and 193,000 to be sent to the camps), troikas of three officials sentenced people without a trial, and interrogators used sleep deprivation and, from 1937, torture, to obtain confessions. By November 1938 about 767,000 had been sentenced under the order and about 387,000 shot. But the NKVD acted on Stalin's orders: he approved the lists and set the quotas, he approved the use of torture in a telegram of 10 January 1939, and he ended the operations when he chose, replacing Yezhov with Beria. So the NKVD was the essential machine of the Terror, but it was an instrument of the leader's will, and the question of why it was used is more important than how." Now each fact is evidence, and there is a limit and a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was the Ryutin platform, and what did the Politburo's response show?
A: A document of 1932 by Martemyan Ryutin that attacked collectivisation and called for Stalin's removal. Ryutin was arrested in September 1932; Stalin is said to have demanded his execution, but the Politburo refused and he got ten years (he was shot in 1937). It showed that real opposition existed and that Stalin could not yet have his way on everything.
Q: Who killed Kirov, when, and how did Stalin use the murder?
A: Leonid Nikolaev shot him at the Smolny in Leningrad on 1 December 1934. Stalin signed a decree that day for fast trials, with no appeal and immediate execution; blamed a conspiracy of old oppositionists; and started the arrests of Zinoviev, Kamenev and others. Historians disagree on whether Stalin arranged the murder, and most who have used the archives think that Nikolaev acted alone.
Q: Name the three heads of the NKVD in the 1930s and what happened to each of the first two.
A: Yagoda (1934–36), shot after the 1938 trial; Yezhov (1936–38), arrested in April 1939 and shot in February 1940; Beria (from late 1938), who ended the mass operations.
Q: Who was tried in the three Moscow show trials, and what were the results?
A: August 1936, Zinoviev and Kamenev with 14 others (all 16 shot); January 1937, Pyatakov, Radek and others (13 shot); March 1938, Bukharin, Rykov, Yagoda and others (18 sentenced to death). The charges (terrorism, sabotage, spying, working for Trotsky) were false and the confessions were forced.
Q: What did Order 00447 provide, and how many people did it affect?
A: It was an NKVD order of 30 July 1937 for an operation against former kulaks, criminals and "anti-Soviet elements", with quotas of 75,950 to be shot and 193,000 to be sent to camps. By November 1938 about 767,000 people had been sentenced and about 387,000 shot.
Q: How many people were arrested and shot in 1937–38?
A: NKVD records give about 1.6 million arrests for political crimes and 681,692 shot (353,074 in 1937 and 328,618 in 1938). Some historians think that the real numbers are higher.
Q: What happened to the army leadership?
A: Marshal Tukhachevsky and seven generals were tried in secret and shot on 11–12 June 1937; of the 5 marshals, 3 were killed, and 13 of 15 army commanders; later research suggests that under one in ten of all officers were removed, but the top ranks were gutted.
Q: Give three methods of the Stalin cult.
A: Press and posters; film such as *Lenin in October* (1937); socialist realism in art and literature; the *Short Course* of 1938; altered photographs; renaming places; mass spectacle.
Q: What did the Constitution of 1936 promise, and why was it important that it did?
A: Adult suffrage, secret ballot, civil and social rights and an elected Supreme Soviet, with the party as the leading force. It was a propaganda statement that the USSR was a democracy, but in practice the rights were on paper and the Terror followed within months.
Q: What was collective security, and what ended it?
A: The policy led by Litvinov from 1933–34 of making alliances with France, Britain and others and working through the League against Germany. It ended with Munich (September 1938), when the USSR was excluded, and the Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939).
Q: Why did the USSR sign the Nazi–Soviet Pact?
A: Stalin thought that Britain and France would not fight for the USSR, the Anglo-French mission of August 1939 had no authority to sign and Poland would not let Soviet troops cross its territory; Germany offered a pact, a share of eastern Europe and time to rebuild the army.

- **Cult**: from the fiftieth birthday (21 December 1929); press, film, socialist realism, the *Short Course* (1938), renamed places; aims (legitimacy, unity, blame, protection) and impact (belief, conformity, a system that depended on one man).
- **Leadership**: personal rule through a small circle; Constitution of 5 December 1936 (rights on paper; one-party state).
- **Foreign policy**: Rapallo and Germany to 1933; League (18 September 1934); French and Czech pacts (May 1935); Popular Front; Spain (from October 1936); China (21 August 1937) and Japan (Khalkhin Gol, 1939; Neutrality Pact, 13 April 1941); Munich; Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939); Winter War; Barbarossa (22 June 1941).
- **Judgement**: Stalin's government was highly effective at keeping power and damaging to the country's strength, and the methods that secured one weakened the other.
