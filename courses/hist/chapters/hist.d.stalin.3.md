## Why Stalin made the Great Turn

### The starting point in 1927

By 1927 the Soviet economy had recovered to roughly its pre-war level under NEP, and Stalin had just defeated Trotsky, Zinoviev and Kamenev in the party (see [Stalin's Russia (1)](#hist.d.stalin.1)). NEP had two weaknesses in the eyes of the leaders. Industry was growing slowly because it had little money to invest, and the state depended on peasants to sell it grain at low prices. After the land seizures of 1917–18 there were millions of small farms, and the peasants ate more of their own grain and sold a smaller share of it than before the war: the marketed share was roughly half the pre-war level. Towns and the army needed bread, and grain was also the main export for paying for imported machines.

### The grain procurement crisis, 1927–28

In the winter of 1927–28 the state bought about two million tonnes of grain less than the year before. The causes were mostly economic. The state set low purchase prices, there were few manufactured goods in the villages that peasants wanted to buy, and many peasants held back grain to wait for better prices or to feed animals. A war scare in 1927 (see below) made the leaders more alarmed. Stalin's response was not to raise prices, as Bukharin and the Right wanted, but to use force. In January and February 1928 he travelled to the Urals and Siberia and ordered local officials to apply **Article 107** of the criminal code, which was meant for speculators, against peasants accused of hoarding grain. Armed squads searched farms and took grain, and some of what was seized was given to poor peasants who informed on their neighbours. This became known as the **Urals–Siberian method**. It worked: grain came in. It also destroyed the trust on which NEP depended, because peasants now saw that the state would take their grain whenever it wished.

:::def The Great Turn
The change in Soviet economic policy from 1928–29 under Stalin: the end of NEP and a "revolution from above" of forced collectivisation of agriculture and rapid, state-planned industrialisation through the Five Year Plans. Stalin gave it the name in his article "The Year of the Great Turn" (7 November 1929).
:::

:::def Urals–Siberian method
The emergency way of collecting grain used in January–February 1928: armed squads and the criminal law (Article 107) were used to seize grain from peasants accused of hoarding. It broke the market in grain and was the first step to forced collectivisation.
:::

### The four reasons the syllabus names

The syllabus asks you to know four reasons for the Great Turn. A strong answer does not just list them. It says which mattered most and how they linked.

:::key Four reasons for the Great Turn
- **Security from invasion.** The Bolsheviks believed the capitalist powers were hostile ("capitalist encirclement"). The war scare of 1927 convinced Stalin that war could come soon. Britain broke off diplomatic relations in May 1927 after a police raid on the Soviet trade body Arcos in London. In June 1927 the Soviet ambassador to Poland, Pyotr Voikov, was assassinated in Warsaw. In April 1927 the Guomindang's attack on the Chinese Communists in Shanghai destroyed a policy that Stalin had backed. Soviet industry was weak: it could not make enough steel, tanks or aircraft for modern war.
- **Self-sufficiency.** The USSR imported machines and technology and paid for them with grain. If grain exports fell or the West refused credit, the plan to modernise would stop. Heavy industry that made machines at home would end the dependence.
- **A fully socialist society and economy.** For Marxists the aim of the revolution was socialism, with industry owned by the state and farming done on a large scale. NEP, with its private traders (the "Nepmen"), prosperous peasants (the "kulaks") and market in grain, was in their eyes a retreat. A socialist economy needed a large working class and collective farms, not millions of small private farmers.
- **Enhancing Stalin's control.** The grain crisis threatened the towns, the army and Stalin's own position. Defeating the Right (Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky) on the question of the peasants strengthened him in the party, and a planned economy gave the centre control of resources. Collective farms put the peasants, the one group outside the party's reach, under the supervision of the state.
:::

Two more points help you rank the reasons. First, **the timing points to the grain crisis**. The Great Turn followed the crisis of 1927–28 and the Right's defeat in 1929, which suggests that the immediate trigger was a political and economic emergency, not a plan made years before. Second, the **ideas were not new**. Preobrazhensky and the Left Opposition had argued since 1924 that industry had to be financed by squeezing the peasants, and Stalin, who had attacked those ideas, took them over.

### What the reasons mean in an essay

Historians stress different reasons. Robert Tucker, in *Stalin in Power: The Revolution from Above, 1928–1941* (1990), saw the Great Turn as Stalin's "revolution from above", driven by his ideological ambition and wish to build a new state. Alec Nove, in *Was Stalin Really Necessary?* (1964) and *An Economic History of the USSR* (1969), stressed that the grain crisis produced the decisions step by step and that the policy which came out was more radical than anyone at first intended. R.W. Davies, whose series *The Industrialisation of Soviet Russia* began in 1980, showed in detail how decisions on targets were taken under pressure of events.

:::note Evidence: Stalin to the managers, 4 February 1931
"We are fifty or a hundred years behind the advanced countries. We must make good this distance in ten years. Either we do it, or we shall go under."

*From J. V. Stalin, "The Tasks of Business Executives", speech of 4 February 1931 to the First All-Union Conference of Leading Personnel of Socialist Industry, in* Works, *vol. 13 (Moscow: Foreign Languages Publishing House, 1954).*

The speech fits the security reason, and it is often said to have predicted the German invasion of June 1941, which came ten years and four months later. Be careful with that claim: Stalin was arguing for speed in 1931, when Hitler was not yet in power, and the "advanced countries" he named were the capitalist powers in general.
:::

:::key How the four reasons fit together
The grain crisis (the trigger) forced a choice between concessions to the peasants and force. Stalin chose force because the other three reasons pointed that way: fear of war made speed seem vital, the wish for self-sufficiency made imports a danger, the aim of socialism made the market in grain a political enemy, and his own interest was to defeat the Right and control the countryside. For the best judgement in an essay, argue that **security and the aim of control were the strongest reasons, because they made a political answer to the grain crisis more attractive than an economic one**, and that the economic problem was real but could have been met by other means.
:::

## Agricultural policies: collectivisation

### The aims

In 1929 the party's plan was cautious: the first Five Year Plan expected only about one fifth of peasant households to join collective farms by 1933. Stalin wanted collective farms for four reasons. The state could take grain at fixed prices without bargaining with millions of households. Large farms could use tractors and so raise output. Labour could be released from the land to work in factories. And the party would end the economic independence of the peasants, whom it saw as a source of capitalism.

:::def Collectivisation
The merging of peasants' small private farms into large collective farms (*kolkhozy*) under state direction, to make grain collection and mechanisation possible and to end private farming. In the Soviet Union it was carried out mainly between 1929 and 1933.
:::

:::def Kulak
Literally "fist": the name for a better-off peasant, who might hire labour or lend money. In the 1920s the number of real kulaks was small, and after 1929 the word was used for any peasant who resisted collectivisation, so it was a political label more than an economic class.
:::

### Voluntary and forced collectivisation

At first collectivisation was meant to be voluntary. Through the 1920s the party encouraged peasants to farm together, with little success: by October 1929 only a small minority of households (under one in ten) belonged to collective farms. Then the pace changed within weeks.

- **November 1929.** The Central Committee sent 25,000 trusted industrial workers (the "Twenty-Five Thousanders") to the countryside to organise collective farms. On 7 November Stalin's article "The Year of the Great Turn" claimed that peasants were joining in millions.
- **27 December 1929.** Stalin told a conference of Marxist agricultural experts that the policy of limiting the kulaks was over and that the party would move to "the liquidation of the kulaks as a class".
- **5 January 1930.** A Central Committee decree set a timetable: the main grain regions were to complete collectivisation by 1931 at the latest, in some regions by the autumn of 1930. Local officials, afraid of being accused of being soft, competed to report high numbers.
- **Methods.** Peasants were pressed to join by threats, by the arrest of those who refused and by raising taxes on individual farmers. Meetings were held at which the village was told to "vote" to join. Many peasants slaughtered their animals rather than hand them over to the collective, and the harvest of livestock in 1929–30 was a disaster.

The result was a surge in the number of households recorded as collectivised: about 21 per cent by January 1930 and about 58 per cent by early March 1930. Resistance grew with it. Women, who often led the protests, took part in riots, officials were attacked, and the OGPU (the political police) recorded well over ten thousand "mass disturbances" in 1930, by its own count. Soldiers in the Red Army, many from villages, were disturbed by letters from home.

:::note Evidence: "Dizzy with Success", 2 March 1930
"…by February 20 of this year 50 per cent of the peasant farms throughout the U.S.S.R. had been collectivised." … "They could have arisen only because some of our comrades have become dizzy with success…"

*Adapted from J. V. Stalin, "Dizzy with Success: Concerning Questions of the Collective-Farm Movement",* Pravda, *no. 60, 2 March 1930, in* Works, *vol. 12 (Moscow: Foreign Languages Publishing House, 1955). Shortened; the phrases are the article's own.*

Stalin was not retreating from collectivisation. He blamed local officials for "excesses" and said that no one should be forced to join, to restore calm before the spring sowing. The proportion of households in collective farms fell to about 23 per cent by June 1930. But the pressure returned in the autumn, and by 1932 about three fifths of households were in collective farms and by 1937 about nine tenths.
:::

:::def Kolkhoz and sovkhoz
A *kolkhoz* (collective farm) was a farm owned and run collectively by its members, who had to deliver a fixed quota of produce to the state and were paid from what was left. A *sovkhoz* (state farm) was owned by the state, and its workers were paid wages like industrial workers. State farms were few: collective farms covered the great majority of the land.
:::

### How the collective farms worked

A collective farm was supposed to be a cooperative of peasants. In practice the state controlled it. First, the farm had to deliver a fixed quota of grain at a low price, and only what remained could be shared among the members. The state fixed the quotas from above, often at a level which left the farm too little. Second, farmers were paid in "labour days" (*trudodni*), the units of work they had done, and received a share of the produce and some cash; in the bad years this was almost nothing. Third, the **Machine and Tractor Stations (MTS)** were state-owned depots that lent tractors and combines to the collective farms in return for part of the harvest. They allowed the state to mechanise farming and also to supervise every farm, because the MTS had political officers and reported on the farm's performance. Fourth, from December 1932 peasants did not receive internal passports, which kept them in their villages.

In February 1935 a new model statute for the collective farm, adopted at a congress of collective farm shock workers, accepted that peasants needed something of their own. Each household could keep a small plot, a cow and a few other animals, and sell surplus produce in the open market. The household plots covered a small share of the land but produced a very large share of vegetables, milk and meat. It was an admission that the system worked badly without incentive.

:::def Machine and Tractor Stations (MTS)
State depots, begun in 1928, that owned the tractors and other machines and hired them to the collective farms for a share of the crop. They allowed the state to mechanise agriculture and to control the collective farms.
:::

### Dekulakisation

Collectivisation was joined to an attack on the kulaks. **Dekulakisation** was the confiscation of the property of the peasants labelled kulaks, their arrest or deportation. A Politburo resolution of 30 January 1930 divided kulaks into three categories: those to be arrested or shot for "counter-revolutionary" activity, those to be deported to remote areas, and those to be moved within their district. Local officials had quotas to fill. The definition of "kulak" was so loose that it included any peasant who had a good house, a few more animals than his neighbours, or the courage to speak against the collective farm, and the label "*podkulachnik*" (kulak's helper) could be stuck on poor peasants too. Neighbours had reasons to denounce one another, and officials had reasons to fill quotas.

:::def Dekulakisation
The confiscation of property from, and the arrest and deportation of, peasants labelled kulaks in 1929–33, announced by Stalin on 27 December 1929 as "the liquidation of the kulaks as a class". It removed the most independent farmers and frightened the others into joining collective farms.
:::

According to the OGPU's own records, about 1.8 million people were deported as "special settlers" to Siberia, the Urals, Kazakhstan and the far north in 1930–31 (the figure used by the historian Viktor Zemskov). Many were families with children, sent in freezing wagons to unprepared places, and many died on the way or in the first winters. Others were dekulakised locally without being deported, and the total number of people affected was therefore larger. Lynne Viola, in *Peasant Rebels under Stalin* (1996), shows that the peasants did not accept all this passively: they resisted by riots, by slaughtering animals, by writing petitions and by spreading rumours that collectivisation was the work of the Antichrist.

### The famine of 1932–33

Collectivisation and requisitioning together produced a catastrophe. In 1930 the harvest was good, and the state took a large share of it, so it demanded high quotas again in the following years. But the harvests of 1931 and 1932 were poor: the loss of animals meant there were not enough draught animals to plough and sow, the collective farms were badly organised, peasants worked without enthusiasm, and the weather was bad in some regions. Davies and Wheatcroft estimate that the real harvest was well below the official figure (55 to 60 million tonnes in 1931–32, according to Wheatcroft, where the official harvest figure was about 69 million). The state, believing that peasants were hiding grain, would not lower its demands, and it kept exporting grain to pay for machinery.

Several measures made the famine worse in the winter of 1932–33:
- **7 August 1932.** A law on the protection of socialist property made theft of collective-farm grain punishable by shooting or ten years in a camp. Peasants called it the "law of spikelets" because it could be used against someone who gleaned a few ears of grain.
- **20 November 1932.** A decree began "blacklisting": villages and farms that failed to deliver quotas were cut off from manufactured goods and were subject to searches for hidden grain.
- **27 December 1932 and 22 January 1933.** The introduction of internal passports in the towns, and then a secret directive of Stalin and Molotov, stopped starving peasants from leaving Ukraine and the Kuban in search of food. In January 1933 the roads and railway stations were guarded.

The famine hit the main grain regions: Ukraine, the North Caucasus (especially the Kuban), the Volga and, in Kazakhstan, the nomadic Kazakhs, whose herds were seized and who were forced to settle. The scale is a matter of estimate because the Soviet authorities concealed the famine and the 1937 census, which would have shown the population losses, was suppressed (see [Stalin's Russia (4)](#hist.d.stalin.4)). Most historians put the **excess deaths in the USSR in 1930–33 between about 5 million and 8 million**; Davies and Wheatcroft, in *The Years of Hunger* (2004), reach a central estimate of about 5.7 million. Ukraine lost around 3.5 to 4 million people and Kazakhstan about 1.5 million, more than a third of all Kazakhs. Robert Conquest, in *The Harvest of Sorrow* (1986), gave a higher estimate of about 7 million deaths in the USSR as a whole.

Relief came in 1933: the state released some seed and grain, and a better harvest ended the famine, but the people who survived were weakened. The Soviet press said nothing about it, and western journalists who reported it were disbelieved or discredited.

:::key Is the famine a "genocide"? The debate (present it factually)
Historians agree that the famine killed millions, that the state's policies of requisitioning, export and the closing of the borders caused it, and that Stalin and the leadership could have reduced it. They disagree on **intention**.
- **Conquest** (*The Harvest of Sorrow*, 1986), and later **Timothy Snyder** (*Bloodlands*, 2010) and **Anne Applebaum** (*Red Famine*, 2017), argue that the regime used the famine to break Ukrainian peasants and Ukrainian national feeling, and that this makes it a deliberate attack on the Ukrainians. They stress the measures aimed at Ukraine in late 1932 and the closing of its borders in January 1933.
- **Davies and Wheatcroft** (*The Years of Hunger*, 2004) argue that the famine was the result of the regime's economic policies and its determination to get grain at any cost. They found no evidence that Stalin planned to kill Ukrainians as such: similar hunger struck Kazakhstan, the Volga and the North Caucasus, and the policies targeted the peasantry as a whole.
- **Recognition.** The Ukrainian parliament recognised the famine (the *Holodomor*, "death by hunger") as a genocide in 2006, and many other parliaments have done the same, including the European Parliament and the German Bundestag in 2022. The Russian government rejects the label.
For an essay on the economic policies, you do not need to settle the legal question. You do need to say that the policies caused the famine, that the regime put its grain targets above the lives of the peasants, and that it was a cost of the policy which a judgement of "success" must weigh.
:::

The statistics need care. Soviet figures for the grain harvest were "biological" estimates of what was standing in the fields, not of what was gathered, and they were too high. Livestock numbers are more reliable, and they show a fall of about 45 per cent in cattle and about 66 per cent in sheep and goats in five years. Grain output did not rise much in the 1930s. It recovered after 1933 and reached a record official figure in 1937, but a good harvest owed much to good weather.

### Success or failure?

The answer depends on the criteria.

:::key Collectivisation: for and against being a success
| Criterion | A case for success | A case for failure |
|---|---|---|
| Did the state get the grain it needed? | State procurement roughly doubled between 1928–29 and 1930–31, and it fed the towns and paid for exports | The state took grain from starving villages; the exports fell after 1931 anyway |
| Did output rise? | Mechanisation through the MTS; a record official harvest in 1937 | Grain output stagnated in the 1930s; livestock halved; productivity fell |
| Did it release labour for industry? | Tens of millions moved to the towns: the urban population rose from about 26 million to about 56 million between the 1926 and 1939 censuses | Many left because of famine and fear; skills were lost |
| Did it end the peasants' independence? | Yes: private farming was largely ended and the party controlled the countryside | Peasants kept the household plot, passive resistance and low effort |
| What was the human cost? | (cited by defenders as the price of survival) | Famine deaths of millions; 1.8 million deported; a lasting loss of trust |
:::

Nove and Fitzpatrick (*Stalin's Peasants*, 1994) agree that collectivisation succeeded as a means of control and extraction, and failed as a way to raise farm output. This is the answer to most essays on this key question: **it was a political and fiscal success and an economic and human failure**.

## Industrial policies

### Reasons and the planning machinery

The industrial plan had the same reasons as the Great Turn: security, self-sufficiency, socialism and Stalin's control. Industrialisation also meant a bigger working class, the social base of Soviet power. It was paid for by taking grain, by raising prices and forcing down consumption (which meant that factory workers also paid), by borrowing at first from abroad and by using prisoners.

:::def Gosplan
The State General Planning Commission (*Gosudarstvennaya planovaya komissiya*), founded in 1921 to draw up economic plans for the USSR. In the 1920s it drew up "control figures", and after 1928 it prepared the Five Year Plans and the yearly plans, which were turned into targets for ministries, regions and factories.
:::

:::def Five Year Plan
A plan, drawn up by Gosplan and approved by the party, setting output targets for every part of the economy for five years. The first began in October 1928 (it was approved in 1929). Targets were commands, not forecasts, and they were often raised during the plan.
:::

Gosplan was set up in 1921 and worked under Gleb Krzhizhanovsky until 1930. The first plan was approved in April–May 1929 in its "optimum" form with high targets, and in 1930–31 Stalin and the leadership raised the targets again. After 1930 industry was run through the Supreme Council of the National Economy (Vesenkha), led by Valerian Kuibyshev and then Sergo Ordzhonikidze, which in 1932 was divided into commissariats for different branches, with Ordzhonikidze as People's Commissar of Heavy Industry. In 1929 the "one-man management" of factories was introduced, which gave managers authority over trade union and party committees and made them responsible for fulfilling the plan.

### The first Five Year Plan, 1928–32

The plan stressed heavy industry (coal, steel, machines, electricity and oil), and put much less into consumer goods. Its targets were extremely ambitious: an increase of industrial output of about 180 per cent, which was then increased. To reach them the party launched campaigns of "storming" (*shturmovshchina*), slogans and socialist competition between factories. The planning was chaotic: shortages of materials, poor quality, hoarding by factory managers, and a very large number of new inexperienced workers who had come from the countryside.

In January 1933 Stalin told the Central Committee that the first plan had been fulfilled in four years and three months. This was a political announcement: the plan had officially started in October 1928, and December 1932 is 51 months later. Heavy industry had grown strongly but most of the major targets had been missed.

### Constructions, centres and major projects

:::key The major projects to know
- **Magnitogorsk** (Urals). A new steel city begun in 1929–30 beside the iron-ore "Magnetic Mountain", based on the American design of the US Steel works at Gary, Indiana, and built by an American firm. The first blast furnace was lit on 31 January 1932. Peasants, Komsomol volunteers, prisoners and "special settlers" built it, many living in tents, dugouts and barracks. Stephen Kotkin's *Magnetic Mountain* (1995) uses the city to argue that Stalinism was a whole way of life and a "civilisation", with its own language and rules for survival. By 1939 the city had about 145,000 people.
- **Dnieper hydroelectric station (Dneprostroi).** A huge dam in Ukraine, begun in 1927 and opened in 1932, designed with the help of an American consultant, Hugh Cooper, and with American turbines.
- **Belomor (White Sea–Baltic) Canal.** A canal of about 227 km, dug between December 1931 and August 1933 mainly by Gulag prisoners with picks, shovels and wheelbarrows. At least 12,000 prisoners died in the building, and some historians give a higher figure. It was praised in a book edited by Maxim Gorky in 1934 and it was too shallow for large ships.
- **Moscow Metro.** The first line (about 11 km) opened on 15 May 1935. Its stations, with marble, chandeliers and mosaics, were built as "palaces of the people" to show what socialism could do, using volunteers, Komsomol members, women and engineers, and some foreign advice.
- **Factories and railways.** The Stalingrad and Kharkov tractor plants, the Gorky car works, the Urals–Kuzbass coal-and-steel link, and the Turksib railway (completed in 1930) between Central Asia and Siberia.
:::

Foreign engineers and firms were hired for many of these projects, especially in the first plan, and the USSR imported large quantities of machinery from Germany and the United States during 1929–32, paid for by grain, timber and other exports. This was another reason why grain mattered so much, and why the Depression, which lowered world prices for those exports, made the task harder.

### The Gulag and the workforce

The state did not rely only on enthusiasm. Millions of peasants came to the towns, and the industrial workforce roughly doubled in the first plan. Unemployment was officially declared ended in October 1930 when the labour exchanges were closed. Prisoners built canals, railways and mines, and the Gulag (the camp system, under the OGPU and then NKVD) grew from the low hundreds of thousands in 1930 to well over a million by the late 1930s (about 1.6 million in camps and colonies at the start of 1939). The workers who came to the new cities were poorly trained, and the turnover of labour was very high.

### The second and third Five Year Plans

The **second plan (1933–37)** had lower targets than the first, because the strain of the first plan had been so great, and its slogan was "mastering technology". It stressed new industrial centres in the Urals and Siberia, transport and new heavy industry, and it promised more consumer goods, but heavy industry still came first and the consumer targets were largely missed. The Great Terror of 1936–38 disrupted it: engineers, managers and planners were arrested as "wreckers" and many were shot, so that the plan results were harder to reach. Ordzhonikidze, the commissar for heavy industry, died in February 1937, and his deputies and many managers were arrested after him.

The **third plan (from 1 January 1938)** put an increasing share of investment into defence industries and into factories in the east, beyond the reach of an invasion from the west. Output of steel, coal and electricity went on growing to 1940, but pig iron and steel barely rose after 1937. The plan was interrupted by the German invasion of 22 June 1941. It is therefore impossible to say whether it would have been fulfilled.

### The Stakhanovite movement

In the night of 30–31 August 1935 the miner Alexei Stakhanov of the Tsentralnaya-Irmino mine in the Donbas cut 102 tonnes of coal in a single shift of under six hours, against a norm of 7 tonnes. That is more than 14 times the norm. (On 19 September he was reported to have cut 227 tonnes.) Pravda called it a world record, and a movement was launched to copy it. The accounts show that the record was prepared with care: other miners cleared the coal, props were set and he had the best equipment.

:::def Stakhanovite movement
A campaign begun in 1935, named after the miner Alexei Stakhanov, to raise output through "record-breaking" individual workers, who were rewarded with high pay, flats, holidays and fame. It aimed to raise productivity and to raise the work norms for everyone else.
:::

The movement had four purposes: to raise productivity, to show that workers could do more with better methods, to raise the norms, and to put the blame for low output on managers and engineers who were said to be "wreckers". Stalin gave it his support in a speech of 17 November 1935 to the first conference of Stakhanovites.

:::note Evidence: Stalin to the Stakhanovites, November 1935
"Life has improved, comrades. Life has become more joyous."

*From J. V. Stalin, speech at the First All-Union Conference of Stakhanovites, 17 November 1935, in* Works, *vol. 14 (London: Red Star Press, 1978).*

The sentence was meant to tell workers that sacrifices were paying off and that wages and shops were improving. It was spoken just after bread rationing was ended (1935), but at a time when real wages were still low, so it is better evidence of what the regime wanted people to believe than of what they lived.
:::

Lewis Siegelbaum, in *Stakhanovism and the Politics of Productivity in the USSR, 1935–1941* (1988), shows that the movement raised output in some factories but also disrupted production, because the records were achieved by special preparation, and caused resentment. Many ordinary workers disliked the Stakhanovites for the raising of norms, and some Stakhanovites were attacked. Managers disliked it because it disorganised the plan and made them targets of blame.

### Assessing the industrial results

:::key The Five Year Plans: for and against being a success
- **For.** Heavy industry grew very fast: steel and pig iron output more than quadrupled between 1928 and 1937, electricity almost tenfold by 1940. New industrial regions were opened in the Urals and Siberia. The USSR in 1941 had the industrial base that produced the tanks, aircraft and guns it needed in the war. The USSR escaped the Depression of the 1930s, with its unemployment. Allen (*From Farm to Factory*, 2003) argued that, judged by investment and growth, the Soviet model worked well for a poor country.
- **Against.** Most of the targets of the first plan were missed. Quality was poor and waste was high. Consumer goods and light industry were starved of resources. The statistics are exaggerated (Soviet figures counted unfinished goods and double-counted) and growth in the first plan was lower than claimed. The human costs were huge: forced labour, famine, a fall in living standards, and an arrested or purged management.
- **The judgement.** The plans were a success as a means of building heavy and defence industries quickly, and a failure when judged by their own targets, efficiency or the welfare of the people.
:::

## Living standards

### Workers in the towns

The towns grew at great speed. The census of 1926 recorded about 26.3 million people in towns (about 18 per cent of the population) and the census of 1939 about 56.1 million (about 33 per cent), an increase of about 30 million. The new arrivals found overcrowded housing, shortages of food and of consumer goods, and long hours.

- **Housing.** Most families lived in a single room in a **communal apartment** (*kommunalka*), where several families shared a kitchen and bathroom, or in a barrack or a hut. In new cities such as Magnitogorsk many people lived in tents, dugouts and barracks for years. The state built far less housing than the growth in population needed.
- **Food and shops.** Rationing of bread began in 1928–29 and extended to most goods in the early 1930s, and queues were normal. Ration cards were tied to the workplace, which gave workers in factories better supplies than others. Rationing ended in 1935. In the early 1930s special "Torgsin" shops sold food and goods for gold, silver and foreign currency, which people sold to buy bread. From the mid-1930s the shops improved and the regime advertised new products, but shortages of clothes, shoes and furniture continued.
- **Real wages.** Money wages rose rapidly, but prices rose faster in the early 1930s, and most estimates (Soviet and Western alike) put real wages at the end of the 1930s below their level in 1928. Welfare was paid through the workplace: nurseries, canteens, rest homes and holidays. These were real gains for some families, especially for those with skills.

### Wage differentials and incentives

In the 1920s the Bolsheviks had tried to keep wage differences small. In a speech of 23 June 1931 Stalin attacked "equalisation" (*uravnilovka*) as a "petty-bourgeois" idea that discouraged skill and effort. After this the state used piece-rates (pay by the amount produced), big bonuses and wide differences between skilled and unskilled workers. Stakhanovites could earn several times the average wage, with flats and holidays. Managers and engineers had still higher pay and privileges: better flats, cars, dachas (country houses) and access to closed shops. The new Soviet elite was a visible sign that the regime now accepted inequality as a means of motivation.

:::def Wage differentials
Differences in pay between categories of worker. Under Stalin, from 1931, the state widened them deliberately, with piece-rates, bonuses and high pay for skilled workers, Stakhanovites and managers, in order to raise productivity.
:::

### Managers

Factory managers (the "red directors") had great responsibility and great risk. They had to meet targets that were often impossible, while materials and workers did not arrive. To succeed they bent the rules: they hoarded materials, bargained with suppliers, and falsified reports. They were rewarded with high pay and privileges, but they could be accused of "wrecking" when the plan failed. The Shakhty trial of 1928 and the Industrial Party trial of 1930 were early signs, and in the Great Terror of 1937–38 managers and engineers were arrested in large numbers.

### Workplace discipline

Discipline became harsher as turnover and absence disrupted production.
- **15 November 1932.** A law made one day's unexcused absence a reason for dismissal, with the loss of ration card and housing.
- **Internal passports (27 December 1932) and labour books.** The passport system limited who could live in the big towns. A **labour book**, which recorded a worker's jobs and any penalties, was introduced for some workers in 1932 and for all workers by a decree of 20 December 1938.
- **1938–40.** Decrees of 1938 and above all of **26 June 1940** forbade workers to leave a job without the permission of the manager and made absence and lateness of more than 20 minutes a criminal offence, punished by months of corrective labour on reduced pay.
- **Trade unions.** They no longer defended workers against management. Their task was to raise output and to run social insurance.

:::def Labour book
A document, introduced in 1932 and made compulsory for all workers by a decree of 20 December 1938, recording a worker's job history and disciplinary record. Managers used it to control who could change jobs.
:::

### How the people lived: what to say in an essay

:::key Living standards: improvement or decline?
| Criterion | Evidence of improvement | Evidence of decline |
|---|---|---|
| Jobs | Unemployment ended (the regime's claim); millions found jobs and trained | Jobs were in poor conditions, with high turnover and forced mobility |
| Pay | Rapid rise in money wages; high pay for the skilled and Stakhanovites | Real wages for most were lower in 1937 than in 1928 |
| Housing | New cities and flats for a privileged few | Overcrowded communal apartments, barracks and dugouts |
| Goods | Shops improved in the mid-1930s; rationing ended in 1935 | Shortages and queues; poor quality; goods for the elite |
| Freedom | Welfare, nurseries and rest homes from the workplace | Labour books, passports, criminal penalties for lateness |
:::

The regime's claim in 1935 that life had become "more joyous" was true for some: skilled workers, Stakhanovites, officials and the educated. For most workers and for the peasants, living standards in the 1930s were lower than in the 1920s, and they recovered only after the war.

## How successful were the policies overall?

:::key Judging Stalin's economic policies: what to say in an essay
| Criterion | Judgement |
|---|---|
| Did the policies meet Stalin's aims of security and self-sufficiency? | Largely yes: a heavy industrial and defence base by 1941, less dependence on imports. |
| Did they create a socialist economy? | Yes in form: private trade and farming were ended and the state controlled the economy. |
| Did they meet their own targets? | Mostly no: the first plan fell short of most targets and the farm targets failed. |
| Did they raise living standards? | No for most people in the 1930s. |
| What did they cost? | Famine, deportation, forced labour: millions of lives. |
| Did they enhance Stalin's control? | Yes: the party and the state controlled the economy and the countryside. |
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **Alec Nove**, *An Economic History of the USSR* (1969), gave a balanced account of results and costs and asked in *Was Stalin Really Necessary?* (1964) whether the same results could have been reached by gentler means. Nove thought that some form of rapid industrialisation was needed, but not the particular policies, and not the cost.
- **R.W. Davies**, in *The Industrialisation of Soviet Russia* (1980 onwards), and **Davies and Wheatcroft**, *The Years of Hunger* (2004), have given the fullest studies of how the decisions were taken. They stress improvisation and the pressure of crisis, not a plan from the start.
- **Robert Allen**, *From Farm to Factory* (2003), argued that, as a model of growth for a poor country, the Soviet system was successful, because forced saving allowed very high investment.
- **Lynne Viola**, *Peasant Rebels under Stalin* (1996), and **Sheila Fitzpatrick**, *Stalin's Peasants* (1994), study the peasants' resistance and adaptation, which showed the limits of the state's power in the villages.
- **Stephen Kotkin**, *Magnetic Mountain* (1995), studied Magnitogorsk and argued that Stalinism was a way of life that people learned to speak and to survive in, not only a set of policies.
- **Robert Conquest**, *The Harvest of Sorrow* (1986), set out the famine and the case that it was aimed at Ukrainians.
Use these to show that you know the debate, but your marks come from your own argument and evidence, not from a list of names.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. A [30] essay gets one mark out of 15 for analysis and judgement (Table A) and one out of 15 for knowledge (Table B). Read the commentary as an examiner would.

:::eg 'Collectivisation was a complete failure.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 2 (4–6): some understanding, descriptive, criteria implicit. Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, but the argument lacks balance; weak judgement. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained balanced argument, a clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): a good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Evaluate" asks for a judgement on the quality or extent of failure. "Complete" is the word to test. Criteria: (1) did it give the state the grain and control it wanted; (2) did it raise output; (3) did it help industrialisation; (4) what did it cost. For "failure": livestock halved, famine, grain output stagnant, peasants resisted. Against "complete": the state got its grain, labour went to the towns, the peasants were controlled, mechanisation began. Judgement: not a complete failure, because it achieved the regime's political aims, but a failure as agriculture and as a human policy.

**A Level 2/3 paragraph (about Table A Level 2, 5 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "Collectivisation was a failure. Stalin forced the peasants to join collective farms in 1929. Many peasants killed their animals instead. There was a famine in 1932–33 in which millions of people died. The kulaks were sent to Siberia. So collectivisation was a complete failure because it did not work and so many people died."

*Commentary.* Accurate and clear, with some correct facts. But it is a **list of bad outcomes** with one verdict. "Complete" is never tested, no criteria are named, and nothing is said about what collectivisation achieved. Table A Level 2; Table B Level 3 for the correct but thin detail.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and give the other side.** "If success means higher farm output, collectivisation failed. Cattle numbers fell from about 70 million in 1928 to about 38 million in 1933, sheep and goats by about two thirds, and the harvests of 1931–32 were well below the official figures. But if success means what Stalin wanted from it, the picture is different. Procurement of grain roughly doubled between 1928–29 and 1930–31, and the grain fed the towns and paid for the machinery of the first Five Year Plan. The state also broke the independence of the peasants, so that the market in grain that had caused the crisis of 1927–28 never returned. Collectivisation therefore failed as agriculture and succeeded as a means of extraction."

*Commentary.* Now the knowledge is selected to prove a point, with two criteria and a mini-judgement. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. But it does not yet weigh the human cost, test "complete", or explain why the state went on with a policy that was not raising output.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view that collectivisation was a complete failure is true if it is judged as a way of farming, partly true if it is judged by what it cost, and false if it is judged by what Stalin wanted from it. The word 'complete' decides the question, because the policy failed on some criteria and succeeded on others. The best judgement is that it was a failure for agriculture and for the peasants, but a success as an instrument of political and fiscal control, which was its main purpose.

The case for failure is strongest on output and on human cost. Livestock, which the peasants slaughtered rather than hand over, fell from about 70 million cattle in 1928 to about 38 million in 1933, and sheep and goats from about 147 million to about 50 million, so the draught animals and the meat and milk of the country were lost for years. Grain output did not rise: the official harvests were exaggerated, and Wheatcroft puts the real harvest of 1931–32 at 55 to 60 million tonnes, below the 1928 level of about 73 million. The cost in lives was greater still. Dekulakisation deported about 1.8 million people in 1930–31, and the famine of 1932–33, in which the state kept demanding and exporting grain, killed between five and eight million, with Davies and Wheatcroft giving a central estimate of 5.7 million. The system that came out of these years, with passports that tied peasants to the villages and labour paid in trudodni, gave peasants little reason to work hard, and in 1935 the regime had to allow household plots, which produced a very large share of milk, meat and vegetables from a small share of the land. These plots are a sign that the collective system did not work as designed.

Yet to call it a complete failure is to ignore why it was introduced. The crisis of 1927–28 showed that the state could not rely on peasants to sell grain at low prices, and Stalin's aim was to take control of grain, and of the countryside. On that test, collectivisation succeeded. State procurement roughly doubled between 1928–29 and 1930–31. The grain fed the towns and was exported to pay for machines, so that the first Five Year Plan could be financed from the countryside. Labour moved to industry: the urban population rose from about 26 million in 1926 to about 56 million in 1939. The party now controlled every village through the collective farm and the MTS, and the independent peasantry, which Bolsheviks had feared since 1921, was ended. Without collectivisation it is hard to see how the state could have taken what it took.

How far this justifies the policy depends on a further criterion: could the aims have been met more cheaply? Nove asked this question in *Was Stalin Really Necessary?* and argued that the costs were not inevitable. The excesses were not an accident, but were built into the timetable of 5 January 1930 and the quotas for dekulakisation, and the famine came from the decision to protect the state's grain targets and exports at the expense of the villages. Viola and Fitzpatrick show that the peasants did not just suffer: they slaughtered their animals, rioted and petitioned, and that is why Stalin had to retreat in March 1930 and again to allow the plots. A policy which required so much force, and then so many concessions, was less of a success than its results suggest.

In conclusion, collectivisation was not a complete failure, because it achieved the regime's main political and fiscal aims quickly and permanently. But it was a failure by the tests of farm output, of the welfare of the peasants and of its cost in lives, and the regime's aims might have been met by a less destructive policy. It is better described as a costly success for the state and a disaster for the countryside."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): the answer examines the key word ("complete"), uses wide-ranging criteria (output, cost, the regime's aims, whether it could have been done differently), argues both sides in detail with links between them, and ends with a supported judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates (5 January 1930), figures with ranges (cattle, sheep and goats, 1.8 million, 5.7 million, and the discrepancy between official and real harvest), named historians used for argument (Nove, Wheatcroft, Davies, Viola, Fitzpatrick). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 800–900 words in 40 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Assess the success of the first three Five Year Plans [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess" asks you to weigh how successful something was, so you need criteria for success, evidence of results, and a judgement that compares the plans and does not merely describe them.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) did they meet their targets; (2) did they build what the USSR needed (heavy industry, defence, new regions); (3) at what cost; (4) were the results lasting. First plan: fast growth but most targets missed (pig iron 6.2 against about 10, steel 5.9 against about 10.4); second plan: lower targets, better organised, disrupted by the Terror; third plan: defence, cut short by the invasion. Judgement: a success in building the base for war, not in efficiency or welfare, and most successful at the second plan.

**A Level 2/3 paragraph (about Table A Level 2, 5 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "The first Five Year Plan began in 1928. It aimed to build up heavy industry such as coal, iron and steel. New towns like Magnitogorsk were built and the Dnieper dam was built. Stalin said the plan was completed in four years. The second Five Year Plan was from 1933 to 1937 and the third was from 1938. They were successful because Russia became a great industrial power."

*Commentary.* Accurate, with dates and examples. But it **describes** the plans, accepts Stalin's claim without testing it, and the last sentence is an assertion. No criteria, no comparison, no costs. Table A Level 2; Table B Level 3.

**Moving to Level 4: test the claim against the figures.** "Stalin declared the first plan fulfilled in four years and three months, but the figures show a more mixed result. Pig iron rose from 3.3 million tonnes in 1928 to 6.2 million in 1932, nearly double, yet the plan as approved in 1929 had asked for about 10 million, so the plan reached about 62 per cent of its target. Coal reached 64.4 million tonnes against a target of 75. The plan was a success in the sense that heavy industry grew very fast, and a failure judged by its own targets. By 1937 the second plan had taken pig iron to 14.5 million tonnes and steel to 17.7 million, over four times the 1928 level of steel."

*Commentary.* Now the knowledge proves a point and there are two criteria (the targets, and the growth). This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 the answer needs the costs, the third plan, and a clear judgement comparing the plans.

**A Level 5 conclusion (and the paragraph that sets it up).**

"The three plans differ in what they achieved and what they cost. The second plan was in some ways the most successful. It set lower targets, concentrated on completing the plants begun in the first plan, and between 1932 and 1937 steel rose from 5.9 to 17.7 million tonnes and electricity from 13.5 to 36.2 billion kWh. Yet its success was limited: the Great Terror from 1936 removed managers, engineers and planners, and the promised increase in consumer goods was not delivered. The third plan, from January 1938, put more money into defence and into the east, and coal and electricity still rose to 1940, but steel and pig iron barely grew after 1937, and the invasion of June 1941 cut it short.

Overall, the plans were successful as a way of building a heavy industrial and military base in a short time, and the USSR of 1941 had far more steel, coal and electricity than that of 1928. They were much less successful when judged by their own targets, by the quality of what was made, or by what they cost. The costs were paid by the peasants, whose grain financed the plans, by the prisoners who dug the Belomor Canal, and by the workers whose living standards fell. The judgement depends on what is meant by success. If it means power, the plans succeeded. If it means efficiency or welfare, they failed. Since the question asks about the regime's own objectives of security and self-sufficiency, the plans must be assessed as having succeeded in the main, but at a cost that the regime itself would not admit."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies **criteria** (targets, building a base, cost), compares the plans, connects results with costs, and reaches a supported judgement that links the answer to the question. With the earlier paragraphs on the first plan, this is Level 5: Table A 14, Table B 13, about 27 out of 30.
:::

:::eg Assess the effects of Stalin's economic policies on the living standards of Soviet workers in the 1930s [30] (plan and Level 4 paragraph)
**Plan.** Criteria: (1) pay and prices; (2) housing and goods; (3) work conditions; (4) the position of different groups. Improvement: unemployment ended, new jobs and training, high pay for skilled workers and Stakhanovites, welfare through the workplace, rationing ended in 1935. Decline: real wages below the 1928 level, overcrowded communal apartments, shortages, harsher discipline. Judgement: the effect was very uneven, and for most workers living standards fell, while a skilled minority gained.

**A Level 4 paragraph.** "The effect of Stalin's economic policies on workers depended on who the worker was. Skilled workers, Stakhanovites and managers gained from the end of equalisation announced on 23 June 1931, which introduced piece-rates and wide differences in pay, and a Stakhanovite could earn several times the average wage and receive a flat. But most workers did not. Money wages rose fast, but prices rose faster in the early 1930s, and most estimates place real wages at the end of the decade below those of 1928. Housing was worst: the urban population grew from about 26 million to about 56 million between the censuses of 1926 and 1939, and most families lived in one room of a communal apartment or in a barrack. Workers also lost freedom, because of the absenteeism law of 15 November 1932, the labour books of 1938 and the decree of 26 June 1940. The regime's claim in 1935 that life had become more joyous was true for a minority."

*Commentary.* A good Level 4 paragraph: it uses a criterion (who gained and who lost), precise knowledge and a mini-judgement. To reach Level 5, the essay would compare the 1930s with the 1920s, say how reliable the wage statistics are, discuss the peasants as well as the town workers, and end with an explicit overall judgement on what "living standards" means (income, goods, housing or security).
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the extent to which the first Five Year Plan achieved its aims." [30]

A student wrote:

"The first Five Year Plan was a great success. It began in 1928 and Stalin said it was finished in four years. Factories were built all over the country and the Soviet Union produced much more coal, steel and electricity. New cities like Magnitogorsk were built. The USSR was now a modern industrial country. The plan was therefore a great success and achieved all its aims."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): relevant and clear, but it **repeats Stalin's claim** instead of testing it, it gives one side only, and the final sentence is an assertion ("all its aims"). No aims are named, so nothing can be measured. Table B Level 3 (7–9): correct facts, but no figures and no precision (how much more coal, steel and electricity?).

**The fix.** Name the aims and test each against evidence. "The plan had three main aims, to raise heavy industry quickly, to make the USSR less dependent on imports, and to provide a base for defence. On the first, output rose strongly: coal from 35.4 to 64.4 million tonnes and electricity from 5.0 to 13.5 billion kWh between 1928 and 1932. But the plan as approved in 1929 had set targets of about 10 million tonnes of pig iron and 22 billion kWh, and the real results were 62 and 61 per cent of these. 'Fulfilled in four years and three months' was a political claim, made in January 1933 when the targets had been missed. The plan did build the base for the later plans, but 'all its aims' is wrong." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was the grain procurement crisis, and how did Stalin respond to it?
A: In the winter of 1927–28 the state collected about two million tonnes less grain than the year before, because of low prices, a shortage of manufactured goods and peasants holding back grain. Stalin went to the Urals and Siberia in January–February 1928 and ordered local officials to use Article 107 of the criminal code against "hoarders" and armed squads to seize grain: the Urals–Siberian method.
Q: Name the four reasons for the Great Turn that the syllabus gives, with one piece of evidence for each.
A: Security from invasion (the war scare of 1927: the Arcos raid in May and the murder of Voikov in June); self-sufficiency (imports of machines paid for by grain exports); a fully socialist society and economy (ending NEP, the Nepmen and the kulaks); enhancing Stalin's control (defeat of the Right, control of the countryside and of resources).
Q: What happened on 27 December 1929 and 2 March 1930?
A: On 27 December 1929 Stalin announced the "liquidation of the kulaks as a class". On 2 March 1930 his article "Dizzy with Success" blamed local officials for excesses and called a halt to forced collectivisation. The share of households in collective farms fell from about 58 per cent in March to about 23 per cent by June 1930, and then rose again.
Q: What is the difference between a kolkhoz and a sovkhoz?
A: A kolkhoz was a collective farm whose members shared what remained after delivering a quota to the state; a sovkhoz was a state farm whose workers were paid wages. Most land was in kolkhozy.
Q: What were the Machine and Tractor Stations?
A: State depots that owned tractors and combines and hired them to the collective farms for part of the harvest. They mechanised farming and also let the state supervise the farms.
Q: How many people were deported in the dekulakisation of 1930–31, and who decided who was a kulak?
A: About 1.8 million (OGPU records, as used by Zemskov). The label was applied by local officials and village committees under quotas from above, so it was loose and political: a "kulak" could be anyone who resisted.
Q: Give three measures that made the famine of 1932–33 worse.
A: Any three of: continued high procurement quotas and exports (1.8 million tonnes in 1932 and in 1933); the law of 7 August 1932; the blacklisting of villages (decree of 20 November 1932); internal passports (27 December 1932); the directive of 22 January 1933 closing the borders of Ukraine and the Kuban to starving peasants.
Q: How do historians disagree about the famine?
A: Conquest, Snyder and Applebaum argue that the regime deliberately targeted Ukraine, so that the famine was a genocide (the *Holodomor*). Davies and Wheatcroft argue that the famine came from policy aimed at getting grain from the whole peasantry, and found no plan to kill Ukrainians as such. All agree that policy caused it, and that millions died.
Q: What were Gosplan and the Five Year Plans?
A: Gosplan, set up in 1921, was the state planning commission that drew up the plans. A Five Year Plan set output targets for the whole economy for five years; the first ran from October 1928 and was declared fulfilled in four years and three months.
Q: Name three major projects of the first two plans and say what each showed.
A: Magnitogorsk (a steel city built from 1929–30, first blast furnace on 31 January 1932; it showed the scale and hardships of the effort); the Dnieper dam (opened 1932, with American help: electric power and foreign technology); the Belomor Canal (1931–33, built by prisoners, at least 12,000 deaths: the use of forced labour); the Moscow Metro (opened 15 May 1935: propaganda as well as transport).
Q: Who was Stakhanov, and why did the movement matter?
A: A miner who cut 102 tonnes of coal in one shift on 30–31 August 1935, over 14 times the norm. The movement raised output in some places and rewarded record-breakers with high pay and privileges, but it disrupted production, raised norms and caused resentment.
Q: What laws and documents increased workplace discipline in the 1930s?
A: The law of 15 November 1932 (dismissal for a day's unexcused absence), internal passports (27 December 1932), labour books (made compulsory by 20 December 1938) and the decree of 26 June 1940 (no leaving a job without permission; lateness of more than 20 minutes a crime).

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the grain procurement crisis of 1927–28 was the main reason for Stalin's Great Turn.
A: Indicative content. For: the crisis of winter 1927–28 (about two million tonnes short) directly led to the Urals–Siberian method and then to collectivisation; the timing of the Great Turn follows it; grain was needed for the towns, the army and exports; the Right's defeat came on the question of how to deal with the peasants. Against or other reasons: security (the 1927 war scare); self-sufficiency in machinery; the ideological aim of socialism and the end of NEP; Stalin's control. The crisis was partly made by state policy (low prices, few goods). Level 5 judges whether the crisis was a trigger or a cause, and argues, for example, that it was the trigger and that security and control explain why Stalin chose force.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which Stalin's collectivisation policy achieved his aims.
A: Indicative content. Aims: secure grain for towns and export; release labour; mechanise; control the peasants; end the kulaks. Achievement: procurement roughly doubled; the urban population rose by about 30 million; the MTS and collective farms gave control; private farming was ended; the kulaks were destroyed. Limits: livestock halved; grain output did not rise; famine in 1932–33; the 1935 household plots; the peasants' resistance and the retreat of March 1930. Level 5 uses the aims as criteria, shows what was achieved and at what cost, and judges that the political aims were met more fully than the economic ones.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The famine of 1932–33 was caused by the policy of collectivisation.' Evaluate this view.
H: List the causes: policy, weather, the way grain was collected, exports.
H: Decide whether "caused by collectivisation" is the same as "caused by Stalin's decisions on grain".
A: Indicative content. For: the loss of livestock and the disorganisation of the collective farms reduced the harvests; the peasants worked without incentive; the state set quotas it knew the farms could not meet; Kazakhstan's famine followed forced settlement. Other factors: weather and poor harvests in 1931 and 1932 (stressed by Wheatcroft and Tauger), the choice to keep exporting grain (1.8 million tonnes in 1932 and 1933), the law of 7 August 1932, blacklists, and closed borders, which were decisions made in the winter of 1932–33 and not part of collectivisation as such. Level 5 separates the underlying cause (collectivisation and procurement) from the decisions that turned a poor harvest into a famine, and avoids deciding the question of genocide.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the success of the Five Year Plans in making the Soviet Union safer from invasion by 1941.
H: Name the criteria first: output of the goods needed for war, independence from imports, location of industry.
H: Use figures, and say how reliable they are.
A: Indicative content. Success: heavy industry grew very fast (steel from about 4 to 17.7 million tonnes, 1928 to 1937; coal from 35.4 to 166 million tonnes by 1940; electricity almost tenfold); new industrial regions in the Urals and Siberia, away from the western border; a machine-building and defence base; less need to import machines. Limits: first-plan targets missed (pig iron about 62 per cent of the 1929 target); poor quality; the Terror of 1937–38 removed managers, engineers and, in the armed forces, many senior officers (see [Stalin's Russia (2)](#hist.d.stalin.2)); steel and pig iron barely grew after 1937; the third plan was cut short in June 1941. Level 5 sets criteria for "safer" (output, independence, location, leadership) and judges whether the gains outweighed the damage.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The living standards of Soviet workers improved in the 1930s.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which workers? Skilled, unskilled, Stakhanovites, managers, new arrivals.
H: Use at least four criteria: pay, housing, goods, freedom.
A: Indicative content. For: unemployment ended; new training; welfare through the workplace; high pay for skilled workers and Stakhanovites; rationing ended in 1935; better shops in the mid-1930s. Against: real wages lower in 1937 than 1928; overcrowded communal apartments and barracks; shortages and queues; the absenteeism law, labour books, the decree of 26 June 1940; peasants and prisoners were worse off. Level 5 distinguishes groups and criteria and judges that the claim is true only for a minority.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the cost of Stalin's economic policies outweighed their achievements.
H: Choose a way to weigh "cost" and "achievement" (lives, output, the war).
H: Do not say "yes" or "no" without deciding what the question is comparing.
A: Indicative content. Costs: the famine (5 to 8 million deaths), the deportation of about 1.8 million, forced labour, a fall in living standards, the loss of livestock, the damage of the Terror. Achievements: heavy and defence industry, new industrial regions, urbanisation and training, the base for the war effort in 1941–45 (a brief closing note only). Level 5 sets out criteria for weighing (human, economic, strategic), and judges. Either view can be argued if it is supported.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the importance of forced labour and coercion, compared with enthusiasm and incentives, in the industrial achievements of the 1930s.
H: Give an example of each: Belomor and Magnitogorsk; Stakhanov and wage differentials.
H: Which can be measured, and which cannot?
A: Indicative content. Coercion: the Gulag, special settlers, the Belomor Canal, the laws of 1932 and 1940, labour books and passports, the fear of the "wrecker" label. Incentives: wage differentials after 1931, bonuses, Stakhanovite rewards, housing for the skilled, propaganda and a genuine belief among some young workers (Kotkin). Level 5 weighs the two, perhaps judging that coercion made the system work, and incentives made it more efficient, and says that the evidence cannot separate them exactly.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Stalin's economic policies from 1928 to 1941 were a success for the state but a failure for the people.' Evaluate this view.
H: List who is "the state" and who is "the people": party, army, industry, workers, peasants, prisoners.
H: Find evidence that the people also gained (education, jobs, social mobility, welfare) and that the state also lost (the damage of the Terror, a weak farm sector).
A: Support: the state obtained heavy industry, defence, control of grain and of the countryside; the people paid with a famine, deportations, forced labour, lower real wages and overcrowded housing. Challenge: social mobility and training for workers and young people (the Stakhanovites and the new managers), nurseries and welfare in the workplace, and the high priority given to education; the state also suffered from inefficiency, a weak agriculture and the destruction of managers in the Terror. A* judgement separates groups (peasants, prisoners, skilled workers, officials), does not treat "the people" as one, and decides which test is more important.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Davies and Wheatcroft, Conquest, and Allen offer different judgements of the economic policies. How far does the evidence of the first Five Year Plan and of collectivisation support each of them?
H: Match each historian with a question: Why did the famine happen? Did the model work? What did it cost?
H: What do you gain and lose by judging policies in terms of growth?
A: Support: Allen's argument that investment and growth were high is supported by the output of steel, coal and electricity; Conquest's argument that the regime used the famine against Ukraine is supported by the measures of November 1932 to January 1933 in Ukraine and the Kuban, but weakened by the famine in Kazakhstan and the Volga; Davies and Wheatcroft's argument for policy driven by economic aims and improvised decisions is supported by the sequence of crisis, emergency measures and radical policy. Challenge: the targets missed, the lower real wages, the farm sector. A* judgement says that the historians answer different questions (growth, intention, decision-making) and so do not simply contradict one another.

:::recap
- **Great Turn (1928–29)**: a response to the grain crisis of 1927–28, driven by security, self-sufficiency, socialism and Stalin's control. The Urals–Siberian method (January–February 1928) ended the market in grain.
- **Collectivisation**: voluntary at first, forced from late 1929. 27 December 1929 "liquidation of the kulaks as a class"; 5 January 1930 timetable; about 21 per cent of households collectivised by January 1930 and about 58 per cent by March; "Dizzy with Success" on 2 March 1930; about 23 per cent by June; about three fifths by 1932 and about nine tenths by 1937. Kolkhozy, sovkhozy and the MTS; household plots allowed in 1935.
- **Dekulakisation**: about 1.8 million deported in 1930–31; "kulak" was a loose political label.
- **Famine, 1932–33**: about 5 to 8 million excess deaths (Davies and Wheatcroft about 5.7 million); Ukraine about 3.5 to 4 million, Kazakhstan about 1.5 million. The law of 7 August 1932, blacklists, passports and the closing of borders made it worse. Historians agree policy caused it and disagree about intention.
- **Livestock**: cattle about 70.5 million (1928) to about 38.4 million (1933); sheep and goats about 146.7 to about 50.2 million.
- **Industry**: first plan from 1 October 1928, declared fulfilled in four years and three months, with most targets missed (pig iron 6.2 million tonnes against about 10). By 1937 steel was 17.7 million tonnes and by 1940 electricity was 48.6 billion kWh. Projects: Magnitogorsk, Dneprostroi, Belomor, Moscow Metro.
- **Stakhanovites**: 102 tonnes of coal in one shift on 30–31 August 1935, over 14 times the norm.
- **Living standards**: the urban population rose from about 26 million (1926) to about 56 million (1939); communal apartments; rationing ended in 1935; real wages lower in 1937 than in 1928; wage differentials from 1931; discipline laws of 15 November 1932, 20 December 1938 and 26 June 1940.
- **Judgement**: a political and fiscal success and an economic and human failure in the countryside; a success in building a heavy-industry and defence base, but at a very high cost and below its own targets.
