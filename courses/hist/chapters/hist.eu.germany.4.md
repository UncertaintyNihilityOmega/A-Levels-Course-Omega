A Germany of dozens of independent states had become a federal empire with a national parliament, and a new great power stood in the centre of Europe. ## After 1866: unification unfinished

### Pressure from the nationalists

The settlement of 1866 pleased no one completely. The North German Confederation gave Germany a national parliament, the Reichstag, but it contained only the north. The four southern states, with about eight million people, stood outside it: Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden and the southern part of Hesse-Darmstadt. The nationalists, above all the **National Liberals**, who had backed Bismarck because he delivered unity, wanted this gap closed. For them 1866 was a stage, not the end. They argued in the Reichstag and the press that the "Main line" (the river dividing north from south) was an unnatural border.

Bismarck did not hurry. He knew that the south was not ready and that France would not accept a united Germany without a fight. In 1870, for example, the liberal government of Baden asked to join the North German Confederation, and Bismarck, who did not want to alarm France and Europe or push the other southern states away, advised waiting. He said in effect that unification would come in its own time. This was a difference between him and the nationalists. He wanted unity only on terms that kept Prussia's power, with the south joining by its own choice, while many of the liberals wanted it at once. The two did not quarrel: the nationalists needed Bismarck, who had the army, and Bismarck needed the nationalists, whose pressure helped him persuade the southern rulers.

### The problem of the southern states

There were several reasons why the southern states did not want to join Prussia on its terms.

:::key Why the south held back from union with Prussia
1. **Religion.** Bavaria was about 70 per cent Catholic, as were large parts of Baden and Württemberg. Many Catholics saw Protestant Prussia as an enemy, and many looked towards Catholic Austria as their natural leader.
2. **Particularism.** Each kingdom or grand duchy had its own dynasty, army, civil service, capital and history. Kings did not wish to become subjects of the Hohenzollerns. Many ordinary people felt they were Bavarians or Württembergers first.
3. **Dislike of Prussian militarism.** The Prussian system of long service and strict discipline seemed alien to the south, and the democrats of Württemberg and the Bavarian "Patriots" opposed it.
4. **Austria.** Many people in the south, especially in Bavaria, had traditional ties to Vienna and had fought on Austria's side in 1866.
5. **Differences in politics.** Baden had a liberal government that wished to join Prussia, but Bavaria's elections in 1869 were won by the Catholic and particularist Patriots, which forced out the pro-German Bavarian prime minister, Prince Chlodwig zu Hohenlohe-Schillingsfürst, in 1870.
:::

Yet the south could not go its own way. After 1866 the southern states had to pay Prussia indemnities, and Prussia, with the strongest army, could threaten them. Bismarck had offered mild terms, with no annexation, but in August 1866 he signed **secret alliances** with Baden, Württemberg and Bavaria (and later Hesse-Darmstadt): in a war they would put their armies under the command of the King of Prussia. The southern states also needed Prussia against France, because the French had once ruled the Rhineland and still hoped to expand towards it.

### The Zollparlament

The Zollverein, the customs union (see [Germany (1)](#hist.eu.germany.1)), continued after 1866, and Bismarck used it to tie the south closer. In 1867 the Zollverein treaties were renewed on a new basis. They created a council and a parliament for customs matters.

:::def Zollparlament
The "customs parliament" of the Zollverein, created in 1867. It was made up of the members of the Reichstag of the North German Confederation together with 85 deputies elected in the southern states, and it could decide only on customs, trade and some taxes.
:::

Bismarck hoped that the Zollparlament would draw the south, step by step, into a common political life. The elections of February and March 1868 showed how wrong he was. Of the 85 southern deputies (48 from Bavaria, 17 from Württemberg, 14 from Baden and 6 from Hesse-Darmstadt), most were opponents of Prussian leadership, including Bavarian Patriots and Württemberg democrats. The Zollparlament opened on 27 April 1868. It could handle trade questions, and no more. When national feeling was raised, the southern deputies stood aside, and Bismarck took the hint: the south could not be brought in by peaceful means.

:::key Lack of political unity: the position by 1870
1. **Economically, the south was tied to the north** through the Zollverein, railways and trade, and its businessmen favoured unity.
2. **Militarily, the south was tied through secret treaties** and by army reforms on Prussian lines.
3. **Politically, the south was divided**: Baden was ready to join, Bavaria and Württemberg were not.
4. **So Bismarck needed an event** that would make southern opinion rally to Prussia willingly: a threat or attack from France.
:::

## Napoleon III's ambitions and the road to war

### The weakness of the Second Empire

**Napoleon III**, nephew of the first Napoleon, had ruled France since 1851 (as emperor from 1852). By 1866 he was 58 and in poor health, and his empire was losing support. His foreign policy had been a string of failures: the attempt to set up an empire in Mexico had ended in 1867 with the execution of Maximilian, and in 1866 he had stood by while Prussia, which he had thought would be weakened by a long war with Austria, won in seven weeks. Prussia's victory at Königgrätz (Sadowa in French) upset the balance of power in Europe and was a blow to French prestige. French opinion demanded "revenge for Sadowa".

At home the opposition grew. In 1869 the opposition parties did well in the elections, and in January 1870 Napoleon appointed a liberal ministry under Émile Ollivier. A plebiscite in May 1870 approved the new liberal empire by about 7.3 million votes to about 1.5 million, and it seemed to give the emperor a fresh start. But the empire still needed success to survive, and the emperor, the empress Eugénie and the army party believed that a victory over Prussia would restore its prestige.

### The Luxembourg crisis, 1867

:::def Luxembourg crisis
The quarrel of 1867 over the Grand Duchy of Luxembourg. Napoleon III tried to buy it from its ruler, King William III of the Netherlands, as compensation for Prussia's gains in 1866. Bismarck first let him go ahead and then exposed the plan to German opinion, which forced France to give up.
:::

**Causes.** After Königgrätz Napoleon III wanted "compensation" for Prussia's growth. In 1866 France's ambassador, Benedetti, had asked for territory on the Rhine, and Bismarck had refused. At Biarritz in 1865 Bismarck had hinted that France might expand in Belgium or Luxembourg, but nothing was agreed. Luxembourg was a small grand duchy, ruled in personal union by the Dutch king, and had been a member of the German Confederation. Prussia still kept a garrison in its fortress. William III needed money, and on 23 March 1867 he agreed in principle to sell the duchy to France for 5 million guilders.

**Why Bismarck encouraged it.** At first Bismarck did not stop the sale. He told the French that it was a matter for the Dutch king, and that he would not object. There were several reasons. He wanted to keep France friendly while the North German Confederation was being built. He also saw that if France took Luxembourg, which most Germans regarded as German, it would seem to be the aggressor, and that nationalist opinion in Germany, and even in the south, would then turn against France. When the plan became known in March 1867, German newspapers and the Reichstag raised an outcry. Bismarck now played the part of the guardian of the nation: he said the Prussian government could not allow it, and in March 1867 he made public the secret military alliances with the southern states, a warning to France that a war with Prussia would be a war with all Germany. Napoleon, who was not ready for war, retreated.

**Outcome and effects.** A conference of the great powers in London led to the Treaty of London of 11 May 1867. Luxembourg's independence and **neutrality** were guaranteed by the powers; the Prussian garrison left and the fortress was dismantled; Luxembourg stayed in the Zollverein. France got nothing.

:::key What the Luxembourg crisis did to relations with France
1. **It embarrassed Napoleon III again.** After Sadowa and Mexico, it was another failure of French policy, and it fed the call for revenge.
2. **It made France distrust Bismarck**, who had seemed to encourage the plan and then gone back on it.
3. **It showed that Prussia would fight** rather than allow France to take German-speaking land, and that the southern states would stand with it.
4. **It worried the southern states.** French ambitions on the Rhine made the south look to Prussia for protection.
5. **War was now more likely.** Each side expected the next crisis to end in a fight, and the French army started to be reformed.
:::

### The Hohenzollern candidature

The immediate cause of the war was a quarrel about the throne of Spain. In September 1868 the Spanish queen, Isabella II, had been overthrown, and the Spanish government under Marshal Prim searched for a new king. One of the candidates was **Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen**, a Catholic member of a south German branch of the Prussian royal family. He was related to Napoleon III's own family.

France objected strongly: it would be enclosed by Hohenzollern rulers in Spain and in Germany, as in the time of Charles V (the sixteenth-century emperor who ruled Spain and Germany together). Bismarck supported the candidature, which he saw as a way of putting pressure on France. King Wilhelm I, as head of the family, was reluctant but agreed that Leopold was free to decide. In June 1870 Leopold accepted.

:::key The sequence of the Hohenzollern crisis, 1870
1. **June**: Leopold accepts the Spanish crown, with Bismarck's backing and Wilhelm's reluctant consent.
2. **Early July**: the news reaches Paris. The French government and press are furious. The foreign minister, the Duke of Gramont, tells the Chamber that France will not allow a foreign power to put a prince on the throne of Charles V.
3. **12 July**: Leopold's father, Prince Karl Anton, withdraws his son's name. It is a diplomatic victory for France.
4. **12–13 July**: Gramont is not satisfied. He wants a further guarantee, so Benedetti is told to ask King Wilhelm, who was taking the waters at Ems, for a promise that the candidature would never be renewed.
5. **13 July**: Wilhelm refuses at Ems and Abeken sends his report to Bismarck.
:::

### The Ems telegram

On the morning of 13 July 1870 Benedetti met the king on the promenade at Ems. Wilhelm had already agreed that the candidature should be withdrawn. When Benedetti demanded that the king promise never to allow it again, Wilhelm refused, politely but firmly. He then refused to receive the ambassador again and sent word through an adjutant. His official, Heinrich Abeken, wrote a report to Bismarck in Berlin, which suggested that Bismarck might tell the embassies and the press. It arrived in the evening (it was received in Berlin at 6.09 p.m.).

Bismarck, who was dining with Roon and Moltke, read the telegram and, in his own account, decided what to do. He had been depressed because the French had won a diplomatic victory by the withdrawal on 12 July; he was thinking of resigning. Now he saw a chance. He shortened the report, cutting out the king's courteous phrases and the explanation that Wilhelm was waiting for news of the Prince, and made the king's refusal seem sharper and final. Moltke said that it now had a different ring: from a parley it had become a challenge. Bismarck published his version that evening to the press and to Prussian embassies.

:::def The Ems telegram
The report sent by Heinrich Abeken from Ems to Bismarck on 13 July 1870 about the king's meeting with the French ambassador Benedetti, and the shortened version Bismarck released to the press. Without adding a word, Bismarck cut it so that it read as though the king had dismissed the ambassador, and it was taken in France as an insult.
:::

The effect was what Bismarck intended. In France the newspapers and crowds in Paris demanded war. In Germany, where the text was read as an insult to the king by the French, there was a wave of patriotic feeling. On 14 July France began to prepare for war. On 15 July the Legislative Body voted the war credits by an overwhelming majority, with Ollivier saying that he accepted the responsibility "with a light heart". On **19 July** France declared war on Prussia.

### Was the Ems telegram the cause of the war?

:::warn Do not say the Ems telegram "caused" the war on its own
Many students write "Bismarck's telegram made France declare war". But the French government had already had its victory on 12 July. It was Gramont's further demand on 12–13 July and the decision of Napoleon III's ministers on 14–15 July to answer the telegram with war that mattered. Bismarck did not force France to fight. He found a way of making it look as if France had gone to war to avenge an insult, and he made it easy for France to make the mistake.
:::

| Cause | Evidence | How important? |
|---|---|---|
| Bismarck's provocation | He backed the candidature; he edited the telegram to make it a "red rag" (Bismarck's phrase: a red rag to the French bull); he wrote later that he wanted France to look the aggressor | Important, but he did not decide what France would do |
| French mistakes | Gramont's demand for a guarantee after Leopold's withdrawal; the cabinet's decision on 14 July to mobilise; belief that the Chassepot and the army would win | Very important: France chose to declare war |
| Napoleon III's weakness | The empire needed a success; the empress and the army party wanted war; he was ill and indecisive | Important as a background cause |
| Long-term rivalry | Sadowa, Luxembourg, French fear of a united Germany | Created the conditions in which a small crisis could lead to war |
| Public opinion in both countries | French crowds shouting "to Berlin"; German outrage at the supposed insult | Made it harder for governments to back down |

Historians disagree about how far Bismarck wanted war. Taylor (*Bismarck: The Man and the Statesman*, 1955) saw an opportunist who profited from French blunders; Steinberg (*Bismarck: A Life*, 2011) judged that Bismarck deliberately provoked France, and Bismarck's own memoirs show him pleased with the result. Michael Howard (*The Franco-Prussian War*, 1961) and Geoffrey Wawro (*The Franco-Prussian War*, 2003) wrote military histories that also show how the French government, believing in its army, walked into a war it was not ready for.

## The Franco-Prussian War, 1870–71

### Why Bismarck was in a strong position

When war came in July 1870, Prussia and its allies were in a much stronger position than France. There were two parts to this: diplomacy and military power.

**Lack of international support for France.**
- **Austria-Hungary** had been reorganised by the Compromise of 1867 (which made Austria and Hungary two kingdoms under one emperor), was weak after 1866, and feared that Russia would attack if it supported France. Some leaders in Vienna wanted revenge for 1866, but the Hungarians held them back, and Austria would have joined France only if the south Germans had turned against Prussia, which they did not do.
- **Italy** wanted Rome, which was held by a French garrison, and so would not ally with France. After the garrison was withdrawn in August 1870, Italy took Rome in September.
- **Russia** was friendly to Prussia after the Alvensleben Convention of 1863, and in October 1870 Bismarck supported the Tsar's decision to cancel the Black Sea clauses of the 1856 treaty (which barred Russia from having warships in the Black Sea). Russia stayed neutral.
- **Britain** was neutral. On 25 July 1870 Bismarck gave *The Times* a draft treaty in Benedetti's handwriting from 1866, in which France was to gain Belgium. British opinion turned against France.
- **The southern German states** did not hesitate. When France declared war, Bavaria, Württemberg and Baden mobilised on 16–17 July under their treaties, and national feeling in the south swung behind Prussia, as Bismarck had hoped. The south German states and the north now fought together.

**The strength of the Prussian army under Moltke.**
- **Organisation.** General **Helmuth von Moltke**, chief of the General Staff, had planned the mobilisation in detail, with railways and the telegraph. Reservists knew where to go and were carried by train to the frontier in days. The three German armies on the border held well over 300,000 men (estimates run to about 380,000), against about 200,000 in the French Army of the Rhine.
- **Training and leadership.** Universal conscription gave Germany a large reserve of trained soldiers. Moltke trained his officers to use their own judgement within his overall plan.
- **Weapons.** The French **Chassepot** rifle was better than the Prussian needle gun: it could hit targets at about 1,500 metres against about 600. But the Prussian **Krupp** steel breech-loading cannon outranged the French bronze muzzle-loaders and fired faster, and in battle after battle German artillery destroyed French formations at long range.
- **Experience.** The army had won in 1864 and 1866.

**The weakness of the French army.** The French army had brave soldiers and good rifles, but it was badly organised. Reservists had to travel to depots and then find their regiments, and many arrived at the front without equipment and supplies; there were no good maps. The emperor, in poor health, took command, but the army of 1870 had no plan and no efficient staff. Its commanders, such as Marshal Bazaine and Marshal MacMahon, failed to work together.

:::key Why Bismarck was in a strong position in 1870
1. **France had no allies**: Austria, Italy, Russia and Britain all stayed out.
2. **The south joined Prussia willingly** because France appeared to be the aggressor.
3. **Moltke's General Staff** mobilised faster, with railways and the telegraph, and led armies with a clear plan.
4. **German artillery and numbers** beat France in the field; the French army had good rifles but poor organisation and leadership.
5. **The war was fought in France**, so the German states escaped the damage.
:::

### From the frontier to Sedan

The French invaded briefly, at Saarbrücken on 2 August, and then fell back. The three German armies crossed the border and in early August won the battles of Wissembourg, Wörth and Spicheren. In mid-August the French Army of the Rhine under Bazaine was driven into the fortress of **Metz** and besieged. Napoleon III and MacMahon gathered a second army at Châlons, which set out to relieve Bazaine. Moltke turned it back and trapped it at **Sedan**, near the Belgian frontier, on 1 September 1870. After a day of bombardment, Napoleon III surrendered on 2 September. About 100,000 French soldiers went into captivity. Bismarck met the emperor on the morning of the surrender.

### The impact of Sedan

:::key The impact of the German victory at Sedan
1. **It ended the Second Empire.** On 4 September 1870 a crowd in Paris forced out the empress's government, and a republic was proclaimed with a Government of National Defence.
2. **It did not end the war.** The new republic refused to accept the loss of territory. Foreign Minister Jules Favre told Bismarck at Ferrières that France would not give up an inch of its land. Léon Gambetta left Paris by balloon and raised new armies. Paris was besieged from 19 September, and Metz and Bazaine's army surrendered on 27 October.
3. **It strengthened German nationalism.** The victory made a united Germany look natural and unstoppable. Princes and peoples in the south were swept up in national enthusiasm, and southern troops had fought side by side with the north.
4. **It made negotiations with the south easier.** After Sedan, no southern ruler could stand out against union, and Bismarck began talks in autumn 1870.
5. **It made the peace harsh.** The German generals and public wanted Alsace and Lorraine, and the war lasted four months more.
:::

### The armistice and the Treaty of Frankfurt

The siege of Paris lasted from 19 September 1870 until the city, short of food, surrendered, and an **armistice** was agreed on **28 January 1871**, ten days after the proclamation of the Empire at Versailles. A National Assembly elected in February voted for peace, and a preliminary treaty was signed in late February. The final **Treaty of Frankfurt** was signed on **10 May 1871**.

:::def The Treaty of Frankfurt (1871)
The peace that ended the Franco-Prussian War, signed on 10 May 1871. France gave up Alsace and the northern part of Lorraine, including Metz, to the new German Empire and paid an indemnity of five billion francs; German troops stayed in parts of France until it was paid, which happened by 1873.
:::

The transfer of Alsace and Lorraine, with about 1.5 million inhabitants, was the part that mattered most. Moltke and the generals wanted the territory as a military frontier; many German nationalists argued that the land was historically German. Bismarck at first doubted the wisdom of it, but accepted. It created a lasting French desire for revenge (*revanche*) and, in Bismarck's later system of alliances, a permanent reason for keeping France isolated.

## The creation of the German Empire, 1871

### Bringing in the south

The war made union with the south possible. Bismarck negotiated in the autumn of 1870 with each southern government at Versailles, where the king's headquarters were, and in Berlin. The terms differed.
- **Baden and Hesse-Darmstadt** (the south of the state) signed on 15 November 1870 and entered on almost the same terms as the north.
- **Bavaria** signed on 23 November 1870 at Versailles. It was given the most generous terms, because Bismarck needed the second largest state: the **concessions to the southern states** included its own army in peacetime under the Bavarian king (although in war under the Emperor's command), its own post and telegraph services and railways, its own taxes on beer and spirits, and the right to keep its own diplomatic representatives. Bavaria also got a permanent seat on the Bundesrat's committee on the army, and chaired its committee on foreign affairs.
- **Württemberg** signed on 25 November 1870, with a smaller list of special rights (post and telegraph, some taxes), and its army was absorbed into the national army with a Württemberg corps.
The treaties came into force on 1 January 1871, which is why that date is sometimes called the birth of the Empire.

### The imperial title

Bismarck was determined that the imperial title should come from the princes and not from the people. In 1849 the Frankfurt Parliament had offered Frederick William IV a crown "from the gutter", as he saw it, and he had refused it (see [Germany (2)](#hist.eu.germany.2)). Wilhelm I would not take a crown from a parliament either, and he did not like the title: he was proud to be King of Prussia, and he feared that "Kaiser" would swallow up Prussia in Germany. Bismarck had to manage both the princes and the king.

His main tool was Bavaria. On 27 November 1870 Bismarck sent King Ludwig II of Bavaria a draft of a letter (the *Kaiserbrief*, or "emperor letter") inviting Wilhelm to take the title of German Emperor. He told Ludwig that the proposal had to come from him as the senior king after Prussia's, as only then would the title rest on a free act of the princes. Ludwig, whose passion was building castles and who was short of money, copied and signed the letter on 30 November (with some changes by his own ministers); historians record that he also received secret payments from Prussian funds. It was handed to Wilhelm in December. Other princes then sent similar letters. In December a deputation from the North German Reichstag, led by its president, Eduard Simson, who had led the Frankfurt deputation to the Prussian king in 1849, came to Versailles to ask Wilhelm to accept, and he agreed.

### The proclamation at Versailles, 18 January 1871

:::def The proclamation of the German Empire
The ceremony on 18 January 1871 in the Hall of Mirrors at the palace of Versailles, at which the German princes and the army acclaimed King Wilhelm I of Prussia as German Emperor (*Deutscher Kaiser*). It took place while Paris was still under siege.
:::

The date, 18 January, was the anniversary of the crowning of the first king in Prussia in 1701. The setting was a conqueror's gesture: the great palace of Louis XIV, the symbol of French power, was now the headquarters of the German armies. The ceremony was attended by princes, generals and soldiers; there were few civilians and no members of the Reichstag. The exact title had been argued over: Wilhelm wanted the grander "Emperor of Germany", while Bismarck, to avoid offending the southern princes, kept to "German Emperor". The Grand Duke of Baden got round the quarrel by leading the cheers simply for **Kaiser Wilhelm**. Wilhelm was 73 and Bismarck 55. According to later accounts, Wilhelm was in a bad temper with Bismarck over the title and would hardly look at him. The new Empire was federal: the German Empire was a union of 25 states, with a Prussian Kaiser at its head.

### The constitution of the Reich

The constitution of the German Empire was adopted on 16 April 1871 and took effect on 4 May. It was in essence the constitution of the North German Confederation with the southern states added, and it was Bismarck's design. It combined a national parliament elected by every man with strong powers in the hands of the Prussian monarch and the princes.

| Body | Who sat in it | Powers | Who controlled it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Kaiser** | The King of Prussia, by inheritance | Represented the Reich abroad; declared war and made peace (war other than defence needed the Bundesrat's consent); commanded the army; appointed and dismissed the Chancellor | The Hohenzollerns |

| **Bundesrat** | Delegates of the 25 states' governments: 58 votes, of which Prussia had 17 | Passed laws with the Reichstag; could block changes to the constitution (14 votes were enough) | The princes and their governments; Prussia could block change on its own |

:::def Reichstag
The lower house of the German parliament, elected by direct, secret ballot by all men over the age of 25. It shared the making of laws and the voting of the budget with the Bundesrat, but the Chancellor was not responsible to it.
:::

:::def Bundesrat
The "federal council" of the German Empire, made up of delegates of the governments of the 25 states, voting in blocs. Prussia had 17 of its 58 votes, enough to block any change to the constitution.
:::

The Bundesrat had 58 votes: Prussia 17, Bavaria 6, Saxony 4, Württemberg 4, Baden 3, Hesse 3, Mecklenburg-Schwerin 2, Brunswick 2 and the other 17 states one each. Because 14 votes could block a change in the constitution, Prussia could stop any reform. The Kaiser was the King of Prussia, the Chancellor was normally also Prussian Minister-President, and Prussia had the largest part of the population and the army.

:::key The character of the 1871 constitution
1. **Federal**: the states kept their own rulers, governments and many powers (education, police, local government). Bavaria and Württemberg kept special rights.
2. **Dominated by Prussia**: the Kaiser, the Chancellor and the army were Prussian, and Prussia could block change.
3. **Democratic in one respect**: the Reichstag was elected by universal manhood suffrage, one of the widest franchises in Europe at the time, and no law passed without its agreement.
4. **Authoritarian in another**: the Chancellor was chosen by the Kaiser, not the Reichstag, and the army was outside parliament's control.
5. **Kleindeutsch**: Austria, and the German-speaking people of Austria, were left out. Alsace-Lorraine was ruled directly by the Empire as an imperial territory (*Reichsland*).
:::

## Why was unification achieved by 1871? Weighing the causes

Sort the causes by what they did, and then decide which one was decisive.

| Factor | How it helped | Its limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Bismarck's diplomacy** | Isolated Austria (1866) and France (1870); used Schleswig-Holstein, Luxembourg and Spain; managed the princes and the Kaiserbrief | He reacted to events and did not control French decisions |
| **Prussian military strength** | Königgrätz and the victory over France; Roon's reforms, Moltke's staff, railways, Krupp guns | Needed the right diplomatic conditions; military victory alone did not bring in the south |
| **Nationalism and liberalism** | The National Liberals, the Nationalverein, mass enthusiasm in 1870 brought the south in | Southern particularism and Catholicism resisted until 1870 |
| **Economic strength** | The Zollverein and Prussia's coal, iron and railways tied the states together and paid for the army (Böhme stressed this) | Economic ties did not create political union (the Zollparlament) |
| **French and Austrian weakness** | Napoleon III's blunders in 1867 and 1870; Austria's defeat and internal problems | These were opportunities, not causes |
| **Chance and events** | The Danish constitution of 1863; the Spanish throne; Sedan | Bismarck made use of chance but did not create it |

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **A.J.P. Taylor** (*Bismarck: The Man and the Statesman*, 1955) saw Bismarck as an opportunist who improvised and exploited the mistakes of others; German unification, in his view, owed as much to others' blunders as to a plan.
- **Otto Pflanze** (*Bismarck and the Development of Germany*, 1963, revised 1990) described a flexible statesman who kept several options open, and credited both his skill and the underlying strength of Prussia and of German nationalism.
- **Jonathan Steinberg** (*Bismarck: A Life*, 2011) stressed his ruthlessness, his understanding of power and his willingness to provoke war in 1870.
- **Michael Howard** (*The Franco-Prussian War*, 1961) and **Geoffrey Wawro** (*The Franco-Prussian War*, 2003) showed how much German success depended on military organisation, and on French weakness.
- **Helmut Böhme** stressed the economic foundations of Prussia's leadership.
Paper 2 rewards your own argument, supported by evidence, not a list of names.
:::

:::key A judgement you can defend
Bismarck's diplomacy was the **decisive** factor, because it created the conditions in which Prussia's army could win and the south could be won over: Austria and France were isolated, and the princes were persuaded to accept a Prussian Kaiser. But it was **not sufficient** on its own. It needed Prussia's military power (Roon, Moltke, the needle gun and Krupp's guns), the long-term pull of nationalism and the Zollverein, and the mistakes of Napoleon III and Gramont. The best answers weigh all of these and make a criterion explicit (what turned the idea of unity into fact?).
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Napoleon III's government chose to go to war with Prussia in July 1870.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Prince Leopold of Hohenzollern was offered the Spanish throne and France did not like it. He withdrew on 12 July. Then Bismarck published the Ems telegram, and the French thought it was an insult to their ambassador. France declared war on 19 July."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes an event; none says why it made the French government choose war. Causation is implied, not explained. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was fear of encirclement. A Hohenzollern on the throne of Spain would mean that France was ruled by hostile dynasties on two frontiers, and France, which had been humiliated at Sadowa in 1866, could not accept this. The government under Ollivier and the foreign minister Gramont forced the withdrawal of Leopold, but then Gramont asked Wilhelm for a promise that the candidature would never return, which went further than the withdrawal had made necessary, and when Wilhelm refused, the government could present the refusal to the Chamber as an insult. A second reason was the weakness of the Second Empire. The emperor had failed in Mexico and in the Luxembourg crisis of 1867, the opposition had won seats in 1869, and although the plebiscite of May 1870 gave him about 7.3 million votes against about 1.5 million, the empire needed a success to survive."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was French confidence in the army. Ministers and generals believed that the Chassepot rifle and the new army were superior, and Ollivier said he accepted war with a light heart. On 15 July the Legislative Body voted the war credits by an overwhelming majority, and the crowds in Paris, who had read Bismarck's edited version of the Ems telegram, shouted for war.

These factors were linked. The candidature was only a spark: it lit a fire because France was already afraid of Prussia, and because a regime that had lost prestige could not afford to look weak again. Confidence in the army made war look safe, so the government rejected the diplomatic victory it had won on 12 July. The decisive factor was the weakness of the empire: Napoleon III and his ministers decided that only a clear victory over Prussia would save their regime, and the telegram gave them the pretext. The French government chose war because it believed it could win and because it believed it had little to lose by risking it."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the humiliation and fear created the willingness to fight; confidence made war look safe; the telegram provided the occasion), with a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

His Majesty writes to me that Count Benedetti spoke to him on the promenade, and in the end very importunately, demanding that he authorise him to telegraph at once that the King bound himself for all future time never again to give his approval if the Hohenzollerns renewed their candidature. His Majesty rejected this somewhat sternly, saying that one must not and could not give such undertakings for ever. His Majesty has since received a letter from the Prince. As he had told Count Benedetti that he was awaiting news from the Prince, he has decided, on the advice of Count Eulenburg and myself, not to receive Count Benedetti again, but to let him be told by an adjutant that His Majesty has now had from the Prince the confirmation of the news Benedetti had from Paris and has nothing further to say to the ambassador. His Majesty leaves it to Your Excellency to decide whether Benedetti's new demand and its rejection should be passed to our ambassadors and the press.

*Adapted from a telegram sent from Ems by Heinrich Abeken, an official of the Prussian foreign ministry in the King's party, to Bismarck in Berlin, 13 July 1870.*

**Source B**

After the news of the renunciation of the Hereditary Prince of Hohenzollern had been officially communicated to the Imperial French Government by the Royal Spanish Government, the French Ambassador made a further demand of His Majesty the King at Ems: that he should authorise him to telegraph to Paris that His Majesty the King bound himself for all future time never again to give his approval if the Hohenzollerns renewed their candidature. His Majesty the King thereupon refused to receive the French Ambassador again, and had the latter informed by the adjutant on duty that His Majesty had nothing further to communicate to the Ambassador.

*Adapted from the version of the Ems telegram edited by Bismarck and released to the press and to Prussian embassies, 13 July 1870.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about what happened at Ems on 13 July 1870.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that Benedetti met the king on the promenade and demanded a promise. Source B does not say where the meeting was. Source B is shorter. They are different because Bismarck wrote Source B so it is biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources agree on the basic facts. Both say that the French ambassador, Benedetti, asked the king to authorise him to telegraph that Wilhelm bound himself 'for all future time' never again to approve a Hohenzollern candidature. Both say that the king then refused to receive Benedetti again and had him told through an adjutant that there was nothing further to say. So both support the claim that a demand was made and that the king ended contact.

The differences lie in what each leaves out and in tone. Source A shows the king's response as measured: he rejected the demand 'somewhat sternly', and he did so after Benedetti had pressed him 'very importunately', but he also explained that he was awaiting a letter from the Prince, and the refusal to see the ambassador again is the result of advice from Eulenburg and Abeken. It ends by leaving to Bismarck whether to publish. Source B removes the king's explanation, the 'sternly', the meeting on the promenade, the letter from the Prince and the advice, and opens with the formal renunciation by the Spanish government, so that the king's refusal appears to follow directly and finally from the 'further demand'. The Ambassador appears as a man who has pressed a further demand after the matter had been settled, and the King as a monarch who ends the audience.

The context explains this. Source A is a private, internal report from an official in the king's party to the Minister-President. Its purpose was to inform Bismarck exactly, in the knowledge that Bismarck would decide what to do, and it has the detail and hesitations of a real conversation. Source B was an official press release prepared by Bismarck on the evening of 13 July. As he later recalled, he made it by cutting words. His purpose was to alter the effect, and after French diplomacy had won on 12 July, he wanted a document that would make the French see an insult and the Germans see a rebuff. Moltke's reaction, that the text now had a different ring, shows what was achieved. The comparison therefore makes the two sources valuable for different reasons. Source A is the better evidence of what happened at Ems, since it is closer to the event and was not written to influence opinion. Source B is poor evidence of the meeting but excellent evidence of Bismarck's aims, since every omission shows what he wanted the public to believe."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the date, the internal and public purposes, Bismarck's motives) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'German unification was achieved by Bismarck's diplomacy.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: Alvensleben (Russia), isolation of Austria in 1866 (Biarritz, Italy), moderate peace of Prague, secret alliances with the south, handling of Luxembourg and the Hohenzollern candidature, Ems, the isolation of France in 1870 (the Times draft treaty), the Kaiserbrief and the treaties with the south.
- Against 1, military strength: Roon's reforms, Moltke, needle gun, Königgrätz, Sedan, Krupp artillery; war not diplomacy defeated Austria and France.
- Against 2, other factors: nationalism and the National Liberals; the Zollverein and economic strength; French mistakes (Gramont's demand, the declaration of war); chance.
- Judgement: diplomacy was the decisive condition, military power the means. Criterion: what turned the wish for unity into a state?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Bismarck's diplomacy was the main reason for unification. In 1863 he made friends with Russia through the Alvensleben Convention. In 1865 he met Napoleon III at Biarritz and in 1866 he made an alliance with Italy, so Austria had no allies when the war began. At Prague he offered Austria easy terms, and so Austria did not join France in 1870. In 1867 he used the Luxembourg crisis to make the south afraid of France, and in 1870 he edited the Ems telegram so that France declared war. The southern states joined and the Empire was proclaimed at Versailles. This shows that Bismarck's diplomacy achieved unification."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet diplomacy alone achieved nothing without the army. Austria was removed from Germany at Königgrätz on 3 July 1866, where Moltke's three armies, brought to Bohemia by railway, and infantry with the needle gun, beat about 215,000 Austrians and Saxons; no diplomatic isolation could have done this. In 1870 the German armies mobilised faster than the French, with well over 300,000 men on the frontier against about 200,000, and Krupp's steel guns beat the French in every battle. It was Sedan on 2 September, where Napoleon III surrendered about 100,000 men, that brought down the Second Empire and swept away the last doubts in the south. Bismarck's diplomatic position was strong because Prussia had already shown, in 1864 and 1866, that it would win. Roon's reforms, which Bismarck forced through against parliament, were his first and most important preparation.

Other forces also mattered. The National Liberals and the nationalist societies created a climate in which the south would accept union, and in July 1870 the German outrage at the supposed insult at Ems was a spontaneous popular movement that no diplomat could produce on demand. The Zollverein had tied the economies together. And Napoleon III and Gramont made mistakes that Bismarck could not have planned: after the withdrawal of Leopold on 12 July France had won, and it was Gramont's demand for a guarantee and the cabinet's decision on 14 July that gave Bismarck his chance. Taylor was right that Bismarck exploited the errors of others.

Overall, the statement is partly true. Bismarck's diplomacy was decisive in the sense that it created the conditions: it isolated Austria and France, it kept the war short and fought on one front, and it handled the princes. But diplomacy was a condition and the army was the means, and neither would have brought the south into a Prussian-led Empire without the nationalism that was strengthened by the war itself. The statement is therefore right that diplomacy shaped the way unification came, but it is wrong to credit it alone."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion and separates condition from means. With an opening paragraph on the case for diplomacy, this is Level 5 (19–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

The proposal that the title of German Emperor be offered to the King of Prussia must come from Your Majesty, not from a popular assembly, or the title would lack legitimacy. I have given Count Holnstein a draft statement for the King of Prussia and, once revised, for the other allied states. The title would show that its privileges rest on a voluntary transfer by the German princes, and the great German dynasties, Prussia included, would lose no standing in Europe through an emperor elected by them. The thanks owed for Bavaria's German policy and the bravery of its army belong to the German people and their history, not to me.

*Adapted (paraphrased) from a letter from Otto von Bismarck, Chancellor of the North German Confederation, to King Ludwig II of Bavaria, Versailles, 27 November 1870.*

**Source B**

We, Wilhelm, by the grace of God King of Prussia, make known that the German princes and free cities have addressed to us the unanimous call to renew the imperial dignity, and we accept it. May God grant that we be always an augmenter of the German Empire, not through conquest, but through the works and gifts of peace in national welfare, freedom and civilisation.

*Adapted from Wilhelm I's Proclamation to the German People, dated Versailles, 17 January 1871, and read out by Bismarck at the ceremony in the Hall of Mirrors on 18 January 1871.*

**Source C**

A painting by Anton von Werner, a German court painter who was present at the ceremony, of the proclamation of the German Empire in the Hall of Mirrors at the palace of Versailles, 18 January 1871. Werner painted several versions between 1877 and 1885, one of them as a present for Bismarck's seventieth birthday. It shows Wilhelm I standing on a low platform with the Grand Duke of Baden next to him, his arm raised to lead the cheers. **Question.** How far do these sources show that the German Empire of 1871 was created by agreement between the German states?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support (agreement): A (title should rest on a "voluntary transfer" by the princes), B (the "unanimous call" of the princes and free cities), D (federal constitution with a Bundesrat of state governments and a Reichstag). Challenge (imposed by Prussia): A (Bismarck drafted the letter and arranged who should propose it, so the agreement is managed), B (written by Wilhelm to look like the princes' initiative, issued in a conquered country), C (a ceremony of princes and army, no parliament, in the palace of a defeated enemy), D (the president is the King of Prussia by right, and he appoints the Chancellor).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a private letter from the architect of the plan, so it is very good evidence of how the agreement was produced and what Bismarck wanted people to believe: he is open about the need to make the offer seem to come from Ludwig. But it is also paraphrased from a translation, and it is only Bismarck's side; Ludwig's own motives (debts, the wish for secure rights) are not here. In my own knowledge, Bavaria had just signed its treaty on 23 November with special rights and Ludwig received secret payments.
- **B**: a public proclamation, written to present the Empire as an act of the princes, so it shows the image the new Reich wanted. It is not evidence of how the decision was reached, and its 'not through conquest' stands in the middle of a war, in the palace of Louis XIV, with Paris under siege.
- **C**: a visual source made years later (1877–85) by a painter who was there but who worked for the court. Its subject is a ceremony of princes and soldiers, which is what it was, but it gives Bismarck the centre of the picture and omits the people. It is strong evidence of how the founders wanted the occasion remembered, weak as evidence of agreement.
- **D**: a legal text, reliable evidence of the structure that was agreed. It shows that the states were represented in the Bundesrat, but also that Prussia held the presidency by inheritance and the Chancellor answered to the Kaiser.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A, B and D support the view that the Empire rested on agreement between the states. Source A shows that Bismarck thought the title must appear to rest on 'a voluntary transfer' by the princes, and in fact the princes had to agree: Bavaria's special rights in the treaty of 23 November and Ludwig's signature on 30 November were the price. Source B presents the Empire as the answer to a 'unanimous call' of the princes and free cities, and Source D, by giving the states a Bundesrat and Reichstag, builds the agreement into law. However, the sources also show how much of the agreement was managed. Source A is the letter of the man who drafted the proposal that was to appear to come from Ludwig, and it was accompanied by secret payments, which I know from other evidence; Source B uses the language of agreement while the proclamation took place in Versailles during the siege of Paris; Source C shows a military ceremony in which princes and officers acclaim the King, with no representative of the people; and Source D shows that the Kaiser, who was by inheritance the King of Prussia, appointed the Chancellor, and that Prussia had a blocking vote. The states did agree, because the southern kings preferred a federal Empire in which they kept their armies, posts and taxes to a Prussian conquest. But it was agreement of rulers under pressure, after a victorious war, and the sources agree only to a limited extent: the Empire was created by agreement among princes that Bismarck had arranged, not by a free agreement between equals."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1870–71; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was Prussian military strength the main reason for German unification by 1871?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Prussian army was very strong. Roon reformed it in the 1860s and Moltke was chief of the General Staff. The Prussians had the needle gun and in 1866 they beat Austria at Königgrätz in the Seven Weeks' War. In 1870 they beat France at Sedan, where Napoleon III was captured, and then they besieged Paris. They had Krupp guns and the railways. The German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles in January 1871. So military strength was the main reason for German unification."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it lists what the army did and then asserts the conclusion. It does not explain how military strength led to unification, does not compare it with any other reason, and does not use the word "main" to reach a judgement. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "Military strength was the necessary means of unification. Austria, which would never have left Germany of its own will, was removed at Königgrätz, where Moltke's armies, moved by railway and armed with the needle gun, defeated Benedek's. In 1870 the German mobilisation put well over 300,000 men on the frontier against about 200,000 French, and Sedan ended the Second Empire. However, it was not the main reason. The army only had the chance to win because Bismarck's diplomacy had isolated Austria and France, and the south came into the Empire because of the nationalism raised by the war, not by force. Strength was the instrument, but diplomacy and nationalism supplied the conditions in which it could be used." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Why did the southern states resist union with Prussia in the late 1860s?
A: Religion (Catholic Bavaria and Württemberg against Protestant Prussia); particularism and loyalty to their own dynasties; dislike of Prussian militarism; ties to Austria; the success of the Bavarian Patriots in 1869.
Q: What was the Zollparlament, and what did the elections of 1868 show?
A: A customs parliament of the northern Reichstag plus 85 southern deputies, with power only over customs and trade. The southern deputies were mostly opponents of Prussia, so it did not draw the south into political union.
Q: Why did Bismarck encourage, and then oppose, France's purchase of Luxembourg in 1867?
A: At first he did not stop it, to keep France friendly and let it appear as the aggressor; when German opinion was roused he used the outcry to refuse, and published the southern alliances. The Treaty of London (11 May 1867) neutralised Luxembourg.
Q: What happened on 12 and 13 July 1870?
A: Prince Karl Anton withdrew his son Leopold's candidature on 12 July; Gramont demanded a guarantee for the future; on 13 July Wilhelm refused Benedetti at Ems and Bismarck published an edited version of Abeken's report.
Q: Give three reasons why Bismarck was in a strong position in 1870.
A: France had no allies (Austria, Italy, Russia and Britain stayed out); Moltke's General Staff, railways and Krupp artillery; the southern states joined Prussia; France's army was badly organised.
Q: What was the impact of Sedan?
A: Napoleon III surrendered with about 100,000 men; the Second Empire fell on 4 September and a republic fought on; German nationalism surged and the southern states were drawn in; it led to the siege of Paris and the harsh peace.
Q: What did the Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871) provide?
A: France ceded Alsace and part of Lorraine (including Metz), paid five billion francs, and German troops stayed until it was paid.
Q: What concessions did Bavaria get when it joined?
A: Its own army in peacetime (under the Emperor in war), post and telegraph, railways, its own beer and spirits taxes, and the chair of the Bundesrat's foreign affairs committee.
Q: Who controlled what in the 1871 constitution?
A: The Kaiser (King of Prussia) controlled the army, foreign policy and the Chancellor; the Chancellor presided over the Bundesrat and answered only to the Kaiser; the Bundesrat (58 votes, Prussia 17) represented the states; the Reichstag was elected by men over 25 but could not remove the Chancellor.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the southern German states did not join the North German Confederation in 1866–70.
A: Indicative content: Catholicism and dislike of Protestant Prussia; particularism and the independence of the dynasties; fear of Prussian militarism; links with Austria and sympathy for it; the Patriots in Bavaria and democrats in Württemberg; the 1868 Zollparlament elections; Bismarck's own caution (he did not want to alarm France). Yet the secret alliances and the Zollverein tied them to Prussia. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: the south was held back by cultural and political attitudes and kept from going its own way by fear of France and economic ties, and concludes which mattered more.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Luxembourg crisis of 1867 ended in the Treaty of London.
A: Indicative content: Napoleon III wanted compensation; William III's sale for 5 million guilders; German public outcry and Bismarck's turn; the southern alliances made public; Napoleon was not ready for war and the French army was unreformed; the great powers (Britain, Russia, Austria) did not want a war and a conference was called; neutrality saved face for France. Level 4: connect the pressure on France with the pressure on Bismarck to avoid war before Prussia was ready, and judge which was decisive.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Ems telegram was the main cause of the Franco-Prussian War.' How far do you agree?
A: For: Bismarck's edit turned a refusal into an insult; the French press and crowds reacted; France declared war within a week. Against: the war credits and the decision to mobilise came from the French government; Gramont's overreaching demand after 12 July; Napoleon III's weakness; the long-term rivalry since Sadowa and Luxembourg; Bismarck's backing of the candidature; the military confidence of both sides. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion such as "what made war unavoidable on 13–19 July?" and judges, e.g., that the telegram was the trigger but not the main cause, which lay in French decisions and in the rivalry that made war likely.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the Franco-Prussian War responsible for the unification of Germany?
A: For: it brought the south in willingly through national feeling; the treaties of November 1870; the proclamation at Versailles; Sedan and the end of the French threat. Against: the groundwork was laid earlier (the Zollverein, the North German Confederation, the secret alliances of 1866, the army); Bismarck's diplomacy; the pressure of the National Liberals; the princes' consent was needed. Level 5: judge that the war was the immediate cause of completion, but only because earlier steps made the south dependent on Prussia.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about Bismarck's aims in 1870–71.
A: Similarities: both reflect Prussian power politics and a willingness to use war (A: the edited telegram as a red rag; B: territory and money taken from a defeated enemy). Differences: A is about how war was provoked, B about how it was ended; A is a private memoir, B an official treaty; A is about Bismarck's tactics, B about the terms Germany imposed, many of which reflect the generals and public opinion as well as Bismarck. Provenance: A was written 28 years later to defend or glorify his role and may exaggerate his foresight (he says he did not change any word, but cut the text); B is a treaty, reliable on its terms but silent on who wanted them. Level 4 explains the differences through purpose and context and judges which is better evidence of his aims.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources show that the war of 1870–71 was fought for German unity?
H: Decide which sources link the war to unity (C, D) and which show other aims (A, B).
H: Use your own knowledge: what did the generals want from the peace, and what did the South German states want?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: C (the title rests on a voluntary act of the German princes, the thanks belong to the German people and history); D (the Empire proclaimed as an augmenter of peace, not conquest). Challenge: A (the war came from a provocation, to preserve Prussian honour and to make France the aggressor, with unity a result and not mentioned); B (the treaty takes territory and money from France, which looks like conquest and set a new quarrel). Evaluation with knowledge: C is a private letter that shows Bismarck's management of the princes; D is a public proclamation whose claim of peaceful aims sits oddly with the siege of Paris; A is retrospective; B shows the generals' demands. Level 5 judges that unity was the result of the war and an aim for the nationalists, but that for Bismarck and the army the war was fought for Prussian security and prestige, with unity achieved on Prussian terms.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The creation of the German Empire in 1871 was a victory for Prussia rather than for Germany.' To what extent do you agree?
H: Set a criterion: who gained power under the constitution, and who made the important decisions?
H: Consider the Bundesrat, the Kaiser, the Chancellor, the Reichstag and the south's special rights.
A: For: Prussian Kaiser, Chancellor and army; Prussia's 17 of 58 votes and veto; Austria excluded; Alsace-Lorraine ruled from Berlin; the Reichstag could not remove the Chancellor. Against: federal structure with special rights for Bavaria and Württemberg; the Reichstag elected by universal manhood suffrage; many Germans saw their nation-state as a fulfilment; Prussia, too, had to share the Empire with the rulers of other states. Level 5 judges that Prussia dominated the structure, but the Empire met the nationalists' goal of unity and a parliament and was built on agreement between rulers, so it was a victory for both, on Prussian terms.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Bismarck deliberately provoked the war of 1870.' How far do you agree?
H: List what Bismarck did in 1870 that made war more likely, and what France did that he could not control.
H: What does the editing of the telegram show, and what does it not show?
A: Support: his support for the Hohenzollern candidature; the edit of the telegram, with the "red rag" comment; the memoirs, which show relief that war was coming; his earlier handling of Luxembourg and the draft treaty published in The Times. Challenge: Wilhelm's reluctance; France had won on 12 July and could have stopped; Gramont and the cabinet chose to escalate; the French public and army party; Taylor's view that Bismarck exploited French errors; Bismarck did not need war to bring in the south in the long run, but wanted it. A* judgement: Bismarck did not force France to declare war, but he took action that he knew would give France the chance, and he made sure that Prussia would be seen as the injured party. Provocation is accurate for the telegram and a poor description of the French choice.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ A painter's version of the proclamation at Versailles hangs in many German history textbooks. How far can historians rely on a painting like Werner's as evidence of how the Empire was founded?
H: When was it painted and for whom, and who was shown?
H: Compare what it shows with Source A and Source D in the worked example above.
A: Strengths: the painter was present; accurate on the setting, the uniforms and the main figures; shows the role of the army and princes. Weaknesses: painted years later (1877–85) for the court and for Bismarck's birthday; it was designed to glorify the founders, and later versions give Bismarck more prominence; no civilians or Reichstag deputies, which hides the nationalist and parliamentary side; a single moment, not the months of bargaining. Compare with the letter to Ludwig (the hidden bargaining) and the constitution (the structure of power). Judgement: reliable for what was done at the ceremony and for how the founders wanted to be remembered; unreliable for how the Empire was made, which needs written sources.
:::

:::recap
- **Unfinished unity after 1866**: the south (Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden, south Hesse) stayed out; Catholic, particularist, anti-Prussian; secret alliances of 1866; Zollparlament (opened 27 April 1868) showed the south's opposition; Bismarck waited.
- **Luxembourg, 1867**: William III's sale for 5 million guilders; Bismarck first let it go, then used German outrage; Treaty of London, 11 May 1867; France humiliated again.
- **Hohenzollern candidature**: Leopold accepts (June 1870); France protests; Karl Anton withdraws him (12 July); Gramont demands a guarantee; Ems, 13 July; Bismarck edits the telegram; France declares war 19 July.
- **Bismarck's strength**: no allies for France (Austria, Italy, Russia, Britain neutral); the southern states joined; Moltke's staff, railways, Krupp guns; the French army badly organised.
- **War**: Metz besieged; Sedan 1–2 September (Napoleon III surrendered); republic 4 September; Paris besieged from 19 September; armistice 28 January 1871; Treaty of Frankfurt 10 May 1871 (Alsace and part of Lorraine; five billion francs).
- **Empire**: treaties with the south (15–25 November 1870); Kaiserbrief 30 November; Wilhelm proclaimed Kaiser at Versailles 18 January 1871; constitution in force 4 May 1871: Kaiser, Chancellor, Bundesrat (58 votes, Prussia 17), Reichstag (men over 25); Prussian dominance; Austria excluded.
- **Judgement**: diplomacy was the decisive condition, military power the means, nationalism and French mistakes the context.
