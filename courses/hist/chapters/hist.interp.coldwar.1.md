Three labels are used in the tags below. They are taught fully in the next chapter, but you need the one-line versions now.

:::key Three labels, in one line each
- **Traditionalist** (also "orthodox"): the Soviet Union was mainly to blame, because of Stalin's expansion and Communist ideology; the American response was necessary and defensive.
- **Revisionist**: the United States was mainly to blame, because of its economic power, its atomic monopoly and Truman's hard line; Soviet actions were chiefly defensive reactions. (In the debate on 1914 "revisionist" meant something else. Define the word each time you use it.)
- **Post-revisionist**: both sides, or neither alone, were responsible; misperception, security fears and domestic politics mattered. After 1991 the Soviet archives produced the "New Cold War history", which often returns attention to Stalin's own aims.
:::

## Background: why the alliance was fragile

The alliance of 1941 was a marriage of necessity. Historians agree on that. What they disagree about is how much damage the earlier quarrels had done, and whether they were bound to come back (the syllabus calls these "inherent tensions"; the next chapter weighs them).

**Before 1941.** The Bolsheviks took power in 1917 and believed that capitalism would be overthrown by revolution across the world; in 1919 they founded the Communist International (Comintern) to help it along. Britain, France, the USA and Japan sent troops to Russia in 1918–20 during the Civil War, which Soviet leaders remembered for decades as proof that the capitalist powers meant to destroy them. The United States did not recognise the Soviet government until 1933. The USSR was not invited to the Munich conference of September 1938 and drew the lesson that Britain and France would rather push Hitler eastwards than stop him. Stalin then signed the Nazi–Soviet Pact on 23 August 1939; the Red Army occupied eastern Poland on 17 September 1939, the Baltic states in 1940, and in the Winter War of 1939–40 attacked Finland, for which the USSR was expelled from the League of Nations. In spring 1940 the Soviet secret police shot about 22,000 Polish officers and other prisoners, in the Katyn forest and elsewhere. The Soviet Union admitted responsibility only in 1990.

**22 June 1941.** Germany invaded the Soviet Union without warning. Churchill, who had been a bitter opponent of Bolshevism, promised help the same day. Roosevelt extended Lend-Lease aid to the USSR in November 1941; over the war the Soviet Union received supplies worth about 11 billion dollars. On 14 August 1941 Roosevelt and Churchill had issued the **Atlantic Charter**: no territorial gains, no frontier changes against the wishes of the peoples concerned, and the right of all peoples to choose their government. The Soviet Union accepted its principles in September 1941, but Stalin's own aim was to keep the borders of 1941 (the lands taken in 1939–40) and to have friendly governments on his western frontier.

:::def Grand Alliance
The wartime partnership of the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union (with the Free French and others) against Germany, Italy and Japan, from 1941 to 1945. It was an alliance against a common enemy and not a union of aims, which is why its quarrels over Poland, Germany and the second front matter to historians of the Cold War.
:::

**The second front.** From July 1941 Stalin pressed for a landing in Western Europe to draw German troops away from the east. Roosevelt hinted in 1942 that it would come that year, but Britain and America landed first in North Africa (November 1942), then in Sicily and Italy (1943), and only on 6 June 1944 in Normandy. Meanwhile the Red Army did most of the fighting: most German soldiers killed in the war died on the Eastern Front, and the Soviet Union lost about 27 million people, a figure Russian historians have used since the 1990s (estimates run lower and higher). The United States lost about 400,000 service men and women. Stalin suspected that the West wanted Russia and Germany to bleed each other white; the Western leaders pointed to the risks of a landing before they were ready. This suspicion is part of the "inherent tension", but the Western case (that the invasion needed shipping, air control and training) is also well documented.

:::def Second front
A Western Allied invasion of north-west Europe to force Germany to divide its armies. Stalin demanded one from 1941; it came on D-Day, 6 June 1944. Historians use the delay both to show Soviet grievance and to show that the alliance worked despite suspicion.
:::

**Tehran, 28 November – 1 December 1943.** The first meeting of Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin agreed that the Western invasion of France would come in May 1944 and that the USSR would join the war against Japan after Germany's defeat. The leaders discussed Poland's future frontiers, with the Soviet Union keeping the territory east of the Curzon Line (the frontier proposed in 1919–20) and Poland receiving German land in compensation in the west. Roosevelt, who believed that personal friendship with Stalin could overcome mistrust, did not want to press hard on Poland.

**Poland.** Poland was both the reason Britain went to war in 1939 and the biggest quarrel of the alliance. Two Polish governments were competing. The government-in-exile in London was recognised by Britain and the USA. In April 1943 Germany announced the discovery of the Katyn graves; the Polish government asked for an investigation by the Red Cross, and on 25 April the USSR broke off relations with it. In July 1944 the Soviet Union set up the Polish Committee of National Liberation at Lublin. When the Warsaw Uprising of the Polish Home Army began on 1 August 1944, the Red Army, near the city on the east bank of the Vistula, did not advance across the river, and Stalin delayed permission for Allied planes to use Soviet airfields. The Uprising was crushed on 2 October with perhaps 150,000 to 200,000 civilians dead and the city destroyed. Soviet defenders say that the army was exhausted after its advance; Western and Polish historians have mostly judged that Stalin was content to see the non-Communist underground destroyed. Either way, the Home Army was gone, and the Lublin Poles were the only ones left.

**The Percentages Agreement, 9 October 1944.** At a meeting in Moscow Churchill wrote on a sheet of paper the percentage of "predominance" the Soviet Union and the other Allies should have in five countries: Romania 90 per cent Russian, Greece 90 per cent British (in accord with the USA), Yugoslavia and Hungary 50–50, and Bulgaria 75 per cent Russian. Stalin ticked it. The only full account of the paper is Churchill's own, written in his memoirs, *Triumph and Tragedy* (1953), and the percentages were never an agreement in any legal sense; but in December 1944, when British troops fought Communist-led forces in Athens, Stalin did not intervene. The episode matters because it shows that the leaders were prepared to bargain in terms of spheres of influence, and that it was Churchill who first proposed it.

:::def Percentages Agreement
An informal deal on 9 October 1944 between Churchill and Stalin, in which Churchill proposed shares of influence in the Balkans (Romania 90 per cent Soviet, Greece 90 per cent British, Yugoslavia and Hungary 50–50, Bulgaria 75 per cent Soviet) and Stalin approved. Known from Churchill's account of 1953. Historians use it to argue that Britain recognised a Soviet sphere in 1944, or that the idea of spheres of influence was a shared practice from which the later quarrel grew.
:::

:::key What the wartime years mean for the question of blame
- **Traditionalist**: Stalin used the alliance to extend Soviet power; Katyn, Poland and the Warsaw Uprising show his methods.
- **Revisionist**: the West delayed the second front, and Stalin's demand for a friendly Poland was a reasonable result of invasions in 1914 and 1941.
- **Post-revisionist**: both sides acted from fear and distrust; the Percentages Agreement shows that spheres of influence were an accepted way of thinking.
:::

## Yalta, February 1945

By February 1945 the Red Army stood on the river Oder, within about 70 kilometres of Berlin, and the war in Europe had a few months to run. The Big Three (Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin, not the Big Three of 1919) met at Yalta, in the Crimea, from 4 to 11 February. Roosevelt was ill (he would die two months later). The main decisions were:

- **Germany**: to be occupied in zones by the USA, Britain and the USSR, with a zone for France carved from the British and American areas; Berlin, though inside the Soviet zone, to be shared by four powers. Germany was to be disarmed and its Nazi organisations destroyed. On reparations the Soviet side proposed that a Moscow commission take as a basis for discussion a total of 22 billion dollars, half of it for the USSR; the British objected to naming a figure; the matter was left to the commission.
- **Poland**: the eastern frontier was to follow the Curzon Line, with "substantial accessions in territory in the north and west" for Poland. The Provisional Government (the Lublin Poles) was to be "reorganized on a broader democratic basis" with democratic leaders from Poland and abroad, and free elections were to be held "as soon as possible".
- **The Declaration on Liberated Europe**: the three powers promised to help the liberated peoples of Europe form interim governments "broadly representative of all democratic elements" and hold free elections, and affirmed "the right of all people to choose the form of government under which they will live".
- **The United Nations**: a conference was called for San Francisco on 25 April 1945; the Security Council would give the five great powers a veto; the Soviet Union would have two additional seats in the General Assembly (for Ukraine and Belarus).
- **The Far East** (a secret protocol, published in 1946): the USSR would enter the war against Japan two or three months after Germany's surrender, and in return would regain South Sakhalin and the Kurile Islands and its former rights at Port Arthur.

:::def Declaration on Liberated Europe
A statement issued at Yalta on 11 February 1945 in which the USA, Britain and the USSR promised to help liberated peoples form representative governments and hold free elections. It is the main document used by traditionalists to show that Stalin broke his word in Eastern Europe, and by revisionists to show that it was vague enough to be interpreted in more than one way.
:::

**What Yalta meant.** The agreement was a compromise. The Red Army already occupied Poland, Romania, Bulgaria and most of Hungary, and Roosevelt wanted Soviet help against Japan (the atomic bomb had not been tested), and the United Nations. Stalin wanted a friendly Poland, and the wording on "free elections" and "broader democratic basis" left room for each side to read the agreement as it liked: to Stalin the existing Lublin government was to be widened with a few additions; to Roosevelt and Churchill it was to be replaced by a new government. In the United States the Republicans later charged that Roosevelt had "sold out" Eastern Europe at Yalta; Roosevelt's defenders said that the Red Army was there and that he had done what he could. Historians still debate how much the Yalta settlement was a victory for Stalin or a recognition of facts.

:::key Yalta in one box
- **What was agreed**: German zones (and a French zone); four-power Berlin; the UN and the veto; Poland's frontiers and a broadened government; the Declaration on Liberated Europe; the Soviet entry against Japan.
- **What was left open**: the amount of reparations; what "broader democratic basis" meant; who would supervise free elections.
- **Use in the debate**: a traditionalist reads Yalta as Stalin gaining concessions and then breaking his promises; a revisionist reads it as the high point of cooperation, which Truman abandoned; a post-revisionist reads it as an ambiguous compromise in which each side believed that it had won something different.
:::

## From Yalta to Potsdam, February to August 1945

**Eastern Europe after Yalta.** Within weeks the Soviet interpretation of "democratic" was clear. In Romania, on 27 February 1945, the Soviet deputy foreign minister Andrei Vyshinsky demanded that King Michael dismiss the government and appoint a Communist-led one; it took office on 6 March under Petru Groza. In Poland the Soviet secret police arrested sixteen leaders of the non-Communist underground in March and tried them in Moscow in June. A Government of National Unity was formed on 28 June, with the London Pole Stanisław Mikołajczyk as deputy prime minister, and Britain and the USA recognised it on 5 July. Truman and Churchill saw these events as breaches of the Declaration on Liberated Europe; Stalin cited the Percentages Agreement and his need for "friendly" governments, and in April 1945 Stalin told the Yugoslav Communist Milovan Djilas (in a conversation recalled in Djilas's memoir of 1962) that whoever occupies a territory imposes on it his own social system.

**Roosevelt's death and Truman.** Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945. His Vice-President, Harry S. Truman, a former senator from Missouri, had been told almost nothing about foreign policy or the atomic bomb. Advisers such as Averell Harriman (ambassador in Moscow) and Admiral William Leahy urged him to be firm. On 23 April, in his first meeting with the Soviet foreign minister Vyacheslav Molotov, Truman insisted (in his own account) that the USSR "carry out" its agreements on Poland. On 8 May the war in Europe ended. On 21 August, a few days after Japan's surrender announcement, Truman ended Lend-Lease, which Soviet leaders took as a sign of American coldness; American officials said that the law required it.

**Potsdam, 17 July – 2 August 1945.** The last meeting of the Big Three took place in the ruins of Germany, in a suburb of Berlin. The Western leaders were Truman, and Churchill, who was replaced halfway through by the new Labour Prime Minister Clement Attlee after the British general election of 5 July (counted on 26 July), with Ernest Bevin as Foreign Secretary. The conference agreed:

- Germany would be treated as a single economic unit and governed by an Allied Control Council in Berlin; the "four Ds" were demilitarisation, denazification, democratisation and decartelisation (the breaking up of the great industrial combines).
- **Reparations**: each power would take reparations from its own zone. The Soviet Union could also take, from the western zones, 10 per cent of industrial equipment not needed for the German peace economy as a free gift, and a further 15 per cent in exchange for food and raw materials from the east. The aim was to avoid a Soviet claim on the whole of Germany and to keep the western zones from being drained.
- **Poland's western frontier**: Poland would administer German land up to the line of the rivers Oder and Neisse, "pending the final determination" at a peace conference. In practice it became permanent, and millions of Germans were expelled from Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary (the Potsdam text called for transfers "in an orderly and humane manner").
- A **Council of Foreign Ministers** would prepare the peace treaties.

On 16 July, the day before the conference opened, the first atomic bomb was tested in New Mexico. On 24 July Truman told Stalin, in an offhand way, that the United States had "a new weapon of unusual destructive force". Stalin showed little reaction; Truman and Churchill thought he had not understood. In fact Soviet physicists had been working on the problem since 1943, and Stalin's spies had told him of the Manhattan Project.

:::def Atomic diplomacy
The use of the American monopoly of the atomic bomb (1945–49) as a means of influencing Soviet behaviour, without necessarily using the bomb. Gar Alperovitz's *Atomic Diplomacy* (1965) argued that Truman delayed Potsdam and used the bomb on Japan partly to impress the USSR. Other historians doubt it and point out that the atomic bomb's use was decided by the need to end the war with Japan. The word "diplomacy" in the label is the claim, not a neutral description.
:::

**Hiroshima and after.** The bombs fell on 6 and 9 August. The USSR, in line with Yalta, declared war on Japan on 8 August and invaded Manchuria. For the traditionalist, the bomb saved lives and was not directed at Russia. For the revisionist Alperovitz, Japan was already near defeat and the bomb was aimed partly at Moscow. For Stalin, whatever the American intention, the bomb meant that American power was now greater than Soviet power in a way the Red Army could not match. On 20 August 1945 he put the secret police chief Lavrentiy Beria in charge of a Special Committee to build a Soviet bomb. The first Soviet test took place on 29 August 1949.

:::key Yalta to Potsdam: what it means for blame
- **Soviet moves** (Romania in March 1945, the trial of the sixteen Poles in June) are the traditionalist's evidence of bad faith; the revisionist answers that Stalin treated Eastern Europe as the Western powers treated Italy, Greece and Japan, where they alone took decisions.
- **Truman's change of tone** (23 April, the end of Lend-Lease on 21 August) is evidence for revisionists that the US abandoned Roosevelt's cooperative approach; traditionalists say that Truman was responding to Soviet actions.
- **The bomb** is the revisionist's best-known piece of evidence. The test of 16 July, the notice to Stalin on 24 July and the bombing of 6 and 9 August all fall within a month.
:::

## The problems of peace-making, 1945–46

After Potsdam no peace treaty was signed with Germany, and the wartime partners found that each new meeting made things worse.

**The Council of Foreign Ministers.** In London (11 September – 2 October 1945) the ministers broke up over Soviet claims in Japan and the character of the governments in Romania and Bulgaria. In Moscow in December 1945 the American Secretary of State James Byrnes secured a compromise: a few non-Communists were to be added to the Romanian and Bulgarian governments, which Western critics called a fig leaf. The treaties with Italy, Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria and Finland were drawn up at the Paris Peace Conference of 1946 and signed on 10 February 1947. None was made with Germany.

**Germany.** Under the Potsdam rules Germany was to be run as one economy, but the four zones drifted apart. France had not been at Potsdam and vetoed central German administrations; the Soviet Union dismantled factories and took goods from its zone and demanded a share of the Ruhr; and Britain and the USA, who had to feed their zones (their food and coal were short in the winter of 1946–47), came to see reparations as a burden. In May 1946 the American military governor, General Lucius Clay, stopped deliveries of reparations from the US zone to the USSR until the Soviet Union agreed to treat Germany as a unit. On 6 September 1946, in a speech at Stuttgart, Byrnes said that the United States would keep its forces in Germany as long as other occupation forces stayed, that the Germans should be given back responsibility for their own government, and that the Oder–Neisse frontier was not final. Many Germans, and the Soviet government, heard it as a signal that the west meant to rebuild western Germany. In the Soviet zone the Communist and Social Democratic parties were merged into the Socialist Unity Party (SED) in April 1946 under Soviet pressure.

**Poland and Eastern Europe.** The elections that were meant to be "free" produced the results Moscow wanted, as the section on Sovietisation below shows.

**Iran, 1946.** British and Soviet troops had occupied Iran in 1941. Under a treaty of 1942 both were to leave within six months of the war's end, that is by 2 March 1946. The Soviet troops stayed, and supported a separatist Azerbaijan government in the north, while the USSR demanded an oil concession. Iran complained to the new United Nations Security Council and the USA protested strongly. In late March the USSR announced that it would withdraw, and an agreement with the Iranian Prime Minister, Ahmad Qavam, promised a joint Soviet–Iranian oil company; the troops left by May, and the Iranian parliament later refused the oil agreement. Traditionalists treat Iran as an early test in which Soviet expansion was stopped by firmness; revisionists note that the USSR did leave and that it had security interests on its border and oil on the Soviet side.

**Turkey.** In 1945–46 the Soviet Union asked Turkey for a revision of the Montreux Convention of 1936 on the Straits, for bases there, and for the return of the provinces of Kars and Ardahan. In a note of 7 August 1946 Moscow proposed joint Soviet–Turkish control of the Straits. The United States sent warships to the Mediterranean (the battleship *Missouri* visited Istanbul in April 1946) and told Turkey to resist. The crisis eased, but it left the American officials who later drafted the Truman Doctrine convinced that Soviet pressure on Turkey was real.

**The Baruch Plan.** On 14 June 1946 Bernard Baruch put to the United Nations a plan for an international authority to control atomic energy, with inspection and no veto on punishment. The United States would keep its bombs until the system worked. The Soviet delegate Andrei Gromyko replied with a counter-proposal to ban atomic weapons and destroy the existing ones first. Neither was accepted, and the arms race began.

:::key Peace-making problems: what each school makes of them
- **Traditionalist**: Iran, Turkey and Poland form a pattern of Soviet pressure on weaker neighbours, resisted only when the West stood firm.
- **Revisionist**: American demands for a free Germany and open markets, the end of reparations from the western zones and the Baruch Plan (which kept the American monopoly) show an American effort to reshape the settlement on American terms.
- **Post-revisionist**: in each case both sides misread the other's motives and took the worst view; Soviet pressure on Turkey and American economic policy in Germany were both partly defensive.
:::

## 1946: the telegrams and the speeches

Within a few months of each other, three documents, and a series of speeches, turned the quarrels into a theory.

**Stalin's speech, 9 February 1946.** In an election speech at the Bolshoi Theatre, Stalin said that capitalism, by its nature, produced crises and wars, and called for three more Five-Year Plans to rebuild and strengthen the country. Western listeners heard it as a declaration of hostility. Soviet historians, and some others since, point out that it was an election speech to a domestic audience about reconstruction.

**The Long Telegram, 22 February 1946.** The US Treasury asked the embassy in Moscow why the Soviet Union would not join the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank. George Kennan, the chargé d'affaires, replied with an analysis so long that it became known as the Long Telegram. He argued that Soviet policy came from "a traditional and instinctive Russian sense of insecurity", which the Communist leaders used for their own purposes; that they needed an outside enemy to justify their rule; that they believed there could be "no permanent modus vivendi" with the capitalist West; and that Soviet power was "impervious to logic of reason" but "highly sensitive to logic of force". The telegram proposed no war, but patient, firm resistance and the strengthening of the West. In July 1947 Kennan published an article in *Foreign Affairs* under the signature "X", which gave the policy its name: **containment**. He later complained that his words had been taken as a call for military confrontation, when he had meant a political and economic one.

:::def Containment
The American policy, formulated by George Kennan in 1946–47, of resisting the expansion of Soviet power by firm and patient counter-pressure at a series of points, without attacking the Soviet Union. It became the guiding idea of American policy in the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan. Historians disagree over whether it was a defensive and necessary response (traditionalists) or the beginning of an American attempt to dominate (revisionists).
:::

**The Iron Curtain speech, 5 March 1946.** Winston Churchill, then Leader of the Opposition, spoke at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, with President Truman on the platform. He said: "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." Behind it, he said, were the capitals of Eastern Europe, and "Communist fifth columns" (secret supporters of an enemy inside a country) worked in other countries. He called for a "fraternal association of the English-speaking peoples". Stalin, in an interview in *Pravda* on 14 March, compared Churchill to Hitler and called the speech a call for war. The speech was Churchill's own, but it came when Truman was ready to hear it.

:::def Iron Curtain
Churchill's phrase of 5 March 1946 for the division of Europe between the states under Soviet control and those outside it, running "from Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic". It became the common name for the border between East and West Europe until 1989.
:::

**Wallace and the Clifford–Elsey Report.** On 12 September 1946 Henry Wallace, the Secretary of Commerce, gave a speech in New York saying that the United States should accept a Soviet sphere in Eastern Europe and should not follow a policy of "getting tough", which he thought would lead to war; Truman dismissed him on 20 September. The same month Truman's counsel Clark Clifford and his assistant George Elsey delivered a secret report to the President which argued that the Soviet leaders aimed at world domination, that Moscow understood only strength, and that the United States must keep military superiority. Truman kept it locked up. Wallace's fall shows that, by late 1946, dissent from the "firm" line was no longer acceptable in Washington.

**The Novikov Telegram, 27 September 1946.** The Soviet ambassador in Washington, Nikolai Novikov, wrote at Molotov's request a report on American policy. He described the foreign policy of the United States as "characterized in the postwar period by a striving for world supremacy", driven, he said, by American monopoly capital, and he claimed that the USA was preparing for another war, against the Soviet Union, which in the eyes of the American imperialists was "the main obstacle" in its way. It is sometimes called the Soviet "Long Telegram". Molotov underlined parts of the copy. Historians disagree on how much it shows what Moscow really believed about the West (revisionists, and since 1991 many others, use it as evidence of real Soviet fear) and how much it shows what an ambassador thought his masters wanted to read.

**Three documents of 1946, side by side**

| Document | Author | Message | Used by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Long Telegram, 22 February | George Kennan, US chargé in Moscow | Soviet policy is driven by insecurity and ideology, and only firmness will stop it | Traditionalists (a fair warning); revisionists (it justified a hard line the US wanted) |
| Fulton speech, 5 March | Churchill | An iron curtain has fallen | Traditionalists (a true picture); Soviet writers (a call to war) |
| Novikov telegram, 27 September | Nikolai Novikov, Soviet ambassador in Washington | US policy aims at world supremacy | Revisionists and post-revisionists (Soviet fear was sincere); traditionalists (Soviet propaganda) |

## The Truman Doctrine, 12 March 1947

**Greece and Turkey.** Greece had been in civil war since 1946 between the government, supported by Britain, and Communist-led guerrillas, who received help from Yugoslavia, Albania and Bulgaria (Stalin himself, under the Percentages Agreement, gave little). Britain, almost bankrupt after the harsh winter of 1946–47, told Washington on 21 February 1947 that it could no longer support Greece and Turkey after 31 March. Dean Acheson, then Under-Secretary of State, warned Congressional leaders on 27 February that, if Greece fell, the infection might spread to the whole of the Near East and Europe. On 12 March Truman addressed Congress and asked for 400 million dollars of aid.

He said: "At the present moment in world history nearly every nation must choose between alternative ways of life." He added that "it must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures". Congress approved the aid in May 1947.

:::def Truman Doctrine
The American commitment, stated by Truman to Congress on 12 March 1947, to support free peoples resisting subjugation by armed minorities or outside pressure; it began with 400 million dollars of aid to Greece and Turkey. Traditionalists treat it as the necessary response to Soviet pressure; revisionists as the point at which the US declared a worldwide crusade and so made the Cold War ideological.
:::

**What was new.** The Doctrine's language was general, though the aid was for two countries. It divided the world into two ways of life, and committed the USA to act in a region where Britain had been the main power. Some historians (post-revisionists among them) add that Truman needed to persuade a Republican-controlled Congress, whose leaders, such as Senator Arthur Vandenberg, were unwilling to vote large sums for foreign aid, and that this is why the language was so strong. Acheson later wrote in his memoirs, *Present at the Creation* (1969), that in explaining policy to the public officials made their points "clearer than truth"; he wrote it about selling Cold War policy in general (the passage is about 1950), but historians quote it to show that officials knew that they were overstating, in 1947 as later. Revisionists use it as proof; traditionalists answer that Congress needed to understand the stakes.

## The Marshall Plan and the Soviet reply, 1947

**The problem.** By spring 1947 western Europe was in a deep crisis: the winter of 1946–47 was the coldest in memory; coal, food and dollars were short; the Communist parties of France and Italy were large (the French Communists won more than a quarter of the votes in 1946). American officials believed that economic despair would lead to political extremism. Kennan's Policy Planning Staff, and Under-Secretary Will Clayton, who had visited Europe, urged large-scale aid.

**The speech.** On 5 June 1947 the Secretary of State, General George C. Marshall, spoke at Harvard. He said that "Our policy is directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos". He invited the European governments to draw up a joint programme, to which the USA would give help, and he did not exclude the Soviet Union.

**The Paris conference.** Britain's foreign secretary Ernest Bevin and France's Georges Bidault called a conference in Paris (27 June – 2 July 1947) and invited Molotov. Molotov came, rejected a joint plan on the ground that it would interfere in national sovereignty and promote Germany's revival, and left on 2 July. Sixteen countries met in Paris from 12 July to draw up the programme. Czechoslovakia and Poland first accepted the invitation, and then withdrew on Stalin's orders (the Czechoslovak government was summoned to Moscow). The Marshall Plan's offer was therefore, in Soviet eyes, an offer that the Soviet bloc could not accept without opening itself to Western influence.

**The Plan in action.** Congress approved the Economic Cooperation Act on 3 April 1948 (it passed shortly after the Czech coup, which helped to win votes). Over four years (1948–52) the USA gave about 13 billion dollars to sixteen countries and western Germany, mostly as grants, through the Economic Cooperation Administration led by Paul Hoffman. The countries formed the Organisation for European Economic Cooperation (OEEC) to share the money. Britain received the most. The aid paid for food, fuel, machinery and raw materials, and, importantly, it was linked to a revival of western Germany.

**The Soviet reply.** The USSR responded in three ways. First, the **Molotov Plan** of July 1947: a series of trade agreements binding Eastern Europe to Moscow, followed by the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (Comecon) in January 1949. Second, the **Cominform**: nine Communist parties (the USSR, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Poland, Romania, Yugoslavia, and the French and Italian parties) met at Szklarska Poręba, Poland, on 22–27 September 1947. Andrei Zhdanov, Stalin's ideological chief, said that the world was divided into "two camps", "the imperialist and anti-democratic camp" led by the USA and "the anti-imperialist and democratic camp" led by the USSR. Third, the Communist parties of western Europe, especially in France and Italy, were told to oppose the Marshall Plan; strikes followed in France in November–December 1947.

:::def Marshall Plan (European Recovery Programme)
The American programme of economic aid to western Europe, 1948–52, proposed by Secretary of State George C. Marshall on 5 June 1947 and authorised on 3 April 1948: about 13 billion dollars to sixteen countries and western Germany. Traditionalists see an act of generosity and a defence against Communism; revisionists see an instrument for opening European markets to American goods and for tying Europe to the United States; post-revisionists add that the Europeans had asked for it, and that Soviet refusal was a choice.
:::

:::def Cominform
The Communist Information Bureau, founded in September 1947 by nine Communist parties to coordinate their policies under Soviet leadership. Zhdanov's speech at its first meeting stated the "two camps" view. It expelled Yugoslavia on 28 June 1948 after Tito refused to accept Stalin's control.
:::

:::key The Marshall Plan: what it means for blame
- **Traditionalist**: the offer was open to all; Stalin refused it for himself and forced others to refuse because he feared Western influence, and so split Europe.
- **Revisionist**: the American conditions (open markets, the revival of German industry, European integration) and the commercial interests of American exporters made the offer one Stalin could not accept; the Plan aimed to bring Europe into the American economic system, and its rejection was foreseeable.
- **Post-revisionist**: both sides were afraid of what the other would do with Germany. Economic historians such as Alan Milward argue that the Plan was less important to Europe's recovery than has been claimed, while others stress its political effects.
:::

## The Sovietisation of Eastern Europe, 1945–48

Between 1945 and 1948 the states of Eastern Europe moved, at different speeds, from coalition governments to one-party rule.

:::def Sovietisation
The process by which the Communist parties, supported by the Red Army and the Soviet secret police, took control of the governments, economies and societies of the states of Eastern Europe and made them follow Soviet models. Historians use the word for the years 1945–48 and for the satellite states after 1948.
:::

:::def Salami tactics
The method, named by the Hungarian Communist leader Mátyás Rákosi, of removing opposition parties slice by slice, by arrests, accusations of fascism, forced mergers and control of the police, so that no single step looked decisive.
:::

| Country | Path to Communist rule | Key dates |
|---|---|---|
| Poland | Lublin Poles recognised by Moscow; Communist control of the police; 1947 election, claimed by the Communist-led bloc with about 80 per cent of the vote, widely held to be falsified; Mikołajczyk fled in October 1947 | June 1945 coalition; 19 January 1947 election |
| Romania | Groza government imposed in March 1945 after Vyshinsky's ultimatum; rigged election November 1946; King Michael forced to abdicate | 6 March 1945; 30 December 1947 |
| Bulgaria | Communist-led Fatherland Front from September 1944; opposition boycotted the election of November 1945; opposition leader Nikola Petkov executed | 23 September 1947 (Petkov) |
| Hungary | Free election of November 1945: Smallholders 57 per cent, Communists about 17 per cent; coalition, but the Communists held the police; Rákosi's salami tactics; a rigged election in August 1947; Social Democrats absorbed in 1948 | 4 November 1945; August 1947 |
| Czechoslovakia | Free election May 1946: Communists 38 per cent, the largest party; Gottwald Prime Minister of a coalition; coup February 1948 | 20–25 February 1948 |
| Yugoslavia and Albania | Communist governments from their own partisan victories; Tito, not Stalin's appointee, led Yugoslavia and broke with Moscow | 28 June 1948 (expulsion from the Cominform) |

**Czechoslovakia, February 1948.** Czechoslovakia was the democracy that most Westerners trusted. Its Communists were genuinely popular, the army and police were partly under their control, and Edvard Beneš was President. On 20 February 1948 twelve non-Communist ministers resigned in protest at the Communist Interior Minister's replacement of police officers, expecting Beneš to refuse and force new elections. Gottwald, backed by armed workers' militias, a general strike threat and Soviet Deputy Foreign Minister Valerian Zorin's presence in Prague, demanded a new government; on 25 February Beneš accepted. On 10 March the Foreign Minister Jan Masaryk, the only prominent non-Communist who remained in office, was found dead below the window of his flat; whether he fell, jumped or was pushed is still disputed, and a Czech inquiry of 2004 judged it was murder. Western opinion was shocked, and the coup speeded up the Brussels Treaty, the passing of the Marshall Plan and the planning of NATO.

**Yugoslavia, 1948.** Tito was the Communist leader who had liberated his country largely by himself and refused to be Stalin's subordinate. The Cominform expelled him on 28 June 1948. Yugoslavia stayed Communist and independent. It is an important reminder that "the Communist bloc" was not a single will; post-revisionists and the New Cold War historians use it to show the limits of Stalin's control, and also the limits of what the West thought it understood.

:::key Sovietisation: what it means for blame
- **Traditionalist**: this was the pattern of Soviet expansion in action, step by step, and the Czech coup proved it.
- **Revisionist**: the USSR had a legitimate interest in friendly neighbours after being invaded twice in thirty years; Stalin moved slowly and took harder lines only after the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan (the timing, they say, is the evidence).
- **Post-revisionist and New Cold War history**: Stalin intended from 1944–45 to control the region but allowed coalitions as long as he could; the hardening of 1947–48 came from the interaction of fear (of Western economic power and of the German question) and ideology. The Soviet archives show that Moscow guided the process closely.
:::

## Germany divided and the Berlin Blockade, 1947–49

**Why Germany mattered.** Germany was the biggest economy in Europe, and neither side could risk the other controlling all of it. By 1947 the quarrel over reparations and unity had become a quarrel over the shape of a western German state.

**Bizonia and the London Conference.** On 1 January 1947 the British and American zones merged economically (Bizonia). In the Moscow conference of March–April 1947 the Foreign Ministers failed to agree on Germany. In February to June 1948 the three western powers and the Benelux states met in London and decided to bring the French zone into a combined area and to set up a West German government. On 20 March 1948 the Soviet representative, Marshal Sokolovsky, walked out of the Allied Control Council, which ended four-power rule of Germany.

**Currency reform.** The western zones had no stable currency; the Reichsmark was almost worthless, and goods were hoarded or traded on the black market. On 20 June 1948 the western powers introduced the Deutsche Mark in their zones. The Soviet side saw this as a decisive step in creating a separate West German state, banned the new money from its zone, and issued its own. On 23 June the Deutsche Mark was extended to West Berlin, and the Soviet authorities saw their position in the city threatened.

**The Blockade.** On 24 June 1948 Soviet forces stopped all road, rail and barge traffic between the western zones and Berlin, and cut the electricity supplied from the east. About two million people lived in the three western sectors, and the city had food and coal for a few weeks. The Western powers had three choices: withdraw from Berlin, force a convoy through the Soviet zone (General Clay wanted this), or supply the city by air. Truman, against the advice of some of his generals, refused to withdraw and chose the Airlift, believing that it avoided firing the first shot. The American operation was called Operation Vittles and began on 26 June; the British operation, Plainfare, followed within days. At first it delivered a few hundred tons a day; with more aircraft, three airfields in the western sectors (Tempelhof, Gatow and a new one at Tegel built in 1948), better organisation and the winter's demand for coal, it grew until on 15–16 April 1949 (the "Easter Parade") over 1,300 flights delivered nearly 13,000 tons in 24 hours. In all the Airlift carried about 2.3 million tons in some 278,000 flights, about three-quarters of the tonnage by American aircraft. The Soviet side did not shoot down an aircraft. The West also stopped exports from the western zones to the east.

The Western powers also sent American B-29 bombers to Britain in July 1948, which Moscow could read as a nuclear signal. The people of West Berlin, led by Ernst Reuter, who became Mayor after elections in December 1948, were seen as heroes of freedom; in the same months the city's government split in two, and East Berlin became the capital of the Soviet zone.

:::def Berlin Blockade and Airlift
The Soviet closure of land and water routes between the western zones and West Berlin from 24 June 1948 to 12 May 1949 (322 days), and the Western air supply of the city, which continued until 30 September 1949. Traditionalists see an attempt by Stalin to force the West out of Berlin; revisionists see a reaction to the creation of a West German state; post-revisionists see an escalation neither side had planned.
:::

**The end of the Blockade.** In the spring of 1949 the Blockade was failing: the Airlift was supplying the city and the Western counter-blockade was hurting the East. After secret talks at the United Nations between the American diplomat Philip Jessup and the Soviet ambassador Jacob Malik, the Soviet Union lifted the Blockade at one minute past midnight on 12 May 1949.

**Two Germanys.** The Parliamentary Council in Bonn, elected by the western state parliaments, drew up the Basic Law, which came into force on 23 May 1949 and created the Federal Republic of Germany. The first Bundestag election took place on 14 August, and Konrad Adenauer became Chancellor on 15 September. On 7 October 1949 the Soviet zone became the German Democratic Republic, with Wilhelm Pieck as President. The division, which neither side had planned in 1945, was complete.

:::key The Berlin Blockade: what it means for blame
- **Traditionalist**: Stalin tried to take West Berlin by pressure; the West stood firm without war.
- **Revisionist**: the currency reform and the plan for a West German state broke the Potsdam agreement; Stalin's response was defensive and limited, and he did not attack the aircraft.
- **Post-revisionist and New Cold War history**: Stalin wanted to stop the creation of a West German state, not to start a war; he miscalculated, and the Blockade achieved the opposite of its aim by speeding up the creation of the Federal Republic and NATO.
:::

## NATO and the end of the monopoly, 1948–49

**The Brussels Treaty.** After the Czech coup and Soviet pressure on Norway and Finland in early 1948, Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands and Luxembourg signed the Brussels Treaty on 17 March 1948, a fifty-year pact of mutual defence. Bevin had proposed a "Western Union" in January 1948 and hoped that the Americans would support it.

**The North Atlantic Treaty.** On 11 June 1948 the US Senate passed the Vandenberg Resolution, which allowed the USA to join regional defence agreements outside the Western Hemisphere. Talks during the Blockade ended in the North Atlantic Treaty, signed in Washington on 4 April 1949 by twelve states: the USA, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Italy, Portugal, Norway, Denmark and Iceland. Its heart was Article 5: "an armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all". Each member would take "such action as it deems necessary, including the use of armed force". It was the first peacetime alliance with European states that the USA had joined since the alliance with France in 1778, and it came into force on 24 August 1949. Greece and Turkey joined in 1952 and West Germany in 1955; the Soviet bloc's Warsaw Pact followed in 1955, which is after the period for this chapter.

**Soviet atomic bomb.** On 29 August 1949 the Soviet Union tested an atomic bomb at Semipalatinsk. American aircraft detected radioactive debris in the atmosphere in early September, and Truman announced the news on 23 September. The American monopoly had lasted just over four years.

**The state of Europe in 1949.** By the end of the year there were two German states, two military and economic blocs, and a frontier from the Baltic to the Adriatic defended on both sides. Armies had been reduced since 1945: the Soviet armed forces fell from about 11 million men to under 3 million by 1948, but still outnumbered Western forces on the continent, while American troops in Europe had fallen from over 3 million at VE Day to a few hundred thousand. Each side saw the other's forces as a threat.

:::key NATO: what it means for blame
- **Traditionalist**: a defensive pact, requested by Europeans who feared the Red Army after Prague and Berlin.
- **Revisionist**: American military commitment in Europe made a division permanent, and it ended any chance of compromise on a neutral Germany.
- **Post-revisionist**: the Europeans pulled the Americans in (an "empire by invitation"); the Soviets saw encirclement; each side's defensive step increased the other's fear (the security dilemma).
:::

## The documents historians argue over

Paper 3 extracts often rest on a small number of famous documents. You do not need to quote them, but you need to know what each is, who made it, and what each school makes of it. Here are ten, each with its provenance in the board's style. None is reproduced at length: where a few words are quoted, they are the document's own words in an English edition.

**1. The Percentages Agreement.** *Winston Churchill's account of his meeting with Stalin in Moscow on 9 October 1944, in* Triumph and Tragedy *(1953).* Churchill describes writing on a half-sheet of paper the shares of Russian and British "predominance" in Romania, Greece, Yugoslavia, Hungary and Bulgaria, and Stalin ticking it. *Use:* shows spheres of influence were accepted by Britain first (revisionists; some post-revisionists); *limit:* the account is Churchill's, written nine years later, and the figures are not a treaty.

**2. The Declaration on Liberated Europe.** *Part of the Report of the Crimea Conference, 11 February 1945; text in the Avalon Project.* The leaders affirm "the right of all people to choose the form of government under which they will live" and promise "free elections of Governments responsive to the will of the people". *Use:* traditionalists cite it as a promise Stalin broke; revisionists note its vagueness; the Soviet side later pointed to Western rule in Italy and Greece.

**3. Stalin to Djilas, 1945.** *Milovan Djilas,* Conversations with Stalin *(1962), recalling a conversation in Moscow in April 1945.* Stalin is recalled as saying that whoever occupies a territory imposes on it his own social system. *Use:* traditionalists cite it as a statement of Soviet aim to impose communism by force; others say it also describes what the West did in its zones. *Limit:* a memoir, written from memory seventeen years later by a man who later broke with Stalin's heirs.

**4. The Long Telegram.** *George Kennan, telegram from the US embassy in Moscow to the Secretary of State, 22 February 1946; text in the National Security Archive.* Soviet policy comes from "a traditional and instinctive Russian sense of insecurity"; the Soviet leaders believe there can be "no permanent modus vivendi" with the capitalist world. *Use:* a traditionalist reads a clear warning; a revisionist reads the document that made American hostility respectable; a post-revisionist notes that "insecurity" is an interpretation which also fits Soviet fear.

**5. The Fulton speech.** *Winston Churchill, address at Westminster College, Fulton, Missouri, 5 March 1946; text in the Fordham Internet Modern History Sourcebook.* "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic an iron curtain has descended across the Continent." *Use:* the Western picture of a divided Europe; Stalin's reply in *Pravda* (14 March 1946) gives the Soviet reading of the speech as a call to war. *Limit:* Churchill was not in office.

**6. The Novikov telegram.** *Nikolai Novikov, Soviet ambassador in Washington, telegram to Molotov, 27 September 1946, with Molotov's underlining; English translation published in Kenneth M. Jensen (ed.),* Origins of the Cold War *(US Institute of Peace).* US foreign policy is "characterized in the postwar period by a striving for world supremacy". *Use:* the Soviet mirror of the Long Telegram; shows what Soviet officials believed or told their masters. *Limit:* an ambassador writing to be read by Molotov; the translations differ in places.

**7. The Truman Doctrine speech.** *Harry S. Truman, address to a joint session of Congress, 12 March 1947; text in the Avalon Project.* "It must be the policy of the United States to support free peoples who are resisting attempted subjugation by armed minorities or by outside pressures." *Use:* traditionalists see a necessary defence; revisionists see the universal commitment that made the Cold War ideological. *Limit:* a speech to win a vote in Congress.

**8. The Marshall Plan speech.** *George C. Marshall, address at Harvard University, 5 June 1947.* "Our policy is directed not against any country or doctrine but against hunger, poverty, desperation and chaos." *Use:* the generosity case; revisionists put it beside the Plan's actual terms. *Limit:* the speech was short and general; the actual programme was drawn up by the Europeans and the Economic Cooperation Act.

**9. Zhdanov's report to the Cominform.** *Andrei Zhdanov, report on the international situation, Szklarska Poręba, 22 September 1947; English translation in the Michigan State University* Seventeen Moments in Soviet History *collection.* The world is divided into "the imperialist and anti-democratic camp" and "the anti-imperialist and democratic camp". *Use:* traditionalists cite it as the Soviet declaration of a Cold War; revisionists and post-revisionists note that it came after the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan, as a reply. *Limit:* a party speech for a Communist audience.

**10. The North Atlantic Treaty, Article 5.** *North Atlantic Treaty, signed in Washington, 4 April 1949; text in the Avalon Project.* "An armed attack against one or more of them in Europe or North America shall be considered an attack against them all." *Use:* the Western view of NATO as defensive; the Soviet view of it as an alliance against the USSR; neither can be proved from the treaty text alone.

:::key How to use a document in an answer
Say **what it is** (nature and origin), **what it shows** (the fact), and **what a historian does with it** (the interpretation). Then say what limits it: whose record, written when, for whom. This is the same skill as Paper 1, used here to explain an *argument* rather than to prove a fact.
:::

## Tagging the events: who uses what

Historians seldom disagree about most of the facts; they disagree about which facts matter and what they show. The table below tags the main events. It is simplified: individual historians do not always fit their school.

| Event or evidence | Traditionalist | Revisionist | Post-revisionist and New Cold War history |
|---|---|---|---|
| Second front delay, 1942–44 | Technical difficulties; Stalin's suspicions were baseless | Evidence of Western willingness to let USSR bear the burden | Both sides' mistrust grew from the delay |
| Percentages Agreement, Oct 1944 | Churchill tried to limit Soviet gains and failed | Britain accepted spheres first | Spheres of influence were a shared habit; the later quarrel was over their limits |
| Yalta, Feb 1945 | Stalin broke his promises on free elections | The high point of cooperation; the West read promises too widely | An ambiguous compromise; each read it in its own favour |
| Soviet actions in Romania and Poland, Mar–Jun 1945 | The start of a pattern of expansion | A reasonable security zone; the West did the same in Italy and Greece | Stalin wanted control but moved cautiously; his methods cost him Western trust |
| Truman's tone of 23 April; Lend-Lease ends, 21 August 1945 | Response to Soviet violations | A break with Roosevelt's cooperation | Both leaders were inexperienced; each misread the other |
| Atomic bomb, 1945–49 | Saved lives, ended the war; no threat to USSR | Used partly to impress Moscow; atomic diplomacy | Fear on both sides; the bomb raised Soviet insecurity |
| Iran and Turkey, 1946 | Soviet probing, stopped by firmness | Soviet security concerns on the border | Both Soviet pressure and American alarm were real |
| Long Telegram, Fulton, 1946 | Accurate analysis | Created a hostile image | Kennan and Churchill expressed a view already forming in Washington |
| Novikov telegram, 1946 | Soviet propaganda | Soviet fears were real | Soviet fear of the USA was sincere; it matches the Western fear |
| Truman Doctrine, 1947 | Necessary response | A global ideological crusade | Overstated to win Congress; partly defensive |
| Marshall Plan and the Soviet reply, 1947 | Generous; Stalin refused | Economic expansion; Stalin could not accept | Mutual fear over Germany; the Plan was welcomed by the Europeans |
| Sovietisation, 1945–48 | Soviet expansion | Defensive security zone, hardened after Marshall | Stalin's control tightened as fear grew; the Czech coup was not inevitable |
| Berlin Blockade, 1948–49 | Stalin tried to force the West out | A reply to Western creation of West Germany | An escalation neither side planned; it speeded up NATO and the FRG |
| NATO, 1949 | Defensive | Made division permanent | Europeans invited it; Soviet fear of encirclement |

## Using this knowledge on Paper 3

A good knowledge paragraph is built on a claim in the extract. In practice this means one of four moves for each fact above.

:::key Four moves for knowledge in Section C
1. **Name the event and its date**, exactly: "On 12 March 1947 Truman asked Congress for 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey."
2. **Say what the extract claims it shows**: "The historian reads this as the moment at which the United States committed itself to a global struggle."
3. **Test it with a second fact**: "But the aid was for two countries; Britain had withdrawn, and Stalin was not supporting the Greek guerrillas."
4. **Say what the choice reveals about the approach**: "By stressing the speech's language and not its limited aim, the extract shows a historian interested in ideology and in the intentions of American leaders."
:::

:::eg Table B: a claim about Yalta tested with knowledge, level by level
**The claim (written for practice; not a historian's words).** "At Yalta Stalin gained a free hand in Eastern Europe."

**What the levels want (Table B).** Level 3 (9–11) is generally accurate and relevant; Level 4 (12–14) is mostly relevant and accurate; Level 6 (18–20) is detailed, accurate and entirely relevant: every fact is doing a job.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Yalta was a conference in February 1945. Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin met and agreed about Germany and Poland. Stalin wanted Poland and Eastern Europe. The Cold War started later, because Stalin took over Eastern Europe. So Stalin gained a free hand at Yalta."

*Commentary.* Accurate in outline and about the right topic, but it has no dates or details, and its last sentence repeats the claim. Level 3.

**A Level 4 paragraph (adds explanation).** "At Yalta on 4–11 February 1945 the Big Three agreed that Poland's eastern frontier would follow the Curzon Line and that its government would be 'reorganized on a broader democratic basis'. The Red Army already occupied Poland, so Stalin held the ground, and the wording on free elections was vague. This supports the claim, because it left Stalin to decide what 'broader' meant."

*Commentary.* Specific and relevant; each fact explains why the claim might be true. But the paragraph only supports the claim and does not test it.

**A Level 6 paragraph (explains, tests and links to the approach).** "The claim is partly supported. At Yalta the Big Three accepted that Poland's eastern frontier would follow the Curzon Line and that the Lublin government would be 'reorganized on a broader democratic basis'. Since the Red Army already held Poland, Romania, Bulgaria and most of Hungary, the conference could not remove Soviet power from those states, and Stalin was able to treat 'broader' as meaning a few additions. But the Declaration on Liberated Europe committed him to 'free elections of Governments responsive to the will of the people', and Roosevelt could and did claim that the Soviet side had agreed to something it then broke: Romania in March 1945 and the trial of sixteen Polish leaders in June are the evidence. The claim therefore holds if 'free hand' means that the West accepted Soviet military control, but not if it means that Stalin was given a licence to impose Communist governments. A historian who writes in this way is concerned with what Stalin gained and not with what the West tried to restrain, which suggests an approach centred on Soviet aims."

*Commentary.* Every sentence of knowledge does a job: the dates and the Curzon Line explain; the Red Army's position and the Declaration test; the Romanian coup and the trial test again; and the last sentence says what the choice of facts reveals about the approach. Level 6.
:::

:::eg Explain the significance of the Marshall Plan for the origins of the Cold War
**What the levels want.** A short significance answer scores on knowledge and on explaining what the fact changed. A good one says what the event was, what it changed, and for whom.

**Answer (about 190 words).** "The Marshall Plan, proposed by the Secretary of State George Marshall on 5 June 1947 and enacted on 3 April 1948, offered American economic aid to Europe, about 13 billion dollars over four years. It mattered in three ways. First, it divided Europe. The offer was open to all, but when Molotov left the Paris conference on 2 July 1947, and Stalin made Czechoslovakia and Poland withdraw, the continent split into those who took American money and those who did not. Second, it prompted the Soviet reply: the Molotov Plan and Comecon, and the Cominform in September 1947, where Zhdanov spoke of two camps. Third, it was linked to the revival of western Germany, which the USSR saw as a threat, and so led to the Berlin crisis of 1948. Historians differ on its purpose: traditionalists see generosity and a defence against Communism; revisionists see an instrument of American economic expansion; others point out that sixteen governments asked for it. Whichever is right, the Plan hardened the division of Europe, which is why it figures in all three schools' accounts."

*Commentary.* Names the date, the sum and the people, explains three consequences with evidence and links the answer to the debate.
:::

:::eg Which events would a revisionist emphasise, and which a traditionalist?
**Question.** Name four events that a traditionalist would emphasise as showing Soviet responsibility, and four that a revisionist would emphasise as showing American responsibility. Explain each choice in a sentence.

**Answer.**
- *Traditionalist (Soviet responsibility):* (1) the Soviet-imposed Groza government in Romania (March 1945) and the trial of sixteen Poles (June 1945), which broke the Declaration on Liberated Europe; (2) the Soviet troops that stayed in Iran after 2 March 1946; (3) the Czech coup of February 1948; (4) the Berlin Blockade of 24 June 1948, which tried to force the West out of the city.
- *Revisionist (American responsibility):* (1) the use of the atomic bombs in August 1945 and Truman's tougher tone from April, which they read as pressure on Moscow; (2) the ending of Lend-Lease on 21 August 1945 and the refusal of a loan to the USSR; (3) the Truman Doctrine of March 1947, which turned a regional matter into a world crusade; (4) the Marshall Plan and the currency reform of June 1948, which they read as the creation of a western economic bloc and a separate German state.
- *Why:* Each school picks the events that fit its central question: those who ask "who broke the agreements of 1945?" look at Soviet acts in Eastern Europe; those who ask "who changed the rules?" look at American economic power and the bomb.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
An extract argues that Stalin's policy in Eastern Europe was driven by security. A student wrote:

"Stalin wanted security in Eastern Europe so he took over Romania, Poland, Hungary, Bulgaria and Czechoslovakia. The Soviet Union had lost 27 million people in the war. Then Truman introduced the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan. This shows that Stalin was not to blame for the Cold War because he was only defending himself."

**The level.** Table B **Level 3**: the facts are accurate (27 million is the usual estimate of Soviet losses) and relevant, but the paragraph lists countries and events with no dates, and its conclusion ("Stalin was not to blame") is the historian's claim restated and not tested. It has the order wrong: the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan (1947) came after Romania (1945) and most of Poland, which matters to the claim. It also says nothing about the Czech coup of 1948, or the elections in Hungary in 1945 which the Communists lost, which test the claim.

**The fix.** "The claim that Stalin's policy was driven by security is supported by the scale of Soviet losses, about 27 million dead, and by the fact that Poland had been the route of invasion in 1914 and 1941. It is also supported by the order of events: Stalin allowed a free election in Hungary in November 1945, in which the Communists won only about 17 per cent, and he took the harsher steps (the Cominform, September 1947; the Czech coup, February 1948) after the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan. But the claim has limits: the Groza government of March 1945 and the trial of the sixteen Poles in June came before any American challenge. A historian who stresses security is asking what Stalin feared and reading Soviet acts as reactions, which shows an approach concerned with Soviet motive rather than with the methods used." This version names the claim, tests it with earlier and later events and says what the emphasis reveals.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What were the Atlantic Charter and Lend-Lease, and what part did each play in the Grand Alliance?
A: The Atlantic Charter (14 August 1941) stated shared war aims: no territorial gains, no frontier changes against peoples' wishes, the right to choose a government. Lend-Lease was American aid in goods, extended to the USSR in November 1941 (about 11 billion dollars in all). Together they gave the alliance its principles and its supplies.
Q: What was agreed at Yalta, 4–11 February 1945, on Germany, Poland and the Far East?
A: Germany: zones for the USA, Britain, the USSR and France, with a four-power Berlin. Poland: the Curzon Line in the east, "accessions in territory" in the north and west, the Lublin government "reorganized on a broader democratic basis" and free elections. Far East: the USSR to enter the war against Japan two or three months after Germany's surrender, for South Sakhalin, the Kuriles and Port Arthur.
Q: What did Potsdam (17 July – 2 August 1945) decide about reparations and Poland's frontier?
A: Each power would take reparations mainly from its own zone, with the USSR taking some equipment from the western zones (10 per cent free, 15 per cent for goods). Poland would administer German land to the Oder–Neisse line pending a peace conference.
Q: Why is 1946 called a turning point in the breakdown of the alliance? Name three documents or speeches.
A: The Long Telegram (22 February, Kennan), the Fulton speech (5 March, Churchill) and the Novikov telegram (27 September) each stated a theory of the other side's hostility. Also the Iran crisis (March–May) and the Stuttgart speech (6 September).
Q: What was the Truman Doctrine, and why did Britain's announcement of 21 February 1947 prompt it?
A: A promise to support free peoples resisting subjugation, with 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey. Britain told the US it could no longer support them after 31 March 1947, so the US took over.
Q: Why did Molotov reject the Marshall Plan, and what did the Soviet Union do instead?
A: He said it would interfere in national sovereignty and revive Germany; the USSR set up the Molotov Plan (trade agreements, then Comecon in January 1949) and the Cominform (September 1947).
Q: Describe the Czech coup of February 1948 in three sentences.
A: On 20 February twelve non-Communist ministers resigned; Gottwald, with armed workers' militias and Soviet pressure, demanded a Communist-led government; Beneš accepted on 25 February. It ended Czechoslovak democracy and shocked the West.
Q: What caused the Berlin Blockade, and how did the West respond?
A: The currency reform of 20 June 1948 and the plan for a West German state; the USSR closed land and water routes from 24 June. The West flew in supplies (Operation Vittles and Plainfare) until the Blockade ended on 12 May 1949.
Q: Name the twelve original members of NATO and state Article 5.
A: USA, Canada, Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Italy, Portugal, Norway, Denmark, Iceland. Article 5: an armed attack on one in Europe or North America is an attack on all.
Q: Put in order: Truman Doctrine; Czech coup; Long Telegram; Marshall's Harvard speech; Berlin Blockade begins; NATO signed.
A: Long Telegram (22 February 1946); Truman Doctrine (12 March 1947); Marshall's speech (5 June 1947); Czech coup (25 February 1948); Blockade begins (24 June 1948); NATO signed (4 April 1949).
:::

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain the significance of the Declaration on Liberated Europe (11 February 1945) for the debate on who was to blame for the Cold War.
A: Indicative content: what it was (a promise by the three powers to help liberated peoples form representative governments and hold free elections; "the right of all people to choose the form of government under which they will live"); what happened (the Groza government in Romania, March 1945; the trial of sixteen Poles, June 1945; rigged elections in Poland in 1947); the debate: traditionalists use it as proof that Stalin broke his word; revisionists point to its vagueness and to Western spheres in Greece and Italy. A top answer explains why the same document is used in opposite ways and what limits it (a diplomatic text with no means of enforcement).
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Long Telegram of 22 February 1946 matters to historians.
A: Indicative content: author and context (Kennan, in reply to a Treasury question); argument (Soviet insecurity and ideology; "no permanent modus vivendi"; "logic of force"); consequence (containment, "X" article in July 1947); the debate (traditionalists treat it as a clear analysis; revisionists as the document that gave a hard line intellectual cover; Kennan later said it was misread as a military call). A top answer links the telegram to the Novikov telegram as its mirror image.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Show how the atomic bomb can be used both to blame the USA and to defend it.
A: Blame: tested on 16 July, mentioned to Stalin on 24 July, used on 6 and 9 August, a monopoly until 29 August 1949; the Baruch Plan; revisionists (Alperovitz) argue the bomb was partly aimed at Moscow. Defend: the decision was military; Truman wanted to end the war; the American monopoly was never used against the USSR, and the USSR began its own programme in 1943 and built the bomb under Beria from August 1945. A top answer says that the same facts support both because the intention cannot be shown from the facts and that is the historians' debate.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Explain how far the Marshall Plan caused the division of Europe.
A: Support: Molotov left Paris on 2 July 1947; Czechoslovakia and Poland were forced to withdraw; the Cominform in September; Comecon in January 1949; Zhdanov's two camps. Limit: the division had begun with Soviet actions in Romania and Poland (1945); the Plan's offer was open; the Soviet Union chose not to take part; Germany's partition was the main reason. A top answer judges that the Plan made the division visible and permanent but did not create it.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Czech coup of February 1948 alarmed the West.
A: It was the last democracy in the east; Masaryk's death on 10 March; the coup occurred without Soviet troops crossing the border, showing how Communists could use legal and extra-legal means; it influenced the Brussels Treaty (17 March), the passing of the Marshall Plan (3 April) and the discussions that led to NATO. A top answer notes that revisionists argue Stalin was reacting to the Marshall Plan.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice; not a historian's words.) "The Berlin Blockade was a Soviet attempt to take over Berlin." Use your knowledge to explain and test this claim.
H: What did the Soviet side do on 24 June 1948, and why?
H: What had the western powers done before it (the London conference, 20 June)?
H: What did the Blockade lead to?
A: Support: the closure of road, rail, barge and electricity; the aim of forcing the Western powers out of a city deep in the Soviet zone; the harassment from April 1948; the earlier walkout from the Allied Control Council (20 March). Test: the Soviet Union did not attack the Airlift; the Blockade followed the currency reform and the London recommendations to set up a West German state; Stalin's apparent aim was to stop that state; the Blockade failed and produced the Federal Republic (23 May 1949) and NATO (4 April 1949). A Level 6 answer concludes that the claim states one aim of the Blockade and leaves out the other, and says that a historian who makes it reads the crisis from the Western position.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice.) "Stalin's policy in Eastern Europe from 1945 to 1948 was defensive." Use your knowledge to explain and test this claim.
H: Which events support "defensive" (invasions of 1914 and 1941; 27 million dead)?
H: Which events do not fit (Romania, March 1945; Poland, 1947; Czechoslovakia, 1948)?
H: What did the Hungarian election of November 1945 show?
A: Support: Soviet losses; the 1941 invasion route through Poland; the delay in imposing Communist rule in Hungary and Czechoslovakia; the Cominform and Czech coup came after the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan; Finland remained independent. Test: Groza (6 March 1945) and the trial of the sixteen (June 1945) came before any American challenge; control of police and elections was the method from the start; rigged elections in Poland (1947) and Hungary (1947); the Soviet word "defensive" does not tell you what the people of Poland and Czechoslovakia wanted. A Level 6 answer asks what "defensive" means and ends by saying what the claim shows about the historian's approach, which centres on Soviet motive.
Q: [15 marks] ★★★ Choose two of the events in the story in dates and explain how a revisionist and a traditionalist would each use them.
H: Choose events where the school's use differs most (the atomic bomb, the Marshall Plan, the Truman Doctrine).
A: Any two, with date, facts and both readings, e.g. the Truman Doctrine (12 March 1947): traditionalist: a necessary response to Soviet pressure on Greece and Turkey, in a region where Britain could no longer act; revisionist: a global ideological commitment that turned a local matter into a world struggle and made agreement harder. A top answer adds a test from the facts (Stalin did little for the Greek guerrillas).

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice.) "The Cold War began because the wartime alliance was always a marriage of convenience." Use your knowledge to explain and test this claim, and say what it shows about the approach of a historian who makes it.
H: What held the alliance together (a common enemy; Lend-Lease; Roosevelt's hope)?
H: What did the leaders do at Tehran and Yalta that shows cooperation?
H: What kind of historian looks to 1917 and 1939 for the cause?
A: Explain: the Western intervention of 1918–20; non-recognition until 1933; the Nazi–Soviet Pact; the delay of the second front; the quarrels over Poland (Katyn, Warsaw Uprising); the Percentages Agreement. Test: the alliance won the war and agreed on German zones, the UN and the Far East; Roosevelt and Stalin built a working relationship; the quarrels deepened only after the war and with Truman's change; the Soviet Union was willing to bargain in 1944–45. The claim is a long-term (structural) one: the historian looks for causes in ideology and the system, not in the decisions of 1945–47. Level 6 sees that the approach plays down the contingency of particular choices.
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ Choose two of the documents in this chapter (the Long Telegram, the Novikov telegram, the Truman Doctrine speech, the Zhdanov report) and say what a historian who blames the other side would do with each.
H: Which of these were written for a public audience and which for a private one?
A: The Long Telegram and the Novikov telegram were private reports; the Truman Doctrine speech and Zhdanov's report were public speeches to win support. A historian who blames the USSR would cite the Long Telegram as insight and the Zhdanov report as Soviet declaration; a historian who blames the USA would cite the Novikov telegram as an accurate picture and the Truman Doctrine as American crusade. The point is that the private documents suggest fear and the public ones suggest aims, and that historians choose between them.
