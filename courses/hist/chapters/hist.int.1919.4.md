:::why
In 1923 France and Belgium had troops in the Ruhr, the German mark was worth nothing and Europe looked ready for another war. By 1926 Germany sat on the Council of the League next to France, American money was rebuilding German factories, and in 1928 fifteen states signed a pact renouncing war. Explaining that change is the central question of this chapter, and the reason it is a favourite essay topic is that historians disagree about how real the change was. Its end point, the Wall Street Crash of October 1929, tests the answer: did a sound structure collapse under an outside shock, or was the structure weak all along?

## Why 1924 was a turning point

The Ruhr crisis taught each of the main players a lesson.
- **France** learned that it could not force Germany to pay alone: the occupation cost more than it brought in, the franc weakened, and Britain and the USA would not support it. Voters turned in May 1924 to the left-wing **Cartel des Gauches**, whose leader **Édouard Herriot** wanted cooperation.
- **Britain** had a Labour government from January to November 1924 under **Ramsay MacDonald**, who was ready to work with France and Germany. After October 1924 the Conservative government of **Stanley Baldwin** took over, with **Austen Chamberlain** as Foreign Secretary, who wanted peace and trade but no new commitments.
- **Germany**, in **Gustav Stresemann**'s hands, saw that only cooperation could bring recovery and the end of the occupation. He was Chancellor for a hundred days in 1923 and Foreign Minister from August 1923 until his death in 1929.
- **The USA**, alarmed by the chaos in Europe, was ready to act, but through money and expert committees, not governments.

New people mattered. **Aristide Briand**, who had been Prime Minister of France several times, was Foreign Minister from April 1925 and was the French voice of conciliation until 1932. The three men who made the Locarno years, **Stresemann, Briand and Chamberlain**, shared a wish to avoid another war, though not the same ideas about how.

## Economic recovery and improved relations

### The Dawes Plan: proposals

In late 1923 the Reparation Commission set up a committee of experts, chaired by the American banker and politician **Charles Dawes**, to find a way of settling the reparations question and balancing the German budget. The committee reported on **9 April 1924**. Its report began with a statement that the experts approached the task "as business men", concerned with "the technical, not the political aspects of the problem". The plan was adopted at the **London Conference** of July–August 1924 (the first at which Germany's representatives took part in the negotiations) and came into force on **1 September 1924**.

:::def The Dawes Plan
The reparations settlement of 1924, drawn up by a committee chaired by Charles Dawes. It set German annual payments on a rising scale, reorganised the German bank and railways under foreign supervision, gave Germany an international loan, and made the French and Belgian troops leave the Ruhr.
:::

Its main proposals:
- **Payments rescheduled.** Germany was to pay **1 billion marks** in the first year, rising to a standard annuity of **2.5 billion marks** by 1928–29. The Plan fixed no total: it dealt with how much to pay each year, not with how long Germany would pay. Payments could be higher if German prosperity rose.
- **A new currency and bank.** The Reichsmark, which replaced the temporary Rentenmark of November 1923, was tied to gold. The Reichsbank was reorganised under an international board, independent of the government.
- **Security for the creditors.** The German railways were made a company, with their bonds a pledge for payments, and a mortgage of 5 billion marks was placed on German industry.
- **A loan.** Germany received a foreign loan of **800 million gold marks**, about half of it raised on the American market.
- **Protection of the mark.** A Transfer Committee, led by an American Agent-General for Reparations (Seymour Parker Gilbert), would see that payments did not endanger the currency.
- **The Ruhr.** Allied armies were to leave; the occupation of the Ruhr ended in 1925.

### The Dawes Plan: the effect on international relations

**Reactions.** France accepted the plan because Herriot's government wanted American support for the franc and an end to the quarrel with Britain; Britain accepted it because it wanted Germany back at work. In Germany the Reichstag approved the necessary laws on 29 August 1924, but only because about half of the deputies of the nationalist DNVP (the German National People's Party, the main nationalist party of the right) voted for them, giving the two-thirds majority needed. The right-wing parties had attacked the foreign supervision as a "second Versailles".

**Effects.**
1. **It took reparations out of politics.** A committee of experts, and not governments, set the payments, which reduced the chances of another Ruhr-style crisis.
2. **It ended the occupation of the Ruhr.** French and Belgian troops left in the summer of 1925, as the plan intended.
3. **It brought American money to Europe.** After 1924 foreign loans, especially American ones, poured into Germany: roughly 25 billion marks in 1924–29 (estimates run higher, up to about 29 billion), against roughly 10 billion marks of reparations paid (estimates differ). Germany rebuilt factories, houses and cities with the loans.
4. **It created a circle.** American loans went to Germany, Germany paid reparations to Britain and France, and Britain and France paid war debts to the USA. As long as the loans kept coming, the system worked.
5. **It tied the USA to Europe** in a financial way, without a political commitment.

:::key The Dawes Plan: strengths and weaknesses
1. **Strengths**: ended the Ruhr crisis; a fair method (payments linked to German prosperity); stabilised the mark; gave France security of payment and Germany a way out of isolation; made the USA a stakeholder.
2. **Weaknesses**: it fixed no total; Germany remained resentful of foreign controls; most of the loans were short-term and could be recalled at once; the system depended on American lending, so it was **fragile**; and it did not settle the war debts, which were the other half of the circle.
3. **Verdict**: a success for the decade of 1924–29, and the foundation of the improvement in relations, but built on borrowed money.
:::

### Stresemann and German recovery

Stresemann's policy is often called **fulfilment** or "revision by agreement": fulfil the obligations of the treaties, as far as possible, to win the trust of the Allies, and use that trust to peacefully revise them. He had three aims, which he set out in a letter of 1925: to settle reparations in a way Germany could bear; to protect Germans living abroad; and to correct the frontiers in the east. In the short term, that meant making Germany a respected member of the western system, ending the occupation and getting American money.

German recovery was real. After the Rentenmark and Dawes, the currency was stable, foreign capital came in, industry modernised, trade grew and wages rose. By the late 1920s production was back to about its pre-war level (historians differ on precise figures), and Germany again had one of the world's great economies. The government and local authorities used loans to build housing, schools, hospitals and sports grounds. The Weimar Republic seemed to have survived its early crises: the Nazis won only 2.6 per cent of the vote in the election of 1928.

But the recovery was fragile:
- It depended on **short-term foreign loans**, which could be recalled.
- Unemployment stayed substantial even in good years, and farmers were in difficulty because of falling prices.
- The foreign debt was not matched by exports.
- The nationalist right, led after 1928 by **Alfred Hugenberg**, and the Nazis (a tiny party in the late 1920s) kept up the attack on the Republic and on "fulfilment".

:::note Stresemann's motives: the historians' debate (stretch for A*)
Was Stresemann a good European or a nationalist using peace as a means? **The case for a cautious nationalist**: his private letter of 7 September 1925 to the former Crown Prince lists as the third task the recovery of Danzig, the Polish Corridor and a correction of the frontier in Upper Silesia, and says that German policy must "finesse" and avoid great decisions until the occupation ends. He refused to guarantee the eastern frontiers at Locarno. **The case for a genuine peacemaker**: **Jonathan Wright**, *Gustav Stresemann: Weimar's Greatest Statesman* (2002), sees a realist who understood that Germany's recovery needed peace, and who sought revision by diplomacy, not war. The letter was written to win nationalist support for the Locarno policy, so it tells you what Stresemann wanted a nationalist audience to hear. Later events, including his Nobel Peace Prize and his support for the League, show a man who worked within the system. A safe conclusion for exam purposes: his methods were peaceful and cooperative, his aims were revisionist; whether he would have accepted the final settlement of the east is unknowable because he died in 1929.
:::

### German entry into the League of Nations

Germany had been excluded from the League in 1920. The Locarno agreements made it a condition that Germany joined; entering was part of the bargain.

**Why Germany joined.**
- It was a **condition of Locarno**, which Germany wanted.
- It meant **equality**: a permanent seat on the Council like Britain and France.
- Membership gave Germany a **platform** to argue for revision of the treaties (the League's Article 19 allowed treaties to be reconsidered) and for the protection of German minorities abroad.
- It helped **economic cooperation** and signalled good faith to American and British bankers.
- Stresemann also hoped that it would strengthen his hand against his own nationalists, who said that it meant accepting the "war guilt" of the treaty.

Germany made conditions: that it would have a permanent Council seat, and that it would not be obliged to let foreign troops cross its territory to take part in sanctions against the Soviet Union (an exemption linked to Article 16 of the Covenant), a point that mattered to a country between East and West that wanted to stay on good terms with Russia.

**The delay.** In March 1926 an attempt to admit Germany failed, because Brazil, Spain and Poland demanded permanent seats of their own too, and Germany refused to join if others were admitted at the same time. The crisis was resolved in September: the Council was enlarged to fourteen seats, with a permanent seat for Germany and a re-eligible seat for Poland (Brazil, refused a permanent seat, left the League in 1926), and the Assembly admitted Germany on **8 September 1926**. On 10 September Stresemann and Briand spoke in the Assembly. Briand's speech, which Stresemann later said was heard in all parts of the world, declared that the era of cannons and machine guns must end, and it is remembered for its appeal for conciliation, arbitration and peace in their place.

**Impact.** Germany's entry made the League a more representative body and gave the Locarno agreements a link to it, since breaches would go to the Council. It marked the end of the Allied attempt to isolate Germany and was the high point of the "spirit of Locarno". But it also gave Germany a way to claim equality and revision, and for Germany's neighbours, especially Poland, it meant a stronger German voice on the Council. When Germany walked out of the League in 1933, it showed how much had depended on Stresemann.

### The Young Plan

The Dawes Plan was meant to be temporary. In 1928 Germany asked for a final settlement, and the Allies, who wanted the occupation to end and reparations to be fixed, agreed. The **Young Committee**, chaired by the American businessman **Owen Young**, met in Paris from February to June 1929 and reported on 7 June.

**Proposals.** The plan fixed the total German debt at about **36 billion marks** (the present value), payable with interest in annuities until **1988**, a total of about **112 billion marks over 59 years**. The two figures do not clash: 36 billion is what all the future instalments were worth in 1929 money (the "present value", because a mark paid in 1988 is worth less than a mark today), while 112 billion is the sum of all the instalments added up to 1988. The 112 billion cannot be set directly beside the 132 billion marks of the 1921 schedule, which included "C" bonds that were never expected to be paid, so 132 billion was a headline figure only. The annual payment started at about 1.7 billion marks and averaged about 2 billion. The foreign controls of the Dawes Plan (the Agent-General, the supervision of the railways and Reichsbank) were removed and Germany took charge of its own payments. A new **Bank for International Settlements** (BIS) in Basel was to handle them. The USA again took part as an adviser and banker, not as a government.

**The Hague Conference.** The plan was discussed at **The Hague** in two sessions, 6–31 August 1929 and 3–20 January 1930. At the first, Britain's Chancellor of the Exchequer, **Philip Snowden**, demanded a larger British share and threatened to let the plan fail; the dispute was settled mainly at Germany's expense. The first conference also agreed the **evacuation of the Rhineland**, which was completed on **30 June 1930**, five years before the date set at Versailles.

**Effects.** The plan cut the German burden and fixed it for the long term. It also ended the occupation. On the other hand, the German right (Hugenberg's DNVP and the Nazis) launched a campaign against it and, in December 1929, a referendum on a "Law against the Enslavement of the German People", which failed to win the support needed (about 14.9 per cent of the electorate voted, with 94.5 per cent of those voting in favour) but gave the Nazis their first national publicity. The Young Plan took effect in May 1930, and, with the Depression, was suspended in 1931 by the Hoover Moratorium.

:::key Dawes, Stresemann, the League and Young: how they fit together
1. **Dawes (1924)** solved the immediate money crisis and ended the Ruhr occupation: the foundation.
2. **Stresemann's policy** used this to make Germany a partner: Locarno (1925), the League (1926).
3. **Young (1929)** replaced the Dawes Plan with a fixed, smaller and permanent debt and ended foreign control of the German economy, together with the evacuation of the Rhineland.
4. All depended on **American money** and on the goodwill of Stresemann and Briand. When one disappeared in 1929, the system looked much weaker.
:::

## International proposals, treaties and conferences

### The Geneva Protocol, 1924

**The problem.** The League's Covenant promised security, but it did not say how, and France above all wanted more: an automatic guarantee that aggression would be punished. Britain, in contrast, wished to avoid automatic commitments. Two left-of-centre prime ministers, **MacDonald** in Britain and **Herriot** in France, took up the question together at the Assembly of 1924.

**The proposals.** The **Geneva Protocol for the Pacific Settlement of International Disputes** was adopted by the League Assembly on **2 October 1924**. It had three pillars:
- **compulsory arbitration**: all disputes were to be settled peacefully, and legal disputes referred to the Permanent Court of International Justice;
- a **definition of the aggressor**: a state that refused arbitration or went to war would be named the aggressor, and the League could apply economic and military sanctions;
- a **disarmament conference** to be held in 1925, with the Protocol only coming into force if it succeeded.

**Support.** France, for security, and many smaller states, for protection from stronger neighbours, welcomed it. MacDonald's government supported it in principle.

**Opposition.** The British Conservative government that took office in November 1924 under **Baldwin**, with **Chamberlain** as Foreign Secretary, would not accept it. Britain's reasons: the Protocol would commit the navy to enforcing sanctions anywhere in the world, perhaps against the USA, which was not in the League and which opposed any limit on its trading rights at sea; the dominions (Canada, Australia and others) did not wish to be dragged into European quarrels; and Chamberlain preferred specific, limited agreements to a general promise. The USA also disapproved, and Germany disliked it because the Protocol seemed to freeze the post-war frontiers. On **12 March 1925** Chamberlain told the League Council that Britain would not ratify. The Protocol was never put into effect.

**Importance.** The failure of the Protocol showed that Britain would not make general commitments, and it directly led to Locarno: Chamberlain suggested that, instead of a global promise, Britain could guarantee a particular frontier, the western one, where its interest was clear. It also showed that the League's security system depended on the great powers' willingness to act.

### The Locarno Treaties, 1925

**Origins.** In **February 1925** Stresemann, encouraged by the British ambassador in Berlin, Lord D'Abernon, sent France a proposal for a **security pact** to guarantee the Franco-German frontier. His reasons: to rule out another occupation of the Rhineland, to prevent an Anglo-French alliance against Germany, and to prepare the way for revision in the east. France, anxious about its security after the failure of the Protocol, wanted a British guarantee and guarantees for its eastern allies. After months of notes, delegates met at **Locarno** on Lake Maggiore, from **5 to 16 October 1925**: Stresemann and the Chancellor Hans Luther for Germany, Briand for France, Chamberlain for Britain, and, for the eastern treaties, the Polish and Czechoslovak foreign ministers. Mussolini came for the closing days. The treaties were initialled on 16 October and signed in London on **1 December 1925**.

**The agreements.** There were several:

| Agreement | Parties | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| **Treaty of Mutual Guarantee** (the Rhineland Pact) | Germany, France, Belgium, with Britain and Italy as guarantors | Germany, France and Belgium accepted their frontiers as fixed at Versailles and promised never to attack one another; they accepted the demilitarisation of the Rhineland; Britain and Italy guaranteed all of this (Articles 1 and 2) |
| **Arbitration conventions** | Germany with France and with Belgium | Disputes to be settled by arbitration or conciliation |
| **Arbitration treaties** | Germany with Poland and with Czechoslovakia | The same, but with no guarantee of the frontiers |
| **Treaties of mutual assistance** | France with Poland and with Czechoslovakia | France would help if either was attacked in breach of the Locarno terms |

The terms of Article 1 and Article 2 of the Rhineland Pact, in the treaty's own words, were that the parties "collectively and severally guarantee" the frontiers between Germany and Belgium and between Germany and France, and "the inviolability of the said frontiers", and that Germany and Belgium, and also Germany and France, "will in no case attack or invade each other or resort to war against each other", except in self-defence or in League action. If a breach was alleged, the Council of the League would decide and the guarantors would help the victim (Article 4). The treaties came into force on **14 September 1926**, after Germany joined the League.

**Achievements.**
1. For the first time Germany **freely accepted** the western frontier of Versailles, in a treaty it negotiated as an equal.
2. It **reduced French fear**: a British and Italian guarantee of the frontier and of the demilitarised Rhineland gave France some of the security it had sought in vain in 1919. Britain was, for the first time since 1919, committed on the continent.
3. It led to **Germany's entry to the League** (1926), the evacuation of the Cologne zone (January 1926) and the end of the Allied military control commission in Germany (the Allied body that inspected German disarmament; January 1927).
4. It created the "**spirit of Locarno**": a belief that disputes could be settled by talk. Chamberlain received the Nobel Peace Prize for 1925, and Briand and Stresemann for 1926.
5. Between 1925 and 1929 the cooperation of the three foreign ministers produced a calmer Europe than in 1923.

**Failures and limits.**
1. **The east was left out.** Germany agreed to settle its disputes with Poland and Czechoslovakia peacefully, but refused to accept their frontiers as permanent; Britain would not guarantee them. Chamberlain is remembered for saying that the Polish Corridor was not worth the bones of a British grenadier. Poland and Czechoslovakia, the new states most afraid of a revisionist Germany, felt that they had been left outside the new order. Stresemann was explicit: there would be no "Eastern Locarno".
2. **Britain's commitment was narrow**: only the western frontier, and only if the Council had agreed that a breach had occurred.
3. **It weakened France's eastern alliances.** France's ties with Poland were now framed within the Covenant, and a French intervention to help Poland could trigger a German reaction that Britain would not support.
4. **It did not touch disarmament or reparations**, or the Soviet Union's fears that Locarno was a western bloc against it (Moscow responded with the Berlin Treaty).
5. **Opposition in Germany**: the DNVP left the government over Locarno, and nationalists saw it as a betrayal of Alsace-Lorraine, though the Reichstag approved the treaties by 291 votes to 174.
6. It was a **treaty of goodwill**: it depended on the intentions of the signatories and was shown to be empty when Germany reoccupied the Rhineland in March 1936.

:::key Locarno: success or failure?
1. **For**: Germany voluntarily accepted the western frontier; France got a guarantee; Britain committed itself; Germany entered the League; the Rhineland evacuation and the end of military control began; the "spirit of Locarno".
2. **Against**: the eastern frontiers were not guaranteed; Britain's guarantee was limited; Germany kept revision of the east as an aim; it left Poland and Czechoslovakia anxious.
3. **Judgement**: Locarno was a real success for security and relations in the west, and a failure to settle the east. It improved the atmosphere more than the structure of European security.
:::

### The Kellogg–Briand Pact, 1928

**Purpose.** On 6 April 1927, the tenth anniversary of America's entry into the First World War, Briand proposed a treaty in which France and the USA would renounce war against each other. His motive was security: a pact of that kind would make the USA a moral ally of France. The US Secretary of State **Frank Kellogg**, who feared an alliance and was under pressure from the peace movement, answered in December 1927 by proposing that the pact be open to every country. After months of negotiation the pact was signed in Paris on **27 August 1928** by fifteen states: the USA, France, Britain, Germany, Italy, Japan, Belgium, Poland, Czechoslovakia and the five British dominions and India. It came into force in July 1929, and more than sixty states had signed by the 1930s. The US Senate approved it by 85 votes to 1.

**Terms.** Article 1: the parties "condemn recourse to war for the solution of international controversies, and renounce it, as an instrument of national policy in their relations with one another". Article 2: they agree that "the settlement or solution of all disputes or conflicts of whatever nature or of whatever origin" shall "never be sought except by pacific means". The pact had no sanctions: in the preamble a state that went to war would lose "the benefits furnished by this Treaty", which means only that other parties would be free of their obligations to it.

:::def The Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928)
A treaty signed in Paris on 27 August 1928 by which the parties renounced war as an instrument of national policy and agreed to settle disputes by peaceful means. It contained no means of enforcement.
:::

**Impact and limitations.**
- It was the **widest** commitment against war yet, welcomed by public opinion, and it committed the USA to a principle without an alliance. Frank Kellogg was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for 1929.
- **Self-defence** was excluded: Kellogg's notes said that the right was not limited by the pact, and each state could decide for itself what self-defence was. Britain also reserved certain regions of the world (the Middle East, the empire) as vital to its safety and the USA kept the Monroe Doctrine.
- **No enforcement**: no sanctions, no machinery, no definition of aggression. It said nothing about how a violation would be punished, so it relied on public opinion.
- **War was not defined**: Japan's occupation of Manchuria in 1931 and later actions were called "incidents", not wars.
- It conflicted with the **Covenant** and with Locarno, which allowed war in certain cases; the pact did not cancel those.
- It was signed by states with different motives: France for security, the USA for goodwill without commitment, Germany to show good faith, the Soviet Union (which joined in February 1929 through the **Litvinov Protocol** with neighbours) to show that it was no outcast.
The pact was a **sign** of improved relations, since states were ready to sign it, but not a **cause** of them. It showed how far good will had gone and how little it was backed by power.

### The Hague Conference, 1929 and the Rhineland

The Young Plan (above) was bound up with the **Rhineland**. By 1929 the Allied occupation had lasted ten years, and Versailles allowed it to run to 1935. Germany saw the occupation as an insult, and Stresemann had worked since Locarno to bring it to an end. At Thoiry in 1926 he had hinted that Germany would pay if France left early, but France refused. By 1929 Briand and the French wished to link the evacuation to the final reparations settlement (Young), not give it for nothing, and the British wished to keep their own interests, so the dispute was part of the bargaining at **The Hague** in August 1929. Stresemann, in poor health, insisted that the evacuation must not wait for the Young Plan. The conference agreed to withdraw the remaining troops, and the evacuation was completed on **30 June 1930**.

**Why it mattered.** The evacuation was the end of the physical symbol of Germany's defeat and a success for Stresemann's strategy: Germany had gained by negotiation what could not be won by force. But it came too late for him: Stresemann died on **3 October 1929**, aged 51, a few weeks after the conference. Many Germans, particularly on the right, gave him little credit.

## Developing relations between the major powers

### France and Germany: the roles of Stresemann and Briand

Between 1925 and 1929 the relationship between France and Germany was the main axis of European diplomacy, and it rested on two men. **Briand** believed that France would be safer through reconciliation with Germany than through coercion, and that a German member of the League would be a restraint. **Stresemann** wanted German equality and the end of the occupation, which were possible only with French agreement. They met often at the League meetings in Geneva, and the pair were awarded the Nobel Prize in 1926.

The high point was the private lunch at **Thoiry**, a village near Geneva, on **17 September 1926**, a week after Germany took its seat. The two men discussed an early evacuation of the Rhineland and the return of the Saar to Germany, in return for German financial help for France, which was in a currency crisis. Nothing came of it: Poincaré, who was Prime Minister again from July 1926 and was stabilising the franc, would not agree, and French opinion distrusted a deal with Germany. Business and trade made more progress: in September 1926 the iron and steel industries of Germany, France, Belgium and Luxembourg formed an **International Steel Cartel**, a sign that the economic links that Stresemann spoke about were growing.

In September 1929, a few weeks before Stresemann died, Briand proposed to the League Assembly a "kind of federal bond" among the states of Europe, and in May 1930 he sent a formal memorandum on a European federal union. The idea of a united Europe was ahead of its time. Britain did not like it, Germany and Italy were cool, and the Depression killed it.

Nevertheless, **suspicion remained**. France began building the **Maginot Line**, a chain of fortifications on the German frontier, approved in 1929–30: a sign that French leaders did not trust the improvement to last. Germany had its own reservations: the army secretly continued to build and test forbidden weapons in the Soviet Union and kept secret reserves of men. Stresemann's aim of revision, especially in the east, remained, and so did French fear.

### Italy and the impact of Mussolini's ambitions

Mussolini had made himself dictator by 1925–26, and his foreign policy was cautious but ambitious. He was glad to take the role of a guarantor at Locarno, which gave Italy a place among the major powers, and he signed the Kellogg–Briand Pact. But he also said that Italy wanted to be a great power with a Mediterranean empire (*mare nostrum*, "our sea"), and he wished for revision of the settlements.

- **Albania.** By the Treaties of Tirana (November 1926 and November 1927) Italy made Albania a protectorate in all but name, with a defensive alliance, which alarmed Yugoslavia and its ally France.
- **Hungary and Bulgaria.** Mussolini signed a treaty of friendship with Hungary in April 1927 and backed revision of Trianon, which cut across the Little Entente and the French alliance system.
- **South Tyrol.** A dispute with Germany arose in 1926 when Stresemann criticised the Italianisation of the German-speaking population, and Mussolini answered sharply.
- **The Soviet Union.** Italy recognised it in February 1924, before France did.

**Impact.** Mussolini was a source of unease, not a threat of war, in these years: he pursued prestige and influence in the Balkans, and was careful not to break with Britain. But he undermined the idea of a Europe united behind Locarno, he created a rival to French influence in south-eastern Europe, and his rhetoric showed that revision was the aim of many states. He was a reminder that the settlement still had its enemies.

### Shifting relations with the Soviet Union

The Soviet Union, ruled after Lenin's death (January 1924) through a leadership struggle that Stalin was winning, was concerned above all with security and economic recovery. Its policy of "**socialism in one country**" (1924–25) meant that it wanted peace and trade while the Comintern still preached revolution abroad. That duality caused repeated crises.

- **Recognition, 1924.** MacDonald's Labour government recognised the Soviet Union on 1 February 1924; Italy followed a few days later and France in October. It looked as if the Soviet Union was being accepted into the system.
- **The Zinoviev letter, October 1924.** The *Daily Mail* published a letter said to come from Grigory Zinoviev, head of the Comintern, urging British Communists to prepare for revolution, four days before the general election of 29 October 1924. The letter was almost certainly forged; but it helped to defeat Labour, and the Conservatives, who took office, were hostile to the Soviet Union.
- **Locarno, 1925.** The Soviet government feared that Locarno was the first step towards a western bloc against it, perhaps with Germany as its spearhead, and that Germany's entry to the League would bring it into the hostile camp.
- **The Treaty of Berlin, 24 April 1926.** Stresemann's reply to the Soviet fears was a treaty with Moscow. It confirmed the Treaty of Rapallo of 1922 (Article 1) and promised that if either party were attacked by a third power, the other would "observe neutrality for the whole of the duration of the conflict" (Article 2), and that neither would join a financial or economic boycott of the other (Article 3). It was valid for five years. Its purpose for Germany was to keep a balance between East and West, which gave it leverage and to reassure Russia, even as Germany joined the League. It worried Poland and the western powers, but Stresemann argued that it did not conflict with Locarno.
- **Break with Britain, 1927.** After a police raid on the offices of the Soviet trade agency **ARCOS** in London on 12 May 1927, which found little, the Conservative government broke off diplomatic relations on **27 May 1927**. Relations were restored by the next Labour government in 1929.
- **Joining the Pact, 1929.** In the same period the Soviet Union adhered to the Kellogg–Briand Pact through the Litvinov Protocol (9 February 1929). It did not join the League until 1934.

:::key The Soviet Union in 1924–29
1. Recognition by Britain, Italy and France (1924) ended its diplomatic isolation.
2. Fear of Communism, as in the Zinoviev letter and ARCOS, kept Britain hostile and stopped the Soviet Union from being a full partner.
3. Germany balanced between the Soviet Union (Rapallo, Berlin) and the West (Locarno, the League).
4. The Soviet Union was **outside** the League system, so the improved relations of the 1920s were only partial.
:::

## Improved relations: reality or illusion?

### The European powers

**Reality.** Between 1924 and 1929 there was no war between European states, and real steps were taken: the Ruhr was evacuated; reparations were settled by the Dawes and Young Plans; Germany was reconciled with the West at Locarno and in the League; the Rhineland was evacuated five years early; the Kellogg–Briand Pact was signed. Trade and living standards grew, and the League was at its strongest. The leaders, Briand, Stresemann and Chamberlain, had a personal commitment to peace.

**Illusion.** The reasons to doubt it are as strong.
1. **Dependence on money from outside.** The recovery of Germany and so of the whole system rested on short-term American loans.
2. **Unfinished business in the east.** Locarno left the eastern frontiers open, Poland and Czechoslovakia anxious, and Germany's aim of revision unsettled.
3. **Disarmament did not progress.** The Preparatory Commission of the League, which began work in 1926, made little headway by 1930; the naval talks at Geneva in 1927 failed. Germany had been disarmed by Versailles, and wished to see other states disarm; France wanted security first.
4. **Mistrust remained.** France built the Maginot Line; Germany rearmed secretly.
5. **The "satisfied" and "revisionist" divide** remained: Italy and Hungary wanted revision, and in Germany the nationalist right grew louder.
6. **The USA and the Soviet Union stayed outside** the League's security system.
7. The Kellogg–Briand Pact and the "spirit of Locarno" were **statements of intent** with no enforcement.

### Japan and the USA

**Reality.** The Washington system held for most of the 1920s. Japan under the Foreign Minister Shidehara cooperated with the West and kept the naval limits; Japan and the USA both signed the Kellogg–Briand Pact; American trade and loans reached Japan; the Pacific was calm. Japan's conflict with the USA did not break out.

**Illusion.** The sources of conflict were unresolved. The Immigration Act of 1924 had insulted Japan, and the naval ratio and the cruiser race continued: the Geneva Naval Conference of 1927 failed, and the London Naval Treaty of 1930 had to patch up the problem of cruisers. In China the Japanese army grew more aggressive: in 1928 the Kwantung Army, the Japanese force in Manchuria, murdered the Chinese warlord Zhang Zuolin, and Japanese troops clashed with Chinese forces at Jinan. Governments in Tokyo were weak, and the army and nationalists were gaining influence. Japan's economy was in difficulty, with a banking crisis in 1927. The foundations of cooperation, American goodwill and Japanese moderation, were fragile.

### The immediate impact of the Wall Street Crash

On **24 October 1929** share prices on the New York Stock Exchange collapsed, and on **29 October** they crashed again, wiping out much of the market's value. For international relations the effects were swift and profound:
- **American lending dried up.** American lending to Europe was already falling in 1928–29, as the boom in shares drew money into the stock market and away from foreign bonds; after the Crash, it stopped, and the short-term loans were called in. Germany, the largest borrower, was the first to suffer, since the whole Dawes–Young system relied on those loans.
- **Unemployment rose** and, in Germany, the coalition and the policy of fulfilment came under pressure. In the Reichstag election of September 1930 the Nazis rose from 12 seats to 107.
- **Governments turned inwards.** The US Smoot–Hawley Tariff (1930) raised duties sharply, and others followed with their own tariffs, so the economic cooperation of the 1920s broke down.
- **The loss of Stresemann** (3 October 1929) came at the worst time: there was no German leader of equal standing to defend cooperation.
- **The conflicts that had been papered over** (reparations, revision, disarmament) returned in the 1930s with extremists in government: the road to the Second World War (the next chapter).

:::key Reality or illusion? A judgement you can defend
The improvement was **real but fragile**. It was real because the causes of the Ruhr crisis were removed (the Dawes Plan, the French withdrawal, German cooperation) and because the leaders, for a time, preferred negotiation to force. It was fragile because it depended on **American money**, on **personal relations** between three men, and on **western Europe only**: the eastern frontiers, disarmament and the problems of the successor states were untouched. The Crash did not create the weaknesses; it showed them. So "illusion" is too strong, but "reality" is too simple: the recovery was genuine, and it rested on foundations that could not take a shock.
:::

## Why did relations improve? Weighing the causes

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | The Dawes Plan; American loans; German recovery; the Young Plan | Removed the Ruhr dispute and gave every government a stake in peace |
| **Leaders and ideas** | Stresemann, Briand and Chamberlain; Herriot and MacDonald; fulfilment and conciliation | Replaced the coercion of Poincaré and Cuno with negotiation |
| **Treaties and institutions** | Locarno; German entry to the League; Kellogg–Briand | Gave states security and a forum; the "spirit of Locarno" |
| **Lessons of 1923** | The failure of the Ruhr; the hyperinflation | Convinced France that coercion did not work and Germany that resistance did not work |
| **The USA** | Dawes and Young; the loans; Kellogg | Held the financial structure together |
| **Weakness of rivals** | The USSR's isolation; Italy's caution; Japan's cooperation | Left no great power actively seeking war |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The Dawes Plan was the **foundation** of the improvement, because it ended the Ruhr crisis and brought American money, without which the political treaties would not have been possible. But money alone did not make peace: **Stresemann's and Briand's choice** of cooperation, made possible by the lessons of 1923, turned economic recovery into Locarno and the League. The treaties gave the improvement its shape and its name; the loans gave it its substance. When the loans stopped in 1929, the improvement stopped too.
:::

:::note The historians (stretch for A*)
**Sally Marks**, *The Illusion of Peace: International Relations in Europe 1918–1933* (1976), gave the period its familiar label: the calm of the later 1920s was a surface, and the underlying conflicts over Germany and the east remained. **Zara Steiner**, *The Lights that Failed* (2005), gives a fuller and more balanced picture of the achievements and failures of the 1920s, and stresses that the settlement was not doomed from the start. **Jonathan Wright**, *Gustav Stresemann* (2002), defends Stresemann's reputation as a peacemaker. For exam purposes, "historians disagree about how much to credit Stresemann and how far the improvement was real" is enough; your own argument from evidence is what is rewarded.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Germany joined the League of Nations in September 1926 because of the Locarno Treaties, which said that Germany had to join. Germany got a seat on the Council. Stresemann wanted Germany to be treated like other countries. Briand made a speech to welcome Germany."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence states a fact, and none explains how it caused Germany to join. The link to Locarno is mentioned without saying why Germany wanted Locarno. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The first reason was that joining was a condition of the Locarno agreements, which Germany wanted. Stresemann had proposed a security pact to France in February 1925 to end the threat of another occupation of the Rhineland, and the treaties of December 1925 only came into force once Germany was a member of the League, so there was no Locarno without League membership. A second reason was equality. Germany had been kept out in 1920 as a defeated power, and the permanent seat on the Council that Germany insisted on as a condition of joining put it on the same level as Britain and France. It meant that Germany could now speak on equal terms in the place where the great powers settled international questions."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with accurate support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was that the League could serve German aims. Stresemann thought membership would let Germany argue for the protection of German minorities abroad, especially in Poland and Czechoslovakia, and give it a platform to ask for revision of the treaties under Article 19 of the Covenant. In his letter of September 1925 to the former Crown Prince he said that Germany would be the spokesman of the whole German cultural community in Geneva. Membership would also help to win American and British goodwill and to reassure the Allies. He also needed to convince German nationalists, who objected to the League as a body of victors, that Germany would gain from it.

These reasons were linked. Stresemann's central aim was to end the occupation and revise the treaties peacefully, which needed the trust of Britain and France. Locarno was the means of winning it, and membership of the League was the price and the proof of that policy; the equality that came with a permanent seat made it acceptable at home. The decisive factor was Locarno: without the treaties, Germany would have had less reason to join, since it had stayed out for six years. But Germany's entry only happened in the end because Stresemann saw that it could be used to serve German interests, so it was a calculation and not a conversion."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the condition of Locarno, the equality demanded, the use that could be made of the League), with a supported conclusion about the most important. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The improvement in international relations in the years 1924–29 was an illusion.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Criterion: how far the improvement rested on foundations that could survive a shock, not just whether there was calm.
- For "illusion": dependence on American loans (short-term); Locarno left the east unguaranteed; Stresemann's private revisionism; no real disarmament (Geneva 1927, the Preparatory Commission); Maginot Line; secret rearmament; Kellogg without sanctions; Mussolini's ambitions in Albania and the Balkans; Japan's grievances, the 1924 Immigration Act and the Kwantung Army; collapse after the Crash.
- Against "illusion": no war in Europe; the Ruhr evacuated; the Dawes and Young Plans settled reparations; Locarno was freely accepted by Germany; Germany in the League; the Rhineland evacuated in 1930; Kellogg signed by fifteen states and then sixty; Washington held in the Pacific.
- Judgement: real but fragile; the improvement was genuine in the west and in economic terms and illusory in the east and in security.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The improvement in relations was real, not an illusion. In 1924 the Dawes Plan ended the Ruhr crisis by rescheduling German payments and giving Germany a loan of 800 million gold marks, so that the French and Belgian troops left the Ruhr in 1925. In 1925 the Locarno Treaties were signed, in which Germany, France and Belgium accepted their frontiers and promised not to attack each other, with Britain and Italy as guarantors. In 1926 Germany joined the League of Nations with a permanent seat on the Council. In 1928 the Kellogg–Briand Pact was signed by fifteen states, who renounced war, and in 1929 the Young Plan reduced the reparations further and the Allied troops agreed to leave the Rhineland early. All this shows that international relations improved and that the improvement was not an illusion."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided: it lists agreements and never asks whether they were secure. The last sentence asserts. An answer made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet much of the improvement rested on weak foundations. The recovery of Germany, on which all else depended, was financed by short-term American loans of roughly 25 billion marks, more than twice what Germany paid in reparations, and the circle of loans, reparations and war debts could only last while American money kept flowing. The treaties left important questions open. At Locarno Germany accepted its western frontier as final but refused to do so in the east, and Britain refused to guarantee it, so Poland and Czechoslovakia were left anxious, and Stresemann's letter of September 1925 to the Crown Prince said that the recovery of Danzig, the Polish Corridor and part of Upper Silesia was a third great task for German policy. The Kellogg–Briand Pact contained no sanctions and exempted self-defence, which each state could define for itself. Meanwhile there was little real disarmament: the naval conference at Geneva failed in 1927, France began building the Maginot Line and the German army secretly trained with the Red Army in the Soviet Union. Outside Europe, the Immigration Act of 1924 and the 5:3 naval ratio left Japan's nationalists resentful, and in 1928 the Kwantung Army murdered a Chinese warlord. When the Wall Street Crash ended American lending in 1929, the structure was left with nothing to hold it together.

On the other hand, to call the improvement an illusion goes too far. Between 1924 and 1929 there was no armed crisis in Europe, comparable with the Ruhr, Corfu or Fiume; the Ruhr was evacuated; reparations were settled by two negotiated plans; and Germany's voluntary acceptance of the western frontier at Locarno was a real change, since in 1919 it had been imposed. French and German foreign ministers met regularly, a German sat on the Council of the League, and by June 1930, five years ahead of the Versailles timetable, the Rhineland was free of foreign troops. These were real changes in behaviour and not just words: the states concerned had less fear of one another in 1929 than in 1923.

Overall, the improvement was real but fragile, and the judgement turns on what 'illusion' means. If it means that nothing really changed, the claim is wrong: the Ruhr crisis ended, Germany was reconciled with France in the west, and a war in the west would have been a surprise in 1928. But if it means that the improvement did not rest on firm foundations, it is largely right: it depended on American loans, on the personal commitment of Stresemann and Briand, and on leaving the eastern frontiers unsettled. The Crash and the death of Stresemann in 1929 showed how much depended on them. The statement is therefore half true: the peace was real, but its security was an illusion."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (what 'illusion' means) and distinguishes the reality of the calm from the security of its foundations. With an opening paragraph on the case for an illusion this is Level 5 (19–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: How far was the Dawes Plan the main reason for the improvement in international relations after 1924?
**What the levels want.** The same as the example above: Level 5 needs a comparative judgement about the Dawes Plan against other factors.

**Plan.** Criterion: which factor, if removed, would have most prevented improvement. For the Dawes Plan: ended the Ruhr crisis and the occupation; stabilised the mark; brought American loans and recovery; reduced the cause of Franco-German quarrel. Against: Stresemann's and Briand's choice of policy; Locarno and the League as political foundations; the lessons of 1923 (which also made the plan possible); the role of the USA outside the plan; the British turn to mediation. Judgement: the Plan was the necessary foundation, but political choice turned it into improved relations.

**A Level 5 paragraph pair and judgement.** "The Dawes Plan has a strong claim to be the main reason. Before it, reparations had divided Britain from France and had led to the occupation of the Ruhr; after it, the question was in the hands of experts and the troops were withdrawn. By scheduling payments in line with German prosperity, by giving the Reichsmark a base in gold and by providing an 800 million mark loan, it removed the immediate pretext for another occupation, and it began a flow of American money that revived German industry and so gave Germany and its neighbours a stake in peace. Without it, there would have been no stable German currency, and no basis for Locarno.

But the Plan only created the conditions. It was accepted because of political choices: Herriot's government, elected in May 1924, preferred a settlement to coercion; the Labour government in London pressed for it; and Stresemann, in September 1923, had ended resistance and decided to cooperate. The key improvements in security, Locarno and the League, were agreements of a different kind: they were the work of Stresemann, Briand and Chamberlain, not of bankers, and they dealt with frontiers and security, which the Plan did not touch. The Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928 had nothing to do with money. And the Plan itself shows the dependence of economics on politics: the experts said that their whole report was based on the hypothesis that the economic and fiscal unity of Germany would be restored, which meant the end of the Ruhr occupation, a political decision. Taking all of this, the Dawes Plan was the necessary foundation and the largest single cause of the first stage of improvement, in 1924–25, but the lasting improvement of 1925–29 was due to the political decisions of the leaders. It was the main economic reason, but the improvement was a product of both."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains one perspective with precise evidence, and the final judgement distinguishes a necessary foundation from a sufficient cause using a clear criterion. Level 5 (18–19).

Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is a letter by Stresemann and Source B is the Locarno Pact. Source A says that Germany wants to change its eastern frontiers, but in Source B it says that it will not attack France or Belgium. They are different because one is a letter and one is a treaty. Both were written in 1925."

*Commentary.* One difference with some support. The similarities ("both in 1925") are trivial, the comparison of the form is a simple label and nothing is explained. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"Both sources show Germany treating its western and eastern frontiers differently. In Source A the western frontier is something to be secured: the pact is to 'guarantee us peace' and to bind Britain and Italy as 'guarantors of the German western frontier', and in Source B Germany does accept the western frontier and promises 'in no case' to 'attack or invade' France or Belgium. Both sources therefore agree that in the west Germany wants peace and a guarantee. Neither says anything about a guarantee in the east: Source B names only the frontiers with Belgium and France, and Source A names the east as a place where change is wanted.

The sources differ on whether the frontiers are final. For Source B the western frontier is one of 'inviolability', fixed by Versailles and to be guaranteed 'collectively and severally' by all five powers, and the language is of permanence. In Source A the eastern frontier is to be 'corrected': Stresemann names Danzig, the Polish Corridor and Upper Silesia, and he treats the western pact as a tool, a means 'to guarantee us peace' while the other aims are pursued. Source B is public and legal; Source A is private and aspirational. One shows what Germany promised, the other what it hoped.

Their context explains the difference. Source A was written on 7 September 1925, while Stresemann was still negotiating, to Crown Prince Wilhelm, the son of the last Kaiser and a symbol for German nationalists, who distrusted any agreement with France. Stresemann's purpose was to reassure them that Locarno and League membership would not mean surrendering German claims, so he stresses the eastern revision and speaks of 'security' as a means of recovering strength. It is therefore good evidence of the aims that Stresemann was willing to state to the right, but it may exaggerate his hopes for the east in order to win support. Source B was agreed on 16 October 1925, in a treaty negotiated with Briand and Chamberlain: a formal, binding text that Germany had to accept freely to gain the guarantee. It is reliable evidence about what Germany promised, but silent on its intentions. We know that Stresemann did refuse an 'Eastern Locarno', and that Germany signed arbitration treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia that did not guarantee their frontiers, which fits Source A and shows that its picture was not merely rhetorical.

Taken together the sources show that Germany's policy in 1925 was one of accepting the west in order to reopen the east: Source B is the visible half, and Source A the private half."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the audience and purpose of a private letter to the Crown Prince, the nature of a signed treaty) and through each source's value as evidence; a closing comparison. A **developed comparison**: Level 4.

**Question.** 'The improvement in international relations in the years 1924–29 was the result of a genuine desire for peace.' How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: B (states 'condemn recourse to war' and promise 'pacific means'); D (Stresemann speaks of Germany's desire for peace, and of the change in the treatment of Germany). Challenge or qualify: A (the experts say they are concerned with 'the technical, not the political', so the improvement began as a matter of money; and the report is based on an 'assumption' that Germany's unity would be restored, so it depended on a political decision); C (Germany and the USSR promise each other neutrality, so Germany keeps an option away from the West, which suggests calculation); D (it is also a politician defending his policy; for the 'vanquished' peace means 'resigning himself', which hints at reluctance).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a committee of financiers, led by an American banker, reporting to the Reparation Commission in April 1924: expert, but its purpose was to find a technical solution that the governments could accept, so it deliberately steers away from politics. It shows that the improvement started from economic need, and not from a change of heart. But the stress on 'effective results' and the 'assumption' about German unity also shows that the experts knew they were asking for a political decision, the end of the Ruhr occupation.
- **B**: a public treaty signed by fifteen states in 1928, and so strong evidence that the governments wanted to be seen as committed to peace. But it has no sanctions and exempts self-defence, and states signed it for different reasons: France for security, the USA for goodwill without commitment, Germany for reputation. It shows a desire for peace as a public principle, and does not prove it was genuine.
- **C**: a treaty signed by Stresemann himself, a year before the Nobel lecture. It confirms Rapallo and promises neutrality, which showed Soviet leaders that Locarno was not directed against them. It does not prove hostility to peace, because it is a promise of neutrality, but it shows that Germany's policy was balancing between East and West, and that it took out insurance against the failure of Locarno.
- **D**: a ceremonial source: a lecture delivered to receive a Nobel Peace Prize, to an international audience, by the leader who has most to gain from being seen as a peacemaker. It is reliable on what Stresemann wanted to say about German feelings (the 'vanquished' sense of loss), but is self-justifying. It admits that others tested 'the sincerity of Germany's desire for peace', which also suggests that sincerity was in doubt.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources B and D support the view that there was a genuine desire for peace, but with qualifications. In Source B fifteen states 'condemn recourse to war' and agree that disputes shall never be settled except by 'pacific means', and Source D has Stresemann present Germany's policy as a quest for peace. But neither is conclusive evidence of sincerity. Source B was a public treaty without sanctions that exempted self-defence, so signing it cost nothing, and Stresemann, in D, was a Nobel prize-winner speaking about his own policy, who admits that Germany's opponents had questioned whether its desire for peace was sincere. Sources A and C suggest a different explanation. In Source A the experts say they were concerned with 'the technical, not the political aspects of the problem', and the Dawes Plan, the foundation of the improvement, was a financial arrangement that stabilised the German currency and got the French and Belgians out of the Ruhr; interest in recovery and in avoiding another crisis was as important as an ideal of peace. Source C shows that Germany, even at the height of cooperation with the West, secured its position with a neutrality treaty with the Soviet Union, so its policy was calculated and not just idealistic. My own knowledge supports this mixed picture: Stresemann's letter of 1925 shows that he wanted to revise the eastern frontiers, but he also sought them by peaceful means, and Briand and Chamberlain wanted peace because France and Britain were tired and short of money. Overall the sources support the view only to a limited extent: a desire for peace existed, particularly among the leaders, but it went with self-interest and economic need, and it was probably the lessons of 1923 and the Dawes Plan that made peace the rational choice."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1924–29; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far did the Locarno Treaties improve international relations in the years 1925–29?" [20]

A student wrote:

"In 1925 Britain, France, Germany, Italy and Belgium met at Locarno in Switzerland. They agreed to guarantee the frontiers between Germany and France and between Germany and Belgium. Britain and Italy were guarantors of the agreement. Germany promised not to attack France. There were also arbitration treaties between Germany and Poland and Czechoslovakia, but these did not guarantee the frontiers. Stresemann, Briand and Chamberlain all won the Nobel Peace Prize. Germany then joined the League of Nations. So Locarno improved international relations a great deal."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it lists the terms without explaining how they improved relations, it does not consider limits (it mentions the east but does not say why it matters), and the last sentence asserts. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, turn each fact into evidence for it, then qualify. "Locarno improved relations in the west because it changed what each side feared. Germany freely accepted the western frontier of Versailles and promised never to attack France or Belgium, so France no longer had to fear a German revenge attack, and Britain and Italy's guarantee of the demilitarised Rhineland gave France the security it had failed to win in 1919. The result was visible in the next two years: Germany joined the League with a permanent Council seat in September 1926, the Cologne zone of the Rhineland was evacuated in January 1926, and the Allied military control commission left in 1927. But the improvement was limited to the west. Germany signed only arbitration treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia and Stresemann refused an 'Eastern Locarno', so the frontiers that mattered most to Germany's revisionists were left open, and Britain's guarantee was narrow. Locarno therefore improved the atmosphere and security in the west, and did little for the east." Now each fact proves a point, and the paragraph weighs the two sides.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What were the main proposals of the Dawes Plan?
A: Payments of 1 billion marks in the first year, rising to 2.5 billion by 1928–29, with no total fixed; a new Reichsmark tied to gold and a Reichsbank under an international board; railway bonds and a mortgage on industry as security; a loan of 800 million gold marks (about half raised in the USA); a Transfer Committee and an Agent-General; and the withdrawal of French and Belgian troops from the Ruhr.
Q: Why did Britain reject the Geneva Protocol in March 1925?
A: The Conservative government (Baldwin, with Austen Chamberlain as Foreign Secretary) did not want an automatic commitment to enforce sanctions anywhere in the world, perhaps against the USA; the dominions did not want to be dragged into European disputes; Chamberlain preferred limited, specific agreements, which led to Locarno.
Q: What did the Locarno Treaties guarantee, and what did they not?
A: They guaranteed the Franco-German and Belgian-German frontiers and the demilitarised Rhineland (Britain and Italy as guarantors); Germany and France, and Germany and Belgium, promised not to attack each other. They did not guarantee Germany's eastern frontiers with Poland and Czechoslovakia: Germany only signed arbitration treaties with them.
Q: Give two limitations of the Kellogg–Briand Pact.
A: Any two of: no sanctions or enforcement; self-defence (as each state defined it) was excluded; war was not defined; Britain and the USA reserved certain interests (certain regions, the Monroe Doctrine); it did not cancel the Covenant or Locarno.
Q: What was agreed at the Hague Conference of 1929?
A: The Young Plan was accepted (subject to a second session in January 1930); the Allies agreed to evacuate the Rhineland, a process completed on 30 June 1930, five years earlier than Versailles required; Snowden forced a change in the division of payments in Britain's favour.
Q: What was the Treaty of Berlin (1926) and why did Germany sign it?
A: A five-year German–Soviet pact promising neutrality if either was attacked and confirming the Treaty of Rapallo; Germany signed it to reassure the Soviet Union, which feared that Locarno and League membership meant a western bloc against it, and to keep a balance between East and West.
Q: What happened to Anglo-Soviet relations between 1924 and 1927?
A: Britain recognised the USSR on 1 February 1924 (Labour); the Zinoviev letter of October 1924 helped Labour to lose the election; the Conservatives were hostile; after the ARCOS raid in May 1927 Britain broke off relations on 27 May 1927.
Q: Why did American lending matter to the improvement in relations, and what happened in 1929?
A: American loans financed the recovery of Germany and let it pay reparations to Britain and France, who paid war debts to the USA: a circle on which the whole system depended. After the Wall Street Crash of October 1929 the lending stopped and the loans were called in, so the circle broke.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Geneva Protocol of 1924 was not put into effect.
A: Indicative content: the Protocol committed members to compulsory arbitration and to sanctions against an aggressor, which suited France and the smaller states but not Britain; the new Conservative government (Baldwin, Chamberlain) refused in March 1925 because it would commit the Royal Navy to enforce sanctions anywhere, possibly against the USA, because the dominions did not want to be drawn into European disputes, and because Britain preferred limited agreements; the USA and Germany were also hostile; it was tied to the success of a disarmament conference that was never held. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects the British objections to the wider issue of commitment (Britain's unwillingness to make general promises) and judges which mattered most (the fear of automatic obligations); it can add that the rejection led to Locarno.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Locarno Treaties were signed in 1925.
A: Indicative content: Germany wanted to end the threat of another Ruhr occupation, to gain equality and a way to revision (Stresemann's note of February 1925); France wanted security after the Geneva Protocol failed and a British guarantee; Britain wanted to settle Franco-German tension without a general commitment (Chamberlain preferred a specific guarantee of the western frontier); the experience of the Ruhr and the Dawes Plan had created a better atmosphere; Italy wanted prestige as a guarantor. Level 4: connect the failure of the Protocol, the lessons of the Ruhr and the individual leaders (Stresemann, Briand, Chamberlain), and judge which was the main reason (the failure of the Protocol, which left France and Britain looking for a limited alternative, and Stresemann's initiative).
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Locarno Treaties were the high point of international cooperation in the 1920s.' How far do you agree?
A: For: Germany freely accepted its western frontier; Britain and Italy guaranteed it; the "spirit of Locarno"; Germany entered the League; the Cologne zone and military control ended; three foreign ministers won Nobel prizes; relations between France and Germany better than at any time since 1919. Against: the Dawes Plan (1924) was the foundation and had a bigger effect on the economy; the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928) involved more states; the Young Plan and the evacuation of the Rhineland (1929–30) came later; Locarno left the east open and was limited to western Europe. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion such as "range and depth of cooperation" and judges, e.g., that Locarno was the high point of political cooperation among the leading European powers, but not of economic cooperation (Dawes, Young) or global participation (Kellogg).
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the Kellogg–Briand Pact a failure?
A: For: no sanctions or machinery; exempted self-defence, which each state could define; Britain and the USA reserved interests; war was not defined, so Japan's attack on Manchuria in 1931 was called an incident; it did not stop the aggression of the 1930s; it conflicted with the Covenant. Against: it was signed by fifteen states and then over sixty, including the USA and the Soviet Union; it expressed a new principle that war was illegal, which the Nuremberg trials later used; it was never intended to be enforced and was a statement of aims; it showed the extent of goodwill in 1928 and helped to bring the USA into the new order of treaties. Level 5 judges against a clear criterion, e.g. "what it set out to achieve", concluding that as a means of preventing war it was a failure, but as a statement of principle and a sign of the improvement it had some value.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about Germany's attitude to the League of Nations.
A: Similarities: both see the League as useful to Germany (A: protection of Germans abroad and a platform as "spokesmen"; B: a change of attitude towards a Germany now "desired"); both are by Stresemann. Differences: A is private and calculating (the League serves German aims, and German policy must "finesse"), B is public and conciliatory (the League and Germany reconciled, with a complaint about being rejected in 1919); A is before entry, B is after. Explanation: A was written to a nationalist audience, the Crown Prince, in September 1925, to justify entry, and so stresses the benefits for German interests; B was spoken in 1927 at the receipt of a Nobel Peace Prize, to an international audience, after Germany had joined, and was aimed to present Germany as a willing member and to justify the policy of fulfilment. Level 4 explains the differences from the contexts and judges which is better evidence of Stresemann's real view (A of his private calculation, B of how he wished to be seen), noting that neither is a neutral source.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources support the view that by 1925 Europe's security rested on firm foundations?
H: Decide what "firm foundations" would mean: guarantees, economic stability, a shared willingness to compromise.
H: Look at C and D for the mechanisms and at A for the motives behind them.

A: Support: C sets out an actual mechanism (a breach to be taken at once to the League Council, with the guarantors to come to the help of the victim); D reflects the experts' confidence in Germany's capacity to recover and pay; B (Nobel lecture) shows Germany's welcome into the League. Challenge: A shows Stresemann using the League and the security pact for his own aims, finessing and avoiding decisions, which suggests that Germany's acceptance was tactical; D is conditional ("would be hopeless if") and depended on a political assumption; C only covers the western frontiers and its own Article 4 limits the immediate help to a "flagrant" breach; and none of the sources says anything about the east. Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How important was the role of the USA in the improvement of international relations in the years 1924–29?

:::recap
- **Turning point**: the failure of the Ruhr occupation and the hyperinflation; new leaders: Herriot and MacDonald (1924), Stresemann, Briand and Chamberlain.
- **Dawes Plan** (report 9 April 1924; in force 1 September 1924): 1 billion marks rising to 2.5 billion, no total, new Reichsmark and Reichsbank, 800 million gold mark loan, troops out of the Ruhr (1925); American loans began the circle of loans, reparations and war debts.
- **Stresemann**: fulfilment and peaceful revision; Germany's recovery was real but depended on short-term loans; his private aims (the letter of 7 September 1925) were revisionist in the east.
- **League entry** (8 September 1926): a condition of Locarno, equality, a platform for revision; Briand welcomed Germany on 10 September.
- **Young Plan** (7 June 1929): about 112 billion marks over 59 years to 1988; BIS; evacuation of the Rhineland by 30 June 1930 (Hague Conference, August 1929).
- **Geneva Protocol** (2 October 1924): arbitration, definition of the aggressor, sanctions; rejected by Britain (12 March 1925).
- **Locarno** (5–16 October 1925; signed 1 December): western frontiers guaranteed by Britain and Italy, arbitration treaties in the east; "spirit of Locarno"; the east not guaranteed.
- **Kellogg–Briand** (27 August 1928): fifteen signatories, over sixty by the 1930s; renounced war but no sanctions; self-defence excluded.
- **Powers**: Briand and Stresemann (Thoiry, 17 September 1926); Mussolini's Albania (Tirana 1926, 1927) and Hungary; the USSR: recognition 1924, Zinoviev letter, Treaty of Berlin (24 April 1926), ARCOS and the break (27 May 1927).
- **Reality or illusion**: real in the west and in finance; illusory in the east, on disarmament and in its dependence on the USA; the Crash (24 and 29 October 1929) and Stresemann's death (3 October 1929) ended it.
:::
