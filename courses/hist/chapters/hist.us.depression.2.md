## The impact of the Great Crash

### The drop in the value of shares and the crisis of confidence

The first impact of the Crash was on wealth. In the week to 29 October 1929 more than $30 billion of share values were lost, and about $14 billion on Black Tuesday alone. Federal government spending in a whole year was about $3 billion, so a single week wiped out many times the government's yearly budget. Share prices did not stop falling. The Dow reached 198.60 on 13 November 1929, rose to 294.07 in April 1930, and then fell almost without a break until it reached 41.22 on 8 July 1932, 89 per cent below the 1929 peak ([Crash, Depression and New Deal (1)](#hist.us.depression.1)).

:::def Crisis of confidence
A loss of belief by consumers, investors and businesses that the economy will recover. People who lose confidence save rather than spend, firms cancel investment and banks lend less, which makes the downturn worse.
:::

Few Americans owned shares, so the direct loss fell on a minority. The real damage came from confidence. Rich investors cut their spending, firms cancelled orders for machinery, and families who feared for their jobs stopped buying the durable goods that had driven the boom. Consumer spending on durable goods fell by about a fifth in 1930, and new car registrations fell sharply within months of the Crash. This was a wave of fear that spread from Wall Street across the economy.

### Bankruptcy for banks, insurance companies and individuals

**Banks.** Many banks had lent money to brokers and bought shares and bonds, or had set up affiliates that sold securities. When prices fell, these assets lost value, and when the borrowers could not repay, the banks lost their money. The Bank of United States in New York, with about 400,000 depositors, failed on 11 December 1930, which was the largest bank failure in American history to that date. A bank that closes takes its customers' savings with it, so banks' own difficulties started a chain of runs and failures that is described in the next section.

**Insurance companies.** Insurance companies invested their premiums in shares, bonds and mortgages. Falling prices cut the value of their investments, and from 1930 more policyholders borrowed against their policies or let them lapse as they lost their jobs. Some smaller companies failed, though the large life insurers survived the early 1930s.

**Individuals.** Investors who had bought on the margin were wiped out in a single day when brokers sold their shares to cover the loans. Investment trusts, which had borrowed to buy shares, collapsed: J.K. Galbraith's famous example is the Goldman Sachs Trading Corporation, whose shares fell from $104 in 1929 to under $2 in 1933. Stories of ruined speculators jumping from skyscrapers were largely myth; Galbraith found little evidence that suicides rose in New York in the autumn of 1929. For millions of people the Crash was a disaster because it came to them second-hand, through a lost job, a closed bank or a mortgage they could not pay.

### The demand for greater regulation

The Crash revealed how little the market was regulated, and public anger grew as more became known. A Senate committee on banking began investigating Wall Street practices in 1932; from January 1933 its chief counsel, **Ferdinand Pecora**, questioned leading bankers in public. The hearings showed that Charles Mitchell of the National City Bank had sold shares to his wife to claim a tax loss and had paid himself large bonuses, and that Albert Wiggin of the Chase National Bank had made money by selling his own bank's shares short as the Crash approached. Mitchell resigned in February 1933 and was tried (and acquitted) of tax evasion in 1934. The result was a wave of laws, passed after Roosevelt took office:
- the **Glass–Steagall Act** (Banking Act of 1933), which separated commercial banking from investment banking and created federal deposit insurance;
- the **Securities Act of 1933**, which made companies tell the truth about the shares they sold;
- the **Securities Exchange Act of 1934**, which created the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** to regulate the stock exchanges and prohibit manipulation and insider dealing.

You will study these in [the next chapter](#hist.us.depression.3). Here the point is that the Crash created the demand for regulation, and that Hoover, who disliked government interference, did little about it before he left office.

:::key The impact of the Great Crash: what to learn
1. **Shares and confidence**: huge paper losses (over $30 billion in a week), fear that cut spending and investment.
2. **Banks, insurers and individuals**: margin investors ruined; trusts and affiliates collapsed; banks lost on loans to brokers and on securities; the Bank of United States failed in December 1930.
3. **Regulation**: the Pecora hearings of 1933 exposed abuses and led to Glass–Steagall and the SEC.
4. The Crash hurt most through confidence and the banks, not because most families owned shares.
:::

## The impact of the Great Depression

### Mass unemployment

There was no official unemployment rate in the 1930s: the government did not measure it until later, and historians have reconstructed it. ### The collapse of industry and the deflation

Industrial production fell by about 47 per cent between 1929 and 1933, and real national output fell by about 30 per cent (Christina Romer, 2003; other series give about 27 per cent). Heavy industries were hit hardest: production of cars fell by about three quarters between 1929 and 1932, and the steel and construction industries worked at a small fraction of capacity. As firms closed, layoffs and wage cuts cut demand for everything else.

:::def Deflation
A general fall in prices. In the Depression, average prices fell by roughly a quarter or more between 1929 and 1933. Deflation sounds good for buyers, but it raises the real burden of debts, which are fixed in money, and it causes firms to cut wages, jobs and output.
:::

Deflation made the slump worse in three ways. First, debtors (farmers, homeowners, businesses) had to repay loans with money that was worth more, so many could not pay and went bankrupt. Second, buyers delayed buying in expectation of lower prices. Third, falling prices cut profits, which led to layoffs. The economist Irving Fisher described this in 1933 as a **debt-deflation** spiral.

### Banks and bank runs

Bank failures came in waves: in the autumn of 1930, in 1931 after the collapse of banks in Austria and Germany and Britain's departure from the gold standard, and in the winter of 1932–33. The Federal Reserve Bulletin's count of suspended banks (including private banks) is 1,350 in 1930, 2,293 in 1931, 1,453 in 1932 and about 4,000 in 1933, a total of 9,096 for the four years. Some banks later reopened, and different sources count a little differently. The deposits locked up in suspended banks rose from about $230 million in 1929 to $837 million in 1930, $1.68 billion in 1931 and $3.6 billion in 1933.

A bank run began with a rumour. Depositors queued to withdraw their savings in cash. A bank lends out most of its deposits, so it cannot pay everyone at once; to meet the demand it called in loans and sold assets at falling prices, and if it could not do so it closed. Each closure frightened customers of other banks, and the money supply, which includes deposits, shrank. A depositor in a failed bank lost savings. Where the banks of a town closed, businesses could not pay wages, so local trade stopped.

### Agricultural depression

Farmers had been in depression since 1920–21 ([Crash, Depression and New Deal (1)](#hist.us.depression.1)), but now prices collapsed. At the end of 1932 the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City reported that grain prices were the lowest of the century and wheat was the lowest in the history of the American grain trade: on 28 December 1932 wheat was quoted at 36¼ cents a bushel and corn at 20 cents. The government's own figures put cash farm income after expenses at about $4.9 billion in 1929 and $1.5 billion in 1932. Fixed debts and falling prices led to foreclosures, in which banks sold farms to recover loans. In Iowa and other Midwest states farmers organised: the **Farmers' Holiday Association**, led by Milo Reno, called a farmers' "holiday" in August 1932, and farmers blocked roads to stop produce going to market. Crowds stopped foreclosure sales, or bought a farm for a few cents ("penny auctions") and gave it back to the owner. In the Great Plains, drought from 1932 added to the misery (this is the Dust Bowl, covered in [the next chapters](#hist.us.depression.4)).

:::key The economic impact of the Depression
1. **Unemployment**: from about 3 per cent in 1929 to between about 21 and 25 per cent in 1933; over 12 million out of work in 1932.
2. **Industry**: production down by about 47 per cent; real output down by about 30 per cent.
3. **Deflation**: prices down by roughly a quarter; debts heavier.
4. **Banks**: 9,096 suspensions in 1930–33; no deposit insurance.
5. **Farms**: prices below the cost of production; income down by about 70 per cent from 1929 to 1932; foreclosures and protests.
:::

### The lack of welfare

Americans who lost their jobs had almost nothing to fall back on. There was no federal unemployment insurance, no federal old-age pension and no national relief system. Help came from families, churches, ethnic societies, private charities and local governments, and from the old **poor law**, which gave relief only to people who had used up their savings, and sometimes only in a poorhouse. These sources could not cope. By the middle of 1932 a quarter of the private charities in New York had closed, and in Atlanta relief for a needy family could be as little as $1.30 a week. Cities ran out of money as tax receipts fell: some stopped paying their teachers. States were nearly as bad. New York State, under Governor Franklin Roosevelt, set up the Temporary Emergency Relief Administration in September 1931, one of the first state programmes, but most states had nothing.

People survived by selling belongings, doubling up in shared homes, living on credit from shopkeepers and queuing at breadlines and soup kitchens. Hunger was real, and so was shame: relief was seen as charity, and many families put off asking for help until they were desperate.

### Hoovervilles

Families who lost their homes, because they could not pay rent or mortgages, built shacks from scrap wood, packing cases, tin and cardboard on empty land at the edge of cities. These shantytowns became known as **Hoovervilles**, a bitter joke at the president's expense; the Democratic publicity chief Charles Michelson is usually credited with popularising the term. Other names mocked Hoover too: "Hoover blankets" were newspapers used for warmth and "Hoover flags" were empty pockets turned out. The Hooverville in Seattle lasted from 1932 to 1937. Their importance for the history is political as well as human: they were visible, daily proof of failure, in sight of the people who still had jobs.

:::def Hooverville
A shantytown of shacks built by homeless and unemployed people in the early 1930s, named in mockery of President Hoover, whom many Americans blamed for the Depression.
:::

### Employment discrimination, families and minorities

The Depression hit everyone, but not equally. A useful way to judge who suffered most is to ask three questions: who lost work first, who had the least to fall back on, and who was shut out of help.

**African Americans.** Most Black Americans were poor before 1929: sharecroppers and tenants in the South, and unskilled workers in the northern cities to which many had moved. They were often "last hired, first fired". As jobs became scarce, white workers took jobs that had been left to Black workers, such as street cleaning and domestic service. In many northern cities Black unemployment was about double that of whites, and in some places it reached as high as half of the workforce. In Chicago and later Harlem campaigns of "Don't Buy Where You Can't Work" protested against stores in Black neighbourhoods that would not employ Black staff. Relief agencies in the South often gave less help to Black families, and the number of lynchings rose at times in the early 1930s. The sharecropping system, already exploitative, left tenants without income when cotton prices collapsed.

**Mexican Americans.** Many Mexican workers in the Southwest were seen as competing for scarce jobs. Federal and local officials ran a campaign of "repatriation": deportation raids in Los Angeles in 1931 and railway tickets offered to families on relief. Historians' estimates of the numbers sent to Mexico in the 1930s run from several hundred thousand to over a million (Francisco Balderrama and Raymond Rodríguez, *Decade of Betrayal*, 1995, put it at more than a million), and about 60 per cent are thought to have been United States citizens. The Mexican-born population of Arizona, California, New Mexico and Texas fell from 616,998 in 1930 to 377,433 in 1940. Hoover's government also restricted immigration generally in September 1930, and about 54,000 people were deported between 1930 and 1932.

**Women.** Women's experience was mixed. Jobs seen as "women's work", such as clerical work, teaching and domestic service, were hit less hard than heavy industry, so the share of women in paid work did not fall. But there was strong pressure on married women to give up jobs to men: many school boards would not employ married teachers, and Section 213 of the Economy Act of 1932 told federal departments, when they had to lay off staff, to dismiss first anyone whose husband or wife also worked for the government. Most of the people dismissed were women, and the rule was repealed in 1937. Women also carried the burden of making family budgets stretch.

**Families.** The Depression put heavy strain on family life. Marriage and birth rates fell, families doubled up, and children left school or went hungry as school budgets were cut. Some unemployed men, ashamed of being unable to provide, withdrew from family life, and tens of thousands of teenagers travelled the country on freight trains in search of work. Sociologists such as Mirra Komarovsky (*The Unemployed Man and His Family*, 1940) recorded how the loss of a father's authority reshaped households, and how mothers and children often took the strain.

:::key Who suffered most? Criteria for judging
1. **Loss of work**: unskilled and Black workers lost jobs first; heavy-industry towns had the highest unemployment.
2. **Resources to fall back on**: farmers and sharecroppers had debts and no savings; the old and the sick had no pensions.
3. **Exclusion from help**: Black and Mexican American families were often refused relief or pushed out; farm and domestic workers had no protection.
4. **Length of suffering**: the South and the farms never recovered fully before 1941.
A top-level answer to "which groups suffered most?" uses these criteria and shows how minorities suffered from both the Depression and discrimination.
:::

:::warn Do not claim that only the poor suffered, or that all suffered equally
Middle-class families lost savings in failed banks and professional workers lost jobs, and some industries (food, films, tobacco) held up better than others. The accurate claim is that the burden fell most heavily on those who had least, and that the poorest also had the least help.
:::

## The response of the Hoover administration

### Hoover and the conflicting views over solutions

**Herbert Hoover** was elected in 1928 as the man who could manage prosperity. He was a mining engineer and self-made millionaire who had organised food relief for Belgium during the First World War and for the Mississippi flood in 1927. He believed in what he called "rugged individualism" and voluntary co-operation: government should help by bringing people together, but not by taking over their responsibilities.

People disagreed about what should be done. Four views mattered.
1. **Let it run its course ("liquidationists").** Some conservatives held that the slump was a necessary cleansing of the excesses of the 1920s. Hoover wrote in his *Memoirs* (1952) that Treasury Secretary Mellon urged him to "liquidate" labour, stocks, farmers and real estate; Hoover says he rejected this advice.
2. **Voluntarism and limited action (Hoover).** Business and charities should maintain wages and relief, and the federal government should support credit and confidence, balance the budget and protect the currency, but not give direct relief to individuals.
3. **More federal action.** Democrats such as Senator Robert Wagner and Speaker John Nance Garner, and progressive Republicans such as Robert La Follette Jr., wanted federal money for relief and public works. Trade unionists and others wanted unemployment insurance. The idea that the government should spend to create demand was only starting; most economists and both parties still thought budgets must be balanced.
4. **Radical alternatives.** Socialists (Norman Thomas) and Communists organised hunger marches, such as the Ford Hunger March of March 1932, and demanded a bigger change.

### What Hoover did

Hoover did more than any earlier president had done in a depression. His measures fall into four groups.

1. **Business and labour.** After the Crash he called business leaders to the White House in November 1929 and secured pledges not to cut wages or lay off workers, and from labour not to demand rises. At first wages did hold up, but as profits collapsed in 1930–31 the pledges failed. In the autumn of 1930 he set up the President's Emergency Committee for Employment, and in August 1931 the President's Organization for Unemployment Relief under Walter Gifford, to co-ordinate private charity. He also pushed up spending on public works, including the great dam on the Colorado River later named after him.
2. **Agriculture.** The **Agricultural Marketing Act** (June 1929) set up the Federal Farm Board with $500 million to lend to farm co-operatives and to buy up surpluses to hold prices. But farmers kept producing, so the surpluses grew faster than the Board could buy them, and when it ran out of money prices fell again. The Smoot–Hawley Tariff (June 1930), meant to protect farmers, did them little good because it hurt their exports.
3. **Banks and credit.** The **Reconstruction Finance Corporation** (22 January 1932) lent public money to banks, insurance companies, railroads and farm credit organisations, to stop failures. It was the largest government rescue in peacetime. The Glass–Steagall Act of February 1932 made it easier for the Federal Reserve to expand credit. The **Federal Home Loan Bank Act** (22 July 1932) set up twelve regional banks to lend to building and loan associations and to help owners keep their homes. Hoover also proposed a one-year **moratorium** on war debts and reparations (June 1931).
4. **Relief and public works.** After refusing for two years, Hoover signed the **Emergency Relief and Construction Act** (21 July 1932), under which the RFC could lend up to $300 million to states for relief and $1.5 billion for self-liquidating public works such as toll bridges. He had vetoed a larger Democratic bill (the Garner–Wagner bill, about $2.1 billion, on 11 July 1932) as unsound.

:::def Reconstruction Finance Corporation (RFC)
A government agency set up in January 1932 with $500 million of capital and the power to borrow $1.5 billion more, to lend to banks, insurance companies, railroads and other firms to prevent their collapse. It was Hoover's main anti-Depression measure; Roosevelt's New Deal expanded it.
:::

:::def Voluntarism
Hoover's belief that Americans should meet the crisis by voluntary co-operation, with business keeping wages and jobs, charities and local governments giving relief, and the federal government acting only to support credit and confidence, not to give direct relief or manage the economy.
:::

### The limits of Hoover's response

Hoover's own explanation of his refusal to fund relief is clear. On 3 February 1931 he said that the issue was not whether people would go hungry or cold, but "the best method by which hunger and cold shall be prevented": voluntary giving and local responsibility, or appropriations from the Federal Treasury. He feared that federal relief would destroy the spirit of mutual aid, create a dependent population and unbalance the budget. His policy also followed the orthodoxy of the time: in June 1932, with the economy shrinking, he signed a Revenue Act that raised taxes (the top rate of income tax went from 25 to 63 per cent), because both parties believed that balanced budgets were essential to confidence.

Critics point to four weaknesses.
- **Scale.** The RFC's lending was cautious and aimed at banks, not at borrowers or workers. Fiorello La Guardia, a Republican congressman from New York, called it a "millionaire's dole". The loan of $90 million to the Central Republic Bank of Chicago, whose former president was Charles Dawes, Coolidge's Vice President, drew angry criticism.
- **Voluntarism failed.** Private charities and local governments ran out of money by 1932.
- **Timing.** Direct relief came late (July 1932) and in the form of loans to states, not grants.
- **Contradictory policy.** He signed Smoot–Hawley, balanced the budget and raised taxes while the economy was falling.

:::key Hoover's response: the case for and against
**For** (he did a lot): conferences and committees; the Farm Board; Smoot–Hawley (meant to protect jobs); the moratorium; the RFC (the largest peacetime rescue to that date); the Home Loan Banks; the Emergency Relief and Construction Act; more public works. **Against** (it was too little, too late, wrongly aimed): voluntarism failed; no direct federal relief until July 1932 and then only loans; the Farm Board could not stop overproduction; Smoot–Hawley hurt trade; the tax increase of 1932 cut demand; the RFC helped banks first.
**Judgement:** he was neither passive nor adequate. He broke new ground, and the New Deal built on the RFC and the Home Loan Banks, but he was held back by his own principles and by the scale of the collapse.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- Many Democrats at the time, and many textbooks since, portrayed Hoover as a do-nothing president, with the Hooverville as his symbol.
- **Joan Hoff Wilson**, in *Herbert Hoover: Forgotten Progressive* (1975), argued that Hoover was an activist who anticipated much of the New Deal in his belief in expert planning, co-operation and government help to business, and that he was blamed unfairly.
- **David Kennedy**, in *Freedom from Fear* (1999), describes a man of great ability whose response was real but limited by a belief in voluntarism, and who was unable to adapt when the crisis overran his ideas.
- **Robert McElvaine**, in *The Great Depression* (1984), focuses on the human cost and on how the "forgotten man" turned to Roosevelt.
Use the debate to show that "Hoover did nothing" is not accurate, and then argue whether what he did was enough.
:::

### The treatment of the Bonus Marchers

The veterans of the First World War had been promised a bonus in 1924 in the form of **Adjusted Service Certificates**, which would be paid in 1945. When the Depression came, they asked to be paid at once. In 1931 Congress let them borrow against their certificates, over Hoover's veto, but in 1932 a bill sponsored by Congressman Wright Patman for immediate payment was introduced.

In May 1932 a group of about 400 veterans left Portland, Oregon, led by a former sergeant, **Walter W. Waters**, to put their case in Washington. By the summer they were joined by others from across the country. The National Park Service puts the number of veterans at between 10,000 and 20,000, and other estimates, counting families, reach about 43,000. They called themselves the **Bonus Expeditionary Force**, but the press called them the **Bonus Army**. Most lived in a camp of shacks and tents on the Anacostia Flats, across the river from the Capitol, kept in order by their own officers and supported by the sympathetic police chief, Pelham Glassford.

The House passed the Patman bill by 211 votes to 176 on 15 June, but the Senate rejected it by 62 votes to 18 on 17 June. Most of the veterans stayed. Congress adjourned in July after voting money for fares home for those who wanted to go. On **28 July** the Washington police tried to clear some empty government buildings; a clash followed in which police shot two veterans, William Hushka and Eric Carlson, who died. The District commissioners asked for federal help, and Hoover ordered the Army to clear the veterans from the city centre. The Army Chief of Staff, **General Douglas MacArthur**, with infantry, cavalry and tanks, drove the veterans out with bayonets and tear gas and, going further than Hoover had ordered, crossed the Anacostia bridge that evening and burned the camp. Hoover had told MacArthur not to cross the bridge; Major Dwight Eisenhower, MacArthur's aide, had advised him not to appear personally. George Patton took part.

Hoover's statement the next day said that "a challenge to the authority of the United States Government has been met, swiftly and firmly". MacArthur told reporters that the crowd was "animated by the essence of revolution". Newspapers were divided: an Associated Press survey of 30 papers found that 21 more or less supported the government, but others, such as the *Chicago Herald and Examiner*, condemned it. Hoover took responsibility for the action. The images of soldiers burning the camp of unarmed veterans hurt him badly in the election campaign, and Congress paid the bonus in 1936 over Roosevelt's veto.

:::key The Bonus Army: what to learn
1. **Cause**: veterans wanted their 1945 bonus paid at once because of unemployment and hunger.
2. **Events**: May–July 1932, a camp on Anacostia Flats; the Senate rejected the Patman bill on 17 June; the clearance of 28 July, with two veterans killed by police and the camp burned by the Army.
3. **Responsibility**: Hoover ordered the Army to clear the centre; MacArthur went beyond his orders; Hoover accepted responsibility.
4. **Significance**: it symbolised the government's lack of sympathy for the unemployed and cost Hoover votes, even though many newspapers backed him.
:::

### The degree of support for welfare

Hoover believed in private charity and local government. Until July 1932 there was no federal money for direct relief, and his Emergency Committee and Relief Organization encouraged giving instead. He did accept that the federal government must act when states could not, but not in the form of a "dole". The result was that federal support for welfare was the among the smallest of any major industrial democracy: Britain had unemployment insurance from 1911, and Germany from 1927. The RFC's relief loans were small compared with the need, and the Emergency Relief and Construction Act of 1932 was a first step on a long road that Roosevelt would travel faster.

## The election of 1932 and the New Deal coalition

### The campaign

The Republicans renominated Hoover without enthusiasm. The Democrats met in Chicago in late June and early July. **Franklin D. Roosevelt**, Governor of New York since 1929, had to win a two-thirds majority and did so on the fourth ballot, after the Speaker, John Nance Garner of Texas, and his supporters switched to him; Garner became the vice-presidential candidate. Roosevelt then did what no nominee had done: he flew to Chicago and accepted in person on **2 July 1932**. He said he was breaking "the absurd traditions" of waiting to be formally told. He pledged "a new deal for the American people", and spoke of the men and women "forgotten in the political philosophy of the Government of the last years".

Roosevelt was a wealthy New Yorker from a patrician family. He had been paralysed by polio in 1921 and returned to politics, and as governor of the largest state he had set up relief through the Temporary Emergency Relief Administration. In a radio speech in April 1932 he spoke of "the forgotten man at the bottom of the economic pyramid", and at Oglethorpe University in May he called for "bold, persistent experimentation": try a method, and if it fails, try another. Yet his platform was cautious. The Democrats promised to cut federal spending by a quarter and balance the budget, and to repeal Prohibition, which was unpopular in cities.

Hoover campaigned unwillingly, defending his record and warning that Democratic policies would be ruinous: in his speech at Madison Square Garden on 31 October he said that the grass would grow in the streets of a hundred cities. He was heckled, and his train was pelted with eggs and tomatoes in some towns. Socialist Norman Thomas won about 880,000 votes and the Communist William Foster about 100,000.

### The result

| | Roosevelt (Democrat) | Hoover (Republican) |
|---|---|---|
| Popular vote | 22,818,740 (57.4%) | 15,760,425 (about 40%) |
| Electoral votes | 472 | 59 |
| States won | 42 | 6 |

Hoover won only six states: Pennsylvania, Connecticut, Delaware, Vermont, New Hampshire and Maine. Roosevelt carried more counties than any candidate before him.

:::key Why Roosevelt won in 1932
1. **The Depression**: with a quarter of workers jobless, voters blamed the party in power. The Republicans were identified with the policies of the 1920s and the Crash.
2. **Hoover's record and image**: voluntarism seemed cold; Hoovervilles; Smoot–Hawley; the tax rise; the Bonus Army; and an unhappy campaign.
3. **Roosevelt's appeal**: optimism, the promise of change, a radio manner that people trusted, and a record in New York. His policies were vague, so he could win votes from many groups.
4. **Organisation and issues**: a united Democratic party after the nomination; the call to end Prohibition; some progressive Republicans (such as George Norris) supported him.
**Judgement:** the Depression decided the election; Hoover's record and Roosevelt's appeal explain the size of the victory.
:::

### The New Deal coalition

The 1932 victory brought together a new alliance of voters behind the Democratic Party. It was not complete in 1932, and it took shape over 1932–36. By 1936 Roosevelt won 46 of 48 states.

:::def New Deal coalition
The alliance of groups that voted for Franklin Roosevelt and the Democratic Party from 1932, including white Southerners, urban workers and ethnic minorities, farmers, trade unionists and, from the mid-1930s, African Americans. It made the Democrats the majority party until the 1960s.
:::

- **Southern whites** had voted Democratic since Reconstruction ([Civil War and Reconstruction (4)](#hist.us.civilwar.4)) and stayed loyal, in part because Roosevelt did not challenge segregation.
- **Urban workers and ethnic minorities** (Irish, Italian and Polish Catholics, Jews) had been mobilised by the Democratic candidate Al Smith in 1928 and moved decisively to Roosevelt in the cities hit hardest by unemployment.
- **Farmers** of the Midwest and West, angry at Republican policy, turned to the Democrats.
- **Trade unionists** backed Roosevelt especially after the Wagner Act of 1935.
- **African Americans**, who had voted Republican as the party of Lincoln, still gave most of their votes to Hoover in 1932, but moved to the Democrats in 1934–36 because of relief and jobs, and in 1934 Chicago elected the first Black Democrat to the House, Arthur Mitchell.
- **Reformers, women and intellectuals** found a place in an administration that included the first woman in the cabinet, Frances Perkins, and Eleanor Roosevelt as a champion of the poor.

:::note A coalition of contradictions
The coalition held together groups with opposed interests: white Southerners who supported segregation and African Americans who suffered from it; city workers who wanted high wages and farmers who wanted high food prices. The main glue was the Depression and the New Deal's benefits. In the long term the contradictions broke the coalition apart, especially over civil rights from the 1960s, but until then it won most presidential elections.
:::

## Weighing the evidence: two questions to be ready for

**Did Hoover do nothing?** No: he did a good deal, and more than any earlier president in a depression. The correct questions are whether it was *enough* and whether it was *well aimed*. A level 5 answer separates **what Hoover did** (the list above), **the scale of the problem** (a quarter of the workforce unemployed, 9,096 bank suspensions), and **his principles** (voluntarism; balanced budgets). It then judges that his measures were the beginning of federal action but were too limited, too late and, in places (tariffs, taxes, the Bonus Army), harmful.

**Which groups suffered most?** Use the criteria in the key box: loss of work, resources, exclusion and length of suffering. African Americans, tenant farmers and unskilled industrial workers have the strongest claim. Minorities were the "main victims" in the sense that they suffered both the Depression and discrimination, but the largest numbers who lost work were white industrial workers.

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Roosevelt won the presidential election of 1932.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Roosevelt won because of the Depression. Lots of people were unemployed and they blamed Hoover. Roosevelt promised a New Deal. He was also a good speaker. Hoover was unpopular because of the Bonus Army."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence states a fact without explaining how it led to Roosevelt's victory. There are no figures or dates. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was the Depression itself. By 1932 about a quarter of the workforce was unemployed, thousands of banks had closed and farm prices were at their lowest, and voters blamed the Republicans, who had been in power since 1921 and who had presided over the Crash. A second reason was Hoover's record. His policy of voluntarism and the refusal to give direct federal relief until July 1932 made him seem uncaring, and the clearing of the Bonus Army by troops on 28 July 1932 turned the unemployed against him."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add a third factor and link them:

"A third reason was Roosevelt's own appeal. At the Democratic convention he flew to Chicago to accept in person, pledging 'a new deal for the American people'; he spoke for the 'forgotten man', and as Governor of New York he had set up relief in 1931. His platform was vague enough to attract many groups, from Southern whites to city workers and farmers, and he promised the repeal of Prohibition, which won votes in the cities.

These reasons were linked. The Depression created the anger; Hoover's record and the Bonus Army gave voters a person to blame; and Roosevelt's optimism offered them something to vote for. The decisive reason was the Depression, because a Republican president would have been blamed for it whatever he had done, and Roosevelt's success, 472 electoral votes to 59, was as much a vote against Hoover as for Roosevelt's programme, which was cautious and deliberately unspecific. Roosevelt's appeal explains why the victory was so large, but the Depression explains why he won."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the Depression created the anger, Hoover's record directed it, Roosevelt gave it hope) and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

During the war we necessarily turned to the Government to solve every difficult economic problem. We were challenged with the choice between the American system of rugged individualism and a European philosophy of diametrically opposed doctrines, which would weaken self-government and individual initiative.

*Adapted from the speech by Herbert Hoover, "Principles and Ideals of the United States Government", New York, 22 October 1928. The phrases "the American system of rugged individualism" and "diametrically opposed doctrines" are quoted.*

**Source B**

This is not an issue as to whether people shall go hungry or cold in the United States. It is solely a question of the best method by which hunger and cold shall be prevented. The question is whether the American people will maintain the spirit of charity and mutual self-help through voluntary giving and the responsibility of local government, or whether they will turn to appropriations out of the Federal Treasury.

*Adapted from a statement by President Herbert Hoover to the press about federal funds for relief, 3 February 1931. The first two sentences are quoted; the third is a shortened paraphrase.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about Hoover's attitude to the role of the federal government.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 5 marks).** "Both sources are by Hoover and both say that the government should not do too much. Source A is about the economy and Source B is about relief. They are from different dates, so Hoover might have changed his mind."

*Commentary.* One similarity, one difference, neither supported with words from the sources. The final sentence is speculation. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The two sources show remarkable consistency. In Source A Hoover contrasts 'the American system of rugged individualism' with an alternative that 'would weaken self-government and individual initiative', and in Source B he distinguishes 'voluntary giving and the responsibility of local government' from 'appropriations out of the Federal Treasury'. In both he describes a choice between individual and community responsibility on one hand and the state on the other, and in both he chooses the first. Both are also public statements by the president or candidate meant to persuade, and both frame the choice in terms of values (initiative, charity, self-help) rather than money.

There are differences of subject and tone. Source A is general and philosophical, about the role of government in the economy in peacetime, and sets America against Europe: it warns about a danger. Source B is specific and practical, a response to a particular demand for money for relief, and it begins by denying that anyone wants people to go hungry. Source A is looking ahead to what Hoover will avoid; Source B is defending a decision that has real consequences for millions.

The context explains both. Source A was delivered in the last weeks of the 1928 election campaign, in Madison Square Garden against the Democratic candidate Al Smith, when the economy was booming and he was winning easily; the rhetoric was cheap, since it described a government that was not expected to be tested. His reference to the war points to his own experience as wartime Food Administrator, which taught him both the power of government and the risk of keeping it. Source B comes two years into the Depression, when the Senate had voted $25 million of federal money for Red Cross relief and Hoover was resisting. The statement is defensive: its opening sentence answers critics who said he did not care. It shows that his philosophy survived the test of a crisis, but its weakness as evidence is that it is a press statement defending a position in a political argument, and it does not show what he did in practice, since the RFC and the farm bill of 1929 show a more active government than either source suggests. As evidence for Hoover's principles the two sources are consistent and valuable; as evidence of his actual policy they are incomplete."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the 1928 campaign, the 1931 relief debate) and through each source's purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Hoover did nothing to tackle the Depression.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which side is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For (agree): voluntarism and no direct federal relief until July 1932; the 1931 statement; vetoed the Garner–Wagner bill; the RFC helped banks and railroads, not people; the Revenue Act raised taxes; Smoot–Hawley; the Bonus Army.
- Against (disagree): conferences of November 1929; Farm Board ($500 million); moratorium; RFC ($500 million + $1.5 billion borrowing); Home Loan Bank Act; Emergency Relief and Construction Act; public works.
- Judgement: he did not do nothing, but he did not do enough, and some of what he did made things worse. Criterion: measures set against the scale of the problem and the alternatives available.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Hoover did nothing to tackle the Depression. He believed in rugged individualism and said that relief should come from charity and not from the Federal Treasury. In February 1931 he said the question was whether people would use voluntary giving or appropriations from the government. Even in 1932 he vetoed the Garner–Wagner relief bill. Unemployment rose from about 3 per cent in 1929 to about 25 per cent in 1933 and 9,096 banks closed. He also sent the army against the Bonus Army in July 1932. This shows that he did nothing."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts what the paragraph has not shown: that the lack of federal relief means that he did "nothing". An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with the other side merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the claim that Hoover did nothing is false, because he did more than any previous president in a depression. In November 1929 he called business leaders to the White House and secured pledges to keep up wages, and he set up committees to organise relief. The Agricultural Marketing Act of June 1929 set up a Farm Board with $500 million to buy surpluses. In 1931 he proposed a moratorium on war debts to ease the European crisis, and in January 1932 he created the Reconstruction Finance Corporation, with $500 million in capital and the right to borrow $1.5 billion, to lend to failing banks and railroads. In July 1932 he signed the Home Loan Bank Act and the Emergency Relief and Construction Act, which allowed loans of up to $300 million to states for relief and $1.5 billion for public works. The RFC became a model for the New Deal, which expanded it, and Joan Hoff Wilson has argued that he was a forgotten progressive who anticipated much of what Roosevelt did.

The real question is whether this was enough, and here the case against Hoover is strong. The measures were small beside the problem: unemployment reached about a quarter of the workforce, and the RFC's relief loans came only in July 1932, three years after the Crash. The Farm Board could not stop overproduction, and its purchases ran out as prices fell to 36¼ cents for wheat. His belief in balanced budgets led him to raise taxes in June 1932, which reduced demand, and Smoot–Hawley (1930) damaged trade. His principle that relief was a matter for charity and local government failed in practice because cities and charities ran out of money, and he vetoed a larger relief bill on 11 July 1932. His reputation was sealed when troops cleared the Bonus Army on 28 July.

Overall, the statement is an exaggeration. Hoover acted on a scale that was new in peacetime, and several of his measures were the foundations of the New Deal. But what he did was limited by his principles and aimed first at banks and business rather than at the jobless, and some of it made the slump worse. The fair verdict is that he did something, but not enough, not soon enough and not in the right place, and that his failure to give direct relief is the strongest reason why he lost in 1932."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (what he did measured against the scale of the problem) and distinguishes "nothing" from "not enough". With an opening paragraph on the case for the statement, this is Level 5 (19–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Minorities suffered most in the Great Depression.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** The same Table B levels as above: Level 5 (17–20) needs analysis of both sides and a clear comparative judgement.

**Plan.** For: African Americans (unemployment about twice the national rate, up to half in some cities, "last hired, first fired", discrimination in relief, sharecroppers); Mexican Americans (repatriation of several hundred thousand to over a million, about 60 per cent citizens). Against: the largest numbers of unemployed were white industrial workers; farmers of all races lost income (income down by about 70 per cent); women's experience was mixed; the elderly had no pension; the middle class lost savings. Criterion: proportion who lost work, resources to fall back on, and exclusion from help.

**A Level 5 paragraph (the judgement).** "Overall, minorities suffered most, but by a particular route. Measured by the share who lost their jobs, African Americans were hit hardest, as their unemployment was about double the national rate and in some northern cities reached about half, and Mexican Americans were additionally forced out of the country by campaigns of repatriation that sent several hundred thousand, and by some estimates over a million, people to Mexico, about 60 per cent of them American citizens. They also had the fewest resources, since many were already poor tenants or unskilled workers, and they were often refused relief. Against this, most of the unemployed, by number, were white industrial workers, and white tenant farmers and small farmers, whose income fell by about 70 per cent, also faced ruin. But these groups were not shut out of help in the way minorities were: the discrimination which made the Depression worse for Black and Mexican American families, in jobs, relief and, later, in the New Deal's exclusions of farm and domestic workers, is the factor that makes the statement mostly true. The statement is right about severity and exclusion, but wrong if it implies that the Depression was mainly a minority experience."

*Commentary.* A clear criterion, evidence on both sides, and a comparative judgement that qualifies the claim. Level 5.

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

A challenge to the authority of the United States Government has been met, swiftly and firmly. Government cannot be coerced by mob rule.

*Adapted from the statement by President Herbert Hoover on the Bonus Army, 29 July 1932. The two sentences are quoted from separate parts of the statement.*

**Source B**

That mob down there was a bad looking mob. It was animated by the essence of revolution. I call them insurrectionists. Few indeed of those cleared were veteran soldiers. The President played it pretty fine in waiting to the last minute, but he didn't have much margin.

*Adapted from General Douglas MacArthur's remarks to reporters on the night of 28 July 1932, as recorded in the American Presidency Project. The sentences are quoted from the news conference but the order has been changed.*

**Source C**

President Hoover chose the course that Lincoln chose, that presidents have always chosen.

*Adapted from an editorial in the Ohio State Journal, Columbus, July 1932, quoted by the National Park Service.*

**Source D**

The attack on the Bonus Army was sheer stupidity, without parallel in American annals.

*Adapted from an editorial in the Chicago Herald and Examiner, July 1932. The phrases "sheer stupidity" and "without parallel in American annals" are quoted by the National Park Service; the rest is a paraphrase.*

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that the government was right to use force against the Bonus Army?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to help interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (a challenge to government, "mob rule"), B (revolution, "insurrectionists"), C (precedent). Challenge: D ("sheer stupidity"). Qualify: B itself says Hoover "played it pretty fine", which suggests he delayed; and none of the sources mentions that the veterans were unarmed, that two were shot by police, or that the camp was burned.

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: Hoover's justification of his own decision, the day after. Its purpose was to defend himself and to shape opinion, so it exaggerates ("mob rule"); but it is strong evidence of how the government wanted the action seen. My knowledge qualifies it: most of the marchers were peaceful, their camp was orderly, and the police chief had been sympathetic; the violence on 28 July began when police tried to clear buildings.
- **B**: MacArthur's late-night remarks, hours after the action. He had gone beyond Hoover's orders by crossing the bridge to clear the main camp, and he had a motive to justify himself. His claim that "few indeed" of those cleared were veterans is not supported by the usual estimates, which put the veterans in the tens of thousands, so on facts it is weak. But it is good evidence of how the military leadership thought of the marchers, and of his belief in a revolutionary threat that other evidence (the Communist party had only a small part in the march) does not support.
- **C**: an editorial from a Columbus paper, a statement of partisan support that compares Hoover with Lincoln, who used force against rebellion. It is a one-line opinion, not evidence of what happened, but it is typical: an Associated Press survey of 30 newspapers found 21 more or less supported the government's action.
- **D**: an editorial in a Chicago paper that supported the Democrats and attacked Hoover; the hyperbole ("without parallel") is typical of political journalism. But it also expresses a reaction that grew as pictures of the burning camp appeared and may be a better guide to how the action damaged Hoover in the election.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A, B and C support the view that the use of force was right, but they are all from people who needed to justify or applaud it. Hoover in Source A presents the action as the defence of the government's authority against 'mob rule', and MacArthur in Source B goes further, claiming that the marchers were insurrectionists 'animated by the essence of revolution'. Source C compares Hoover with Lincoln. But the evidence for their central claim is weak. B's claim that few of those cleared were veterans contradicts the usual estimates that the marchers were mainly veterans; the camp had been orderly under General Glassford; and the only deaths on 28 July were two veterans shot by police. Hoover in fact told MacArthur not to cross the Anacostia bridge, and MacArthur's burning of the camp went beyond his orders, which suggests that even the government's own account is not the whole story. Source D, with its attack on 'sheer stupidity', challenges the view, and reflects the revulsion that grew, though it is a partisan editorial. The press was divided, with 21 of 30 papers surveyed supporting the government, so C may be the more typical contemporary reaction, and D the more lasting one. On balance, the sources support the government's right to clear buildings and to end an occupation of the capital, but they do not justify the scale of the force or the burning of the camp, and the political verdict of the 1932 election was against Hoover. The sources agree with the view only to a limited extent."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of July 1932; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; and the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "To what extent was Hoover responsible for the Depression in the United States?" [20]

A student wrote:

"Hoover was president when the Crash happened in 1929. He was famous for saying that prosperity was just around the corner but it never came. He did not give any money to the unemployed, so people lived in Hoovervilles, which were named after him. He also sent the army against the Bonus Army. Roosevelt won the election in 1932 and this shows that people blamed Hoover for the Depression. So Hoover was completely responsible."

**The level.** There are some accurate points (Hoovervilles, the Bonus Army, the election), but the paragraph treats **blame** as **cause**: it argues that Hoover was blamed, not that he caused the Depression. It repeats a saying ("prosperity is just around the corner") that no reliable record shows Hoover using, and it asserts that he gave no help, which is wrong. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description, few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, separate causes from responses, and use only evidence that proves it. "Hoover was responsible for some of the Depression's severity but not for its origins. The causes were the weaknesses of the 1920s (farm debt, unit banks, unequal incomes), the Crash and the Fed's mistakes, which began before he took office in March 1929; recession began in August 1929. However, his own policies made things worse in three ways: he signed the Smoot–Hawley Tariff in June 1930, which led to retaliation and a fall in world trade of about two thirds; he raised taxes in June 1932 to balance the budget; and he refused direct federal relief until July 1932. So he was responsible for deepening the Depression, rather than causing it." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: How much did share values fall in the week of the Crash, and why did the effects spread beyond shareholders?
A: More than $30 billion was lost in the week to 29 October 1929 (about $14 billion on Black Tuesday). The effects spread through lost confidence, margin calls, and losses at banks and investment trusts that held shares or had lent to brokers.
Q: Give the unemployment rate in 1929 and 1933, and explain why estimates of 1933 differ.
A: About 3.2 per cent in 1929; 24.9 per cent in 1933 (Lebergott) or 20.6 per cent (Darby). Lebergott counts people on emergency work relief as unemployed, Darby counts them as employed.
Q: Give three reasons why bank failures made the Depression worse.
A: Depositors lost savings; the money supply shrank; surviving banks lent less; local businesses lost credit and could not pay wages; fear caused runs on other banks (9,096 suspensions in 1930–33, no deposit insurance).
Q: What is deflation and why was it harmful?
A: A general fall in prices. It raised the real burden of debts, led firms to cut wages and jobs, and caused buyers to postpone spending; the debt-deflation spiral.
Q: What was a Hooverville?
A: A shantytown built by homeless people from scrap on the edge of a city, named after President Hoover as a mockery of his failure to help.
Q: Name four things Hoover did to combat the Depression.
A: Any four of: conferences with business and labour (November 1929); the Federal Farm Board (June 1929); Smoot–Hawley (June 1930); the war-debt moratorium (June 1931); the RFC (January 1932); Federal Home Loan Bank Act (July 1932); Emergency Relief and Construction Act (July 1932).
Q: Why did the Bonus Army go to Washington, and what happened on 28 July 1932?
A: Veterans wanted the bonus due in 1945 paid at once because of unemployment. After the Senate rejected the Patman bill (17 June), police clashed with veterans on 28 July and shot two dead; Hoover ordered the Army to clear the veterans; MacArthur drove them out with tanks and tear gas and burned their camp.
Q: Give the result of the 1932 election.
A: Roosevelt won 57.4 per cent of the popular vote and 472 electoral votes to Hoover's 59; Hoover won six states.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why so many American banks failed between 1930 and 1933.
A: Indicative content: banks had lent to brokers and held shares and bonds that lost value; farm debts could not be repaid; the system was made up of thousands of small unit banks with no branches to spread risk; there was no deposit insurance, so rumours caused bank runs; failures reduced the money supply and credit; the Federal Reserve did not act as lender of last resort; the international crisis of 1931 (Britain leaving the gold standard) led the Fed to raise rates. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: the weaknesses of the banks made them vulnerable, and fear and the Fed's inaction turned failures into panics, and it judges the decisive factor, such as the lack of deposit insurance and a lender of last resort.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Hoovervilles appeared in American cities in the early 1930s.
A: Indicative content: mass unemployment (about a quarter of the workforce in 1933) and wage cuts; evictions for unpaid rent and foreclosures of mortgages; no federal unemployment insurance or relief, and exhausted charities and city governments; migration of jobless people to cities; the public blamed Hoover (the name). Level 4 connects unemployment, lack of welfare and homelessness, and judges which was most important, such as the lack of welfare, since unemployment alone need not have led to homelessness if relief had existed.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Mass unemployment was the most serious effect of the Great Depression.' How far do you agree?
A: For: unemployment rose from about 3 per cent to between 21 and 25 per cent; over 12 million out of work in 1932; no insurance or relief; loss of income led to hunger, homelessness (Hoovervilles) and family strain; it lasted for years. Against: bank failures destroyed savings (9,096 suspensions) and credit; the farm depression and foreclosures; deflation; the loss of confidence in capitalism and democracy; the effect on minorities. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "which effect caused the greatest suffering and the greatest long-term change", and judges, e.g., that unemployment caused the most suffering but was itself partly caused by the banking collapse and deflation, so unemployment is the most serious effect but not the most fundamental.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Hoover's response to the Depression failed because he refused to give federal help to the unemployed.' How far do you agree?
A: For: no direct relief until July 1932, and then only loans; the 1931 statement and the veto of Garner–Wagner; voluntarism failed as charities and cities ran out of money; the RFC helped banks first; the Bonus Army showed lack of sympathy. Against: his other policies also failed (Farm Board, Smoot–Hawley, the 1932 tax rise, the Fed's money policy); the scale of the collapse was beyond any 1930s policy; the RFC and Home Loan Banks were significant; the world economy also collapsed; his image and communication. Level 5 judges, e.g., that the refusal of relief was the biggest political failure, but the economic failure came from tariffs, taxes and banking, so the statement is only partly true.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and D (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about Roosevelt's approach in 1932.
A: Similarities: both express a willingness to act and to experiment, without a detailed programme; both are public statements by Roosevelt to win support. Differences: A is a pledge of a "new deal" for the "forgotten" men and women (political, emotional, a slogan for the campaign); D is a practical statement of method, in a university address, that stresses trial and error and has no promise. A is a nomination acceptance with a mass audience (radio, convention), D a more reflective speech to students. Explanation: A had to unite the party and win votes; D was an invitation to think about government's role. Evidence value: both show his vagueness (a strength in winning votes, a weakness as evidence of policy); the Democratic platform was cautious (a balanced budget). A Level 4 answer explains the differences through purpose and audience and judges which is better evidence of what the New Deal would be.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources support the view that Roosevelt won the 1932 election because Hoover's response to the Depression had failed?
H: Sort the sources: which attack Hoover's response (C), which are by Hoover himself (B), which are by Roosevelt (A, D).
H: Use your own knowledge: what do the figures of 472 and 59 electoral votes tell you? What else helped Roosevelt?
H: Evaluate each source by its author and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: C (La Guardia, a Republican) attacks the RFC as help for the rich, which shows that criticism came from within Hoover's party; B shows a defensive Hoover who predicts disaster rather than offering a remedy; A and D show Roosevelt offering action against a background of failure. Challenge: A and D are vague and show that Roosevelt won by offering hope and flexibility, not by a better programme; the Democratic platform was cautious; B suggests Hoover still had a case (the fear of untested policies); C is the view of one man. With knowledge: Hoover's measures were substantial (RFC, Home Loan Banks), but unemployment was about a quarter and 9,096 banks had suspended; the Bonus Army and Hoovervilles; Roosevelt's personality; the end of Prohibition. Evaluation: A and B are campaign speeches (A has a motive to promise, B to warn); C is an opposition-minded Republican, but one who supported the principle of relief; D is a speech to students. Level 5 judges how far: the failure of Hoover's response, as seen in C and B, was the largest factor, but Roosevelt's appeal (A, D) explains why voters chose him rather than a third-party candidate, so the sources agree to a large extent but not completely.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent did the election of 1932 create the New Deal coalition?
H: List the groups in the coalition and ask when each joined.
H: Separate the vote against Hoover from a lasting change in party loyalty.
A: For: Roosevelt won 42 states and 57.4 per cent; the Democrats gained in cities, the Midwest and West as well as the South; the 1932 result brought urban workers, ethnic minorities and farmers to the party; it began a period of Democratic dominance. Against: much of the vote was against Hoover; Al Smith in 1928 had already won urban Catholics; African Americans still voted mainly Republican in 1932 and moved in 1934–36; unions joined after the Wagner Act (1935); the coalition was formed by the New Deal itself, and by the 1934 and 1936 elections (46 of 48 states in 1936). Level 5 judges that 1932 began the coalition, but it was made by what the New Deal delivered between 1933 and 1936, so the election was a necessary starting point, not the creation.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Historians have argued both that Hoover was a reactionary who did nothing and that he was a forgotten progressive. How far does the evidence of 1929–32 support either view?
H: Match each view to the evidence: what would a "reactionary" have done, and what would a "progressive" have done?
H: Ask what Hoff Wilson's argument leaves out: the effects of his policy on those it was meant to help.
A: Reactionary: voluntarism; refusal of direct relief; vetoes; balanced budgets; Smoot–Hawley; the Bonus Army. Progressive: the Farm Board; conferences and committees; the RFC and Home Loan Banks; public works; the moratorium; the Emergency Relief and Construction Act; his earlier career as organiser of relief. A* judgement: both labels miss the point; Hoover was a believer in expert-led voluntary cooperation (Hoff Wilson's progressivism), whose principles allowed action for business and banks but not for individuals, and the scale of the crisis revealed the limits of his philosophy; he was a transitional figure whose measures were adopted and extended by Roosevelt.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ 'The Depression changed American politics more than it changed American society.' How far does the evidence of 1929–33 support this view?
H: Name two changes in politics (the 1932 election, the coalition, the idea of federal responsibility) and two in society (families, minorities, attitudes to work and relief).
H: Ask which changes lasted and which were temporary by 1933.
A: Politics: Roosevelt's landslide; the Democratic majority; demand for regulation and for federal responsibility for welfare; the end of laissez-faire as orthodoxy; the Bonus Army and Hoover's fall. Society: mass unemployment and poverty; Hoovervilles; strain on families (lower marriage and birth rates); repatriation of Mexican Americans; discrimination against African Americans and women; loss of faith in business leaders. A* judgement: by 1933 the political change was clearer and more lasting, since the party system was reshaped, while the social effects were deep but mostly short-term during these years, except that they shaped attitudes to security, which the New Deal then turned into policy.

:::recap
- **Crash**: over $30 billion lost in a week; confidence and spending fell; margin investors and trusts ruined; Bank of United States failed (11 December 1930); the Pecora hearings of 1933 led to Glass–Steagall and the SEC.
- **Depression**: unemployment about 3 per cent (1929) to 21–25 per cent (1933); industrial output down by about 47 per cent; prices down by roughly a quarter; 9,096 bank suspensions in 1930–33; farm income down by about 70 per cent; grain prices at record lows.
- **Welfare and society**: no federal relief or insurance; charities and cities broke; Hoovervilles; African Americans (unemployment about double), Mexican Americans (repatriation), women (Section 213), families under strain.
- **Hoover**: voluntarism; conferences (1929); Farm Board; Smoot–Hawley; moratorium; RFC (22 January 1932); Home Loan Banks; Emergency Relief and Construction Act (July 1932); Revenue Act 1932 (taxes up); not nothing, but too little and too late.
- **Bonus Army**: 1932; Senate rejected the bill 17 June; cleared 28 July by troops under MacArthur; two veterans shot by police; the camp burned.
- **1932**: Roosevelt 57.4 per cent, 472 electoral votes to 59; the New Deal coalition of Southern whites, urban workers and ethnic groups, farmers, unions, and later African Americans.
- **Judgement**: Hoover did something but not enough; the Depression decided the election.
