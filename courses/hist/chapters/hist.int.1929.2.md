With model Paper 1 and Paper 2 answers built level by level.

:::why
The League of Nations was created in 1919 so that there would never be another world war. Twenty years later there was one. Between those dates the League faced three tests, in Asia, Africa and Europe, and failed each of them in a different way. Understanding how and why is the heart of this option: it shows whether the idea of collective security could ever have worked, and it explains why the democracies, the dictators and the USSR ended up making their own arrangements outside Geneva. It also trains the skill examiners reward most in an essay: separating causes that lay inside the League from causes that lay with the great powers that made it up.
:::

## Manchuria, 1931–33

### The Japanese conquest

By 1931 Japan held a lease on the South Manchurian Railway and had large investments in Manchuria, the Chinese region north of the Great Wall (chapter 1 explains why the army saw it as a "lifeline"). The slump had made the army's case stronger, and China, divided between the Nationalist government of Chiang Kai-shek and regional warlords, was weak.

On the night of 18 September 1931 there was an explosion on the railway line near Mukden. It did little damage (a train passed soon afterwards), but the Kwantung Army blamed Chinese saboteurs and used it as an excuse to seize Mukden and then the rest of southern Manchuria. Historians agree that Japanese staff officers planned the incident. The government in Tokyo, where the prime minister was weak, could not or would not control them. By early 1932 Japan held all the main cities. On 1 March 1932 it proclaimed an independent state of **Manchukuo**, with the last Chinese emperor, Puyi, as its head. It was a puppet: Japanese officials ran it.

### The League's response

China did not fight. It appealed to the League, under Article 11 of the Covenant, on 21 September 1931. What followed shows how the League's methods worked, and did not.
- **Delay and requests.** On 30 September the Council asked both sides to withdraw their troops. Japan said it would, but its army kept advancing.
- **A vote that bound no one.** On 24 October the Council voted 13 to 1 for a resolution that Japan should withdraw by 16 November. Japan was the one vote against, and Council decisions needed unanimity, so it was only advice. Japan ignored it.
- **Investigation.** On 10 December 1931 the Council agreed, with Japan's consent, to send a commission of five (from Britain, the USA, France, Germany and Italy), led by the Earl of Lytton. It reached the Far East in February 1932 and spent months in Japan, China and Manchuria. In the meantime Japan began a separate attack on Shanghai on 28 January 1932, which ended in a ceasefire in May.
- **The Lytton Report**, signed on 4 September 1932 and published on 2 October 1932. It said that the Japanese operations of 18–19 September 1931 could not be regarded as legitimate self-defence, and that Manchukuo had not been created by a genuine and spontaneous independence movement and could not have been formed without Japanese troops. But it was not wholly one-sided: it acknowledged that China had given Japan real grievances (boycotts, disorder and attacks on Japanese rights) and that Japan had special interests in Manchuria. It recommended that Manchuria become a self-governing region under Chinese sovereignty, demilitarised, with Japanese interests recognised.
- **The Assembly's decision.** Japan had recognised Manchukuo on 15 September 1932. On 24 February 1933 the Assembly adopted the report by 42 votes to 1 (Japan), with Siam abstaining. The head of the Japanese delegation, Matsuoka Yosuke, walked out. On 27 March 1933 Japan gave notice that it would leave the League. It invaded the neighbouring province of Jehol in the same months, and by the Tanggu Truce of 31 May 1933 China accepted a demilitarised zone south of the Great Wall.

The League never imposed sanctions on Japan, not even an arms embargo. The USA, which was not a member, acted separately: on 7 January 1932 Stimson announced that the USA would not recognise any situation or agreement brought about by means that broke the Pact of Paris (the Kellogg–Briand Pact of 1928, which you met in the 1919–29 chapters: states renounced war as a tool of policy). That became known as the **Stimson Doctrine**, but it was only a statement.

:::def Stimson Doctrine
The policy announced by the American Secretary of State, Henry Stimson, on 7 January 1932, that the USA would not recognise any situation, treaty or agreement brought about by means contrary to the Pact of Paris of 1928. It was non-recognition, not action.
:::

### Why did the League fail over Manchuria?

:::key Why the League failed to stop Japan
1. **Distance and priorities.** Manchuria was on the other side of the world from Geneva. Britain and France, the two powers that ran the League, were concerned with Europe, with the Depression, and with the Disarmament Conference that was just about to open.
2. **No force and no will to use it.** Economic sanctions on Japan, a major trading partner, were unattractive in a slump; military action meant a naval war in the Pacific, which Britain (whose Singapore base was unfinished) and France did not want. Each hoped that someone else, especially the USA, would take the lead.
3. **A great power.** Japan was a permanent member of the Council, with a veto. It could not be coerced like a small state.
4. **Slow procedures.** Commissions and reports took a year, during which Japan completed the conquest. By the time the Report came out, Manchukuo existed.
5. **Western ambiguity.** Some Western politicians thought that China was in disorder, that Japan had real grievances, and that a strong Japan could be a barrier against communism.
:::

The result was a lesson that all the dictators drew: a great power could break the Covenant and treaties of 1922 and 1928 and the League would do no more than write a report. Japan left, with its conquest intact. The historian Christopher Thorne, in *The Limits of Foreign Policy: the West, the League and the Far Eastern Crisis of 1931–1933* (1972), studied how far each Western government was limited in what it could do (by the slump, by its priorities in Europe, by distrust of its partners); that is a good approach to the question "could the League have stopped Japan?".

:::exam What this means for the key question
Manchuria was the League's first major failure and its model. Notice the pattern that repeats in 1935: a clear verdict on who was in the wrong (the Lytton Report, the Council's declaration on Italy), then no action to make the verdict count, because the powers whose action was needed (Britain and France) would not pay the price.
:::

## Abyssinia, 1935–36

### Why Italy attacked

Abyssinia (Ethiopia) was one of the few independent African states and, since 1923, a member of the League; Italy itself had sponsored its entry. Mussolini wanted to avenge Italy's defeat by the Ethiopians at Adwa in 1896 and to give Italy an empire in East Africa, where it already held Eritrea and Somaliland. The slump and the need for success to keep the regime popular (chapter 1) added pressure. He also thought the moment was good: he believed that Britain and France, worried about Hitler, would not stand in his way. In January 1935 the French Foreign Minister Laval reached agreements with him in Rome, and Mussolini took them to mean that France would not object to Italian action in Abyssinia; at Stresa in April, nothing was said about Africa.

### The League and the crisis

- **Walwal.** On 5 December 1934 Italian and Ethiopian troops clashed at the Walwal waterhole, inside Ethiopia. Italy demanded an apology and compensation, and Haile Selassie appealed to the League. The arbitrators eventually blamed neither side (4 September 1935).
- **Talks outside the League.** Britain and France tried to buy Mussolini off with offers of territory, in talks in Paris and in Geneva in the summer of 1935. In Britain the Peace Ballot of 1934–35 showed enormous public support for the League: of about 11.5 million people who answered, about ten million backed economic sanctions against an aggressor, and about 6.8 million backed military action if necessary. On 11 September the British Foreign Secretary, Sir Samuel Hoare, told the Assembly that Britain stood for the "collective maintenance of the Covenant in its entirety". Mussolini went on massing troops.
- **The invasion.** On 3 October 1935 Italian forces under General De Bono, and soon Marshal Badoglio, attacked from Eritrea without a declaration of war, with a force that grew to hundreds of thousands of men, aircraft, tanks and poison gas. The Ethiopians had few modern weapons.
- **Condemnation.** The League Council found that Italy had gone to war in breach of the Covenant, and in the Assembly most members supported it. This was the first time the League declared an aggressor.
- **Sanctions** came into force on 18 November 1935. Fifty states agreed to them; Austria, Hungary and Albania held back. They banned arms sales to Italy, loans and credits to Italy, imports from Italy, and exports to Italy of rubber, tin and some metals. But they did **not** include oil, coal or steel, the materials Italy's army needed most, and Britain did not close the Suez Canal to Italian ships. The USA, outside the League, went on selling oil to Italy, and by some accounts sold more than before.

:::def Sanctions
Economic or other penalties imposed by states on a state that has broken international law or a treaty, to force it to stop. Under Article 16 of the Covenant, members were to cut off trade and financial relations with a covenant-breaker.
:::

### The Hoare–Laval plan

While sanctions were in force, the British and French governments tried to end the war on terms Mussolini would accept. In December 1935 Hoare, on his way to Switzerland for a holiday, met Laval in Paris, and they agreed a plan to give Italy large parts of Abyssinia: land in Tigre in the north and in the Ogaden in the south, and a zone of Italian economic influence in the south, leaving the Emperor a smaller state with a narrow outlet to the sea. The plan was leaked in the press. In Britain, where the government had just fought and won the general election of 14 November 1935 on a promise to support the League, the public felt betrayed. Hoare resigned on 18 December 1935 and was replaced by Anthony Eden, and the plan was dropped.

That meant that the League had lost on both counts: its sanctions were too weak to stop the war, and its two leading members had been caught trying to reward the aggressor. Mussolini pressed on. His forces used mustard gas against soldiers and civilians, and they entered the capital Addis Ababa on 5 May 1936. The Emperor had left on 2 May. Mussolini announced the annexation of Ethiopia on 9 May 1936, and the King of Italy was proclaimed Emperor.

### The end of the crisis

Haile Selassie came to Geneva and addressed the Assembly on 30 June 1936. Italian journalists in the gallery whistled and booed, and had to be removed. He asked what states were willing to do for Ethiopia, said that it was "international morality" which was at stake, and ended: "What reply shall I have to take back to my people?" The Assembly did nothing. (A note on numbers: the course says fifty states agreed to sanctions, because fifty voted for them in 1935 while Austria, Hungary and Albania held back. The Emperor, in his speech, spoke first of "fifty nations" that had judged Italy the aggressor, and then of "fifty-two nations", meaning all the League members except Italy and Ethiopia, to whom he was appealing for help.) Britain, whose Chancellor of the Exchequer, Neville Chamberlain, called continuing sanctions "the very midsummer of madness", proposed that they be lifted, and on 15 July 1936 they were.

:::key Why the League failed to stop Italy
1. **Britain and France put their own interests first.** They wanted to keep Mussolini as an ally against Hitler (the Stresa Front, which collapsed anyway) and feared a war in the Mediterranean that they had no wish to fight alone.
2. **The sanctions were weak** because the powers did not want to risk war: no oil, no Suez closure, no military force.
3. **Secret diplomacy** (the Hoare–Laval plan) destroyed the League's moral authority and showed that the great powers would bargain over a member's territory.
4. **Absent powers**: the USA kept trading; Germany, no longer a member, supplied coal to Italy.
5. **Slow decisions**: ten months from Walwal to the invasion, and a war that was half won before sanctions had any effect.
:::

### The effects of the Abyssinian crisis

- **Italy** left the Stresa Front and drifted towards Germany: the Rome–Berlin Axis followed (chapter 1).
- **The League** was fatally discredited. In 1936 several small states (the Scandinavian countries, the Netherlands and Belgium among them) said that they could no longer rely on the League for their security, and would not feel bound to join sanctions again; Belgium went back to neutrality in October.
- **Britain and France** had gained nothing: they lost Italy, and showed Hitler that sanctions would not be followed by force. In March 1936, two months before Addis Ababa fell, Hitler's troops had entered the Rhineland.

:::exam What this means for the key question
Abyssinia is the best case study for the claim that "the League failed because of Britain and France". It was not that they lacked the power: the Mediterranean fleet could have closed Suez. It was that they chose not to use it, so the answer to "why did the League fail?" is as much "why would its leaders not act?" as "why was it weak?". Be ready to argue both sides.
:::

## Changing attitudes of the major powers to the League

By 1936 the League had lost most of its great powers. The table gives the dates the syllabus names; read the notes below, because "withdrawal" is a more complicated word than it seems.

| Power | What happened | Dates |
|---|---|---|
| Japan | Left after the Assembly's vote on the Lytton Report | Notice 27 March 1933; took effect 27 March 1935 |
| Germany | Hitler left the Disarmament Conference and announced withdrawal | Announced 14 October 1933; took effect in October 1935 |
| Italy | Stopped taking part after the sanctions and the conquest of Abyssinia | From 1936; notice 11 December 1937; took effect in December 1939 |
| USSR | Admitted with a permanent seat on the Council; expelled after attacking Finland | Admitted 18 September 1934; expelled 14 December 1939 |
| USA | Never joined | Senate rejected the Covenant in 1919–20 |

:::warn A notice is not a departure
Article 1 of the Covenant says that a member may leave after two years' notice. So Japan, which gave notice on 27 March 1933, was legally a member until 27 March 1935; Germany, which announced withdrawal in October 1933, until October 1935; and Italy, which gave notice in December 1937, until December 1939. The syllabus gives "Italy, 1936" because Italy stopped taking part in the League's work in 1936, after the sanctions, although its formal notice came a year later. In an exam, write "announced its withdrawal in …" and, for Italy, "stopped taking part in 1936 and gave notice in December 1937". Do not write "Italy left in 1936".
:::

### Japan, Germany and Italy

Each left for a reason of its own. **Japan** left because the League condemned its conquest of Manchuria and would not recognise Manchukuo. **Germany** left because Hitler wanted freedom to rearm: leaving gave him an excuse, equality of treatment, and a plebiscite (12 November 1933) that backed him. **Italy** stopped taking part because the League had imposed sanctions on it, which it saw as an insult, and had refused to recognise its conquest. In each case the departure was a symptom, not the cause, of the League's weakness: the three powers had already decided that their ambitions mattered more than the League's rules.

### The admission of the USSR, 1934

The USSR had been hostile to the League since 1919, calling it an instrument of the imperialist powers, and for fifteen years it was outside it. After Hitler's victory (chapter 1) it changed its policy, and its Commissar for Foreign Affairs, Maxim Litvinov, became one of the League's strongest supporters. The Assembly voted on 18 September 1934 by 39 votes to 3 (with seven abstentions) to admit the USSR, which received a permanent seat on the Council. France, which wanted an ally against Germany, led the campaign for the admission. It looked like a gain for the League: another great power in, as three had left. But it could not make up for the losses. The Soviet Union's membership was short. It lasted until 14 December 1939, when the League expelled it for its attack on Finland (30 November 1939), a few months after it had made a pact with Hitler's Germany. In the years between, many in Britain and France, and in Poland and Romania, distrusted Soviet motives, which meant that it was never fully trusted as a partner of the League.

### Negotiations outside the League

Increasingly the powers settled their business **outside** Geneva, which undermined the League's authority:
- The **Four-Power Pact** (initialled in June 1933 by Britain, France, Germany and Italy), proposed by Mussolini, would have let the four great European powers decide Europe's affairs among themselves; the smaller states, and the League, protested, and it was never ratified.
- The **Stresa Front** (April 1935) was a front of three powers, which met without the League.
- The **Anglo-German Naval Agreement** (June 1935) was a bilateral deal between Britain and Germany.
- The **Hoare–Laval plan** (December 1935) was a private deal between Britain and France to settle a League member's fate.
- The **Anglo-Italian "gentlemen's agreement"** (January 1937) and, in 1938, the **Munich Agreement** (chapter 3) were deals between great powers over the heads of the victims.

The more the great powers used direct deals, the less the League seemed to matter, and the smaller states drew their own conclusion: Belgium (1936), for example, went back to a policy of neutrality.

:::exam What this means for the key question
The League was a club, and a club cannot stop its members acting. By 1936 three of the five great powers that mattered had left or stopped taking part, the USA had never joined, and the powers that stayed (Britain, France) increasingly acted outside it. When asked about "attitudes of major powers", show the change over time and say whether the League's weakness caused these attitudes or resulted from them.
:::

## The Spanish Civil War, 1936–39

### How the war began and why the League did nothing

In the Spanish election of 16 February 1936 the Popular Front of republicans, socialists and communists narrowly beat the right. Landowners, the Church, the army and the fascist Falange feared revolution. On 17–18 July 1936 army officers rose in Spanish Morocco and on the mainland against the elected government, and General Francisco Franco soon became the leader of the rebels, who called themselves the Nationalists. The rising failed in Madrid, Barcelona and other big cities but held much of the north and the south-west, and Spain split into two armies. Franco's best troops were stranded in Morocco, because the navy stayed loyal to the Republic. Within days Hitler and Mussolini lent him aircraft to carry them across the Strait of Gibraltar: foreign intervention began in the first fortnight.

The League did nothing of substance. The Spanish government appealed to it, for instance in September 1936, and the Assembly and Council passed resolutions about the war in the following two years, but the League took no action. There were four reasons.
1. **A civil war was not what the Covenant was written for.** The Covenant dealt with war between states. Once Germany and Italy intervened it was a foreign war, but by then the powers had taken the matter out of the League's hands.
2. **The League's authority was gone.** Only two days before the rising the League had ended its sanctions on Italy (15 July 1936), and it was in no position to condemn Italian intervention in Spain.
3. **Britain and France did not want to risk a European war.** They feared that taking sides would draw in Germany, Italy and the USSR and turn the war into a general war between left and right. The two powers whose action was needed chose to keep the League out of it.
4. **Unanimity and sanctions.** Any decision against Germany and Italy would have needed their votes, and enforcing it would have needed the force no one wished to use.

### The Non-Intervention Agreement, 1936

The French government of the Popular Front, under Léon Blum, first thought of helping the Republic and sent a few aircraft. But the French right was bitterly opposed, many Radicals (members of the Radical Party, a centre-left party in Blum's coalition) were cautious, and the British Conservative government, led by Stanley Baldwin, made it clear that it would not support France if France was drawn into a quarrel with Germany or Italy. On 2 August 1936 France proposed an agreement under which the European states would not supply arms or men to either side. Britain agreed, and on 15 August Britain and France exchanged declarations on it. Italy signed on 21 August, the USSR on 23 August and Germany on 24 August; in the end 27 states took part. A **Non-Intervention Committee** of their representatives first met in London on 9 September 1936.

:::def Non-Intervention Agreement
The agreement of August 1936, proposed by France and supported by Britain, in which about 27 European states promised not to send arms, troops or volunteers to either side in the Spanish Civil War. A committee in London was to supervise it.
:::

The idea of the agreement was to stop the war becoming European. The British Foreign Secretary, Anthony Eden, said in the House of Commons on 29 October 1936 that it was meant "to prevent civil war, savage in itself, from passing the boundaries of Spain", and that there was no alternative except to allow arms to be exported freely to both sides. ### Why the agreement failed

:::key Why Non-Intervention failed
1. **Intervention by Germany, Italy and the USSR.**
   - *Germany* sent aircraft from July 1936 and then the Condor Legion, a force of several thousand airmen, tank crews and gunners, from November 1936. It gave Hitler a chance to test his new aircraft and tanks, to weaken France's position (a fascist Spain on the French frontier) and to defeat communism. The Condor Legion was part of the German air force which, with Italian aircraft, bombed the Basque town of Guernica on 26 April 1937; estimates of the dead range from the low hundreds to over a thousand.
   - *Italy* sent tens of thousands of "volunteers" (the Corpo Truppe Volontarie), aircraft and submarines; Mussolini wanted a friendly Spain, a stronger position in the Mediterranean and the glory of a victory. His troops were beaten at Guadalajara in March 1937 but the effort continued.
   - *The USSR* began sending tanks, aircraft, pilots and advisers in October 1936, once it was clear that Germany and Italy had ignored the agreement. Stalin wanted to protect the Popular Front, to prevent a fascist Spain and to keep the support of communists everywhere. The Republic paid with its gold reserves.
2. **Lack of commitment by Britain and France.** They did not inspect Italian and German ports or cargoes; the Non-Intervention Committee had no power to punish and relied on the word of the states it was watching. The naval patrols set up in 1937 broke down when Germany and Italy withdrew from them in June. France sometimes let arms cross its frontier in 1936 and 1938, but it did not do more because it needed British support. Britain's government wanted above all to avoid a war; many Conservatives disliked the Republic as a revolutionary government and saw Franco as a defender of order, and Britain was not willing to confront Hitler and Mussolini over Spain. The agreement therefore bound the democracies but not the dictators: the Republic, which as the elected government would normally have the right to buy arms, could not do so openly, while Franco received what he needed from Germany and Italy.
3. **The International Brigades and other volunteers.** About 32,000 foreign volunteers, from France, Britain, the USA, Germany, Italy, Poland and many other countries, fought for the Republic. They were recruited and organised through the Comintern, trained at Albacete from October 1936, and fought at Madrid (November 1936), Jarama and later at the Ebro; never more than about 18,000 were in Spain at one time. The Republic's supporters in the West saw them as heroes of the fight against fascism. But their presence broke the Agreement, and gave Hitler and Mussolini an excuse for sending more. Franco had foreign volunteers too: Portuguese, Irish and, above all, thousands of Moroccan soldiers. In September 1938 the Republican government announced to the League that the Brigades would be withdrawn, in the hope that Germany and Italy would then withdraw their forces; they did not.
:::

The Non-Intervention Committee continued to meet until 1939, and the end was as the start: Franco declared victory on 1 April 1939, and Britain and France had recognised his government on 27 February. The war killed hundreds of thousands of people, soldiers and civilians, on both sides.

:::exam What this means for the key question
Spain is the third crisis, and in some ways the most revealing: this time the League was never given the chance to fail, because the powers took the matter out of its hands. It showed a Europe divided on ideological lines, democracies that would not defend a sister democracy, and dictators ready to cooperate. Hitler, Mussolini and Stalin all drew conclusions about the will of Britain and France.
:::

## Why the League failed to keep the peace

The syllabus gives five causes. Treat each as a claim about how the League worked, and for each ask whether it explains the failure on its own or only with the others.

### Inability to achieve widespread disarmament

Article 8 of the Covenant promised the reduction of armaments, and the 1920s produced the Washington and London naval treaties but no general agreement. The World Disarmament Conference (1932–34, chapter 1) failed. The consequences for the League were that Germany, Japan and Italy rearmed without limit, and that the League's argument, that peace is kept by shared restraint, was less persuasive than the dictators' argument that peace is kept by strength. The failure also showed how far the powers distrusted each other's promises.

### The absence of major powers

The USA never joined, and Germany (until 1926), the USSR (until 1934) and later Japan, Germany and Italy were outside. At every stage some great power was not in a position to be asked to share the burden. But look at the facts. In the two big crises, Manchuria and Abyssinia, the aggressors were **members and permanent members of the Council**, so their absence cannot explain the failure; what mattered was whether Britain and France would act against them. The absence of the USA mattered in a narrower way: it made sanctions less effective (oil to Italy; trade with Japan) and it meant that Britain and France could not count on American help.

### Reliance on Britain and France

The League's two leading members supplied its leadership and most of its power. In 1931 and 1935 they were the only members who could have used a navy against Japan or Italy. They did not, and the reasons are the same each time: their own security (a revived Germany), their economies (the Depression), their imperial priorities and their divisions with each other (France would not take a lead against Italy, Britain would not take a lead anywhere). A League that depended on two reluctant powers could not be stronger than they were. And their own national interests, not the Covenant, decided what they did: Hoare and Laval's plan shows that clearly.

### The effects of the Great Depression

The slump had three effects. It hurt trade and made governments choose protection, so that the economic sanctions the League relied on were costly. It took away money and attention from foreign policy. And it helped to bring to power the governments in Germany, Japan and Italy that challenged the League (chapter 1). Notice that Britain and France said the Depression was the reason they could not afford to act: it worked as an excuse as well as a cause.

### Increasing nationalism

Nationalism, the placing of the nation's interests and honour above international law, drove all three aggressors. Japan claimed a mission in Asia; Mussolini wanted to build a new Roman empire; Hitler's nationalism was racial and aggressive. They saw the League as a body of status-quo powers that held down "have-not" nations. But there was also nationalism among the democracies: each put its own interests first. The League asked states to give up some of their freedom of action for the sake of the common peace; in the 1930s fewer and fewer were willing to do so.

:::key Putting the causes in order
| Cause | Evidence | Inside or outside the League? | How decisive? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Failure of disarmament | Geneva 1932–34; naval limits end 1936 | Outside (the great powers' choice) | Medium: it removed restraints, but did not itself cause the failures |
| Absence of major powers | USA never joined; Japan, Germany and Italy left | Both: their absence and their membership | Limited: Japan and Italy were members when they attacked |
| Reliance on Britain and France | 1931, 1935 and 1936 all turn on their refusal | Inside (the League's design) | High: it explains each crisis |

A good answer ends by choosing: the League failed because it depended on the will of its strongest members to act together against a great power, and from 1931 that will was missing, for reasons that came from the Depression and from nationalism.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **Christopher Thorne**, *The Limits of Foreign Policy: the West, the League and the Far Eastern Crisis of 1931–1933* (1972), examined what the British, French and American governments could and could not do in the first test, and stressed the limits imposed by the slump, other priorities and mistrust among the Western states.
- **Zara Steiner**, *The Triumph of the Dark: European International History 1933–1939* (2011), treats the League as one of several instruments through which governments pursued their national interests, and shows that those interests, not the Covenant, decided what was done.
- **Ruth Henig**, *The League of Nations* (2010), a short modern history of the whole life of the League, shows its work in the 1920s and its failure in the 1930s in one story.
The question behind them is whether the League failed because of **what it was** (a body with no force and a rule of unanimity) or because of **what its members did** (Britain and France above all). A top answer takes a position and says which evidence decides it.

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the League failed to stop Italy invading Abyssinia.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Italy invaded Abyssinia on 3 October 1935. The League imposed sanctions on Italy in November 1935 but they did not include oil. Britain and France did not close the Suez Canal. The Hoare–Laval plan was leaked in December 1935. Italy won the war and the League ended the sanctions in July 1936. So the League failed."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence says what happened; none says why it stopped the League acting. "So the League failed" is an assertion. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was that Britain and France, which controlled the League, would not risk a war. The sanctions of November 1935 banned arms, loans and some raw materials but not oil, coal or steel, because an oil embargo might have led Mussolini to attack the British fleet, and Britain and France had no wish to fight alone. Britain also left the Suez Canal open to Italian ships, which meant that Mussolini could supply his army in East Africa. A second reason was that the two powers put their relations with Mussolini first. France wanted Italy as a friend against Germany, and Britain wanted Italy in the Stresa Front, so they tried to buy him off: the Hoare–Laval plan of December 1935 would have given Italy large parts of Abyssinia, which showed that the League's leaders did not mean the sanctions to work."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the structural reason and show how the factors work together:

"A third reason was weakness built into the League. The Covenant gave members a duty to apply economic sanctions but not to fight, and the USA, which was not a member, went on selling oil to Italy, so the sanctions that did exist were leaky. The League was also slow: the dispute dragged on for ten months after the incident at Walwal, and sanctions came into force only about six weeks after the invasion, by which time Italy's army, with aircraft, tanks and mustard gas, was winning.

These factors were linked. The League could not use force because its members would not agree to; the members would not agree because Britain and France were afraid of Germany and put Mussolini's friendship above Abyssinia's independence; and that fear explains both the weak sanctions and the Hoare–Laval plan. The decisive factor was the unwillingness of Britain and France to risk a Mediterranean war: with oil sanctions and the closure of Suez the invasion could have been stopped or made much harder, but they preferred a compromise that kept Mussolini on their side. In the end they lost him anyway."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (fear of Germany led to weak sanctions and to the plan), with a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The League failed in the 1930s because of the absence of the great powers.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the USA never joined (oil to Italy; no support for sanctions on Japan); Germany left in 1933, Japan in 1933, Italy stopped taking part in 1936; the USSR was absent until 1934 and expelled in 1939. The League was left with Britain and France.
- Against 1: the aggressors in the two main crises (Japan 1931, Italy 1935) were **members** with a seat on the Council, so absence cannot explain these failures.
- Against 2: Britain and France, who were present and in charge, would not act (Suez open, no oil sanctions, Hoare–Laval).
- Against 3: the structure (unanimity, no force), the Depression and nationalism.
- Judgement: absence was a result and a weakness, not the main cause. Criterion: would the League have succeeded if the absent powers had been present?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The League failed because of the absence of the great powers. The USA never joined the League, so it could not support sanctions and kept trading with Italy. Germany left in October 1933 and Japan in March 1933, and Italy stopped taking part in 1936. The USSR only joined in 1934. This left the League with only Britain and France, which were not strong enough to stop aggressors. So the absence of the great powers was the main reason for the League's failure."

*Commentary.* Accurate, with dates, but it argues one side only and the last sentence asserts what it should prove. Notice also that it treats withdrawal as a cause without asking whether leaving was a result. An answer made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet absence cannot be the main reason, because in the two crises that destroyed the League the aggressor was a member. Japan was a permanent member of the Council when it attacked Manchuria in 1931 and Italy was a member when it invaded Abyssinia in 1935, which is why the unanimity rule gave each a veto. Japan and Italy did not leave before they attacked; they left after the League condemned them (Japan in March 1933, Italy by 1936). Absence was therefore an effect of the League's failure to restrain them, not its cause.

What explains the failure in both crises is that the two powers which were present, Britain and France, would not act. Both had the navies to confront Japan and Italy, and in 1935 Britain's Mediterranean Fleet could have closed Suez. They did not, because they feared Germany and the slump, and wanted Mussolini as a friend: the Hoare–Laval plan was written by the British and French themselves. The USA's absence mattered, but at the margin: oil exports to Italy made sanctions leaky, but the oil embargo that might have stopped Italy was never tried by League members, so the absence of the USA cannot explain the failure to attempt it. Structural weaknesses, the rule of unanimity and the lack of any force, and the effects of the Depression and nationalism, made the reluctance of Britain and France more damaging.

Overall, absence was a real weakness, since the League could never be universal and relied on the leading members alone, but it was not the main reason. The decisive reason was that its strongest members were present and would not use their strength. The statement is true of the League's condition by 1936, but wrong as an explanation of how it got there."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (cause or consequence; present or absent) and distinguishes the League's condition from the reasons for it. With an opening paragraph on the case for absence, this is Level 5 (18–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

... the American Government cannot admit the legality of any situation de facto (in fact, whatever the law says), nor does it intend to recognise any treaty or agreement entered into between those Governments which may impair the treaty rights of the United States or its citizens in China ... and it does not intend to recognise any situation, treaty or agreement which may be brought about by means contrary to the covenants and obligations of the Pact of Paris of August 27, 1928.

*Adapted from the note sent by the American Secretary of State, Henry L. Stimson, to the Japanese and Chinese governments, 7 January 1932. Shortened.*

**Source B**

The military operations of the Japanese troops during this night ... cannot be regarded as measures of legitimate self-defence. ... the present regime cannot be considered to have been called into existence by a genuine and spontaneous independence movement.

*Adapted from the Report of the League of Nations Commission of Enquiry under the Earl of Lytton, 1932 (published on 2 October 1932): the first sentence is from chapter IV, on the night of 18–19 September 1931; the second from chapter VI, on Manchukuo. Shortened.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about international reactions to Japan's conquest of Manchuria.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is from the American government and Source B is from the League. Source A says the USA will not recognise anything that breaks the Pact of Paris. Source B says that Japan was not defending itself. They both disagree with Japan. Source A is biased because America wanted to protect its own rights."

*Commentary.* One similarity, asserted without much support, and no real difference. The comment on bias is a generalisation: it does not say how the American interest affects what the source says. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources have the same attitude to Japan's action but express it in different ways. Both refuse to give Japan's conquest legal respectability: Source A says that the USA 'cannot admit the legality of any situation de facto' and will not recognise anything 'brought about by means contrary to' the Pact of Paris, and Source B denies that Japan's operations were 'legitimate self-defence' and that Manchukuo was 'genuine and spontaneous'. Both therefore rest on a legal and moral condemnation, not on any threat of action.

The differences are in what each concerns and what each does. Source A is about the future: it is a refusal to recognise. It is also about the interests of the author: it protects 'the treaty rights of the United States or its citizens in China' and does not go beyond non-recognition. Source B is a finding about the past, an investigation of two facts, who was to blame on 18–19 September and whether Manchukuo was spontaneous, and it was the work of five people from five countries. In the full report it also made recommendations and recognised Japanese interests.

The context explains the differences. Source A was written on 7 January 1932, only four months after Mukden, a few days after Japan's capture of Chinchow (a Manchurian city that Japan took in January 1932), by a government outside the League, which had no obligation to act and whose note Britain declined to copy. Stimson had no support at home for action beyond a statement, and the Depression meant that the USA was thinking of its own economy, so the note is evidence of the limit of what the USA would do. Source B was produced after a year of investigation by a body that the League had set up, in part because action was not wanted, and published after Manchukuo existed and Japan had recognised it. It is stronger evidence on the facts but, as a League report, it reflects the League's habit of studying and recommending: neither the League nor the USA used force or sanctions, and that is why Japan could ignore both."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the date, the author's interests, the League's methods) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final point about what both together show. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

Should any Member of the League resort to war in disregard of its covenants under Articles 12, 13 or 15, it shall ipso facto (by that very fact) be deemed to have committed an act of war against all other Members of the League, which hereby undertake immediately to subject it to the severance of all trade or financial relations ...

*From Article 16 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, 1919. Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Source B**

I, Haile Selassie I, Emperor of Ethiopia, am here today to claim that justice which is due to my people, and the assistance promised to it eight months ago, when fifty nations asserted that aggression had been committed in violation of international treaties. ... I ask the fifty-two nations, who have given the Ethiopian people a promise to help them in their resistance to the aggressor, what are they willing to do for Ethiopia? ... What reply shall I have to take back to my people?

*From the address of Emperor Haile Selassie I of Ethiopia to the Assembly of the League of Nations, Geneva, 30 June 1936. English translation as published on Wikisource. Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Source C**

In this sphere the League has done nothing. ... Two States, Ethiopia and Austria, have lost their independent existence in consequence of violent aggression. ... The League of Nations was not any more universal during the first twelve years of its existence than it is to-day.

*From the speech of Maxim Litvinov, Soviet People's Commissar for Foreign Affairs, to the Assembly of the League of Nations, Geneva, 21 September 1938, as printed in the League's Official Journal, Special Supplement No. 183. Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Source D**

A very large painting in black, white and shades of grey. A woman screams over her dead child; a horse and a bull stand among broken bodies; a fallen soldier holds a broken sword. There are no aircraft or bombs in the picture.

*A description of Guernica, an oil painting by Pablo Picasso, 349.3 by 776.6 centimetres, painted in May and June 1937 for the Spanish Republic's pavilion at the Paris International Exposition, in response to the bombing of the Basque town of Guernica on 26 April 1937; now in the Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofía, Madrid.*

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that the League failed because its members would not keep the promises they had made to each other?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (the promise: an aggressor is deemed to have committed an act of war against all members, who "undertake immediately" to cut trade); B (the promise "to help" was made by fifty-two nations and Ethiopia asks what they will do); C ("the League has done nothing" about the loss of Ethiopia and Austria); D (a Spanish town destroyed by German and Italian aircraft in a war in which those states had promised non-intervention). Challenge or qualify: A (the Covenant itself promised only economic measures, not force, so the promise was limited); C (Litvinov says the League was never universal, which suggests the problem was structural; and he speaks for the USSR, which broke the Non-Intervention promise); D (a picture does not say who made or broke any promise).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context.**
- **A**: the founding promise, but it is a text of 1919, not evidence of what happened in the 1930s. It must be read with what it leaves out: nothing in Article 16 required members to fight, and Council decisions on military action were only recommendations. Strong evidence of what members had agreed, weak evidence on whether the failure was a broken promise or a flawed promise.
- **B**: a plea from the victim of the failure, to an audience that had just whistled at him, so its purpose is to shame the Assembly. That makes it emotive and one-sided. But its facts are accurate: fifty states had agreed in 1935 to apply sanctions to Italy, and the sanctions were lifted two weeks later. Strong evidence of what Ethiopia expected and of the gap between word and deed; it does not explain why the members did not act.
- **C**: a speech in September 1938, after Austria and Ethiopia had been lost and a week before Munich. The USSR had its own agenda: it wanted collective security, and had been excluded from the talks about Czechoslovakia, so it had reason to blame the League's leaders, Britain and France. But the speech also contains an admission that the League was never universal, which supports the structural view against the "broken promise" view. Useful, with a bias that has to be named.
- **D**: a work of art, not a document: Picasso painted it for the Republic's pavilion and the Republic wanted it to rouse opinion against Franco's backers. It shows the human result of the failure of non-intervention, not the failure of the League, which had been left out. Evidence of the horror and of the propaganda war; very limited on promises.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "The sources support the view that members broke promises to a considerable extent, though not wholly. Source A shows how large the promise was: every member agreed that an aggressor had made war on all of them and would be cut off at once. Sources B and C show that the promise was not kept: fifty-two nations had promised help to Ethiopia but none sent it, and by September 1938 the USSR's Commissar could say that Ethiopia and Austria had been lost while 'the League has done nothing'. Source D adds the Spanish case: Germany and Italy had promised not to intervene and then bombed a Spanish town, while Britain and France, who had organised the promise, looked away. But the sources also show limits to the claim. Article 16 asked only for economic action, so the promise was less than it seems; Litvinov's point that the League 'was not any more universal' earlier suggests that weakness was part of its design, and Source D is about non-intervention, not the League. Taken with what we know, that the League's two leading members refused oil sanctions in 1935 and left Suez open, the view is substantially right. The League failed to a large extent because its members would not do what they had said they would do, but it is also true that they had promised less than the Covenant's language implies."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1919–38; the answer explains where the sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far did the Spanish Civil War show the weakness of the League of Nations?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Spanish Civil War began in July 1936 when General Franco led a rising against the Republic. Britain and France proposed the Non-Intervention Agreement in August 1936 and 27 states signed it. A committee met in London from 9 September 1936. Germany, Italy and the USSR all sent help to Spain. Germany sent the Condor Legion, which bombed Guernica on 26 April 1937. The International Brigades fought for the Republic and about 32,000 volunteers took part. In the end Franco won in April 1939. So the League was weak."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story of the war and says almost nothing about the League. The League is mentioned only in the last sentence, which asserts weakness without explaining it. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument and make the League the subject. "The Spanish Civil War showed the League's weakness mainly by showing its irrelevance. When the war began, the League was never asked to act, and when the Republic appealed it received only resolutions, because Britain and France had already decided to deal with the war outside the League, in the Non-Intervention Committee that met in London from 9 September 1936. The reason is that the League's credibility had collapsed after Abyssinia: sanctions on Italy had been lifted on 15 July 1936, two days before the rising, and Italy was now one of the intervening states. But the war showed other things more clearly than the League's own weakness: the failure of Britain and France to confront Germany and Italy, and the decision of the dictators and the USSR to ignore a pledge they had signed. So the war confirmed the weakness of the League more than it caused it." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What happened at Mukden on 18 September 1931, and what did the League Council do on 24 October 1931?
A: An explosion on the South Manchurian Railway, staged by Japanese officers, was blamed on Chinese saboteurs and used to begin the conquest of Manchuria. On 24 October the Council voted 13 to 1 for a resolution that Japan must withdraw by 16 November; Japan voted against, decisions needed unanimity, and it was ignored.
Q: What did the Lytton Report say, and what did the Assembly do with it?
A: Japan's operations were not legitimate self-defence and Manchukuo was not a genuine independence movement, but Japan had real interests; it proposed an autonomous Manchuria under Chinese sovereignty. The Assembly adopted it on 24 February 1933 by 42 votes to 1; Japan walked out and gave notice on 27 March 1933. No sanctions were imposed.
Q: Why were the League's sanctions on Italy ineffective?
A: They began on 18 November 1935, six weeks after the invasion, and left out oil, coal and steel; Britain did not close the Suez Canal; the USA, outside the League, kept selling oil. Britain and France did not want to risk a war with Italy.
Q: What was the Hoare–Laval plan and what was its result?
A: A secret proposal of December 1935 by the British and French foreign ministers to give Italy large parts of Abyssinia in return for peace. It was leaked, public opinion in Britain was outraged, Hoare resigned on 18 December and the plan was dropped, but the League's credibility was badly damaged.
Q: When did Italy leave the League, and why is "1936" misleading?
A: Italy stopped taking part in 1936, after the sanctions and the conquest; it gave notice on 11 December 1937, and under the two-year rule its membership ended in December 1939.
Q: Why did the USSR join the League in 1934, and what ended its membership?
A: Fear of Hitler and Japan changed Soviet policy to collective security, and France led the campaign for its admission (39 votes to 3, with a permanent Council seat). It was expelled on 14 December 1939 after attacking Finland.
Q: Why did the League not respond to the Spanish Civil War?
A: It was a civil war, not a war between states; the League's authority had collapsed after Abyssinia (sanctions lifted 15 July 1936); and Britain and France wanted to prevent a European war and so dealt with it outside the League through Non-Intervention.
Q: What was the Non-Intervention Agreement, and what were the main reasons it failed?
A: A French proposal of August 1936, accepted by 27 states, that none would send arms or men to either side (committee in London from 9 September 1936). It failed because Germany, Italy and the USSR intervened, Britain and France did little to enforce it, and volunteers (the International Brigades, about 32,000) went to fight.
Q: What was the Condor Legion?
A: The German force of airmen, tank crews and gunners sent to help Franco from November 1936; its aircraft, with Italian ones, bombed Guernica on 26 April 1937.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the League failed to stop Japan taking over Manchuria in 1931–33.
A: Indicative content: Manchuria was far from Europe and the powers' priorities were elsewhere (the Depression, the Disarmament Conference); Britain and France would not use force or sanctions against a great power and hoped that the USA would lead; Japan sat on the Council and could veto decisions (the vote of 24 October 1931 was 13 to 1, but not unanimous); the League's procedures were slow (the Lytton Commission was agreed on 10 December 1931 and reported in October 1932), by which time Manchukuo existed; the USA was not a member and offered only non-recognition. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these (distance and the Depression reduced the will to act; no force and a veto made action impossible even if the will existed; slowness let Japan finish the conquest) and judges the decisive factor, for example that Britain and France were unwilling to act at all.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Germany left the League of Nations in October 1933.
A: Indicative content: Hitler's government wanted freedom to rearm; Germany had been denied "equality" at the Disarmament Conference (the French and British demanded a trial period); leaving gave him an excuse and a way to appeal to German nationalism (the plebiscite of 12 November 1933); the League had a weak record (Manchuria) and could not stop him; Japan's departure gave a precedent. Level 4: connect the aim (rearmament) to the opportunity (the dispute over equality) and the evidence of the League's weakness, and judge that the main reason was the wish to rearm.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Britain and France were mainly to blame for the failure of the League over Abyssinia.' How far do you agree?
A: For: weak sanctions (no oil, Suez open); the wish to keep Mussolini as an ally against Hitler; the Hoare–Laval plan; no military preparation; the Peace Ballot showed that British opinion would have backed stronger action. Against: the USA's oil trade; the League's own structure (no force, slow procedure); Mussolini's determination; the fear of Hitler, which was a real danger; the Depression. Level 5 uses a criterion, for example "who could have prevented the conquest?", and judges that Britain and France were mainly to blame because only they had the means, but that the League's design and the German threat explain why they felt unable to use it.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent did the Non-Intervention Agreement fail because of the actions of Germany, Italy and the USSR?
A: For: Germany's aircraft, then the Condor Legion from November 1936; Italian troops, aircraft and submarines; Soviet arms and advisers from October 1936 and the Comintern's International Brigades. Against: Britain and France did not inspect ports or enforce the rules, the committee had no power to punish and trusted the word of the states it watched; the naval patrols of 1937 collapsed; France's divisions and its dependence on Britain; the Agreement applied to the democracies but not to the dictators. Level 5 judges that intervention made the Agreement fail in practice, but that the democracies' lack of commitment is why the breaches were never punished; so the lack of commitment was more fundamental.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above; B is two short quotations by two speakers, to be taken together). Compare these two sources as evidence about British attitudes to the policy of non-intervention in 1936.
A: Similarities: both are speeches in the Commons on the same day and concern non-intervention; both accept that the aim was to avoid a wider war (A states it; B's two speakers say the policy has not met Sinclair's "expectations" and has not meant "fair play", in Greenwood's words). Differences: A defends the policy as the only way to keep the peace and sees no alternative; B, from opposition benches, says it has not worked and has not been applied fairly. Explanation: A is the Foreign Secretary defending government policy, with a reason to claim it is the best policy; B is from a Liberal and a Labour speaker, who as opposition had a reason to criticise and (Greenwood) wanted the Republic to be allowed to buy arms; the debate took place after evidence that Germany and Italy were breaking the agreement. Level 4 explains the differences through these roles and judges that both together show a policy defended as necessary and criticised as unfair, and that neither is proof of what the policy achieved.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was the Great Depression responsible for the failure of the League of Nations in the 1930s?
H: Link the slump to specific decisions: which crises, which governments?
H: Then set against it the causes that were not economic.
A: For: the Depression made Britain and France unwilling to bear the cost of sanctions or rearmament and inward-looking in 1931–33 (Manchuria); it brought to power or strengthened the governments in Germany, Japan and Italy that attacked the system; it fuelled protection and nationalism; it was cited as a reason not to act. Against: Britain and France's own choices and fear of Germany; the Covenant's weakness (unanimity, no force); the absence of the USA; the ambitions of the dictators (Hitler's, Mussolini's and the Japanese army's) which predated the slump; by 1935–36 the Depression was easing in Britain, but the failure over Abyssinia was greater. Level 5 judges, for example, that the Depression was decisive in 1931–33 and the underlying condition of the whole period, but that after 1933 the choices of the powers explain the failure more.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The League's failure over Manchuria was more important than its failure over Abyssinia in destroying its authority.' Evaluate this view.
H: Set a criterion: what does "destroying its authority" mean? Loss of members? Loss of belief by small states? Encouragement to aggressors?
H: Compare the events that followed each failure.
A: Manchuria: the first test, showed that a great power could defy it, Japan left (1933), and the dictators noticed; but it was far away and the League had condemned Japan by 42 votes to 1. Abyssinia: a European and African member destroyed, sanctions that failed, the Hoare–Laval scandal, Italy's departure, and small states lost faith (several said in 1936 that they would not join sanctions again). Level 5 judges, for example, that Manchuria showed the weakness and Abyssinia destroyed belief in the League, because the League had tried and been seen to fail in Europe's own sphere, at the moment when Hitler was rearming.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far did the admission of the USSR in 1934 strengthen the League?
H: Strength can mean power, authority and unity. Check each.
H: Look at what happened to the other major powers at the same time, and at Western attitudes to the USSR.
A: For: another great power, and a permanent Council seat; Litvinov argued for collective security and sanctions; the USSR supported action over Abyssinia; it gave Britain and France a potential ally against Germany (the Franco-Soviet Pact, 1935). Against: Japan and Germany had left in 1933, and Italy stopped taking part in 1936, so the League was weaker overall; the West distrusted the USSR (the Comintern, the purges); the Soviet army was far away and Poland and Romania would not let it cross; membership ended in December 1939 when the League expelled it. Level 5 judges that the admission strengthened the League only on paper: it replaced one great power with others but left the key problem, the unwillingness of Britain and France to act, untouched.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Christopher Thorne (1972) argued that Western governments were limited in what they could do about Japan in 1931–33. How far does the evidence support the view that the League could not have stopped Japan?
H: List the limits on action (the Depression, the Singapore base, priorities in Europe, American attitudes). Which were real and which were excuses?
H: Consider what would have happened if Britain and the USA had imposed an economic embargo and what Japan was dependent on.
A: Support: the slump limited governments; Britain and France were absorbed in Europe and the Disarmament Conference; the British Far Eastern fleet was weak; the USA would not join collective action; a naval war against Japan would have been long and uncertain; Japan was a major trading partner. Challenge: an embargo, especially of oil and raw materials from the USA, Britain and the Dutch East Indies, would have hurt Japan, whose army was independent but whose economy depended on imports; Japan was not yet ready for a major war; no embargo or even an arms ban was attempted; the League could have refused recognition and gone further than the Lytton Report. A* judgement: the League could not easily have forced Japan out of Manchuria, but it could have made the conquest costlier; the fact that no member tried shows that the explanation lies in will as much as in power.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The League was always bound to fail.' Evaluate this view with reference to Manchuria, Abyssinia and Spain.
H: What did the League need in order to succeed? List it, then ask whether the 1930s lacked it.
H: Remember the successes of the 1920s (Aaland, Greco-Bulgarian dispute) and what they depended on.
A: Support: it had no force and relied on unanimity; it depended on Britain and France who would not act; the USA never joined; it could only coerce small states. Challenge: it settled disputes in the 1920s when the great powers agreed; with a different policy in 1931 or 1935 (a firm line by Britain and France, with the US and USSR at least not hostile) it might have worked; the failures came from decisions of governments in a period of depression and extremism, not from a design that made them inevitable. A* judgement: the League's design limited what it could do, but it was not doomed: it succeeded when its leading members agreed, and in the 1930s they did not; the fact that the League failed does not show that failure was inevitable, only that the conditions for success were absent.
