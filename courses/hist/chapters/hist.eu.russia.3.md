Eight months later a party that most Russians had barely heard of in the spring took power in the capital almost without a fight. Explaining both events is the heart of this topic: one is the story of a war that no government could win or end, the other of a party that offered simple answers when everyone else offered delay. ## Russia at war, 1914–16

The war that began in August 1914 was meant to be short. Russia mobilised faster than Germany expected and attacked in East Prussia, and in the first months the capital's name was changed from the German-sounding St Petersburg to the Russian **Petrograd**. A wave of patriotism took even the strikers of July 1914 back to work, and the Duma, with the exception of the socialists, voted to support the war. The regime had a rare chance to unite the country. It lost it within two years.

### Defeats and the Tsar's position

Russia had the largest army in Europe, but it was badly led and poorly supplied. Industry could not make enough rifles and shells, the railways were thin, and the generals were chosen by favour as often as by skill.
- **Tannenberg, 26–30 August 1914 (NS).** A Russian army was surrounded in East Prussia by a smaller German force. About 92,000 men were taken prisoner, and the army's commander, General Samsonov, shot himself. A second defeat followed at the Masurian Lakes in September.
- **The Great Retreat, 1915.** A German and Austrian offensive from May 1915 drove the Russian army out of Poland, much of Lithuania and part of Belarus and Galicia. The army was short of shells and, in places, of rifles. Millions of civilians fled east, adding to the burden on towns and railways.
- **The Brusilov offensive, June 1916.** General Aleksei Brusilov's attack in Galicia was Russia's greatest success of the war, and it broke the Austrian army. But it cost Russia very heavy losses (estimates run from about half a million to more than a million men) and could not be followed up. By the end of 1916 the army had lost millions of men killed, wounded and captured, and about 15 million men, mostly peasants, had been called up.

:::def Great Retreat
The Russian army's withdrawal in 1915 from Poland and much of the western borderlands before the German and Austrian offensive. It cost Russia some of its richest industrial and farming land, created millions of refugees and badly damaged morale.
:::

Defeat mattered politically because of one decision. In **August 1915** (23 August OS; 5 September NS) Nicholas II dismissed his cousin, the Grand Duke Nicholas, and took personal command of the army, leaving for headquarters (the Stavka) at Mogilev. Most of his ministers begged him not to. The army was in retreat, and from this moment every defeat was the Tsar's own. He had also left the capital to his wife.

:::key How the war weakened the Tsar's position
1. **Personal responsibility.** After August 1915 the Tsar commanded the army, so he was blamed for defeats as no Romanov had been before.
2. **Loss of loyalty.** The soldiers were mostly peasants who had been taken from their land; by 1917 they were tired, ill-fed and no longer sure what they were fighting for.
3. **Loss of support among the elite.** Landowners, industrialists, Duma members and senior officers began to see the Tsar's circle as the reason the war was being lost.
4. **Absence from the capital.** While he was at the front, the government at home was run in his name by a Tsarina whom the public distrusted.
:::

### Weaknesses in the government during the war

The government failed to use the patriotism of 1914. It also failed to use the many people who offered help.
- **A quarrel with the Duma.** In August 1915 most Duma parties, from the Octobrists to the Kadets, formed the **Progressive Bloc** and asked for a "ministry of public confidence", a government that the Duma could trust. The Tsar refused, and on 3 September 1915 he suspended the Duma. The people who most wanted to win the war, the property owners and the middle class, were pushed into opposition.
- **Voluntary effort.** Zemstvo and town councils formed unions (the Zemgor) to look after the wounded and refugees, and industrialists formed War Industries Committees to organise production. The ministers distrusted them as rivals, and many officials tried to limit their work.
- **The Tsarina and Rasputin.** The Tsarina Alexandra, a German-born princess, advised the Tsar by letter and chose ministers with the help of **Grigory Rasputin**, a peasant mystic who seemed able to ease the suffering of her son Alexei, the heir, who had haemophilia. Rumour made Rasputin a lover of the Tsarina and Alexandra a German spy, neither of which was true. But the public believed it, and the Tsar's authority suffered.
- **"Ministerial leapfrog".** Between 1915 and 1917 ministers were dismissed and replaced one after another, so that no minister had time to learn his job. Between the start of 1916 and the Revolution there were four different Prime Ministers (Goremykin, Stürmer, Trepov and Golitsyn).
- **The attack from the Duma.** On 1 November 1916 (14 November NS) the Kadet leader Pavel Milyukov attacked the government in the Duma, listing its failures and asking of each, "Is this stupidity or is it treason?" The speech was circulated across the country.
- **The murder of Rasputin.** On the night of 16–17 December 1916 Rasputin was shot and thrown into the Neva by a group of conservatives, including Prince Felix Yusupov and the Grand Duke Dmitri Pavlovich, who thought his removal would save the monarchy. It did not: the Tsarina still ruled, and the murder, by members of the Tsar's own family circle, showed that even the elite had lost faith.

:::def Ministerial leapfrog
The wartime habit of changing ministers constantly, often on the advice of the Tsarina and Rasputin, so that policy was incoherent and the government seemed incompetent.
:::

:::def Progressive Bloc
A grouping of Duma parties formed in August 1915, with a majority in the Duma, which asked for a government that the Duma could trust and for moderate reforms. Its failure to win concessions showed moderates that the Tsar would not change.
:::

### Economic and social problems on the home front

The war put an impossible strain on the economy.
- **Inflation.** The government paid for the war by printing money instead of by raising taxes. The quantity of paper money in circulation roughly quintupled between 1914 and 1917. Prices rose faster than wages, so workers' real wages fell. Strikes grew in 1916 and January 1917.
- **Food shortages.** Russia grew enough grain to feed itself, but not enough reached the cities. The railways were choked with military traffic, and locomotives and wagons wore out. The very cold winter of 1916–17 left trains snowed in. Peasants held back grain because money bought almost nothing, and in late 1916 the government tried to force them to sell at fixed prices (a system of compulsory deliveries). Queues for bread grew long in Petrograd, a city swollen with war workers. By February 1917 there were rumours that bread would be rationed.
- **The peasants in uniform.** Taking about 15 million men for the army and then requisitioning horses left the villages short of labour. Women ran the farms, and a peasant army that did not understand the war's aims still wanted what peasants had wanted since 1861: the land.
- **Land seizures.** From the spring of 1917, as the old authorities collapsed, villages began to take over the estates of landowners, church and Crown, divide them among themselves and refuse to pay rent. The village assemblies and the local land committees were doing what the peasants thought the Revolution was for. Soldiers at the front who heard about it began to desert to go home in time for the share-out. The government could not stop them, and it did not dare to authorise them.

:::key Why Tsarism could not survive the war
1. **Defeat**: the Tsar was personally in charge of a losing war.
2. **Incompetence**: ministerial leapfrog, the Tsarina's influence and the quarrel with the Duma.
3. **Hardship**: inflation, transport breakdown, cold and bread shortages in the cities.
4. **Loss of support**: the army, the middle class and part of the elite no longer wanted to defend the Tsar. In 1905 the army had saved him; in 1917 it did not.
:::

## The February Revolution, 1917

### A week in Petrograd

Nobody planned the revolution that began in Petrograd on **23 February 1917** (8 March NS). It began on International Women's Day, when women textile workers in the Vyborg district, tired of queuing for bread, went on strike and called on the men in the metal factories to join them. About 90,000 workers were on strike by the end of the day (a figure often quoted; the exact number is uncertain).

| Date (OS) | What happened |
|---|---|
| 23 February | Women workers strike for bread; crowds march to the centre, shouting "Bread!" |
| 24 February | Around 150,000–200,000 strikers; students and others join; Cossacks (a community of horsemen soldier-farmers who served the Tsar) do not charge the crowds |
| 25 February | A general strike closes most factories. The Tsar, at Mogilev, orders the Petrograd commander, General Khabalov, to end the disorders by the next day |
| 26 February | Troops fire on crowds in the city centre (Znamenskaya Square); about 40 people are killed. That night a Guards company mutinies. The Duma president, Mikhail Rodzianko, telegraphs the Tsar that "anarchy" reigns in the capital; the Tsar suspends the Duma |
| 27 February | The Volynsky regiment, then others, mutiny and join the crowd. By the evening tens of thousands of soldiers have gone over to the Revolution; the arsenal is seized and prisons are opened. The Duma forms a Provisional Committee, and the Petrograd Soviet meets for the first time |
| 28 February–1 March | Garrisons in Moscow, Kronstadt and elsewhere join; the Tsar's train, on its way to the capital, is diverted |
| 2 March | Nicholas II abdicates |

Three things in this story deserve attention.
- **The soldiers.** The crowd could be shot at. It could not defeat the army. What decided the week was that the garrison, about 150,000 men in the city, most of them raw recruits and many of them peasants who feared being sent to the front, refused to shoot their own people and then joined them. The regime had no force left it could trust.
- **No leaders.** The socialist parties, including the Bolsheviks, were taken by surprise; the local Bolshevik committee had advised against a strike on 23 February. Lenin, the Bolshevik leader, was in exile in Switzerland, and Trotsky, a revolutionary socialist, in exile in New York.
- **The Tsar's absence.** Nicholas II was far away at the Stavka, and he went on thinking it was a riot until it was too late. The Tsarina wrote to him that it was a "hooligan movement".

:::def February Revolution
The revolution in Petrograd of 23 February–2 March 1917 (OS; 8–15 March NS), in which bread strikes became a general strike, the garrison mutinied and the Tsar abdicated, ending the Romanov monarchy.
:::

### The abdication of the Tsar

Nicholas set out for Tsarskoe Selo on 28 February, but revolutionary railwaymen turned his train aside and it stopped at Pskov, the headquarters of the northern front, on 1 March. There General Ruzsky told him that only a change of government could save the situation. The Chief of Staff, General Alekseev, asked the front commanders for advice, and they all advised him to abdicate to save the army and the war effort. On 2 March, with the Duma's representatives Guchkov and Shulgin on their way to Pskov, Nicholas signed a manifesto giving up the throne. Because he did not want to be separated from his son Alexei, who had haemophilia, he named his brother, the **Grand Duke Michael**, as his successor. On 3 March Michael, told that his safety could not be guaranteed, refused the crown until a Constituent Assembly should decide. The Romanov dynasty had ruled since 1613, and it had ended after 304 years.

:::note A closer look: the abdication manifesto
The manifesto, dated 2 March 1917 at Pskov, begins by saying that "the cruel foe is making his last effort" and that the war must be won. It says the Tsar thought it best, "in agreement with the State Duma", to abdicate. It hands the inheritance to Michael with a request that he rule in co-operation with the representatives of the people. *Adapted from the abdication manifesto of Nicholas II, 2 March 1917, in the translation in Frank Golder (ed.), Documents of Russian History, 1914–1917 (1927).* Notice what the Tsar does not say. He says nothing of the strikes or the mutiny, and presents his decision as a sacrifice for the war effort. It is a useful source on how he wished to be seen, not on why he fell.
:::

### Causes and effects of the February Revolution

| Type | Causes | How they worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | Autocracy that refused real reform; poverty of the peasants; a growing and angry working class; the failure of the post-1905 settlement | Created a stock of grievance and a political opposition that was ready to act |
| War | Defeat; the Tsar's command; incompetence; Rasputin | Destroyed the loyalty of the elite and the army |
| Economic | Inflation; transport breakdown; bread queues; the winter | Brought hungry workers on to the streets |
| Trigger | International Women's Day strike (23 February); the Tsar's order to shoot; the mutiny of the garrison | Turned a bread strike into a revolution in days |

The **effects** were immediate. The monarchy was gone, and it was not obvious who ruled. There were now two centres of power in Petrograd, in one building: the Tauride Palace, the home of the Duma.

### Dual power

On 27 February the Duma's leaders, who did not want to be seen to be overthrowing the Tsar, formed a **Provisional Committee** to restore order. On the same day, in another wing of the same palace, socialist intellectuals and workers' leaders revived the **Petrograd Soviet** of Workers' Deputies, which had been created in 1905. Soldiers' delegates joined it the next day, and it became the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies. Its leaders were mostly Mensheviks and Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs, the peasants' party; by the autumn the SRs had split into a Left wing that backed Soviet power and a Right wing that did not), and it published its own newspaper, *Izvestiya*.

The Soviet could have taken power but chose not to. Its leaders believed that Russia was not ready for a socialist revolution, and that the next stage should be a "bourgeois-democratic" republic (bourgeois: the property-owning middle class) run by the liberals. So on 2 March the Provisional Committee formed the **Provisional Government**, and the Soviet agreed to support it so long as it carried out an agreed programme. But the Soviet kept control of the soldiers and the railways, and that was real power.

:::def Dual power
The situation in Russia from March to October 1917 in which the Provisional Government held legal authority but the Petrograd Soviet, supported by the soldiers and workers, held the real power to enforce or block its decisions. Neither could rule without the other.
:::

The key document was **Order No. 1**, issued by the Soviet on 1 March, which told the garrison of Petrograd to elect committees in every unit, to obey the Duma's orders only where they did not conflict with the Soviet's, and to keep weapons under the control of the soldiers' committees and not hand them to officers, even on command. Soldiers off duty kept the civil rights of other citizens, the compulsory saluting outside service was abolished and officers were no longer addressed with titles such as "Your Excellency". It did not allow soldiers to elect their officers, as is often said, but it broke the officers' authority. The order was meant for the capital, but it spread to the front within days and became the symbol of the soldiers' revolution. It meant that any government that wished to command the army needed the consent of the Soviet.

:::eg A closer look: reading Order No. 1
The order was addressed to the Petrograd garrison and to the soldiers of the Guard, army, artillery and fleet. Its fourth point says that the orders of the Duma's military commission "are to be fulfilled only in those cases which do not contradict the orders and decisions" of the Soviet. *Adapted from Order No. 1 of the Petrograd Soviet of Workers' and Soldiers' Deputies, 1 March 1917.*

**What it shows.** The rank and file now looked to the Soviet, not to the officers or the government, for authority. The Provisional Government, which had no army of its own, had to accept it.

**What it does not show.** The Soviet's leaders did not want to destroy the army; they wanted to protect the revolution from a counter-revolution by the officers. The order was about the Petrograd garrison and was never intended to run the whole army. Its spread to the front was a consequence of the soldiers' own action.
:::

## The Provisional Government, March–October 1917

### Formation and aims

The Provisional Government that took office on 2 March was a government of liberals and moderate conservatives under **Prince Georgy Lvov**, who was also Interior Minister. It included Pavel Milyukov (Kadet leader) as Foreign Minister, Alexander Guchkov (Octobrist) as War Minister and, as the only socialist, **Alexander Kerensky**, an SR, who as Minister of Justice was also a leading figure in the Soviet and therefore a link between the two.

Its programme, published on 3 March, was liberal and democratic:
- an amnesty for political prisoners, and freedom of speech, press, assembly and strikes;
- an end to restrictions based on class, religion or nationality;
- the police to be replaced by a people's militia, and elections to local government;
- the election of a **Constituent Assembly**, on the basis of universal suffrage, to decide the form of government and the future of Russia;
- soldiers who had made the revolution to keep the weapons and civil rights they had won.

It also decided to continue the war. The death penalty was abolished (it was restored at the front in July), and Russia, with its new freedoms, became for a time one of the freest countries in Europe.

:::def Provisional Government
The government formed on 2 March 1917 by the Duma's Provisional Committee after the Tsar's abdication. It was meant to rule until a Constituent Assembly could decide the future of Russia, and it never had that chance.
:::

:::def Constituent Assembly
A body elected to write a constitution and decide the form of government. The Provisional Government promised one from March 1917, but the elections were delayed by the war and were held only in November, after the Bolsheviks had taken power.
:::

### Why the Provisional Government failed

The government faced problems that no government could have solved quickly. It also made its own mistakes. The syllabus asks about four challenges in particular, and each one fed the others.

**1. Failure to end the war.** The government and most of the Soviet's leaders believed that Russia had to keep fighting. Milyukov wanted to win the Straits and Constantinople (the waterway from the Black Sea to the Mediterranean, and the Turkish capital that controlled it). The Soviet's moderates wanted only "defence of the revolution" and a peace without annexations (without taking over any country's land). But the government had promised the Allies, Britain and France, that Russia would stay in the war, and it depended on their loans. There were also practical reasons: a sudden peace would mean losing the war on German terms, and many liberals still believed that Russia's honour was at stake. The result was that it satisfied nobody.
- **The April crisis.** On 18 April Milyukov sent the Allies a note repeating Russia's war aims. Soldiers and workers marched in protest on 20–21 April. Milyukov and Guchkov resigned, and on 5 May a **coalition** government was formed with six socialists. Kerensky became Minister of War.
- **The June offensive.** Kerensky toured the front and, on 18 June, ordered an attack in Galicia. It gained a little ground and then was thrown back by a German counter-attack in July. Thousands of soldiers deserted, and the army never recovered. Soldiers, who were willing to defend Russia but not to attack, blamed the government. The offensive discredited both Kerensky and the Soviet moderates who supported him.

**2. The need for land reform.** The peasants wanted the landowners' land and could not understand why they should wait. The government said that only the Constituent Assembly could decide something so important and, with the war on, it could not meet. Land committees were set up to collect information, but the peasants seized estates without waiting. The government was seen as the landlords' friend. Because most soldiers were peasants, the land question and the war question were really the same question.

**3. The economy.** Inflation, strikes and shortages of food and fuel continued and grew worse. Wages could not keep up with prices, factory owners closed factories, and the workers, who had hoped that the revolution would bring improvement, began to form factory committees and to demand workers' control of production.

**4. Weakness and division.** Because of dual power the government could not be sure of being obeyed. The Soviet was often more radical than its own leaders: it could not be controlled. And the government itself was divided. The liberals wanted to keep order and restore discipline; the socialists in the coalition wanted to protect the revolution. The unelected character of the government also weakened it: it had not been elected, and it was waiting for an Assembly that kept being postponed. National groups, such as the Ukrainians and Finns, began to ask for autonomy, which the government mostly refused to discuss.

:::key Four reasons why the Provisional Government failed
1. **The war**: it would not or could not end it, and the June offensive ended in disaster.
2. **The land**: it asked the peasants to wait, and they did not.
3. **The economy**: inflation, shortages and unemployment continued.
4. **Dual power and division**: it lacked authority, because the Soviet controlled the soldiers and the railways, and its coalitions kept breaking up.
Every month of delay made the Provisional Government weaker and its opponents stronger.
:::

### The July Days

In early July the pressure broke. On **3 July**, after the news of the failure of the offensive and when a coalition crisis had left the government weak, the First Machine-Gun Regiment, which had been ordered to the front, rose in Petrograd and called on the Soviet to take power. The next day tens of thousands of workers and soldiers, including sailors from the Kronstadt naval base, marched on the Tauride Palace with banners reading "All power to the Soviets".

The Bolsheviks had not planned the rising. Their leaders had at first tried to hold their supporters back, but the rank and file were already moving, and the party joined to try to lead it. The Soviet's moderate leaders (the Menshevik Tsereteli and the SR Chernov) refused to take power. Loyal troops arrived on 5 July, and the demonstration melted away.

The government used it to attack the Bolsheviks. It published documents claiming that Lenin was a German agent, closed *Pravda* (the Bolshevik newspaper), and arrested leading Bolsheviks, including Lev Kamenev and, soon afterwards, Trotsky. **Lenin** fled, disguised, to Finland. On 8 July **Kerensky** replaced Lvov as Prime Minister. On 18 July General **Lavr Kornilov** became Commander-in-Chief.

For a few weeks the Bolsheviks seemed finished. But the July Days had also shown the weakness of the government, which could only survive with the help of the Soviet's moderates. It had not been able to defeat a few thousand men without calling in troops from the front.

### The Kornilov Revolt

By August Kerensky was caught between the left and the right. Conservatives and many officers wanted a firm hand to restore discipline. Kornilov, a brave general with little political skill, wanted the Soviet's power limited and the army restored. The details of what happened are still disputed: it seems that Kerensky and Kornilov each misunderstood the other's intentions, partly through the interference of a go-between, the former minister Vladimir Lvov (no relation to Prince Georgy Lvov), and Kornilov had begun to move troops towards the capital.

On **27 August**, believing that Kornilov was demanding full power, Kerensky dismissed him and denounced him as a traitor. Kornilov refused to go, and the troops under General Krymov went on towards Petrograd. Kerensky, with no troops he could trust, turned to the Soviet. Railwaymen stopped or diverted trains, telegraph operators held up messages, the Soviet's agitators spoke to the advancing troops, and the soldiers halted without a battle. Krymov shot himself and Kornilov was arrested on 1 September.

The results were important:
- **The Bolsheviks were rescued.** Kerensky released Bolsheviks from prison, and the Soviet, which armed workers' detachments (the **Red Guards**) to defend Petrograd with weapons from the government's own arsenals, did not give them back.
- **The government lost the right.** The officers and the conservatives believed Kerensky had betrayed Kornilov; the army's loyalty to Kerensky ended.
- **The government lost the left.** To the workers and soldiers, Kerensky had been in contact with a general who wanted to destroy the Soviet. It confirmed their suspicion that the "bourgeois" government could not be trusted.

:::def Kornilov Revolt
The attempt in late August 1917 (OS) by the Commander-in-Chief, General Lavr Kornilov, to bring troops to Petrograd, after a confused quarrel with Kerensky. It failed, but it destroyed the army's trust in Kerensky and made the Bolsheviks look like the defenders of the revolution.
:::

:::def Red Guards
Armed workers' detachments formed in Petrograd and other cities in 1917, first in the factories and then in the districts. After the Kornilov Revolt they were armed in large numbers and became the Bolsheviks' main fighting force in October.
:::

## The Bolsheviks and the October Revolution

### Who were the Bolsheviks in February 1917?

In the spring of 1917 the Bolsheviks were a small party. They had perhaps 24,000 members in February, most of them in Petrograd and Moscow. They were the minority wing of the Social Democrats whose leaders had been in prison, in Siberia or abroad since 1914, because they had opposed the war. At first, the Bolshevik leaders in Petrograd, Kamenev and Stalin, who returned from exile in March, supported the Provisional Government "in so far as" it fought against reaction, and spoke of reunion with the Mensheviks.

### Lenin's return and the April Theses

Lenin had spent the war in Switzerland. The German government, which hoped that revolution in Russia would weaken the Allies, allowed him and about thirty other exiles to cross Germany in a sealed train, and he arrived at the Finland Station in Petrograd on **3 April** (16 April NS). The next day he read to Bolshevik meetings a short programme that shocked his colleagues, the **April Theses**, which *Pravda* (the Bolshevik newspaper) published on 7 April.

:::eg A closer look: the April Theses
Lenin told his party that the Russian revolution had reached a new stage. In his words, Russia was "passing from the first stage of the revolution" to a second, which must give power to the workers and the poorest peasants. His main points were:
- the war remained a predatory, imperialist war (a robbers' war fought by great powers for land and profit) under the new government, and there must be **no concessions** to "revolutionary defencism" (the view that Russia must keep fighting to defend the revolution);
- "no support for the Provisional Government", whose promises should be exposed as false;
- "the Soviets of Workers' Deputies are the only possible form of revolutionary government": the Bolsheviks must win a majority in them by explaining their programme patiently, and then take power;
- the land: confiscate all the landed estates and nationalise all land, with the local Soviets of Agricultural Labourers' and Peasants' Deputies deciding how to use it.
*Adapted from Lenin, The Tasks of the Proletariat in the Present Revolution (the April Theses), published in Pravda, 7 April 1917.*

**Why it mattered.** Every other party told Russians to wait for the Constituent Assembly. Lenin offered the three things that soldiers, workers and peasants wanted at once, and he made them the party's slogans: **"Peace, Land and Bread"** and **"All power to the Soviets"**. At first most Bolsheviks thought he was wrong. By the end of April the party had accepted his line.
:::

### Why the Bolsheviks grew

Bolshevik membership rose from about 24,000 in February to over 200,000 by late summer (the party's own figures claimed about 240,000 by August; historians warn that these are uncertain). The party had advantages that the moderate socialists did not.
- **Promises that matched the people's wishes.** "Peace, Land and Bread" was a programme that anyone could understand. The Mensheviks and the SRs, who sat in the government after May, had to defend the war and delay on land. The Bolsheviks, who were out of the government, could criticise everything.
- **Lenin's leadership.** Lenin gave the party a clear aim and a sense of timing. He changed the party's direction in April, and in September and October he insisted, against many colleagues, that the moment for action had come. But he was in hiding from July to October, and some of the decisive work was done by others.
- **Organisation.** The Bolsheviks were a disciplined party with a newspaper (*Pravda*), a network of cells in factories and barracks, and a military organisation inside the garrison. They were well suited to winning elections to soviets, factory committees and trade unions.
- **The Red Guards and the garrison.** After the Kornilov Revolt, armed workers' detachments of tens of thousands of men existed, and units of the garrison and the fleet were pro-Bolshevik.
- **The opposition's mistakes.** Every failure of the Provisional Government (the offensive, the delay on land, the Kornilov affair) was a gain for the one party that had never been part of it.

By September the Bolsheviks had won majorities in the soviets of the main cities. On **31 August** the Petrograd Soviet passed a Bolshevik resolution for the first time, and the Moscow Soviet followed in early September. On **25 September** Leon Trotsky, who had joined the Bolsheviks in the summer and had been released from prison after Kornilov, was elected chairman of the Petrograd Soviet, a post he had held in 1905.

:::def Petrograd Soviet
The council of workers' and soldiers' deputies of the capital, set up on 27 February 1917. At first controlled by Mensheviks and SRs, it passed under Bolshevik control in September and gave the Bolsheviks a legal platform for the October rising.
:::

:::warn Do not overstate Bolshevik numbers or support
The Bolsheviks were not a mass party in the spring of 1917, and they did not have majority support across Russia in October. They won a majority in the Petrograd and Moscow Soviets and had strong support among the garrison, the workers and the sailors, but the peasants, who were most of the population, voted mainly for the SRs. In the elections to the Constituent Assembly held in November (see [Russia (4)](#hist.eu.russia.4)) the Bolsheviks won about a quarter of the votes. Say that they had decisive support **where it mattered, in the cities and the army**, rather than that "the people" supported them.
:::

### The road to October

By late summer the question was no longer whether the government would fall, but who would pick it up.

**The decision to rise.** In September, Lenin, still in hiding in Finland, wrote letters to the Central Committee demanding that the party take power at once, while the Bolsheviks had majorities in the capitals. Many of his colleagues hesitated. He returned secretly to Petrograd in early October, and on **10 October** the Central Committee voted, by ten votes to two, for an armed rising. The two against, Kamenev and Zinoviev, argued that the party should wait for the Constituent Assembly and the Second Congress of Soviets, which was to meet in late October, and made their opposition public in a newspaper on 18 October.

**The Military Revolutionary Committee.** The decisive step was organised by the Petrograd Soviet, not by the party. When Kerensky ordered units of the garrison to the front, the Soviet, under Trotsky, set up on **12 October** a **Military Revolutionary Committee** (MRC) with the claim of protecting the revolution and the city. By the third week of October the MRC had won over the garrison, sent its commissars to the units, and was giving orders that the army commanders could no longer disobey. This allowed the rising to be presented as an act of the Soviet, in defence of the Congress of Soviets, and not as a party coup. It gave Trotsky a legal cover and many soldiers a reason to obey.

:::def Military Revolutionary Committee
A committee of the Petrograd Soviet, set up on 12 October 1917, which took control of the garrison and organised the rising of 24–25 October. Its leaders included Trotsky and, among the Bolsheviks, Vladimir Antonov-Ovseenko and Nikolai Podvoisky.
:::

### The events of the October Revolution, 24–26 October

| Time (OS) | What happened |
|---|---|
| 24 October, early morning | Kerensky orders the closure of the Bolshevik newspapers and the arrest of the MRC leaders. The MRC treats this as the start of the counter-revolution, reopens the presses and orders its units to defend the Soviet |
| 24 October, day and night | Red Guards, soldiers and sailors occupy bridges, railway stations, the telephone exchange, the post and telegraph offices and the State Bank. Lenin leaves his hiding place and goes to the Smolny Institute, the headquarters of the Soviet, after dark |
| 25 October, morning | Kerensky leaves the city in a car to find loyal troops at the front. The MRC issues a proclamation that the Provisional Government has been deposed |
| 25 October, evening | The cruiser *Aurora* fires a blank shot as the signal for the attack on the Winter Palace, where the ministers are meeting under the protection of cadets, a women's battalion and Cossacks. The Second Congress of Soviets opens at the Smolny |
| 26 October, about 2 a.m. | The Winter Palace is taken with little fighting and the ministers arrested. The Congress, after the Mensheviks and Right SRs walk out in protest, approves the transfer of power to the Soviets |
| 26 October, evening | The Congress adopts Lenin's decrees on peace and land and elects a government, the Council of People's Commissars (**Sovnarkom**), with Lenin as chairman |

On the morning of 25 October the MRC's announcement read, in the words of the American eyewitness John Reed: "The Provisional Government is deposed", and "the State Power has passed into the hands of the organ of the Petrograd Soviet". It was somewhat ahead of events, since the ministers were still at large in the Winter Palace. The Palace itself was not stormed in a great battle. Reed described men pouring in through the Palace gates and corridors, "without song or cheer", until the cadets were disarmed. Very few people were killed. The famous images of a mass assault come from a film made by Sergei Eisenstein in 1928, not from the events.

The Congress was the second element of the Bolsheviks' success. It was elected by soviets all over Russia and its delegates were mostly in favour of Soviet power: of about 650 delegates, around 390 were Bolsheviks by the most-cited count (other counts are lower), and many of the SR delegates were Left SRs who also wanted power for the Soviets. The Mensheviks and Right SRs walked out. That left a Bolshevik and Left SR majority, which was able to claim that Soviet power had been given by the representatives of the workers, soldiers and peasants.

The Left SRs refused at first to join the government and Sovnarkom was entirely Bolshevik. Its first members included Lenin as chairman, Trotsky as Commissar for Foreign Affairs and Stalin as Commissar for Nationalities. The Bolsheviks' control of Petrograd did not mean control of Russia. Kerensky, who had escaped, raised a force of Cossacks under General Krasnov that was beaten outside the city on 30 October. Fighting in Moscow lasted about a week. The railwaymen's union, the Vikzhel, demanded a government of all the socialist parties, and negotiations dragged on in early November. The power of Sovnarkom outside the two capitals depended on the Soviets elsewhere, and it took months to win it: the rest of this story is told in [Russia (4)](#hist.eu.russia.4).

:::def October Revolution
The seizure of power in Petrograd by the Military Revolutionary Committee of the Petrograd Soviet, led by the Bolsheviks, on 24–26 October 1917 (OS; 6–8 November NS), followed by the approval of the Second Congress of Soviets and the formation of Sovnarkom under Lenin.
:::

:::def Sovnarkom
The Council of People's Commissars, the government formed by the Second Congress of Soviets on 26 October 1917, with Lenin as chairman. Its "commissars" ran the ministries and its decrees had the force of law until a constitution was adopted in 1918.
:::

## Why did the Bolsheviks win? Methods, promises and leadership

The syllabus asks about the methods the Bolsheviks used. They fall under four headings.

**1. Bolshevik promises.** "Peace, Land and Bread" and "All power to the Soviets" were slogans. They worked because they were concrete and because they said what millions already wanted. On 26 October, the Congress turned the first two into law. The Decree on Peace proposed an immediate peace without annexations. The **Decree on Land** declared that "landed proprietorship is abolished forthwith without any compensation", and that all landed estates, with crown, monastery and church lands, were to be put at the disposal of the local land committees and the Soviets of peasants' deputies, until the Constituent Assembly should decide. It was based on the demands of the peasants, collected from their own petitions (its "Peasant Mandate" said "private ownership of land shall be abolished forever" and that the "employment of hired labour is not permitted"). The Decree stated that the land of ordinary peasants and Cossacks was not to be confiscated. It was borrowed from the SR programme, but the Bolsheviks put it into law first. The Decree on Land legalised what the peasants were already doing; it won the peasants' acceptance, though not their love.

**2. Lenin's leadership.** Lenin changed the party's course in April, argued for the seizure of power in September and October against the doubts of his closest colleagues, and judged the moment correctly: the Provisional Government was weak, the Congress of Soviets was about to meet, and a delay might have let the Constituent Assembly, which the Bolsheviks would not control, meet. The historian Robert Service, in his biography *Lenin* (2000), portrays a driven and ruthless leader. But Lenin did not carry out the rising himself, and his main writings of September were sent from Finland.

**3. The Petrograd Soviet and Trotsky.** The Soviet was the legal platform of the revolution. As its chairman, Trotsky used it, and the Military Revolutionary Committee, to win the garrison without a battle and to present the rising as the defence of the Congress of Soviets. Trotsky's skill as an organiser and speaker was perhaps as important as Lenin's will. He later wrote his own *History of the Russian Revolution* (1930–32), a gripping account but one by a leading actor, so it must be read as evidence of what he wanted people to believe.

**4. The Military Revolutionary Committee and the Red Guards.** The MRC provided an organisation for the rising and the Red Guards provided the armed force. Their action in the capital was quick, planned and well coordinated. Against them, the Provisional Government had virtually no defenders. A few hundred cadets and a women's battalion did not make an army.

:::key Why the Bolsheviks won in October 1917
1. **The Provisional Government was weak**: it had lost the army, the workers, the peasants and the right, and it had not delivered peace, land or bread.
2. **The Bolsheviks' message fitted**: "Peace, Land and Bread" and "All power to the Soviets".
3. **They controlled the soviets and the garrison** in Petrograd and Moscow, and had armed Red Guards.
4. **Leadership**: Lenin's will and timing, and Trotsky's organisation of the MRC.
5. **Luck and the opposition's mistakes**: the Kornilov affair, Kerensky's isolation, and the moderate socialists' loss of support for supporting the war.
:::

### Was October a coup or a popular revolution?

This is the great debate about 1917.
- **Richard Pipes**, in *The Russian Revolution* (1990), argued that October was a coup by a small, ruthless party led by Lenin, which took advantage of the chaos. He stressed Lenin's role and the weakness of the Bolsheviks' mass support.
- **Alexander Rabinowitch**, in *The Bolsheviks Come to Power* (1976), argued that the Petrograd Bolsheviks were a flexible, democratic party with real popular support in the capital, and that the rising was a response to the mood of the workers and the garrison, not Lenin's plot.
- **Orlando Figes**, in *A People's Tragedy* (1996), took a middle view: the Bolsheviks won because their slogans caught the mood of the poor, and because the other parties had failed, but the October seizure itself was a minority act, which turned the people's revolution into the party's dictatorship.
- **Sheila Fitzpatrick**, in *The Russian Revolution* (1982), stressed the social history of the workers, soldiers and peasants who gave the Bolsheviks their power.

You do not need to choose a historian. You need to see that both readings are supported by evidence. The planning and the small number of those who fought look like a coup. The majorities in the soviets, the mood of the garrison and the approval of the Congress look like popular support. A good judgement is that **October was a planned seizure of power which succeeded because it was carried out on the crest of a popular movement**.

:::note The debate in one line (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
Compare the **February** and **October** revolutions. February was spontaneous, leaderless and popular; October was planned, led and carried out by a minority. The Bolsheviks' real skill was to turn a popular revolution that was already under way into a one-party government.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the Provisional Government continued the war in 1917.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The Provisional Government continued the war because it did not want to lose. Milyukov wrote a note in April to say that Russia would carry on. Kerensky organised the June offensive but it failed. The government also had promised the Allies that it would keep fighting. The Soviet wanted peace but the government did not listen."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence only states what happened. The answer says that the government "did not want to lose" without explaining why. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was the alliance. Britain and France had lent Russia large sums, and the new government depended on their loans and on their recognition. Milyukov, as Foreign Minister, wanted to keep the promises made by the Tsar, including the aim of getting the Straits and Constantinople, and his note of 18 April told the Allies that Russia would continue to the end. A second reason was that the ministers believed that a separate peace would be a disaster. Germany would impose harsh terms, and the army, which the government was trying to rebuild, would have nothing to fight for. Many liberals also believed that Russia's honour and its place among the Great Powers depended on staying in the war."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the socialists and the army, and show how the factors work together:

"A third reason was that, after May, the government included socialists such as Kerensky, who did not want peace at any price either. They believed in 'revolutionary defencism': Russia must defend the revolution against German militarism, and a peace without annexations could only come from a general negotiation among all the powers, not by Russia leaving alone. Kerensky, as Minister of War from May, believed that a successful offensive would restore the army's discipline and force the Allies and Germany to negotiate, and the June offensive was launched on that belief.

These factors were linked. The alliance and the loans made the war a condition of the government's survival; the liberals' view of Russia's honour made peace seem a betrayal; and the socialists' defencism meant that the Soviet moderates, who might have forced a change of policy, supported the war too. The most important reason was the dependence on the Allies, because it made the other arguments practical: without British and French money and recognition, a government that had no elected mandate would have fallen at once. The decision to continue the war was therefore not an accident but a product of the government's weakness and of its belief that it was only temporary: it could not change Russia's alliances, and it could not ask the people whether to end the war because there was no Constituent Assembly to ask."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the alliance made the war necessary, honour made it acceptable, defencism made it a revolutionary duty), and a supported conclusion about the main reason. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Lenin's leadership was the main reason for the Bolshevik seizure of power.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: April Theses (3–4 April) and slogans; his letters of September demanding action; the vote of 10 October against Kamenev and Zinoviev; his arrival at the Smolny on 24 October; Sovnarkom and the Decrees on Peace and Land.
- Against 1, the government: the war, the June offensive, the July Days, the land question, Kornilov; the Provisional Government lost the army and the cities.
- Against 2, others: Trotsky and the Petrograd Soviet; the MRC; the Red Guards; the garrison; the party's organisation.
- Against 3, the people: the soldiers, workers and peasants whose mood made the slogans work.
- Judgement: Lenin was necessary for the timing and the decision, but the government's collapse and the Soviet's organisation made it possible. Criterion: what turned the opportunity into success.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Lenin was the most important reason for the Bolshevik seizure of power. When he returned to Russia in April, he persuaded the Bolsheviks to give up support for the Provisional Government with the April Theses and to demand 'All power to the Soviets'. He wrote that the war must end and that the land must be given to the peasants, and these promises of 'Peace, Land and Bread' won the party followers. In September he sent letters from Finland urging the Central Committee to take power, and on 10 October it voted ten to two to do so, against Kamenev and Zinoviev. Without Lenin the Bolsheviks would not have acted, so his leadership was the main reason."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: the party voting to rise is not the same as the rising succeeding. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet Lenin's leadership explains why the Bolsheviks tried to seize power, not why they succeeded. The Provisional Government had by October lost all its supporters. The June offensive had wrecked the army; the government's refusal to allow land reform until the Constituent Assembly had let the peasants take the land themselves; and the Kornilov Revolt, which began as a muddled quarrel between Kerensky and his general, had destroyed the trust of the right and the left. When the Bolsheviks acted, the government had no troops it could call on but cadets and a women's battalion. A rising by a party that had been no more than 24,000 strong in February would not have succeeded against a government with any support; it succeeded because Kerensky's government was empty.

The success of the rising also owed much to others and to organisation. Trotsky, as chairman of the Petrograd Soviet from 25 September, used the Military Revolutionary Committee, set up on 12 October, to win the garrison before the rising began, and presented the seizure of power as the defence of the Congress of Soviets, which turned many undecided soldiers into supporters. The Red Guards, armed during the Kornilov crisis, and the Bolsheviks' disciplined cells in the factories and barracks provided the force. Lenin's own letters were written in hiding, and his colleagues Kamenev and Zinoviev showed that many Bolsheviks thought the rising a mistake. Finally, the slogans worked because the people wanted peace and land: Lenin gave voice to a mood he had not created.

Overall, Lenin's leadership was the decisive reason for the timing of the seizure of power, because it was his insistence in September and October that made the party act before the Constituent Assembly could meet. But it was not the main reason for the success of the seizure of power. That was the collapse of the Provisional Government, which gave the Bolsheviks an opening, and the organisation of the Soviet and the MRC, which let them use it. Lenin was therefore necessary but not sufficient; the statement is true of the decision, but not of the victory."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (the difference between deciding to act and succeeding) and distinguishes necessary from decisive causes. With an opening paragraph on Lenin's role, this is Level 5 (18–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

The Provisional Government has been deposed. State power has passed into the hands of the Military Revolutionary Committee, which stands at the head of the Petrograd proletariat and garrison. The cause for which the people have fought, the immediate offer of a democratic peace, the abolition of landed proprietorship, workers' control over production and the establishment of Soviet power, has been secured. Long live the revolution of workers, soldiers and peasants!

*Adapted from "To the Citizens of Russia!", a proclamation of the Military Revolutionary Committee of the Petrograd Soviet, written by Lenin and issued at 10 a.m. on 25 October 1917 (7 November NS). Proletariat: the industrial working class.*

**Source B**

Maniacs have raised a revolt against the only governmental power chosen by the people, the Provisional Government. The Petrograd Soviet has declared the Provisional Government deposed and demands the transfer of power under threat of bombarding the Winter Palace. The government will surrender its power only to the Constituent Assembly. It has decided not to yield.

*Adapted from two messages of the Provisional Government on 25 October 1917 (7 November NS): the first sentence from its appeal "To the People", published in Izvestiya on 26 October; the rest from its telegram "To the Army", sent to army headquarters at 7 p.m. and signed by A. Konovalov, the acting Prime Minister.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the situation in Petrograd on 25 October 1917.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 5 marks).** "Source A says the Provisional Government has been deposed. Source B says the Provisional Government will not yield. So they disagree about whether the government has fallen. Source A is from the Bolsheviks and Source B is from the Provisional Government, so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14 marks).**

"The sources share some common ground. Both claim to act in the name of the people: Source A for 'workers, soldiers and peasants', Source B for 'the only governmental power chosen by the people'. Both also agree on the central fact that the Petrograd Soviet has declared the government deposed, and both invoke a democratic authority that will decide the future: for Source A it is the Soviet power, for Source B the Constituent Assembly.

On the situation, however, they are directly opposed. Source A says the government 'has been deposed' and that power has passed to the Military Revolutionary Committee. Source B calls the rising a revolt of 'maniacs' and says the government 'has decided not to yield'. Source A speaks as if everything is finished, Source B as if the outcome is open. A second difference is the aims: Source A lists the promises of peace, land and workers' control, whilst Source B offers only the defence of the existing legal order and the promise of a Constituent Assembly.

The context explains these differences. Source A was written by Lenin at 10 a.m., before the Winter Palace was taken and while the ministers were still free: it was meant to announce the success of the rising and to persuade soldiers and workers, who were waiting for orders, that resistance was useless. It was ahead of events, but not wrong, since the government had no force to resist. Its purpose is propaganda, and its promises of peace and land are the Bolshevik programme, not a report of what had been done. Source B was issued later the same day, after the MRC had threatened to bombard the Winter Palace, as the source itself says. Its purpose was to rally loyal troops and the public, hence the language of 'maniacs'. Its claim that the government was 'chosen by the people' is questionable, because the Provisional Government had never been elected, which is the reason it spoke of the Assembly. For the situation on 25 October, Source A is stronger on what had happened in the city, since it was confirmed within hours by the fall of the Palace; Source B is stronger evidence of the government's helplessness, since its only weapon was an appeal."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the time of day, the state of the siege) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that the Bolsheviks won power in October 1917 because they gave the people what they wanted?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (the Bolshevik programme: end the war, give the land, Soviet power), B (the Decree on Land delivers what A promised, and the peasants' demand), D (soldiers wanted rights and dignity, which the Soviet gave). Challenge or qualify: C (shows the Palace taken by force, with the ministers arrested, rather than the people choosing); D (it is an order of the Soviet in March, issued before the Bolsheviks had any influence, so it supports the Soviet's appeal, not the Bolsheviks'); B (the Decree is a temporary measure that borrowed from the SRs); A (a programme is not support).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: Lenin's own programme, written for his party, to persuade his colleagues. It is strong evidence of what the Bolsheviks **offered** and weak evidence of what the people **wanted**. The cool reception it first got from most Bolshevik leaders shows that it went beyond what his own party expected.
- **B**: an official decree passed in a hurry, with the Left SRs, and it was based on the SR land programme and on 242 peasant petitions. It is strong evidence that the Bolsheviks adopted the peasants' demand to win support, and it only legalised what the peasants were doing. It is weaker as evidence that the peasants then supported the Bolsheviks, because they voted mainly for the SRs in November.
- **C**: an eyewitness, close to the events, but a committed socialist, writing in 1919 to defend the revolution. His picture of an orderly, disciplined crowd is partial, but the main fact is confirmed by other accounts: the ministers were arrested with little fighting. It is strong evidence that the government had no defenders, weak evidence of popular consent.
- **D**: the Soviet's order, not a Bolshevik document, and eight months before October. It shows what soldiers wanted (committees, dignity, control of arms), which the Provisional Government never accepted in full. It explains why the garrison turned to the Soviet and later to the party that controlled it.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A and B show a link between the Bolshevik programme and what the people wanted. In Source A Lenin demanded 'no support for the Provisional Government' and the nationalisation of land, and in Source B the new government delivered a Decree on Land within a day of taking power. Source D supports the idea from another angle: the soldiers wanted a say and respect, which the Soviet, and later the Bolsheviks who dominated it, gave them. But the sources also qualify the view. Source A is a programme, written to persuade his own party, and many Bolsheviks thought it mistaken, so it shows what Lenin offered rather than what the people already wanted. Source B borrowed from the SRs and, as the November elections showed, the peasants did not vote for the Bolsheviks. Source C shows that the rising succeeded because the Provisional Government had no defenders, and not because the crowds at the Palace had chosen the Bolsheviks. On balance the sources support the view to a considerable extent: the party won power because it promised peace, land and workers' control and adopted the demands of soldiers and peasants. But the sources show equally that its victory was a result of the government's weakness and of the Soviet's organisation. They agree only partly: the Bolsheviks won the support of the garrison and the capital's workers by giving them what they wanted, but they did not win Russia."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1917; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "To what extent was the First World War responsible for the fall of the Tsar?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The First World War began in 1914 and Russia was soon defeated at Tannenberg, where 92,000 men were taken prisoner. Then there was the Great Retreat in 1915. In 1915 the Tsar took command of the army. The Tsarina, who was German, ran the country with Rasputin, who was murdered in December 1916. In February 1917 women went on strike in Petrograd on International Women's Day and the soldiers joined them. On 2 March the Tsar abdicated and his brother Michael refused the throne. So the war caused the Tsar to fall."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story from 1914 to 1917 and then asserts the conclusion in the last sentence. It never explains how the war weakened the regime, and it never considers any other cause, such as the failure of political reform since 1905. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The war was the main reason for the fall of the Tsar because it destroyed the three supports of his rule. It cost him the loyalty of the army: by the winter of 1916–17 millions had been killed, wounded or captured and a garrison of raw recruits in Petrograd was unwilling to fire on women asking for bread. It cost him the support of the elite, because his decision to take command in 1915 made every defeat his own, and the Progressive Bloc, whom he refused to meet, was pushed into opposition. And it cost him the loyalty of the cities, because inflation and transport breakdown caused the bread queues of February. However, it was not the only reason: the Tsar's refusal after 1905 to share power with the Duma made it impossible to use the elite's willingness to help in 1914." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give two defeats of the Russian army in 1914–15 and say why the Tsar's decision to take command mattered.
A: Tannenberg (August 1914) and the Great Retreat of 1915. After he took command on 23 August 1915 every defeat was blamed on the Tsar personally, and he left the home government to the Tsarina and Rasputin.
Q: What was the Progressive Bloc, and why did its failure matter?
A: A majority grouping of Duma parties formed in August 1915 that asked for a "ministry of public confidence". The Tsar refused and suspended the Duma, which pushed moderate and propertied opinion into opposition.
Q: Why did the February Revolution succeed so quickly?
A: A bread strike on 23 February became a general strike; the Tsar ordered the troops to restore order; on 26–27 February the garrison refused to fire and then joined the crowd, so the regime had no force left.
Q: What was dual power, and what was Order No. 1?
A: Dual power was the sharing of authority between the Provisional Government (legal authority) and the Petrograd Soviet (control of soldiers and railways). Order No. 1 (1 March) set up soldiers' committees and said the Duma's orders were to be obeyed only where they did not conflict with the Soviet's.
Q: Give three reasons why the Provisional Government failed.
A: It continued the war and the June offensive failed; it asked the peasants to wait for land reform until the Constituent Assembly; inflation and shortages continued; it was weakened by dual power and by coalitions that kept breaking up.
Q: What happened in the July Days, and what was the result for the Bolsheviks?
A: On 3–5 July soldiers and workers rose in Petrograd calling for Soviet power. The Bolsheviks had not planned it, but they were blamed: Lenin fled to Finland and Trotsky and Kamenev were arrested. Kerensky became Prime Minister on 8 July.
Q: How did the Kornilov Revolt help the Bolsheviks?
A: Kerensky had to ask the Soviet for help; the Red Guards were armed and Bolsheviks released from prison; the army's trust in Kerensky ended, and the Bolsheviks looked like the defenders of the revolution. They won a majority in the Petrograd Soviet on 31 August.
Q: What was the Military Revolutionary Committee, and what did it do?
A: A body of the Petrograd Soviet, set up on 12 October, which took control of the garrison. It organised the rising of 24–25 October, which was presented as the defence of the Congress of Soviets.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Petrograd garrison joined the February Revolution.
A: Indicative content: most of the garrison were raw recruits and reservists, many of them peasants who feared being sent to the front; they were crowded into barracks in a city short of bread, and they shared the crowds' hunger and anger; the order on 25–26 February to shoot at demonstrators was something many refused to do, and the Pavlovsky Guards and Volynsky regiment mutinied on 26–27 February; officers had lost authority because of the war's defeats; once some units had mutinied the others had little choice. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: the war put peasants in uniform in the capital, hunger gave them a cause in common with the strikers, and the order to shoot was the trigger; it judges which mattered most (e.g. that the war had already destroyed their loyalty, so the order to shoot was only the trigger).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Provisional Government failed to solve the land question in 1917.
A: Indicative content: the government and the moderate socialists believed that only the Constituent Assembly could legally decide such a large change, and that the Assembly could not be elected until after the war; the liberals in the government represented landowners; the land committees set up in 1917 collected information but had no power; fear of angering the soldiers, many of whom wanted land, and of splitting the Allies' support; the peasants lost patience and seized land in the summer and autumn. Level 4: connect the legal caution, the political make-up of the government and the war, and judge that the decision to wait for the Assembly, which was linked to the war, was most important.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the Provisional Government's decision to continue the war the main reason for its fall?
A: For: the war cost it the army (the June offensive, desertions), the workers (inflation and shortages), and the soldiers' trust (the April note); the Bolsheviks' "Peace" slogan; Kornilov as a result of the army crisis. Against: the land question; dual power and the weakness of the government; economic collapse; Kerensky's mistakes (Kornilov); the Bolsheviks' organisation and leadership. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that the war was the underlying cause because it made the land, bread and army problems impossible to solve, but that the final collapse came from the government's isolation after the Kornilov affair and from the Bolsheviks' use of the Soviet.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Provisional Government failed because of its own mistakes.' How far do you agree?
A: For: refusing to end the war; the April note and the June offensive; delaying land reform and the Constituent Assembly; the handling of Kornilov. Against: it inherited a crisis from Tsarism (war, inflation, transport collapse); dual power gave it no real authority; Allied pressure; the Bolsheviks' ruthless exploitation of its difficulties; chance (Lenin's return). Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "could another government have done better?", and judges, e.g., that its mistakes were real (delay on land and war) but that they came from the limits on its power, so the structure of dual power mattered more.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the weakness of the Tsar's government in early 1917.
A: Similarities: both show a ruler who has lost control (A: "anarchy in the capital … the government is paralysed"; B: the Tsar gives up the throne); both refer to the interests of the war and the country; both involve the Duma (A is from its president, B says the abdication was "in agreement with the State Duma"). Differences: A is an urgent warning that asks for a new government and describes disorder; B presents abdication as a voluntary sacrifice and never mentions the revolution; A is on 26 February, before the mutiny, B four days later. Explanation: the Tsar had ignored A (he is said to have dismissed it as nonsense) and the speed of events made B necessary; B was drafted at the Stavka to save the monarchy through Michael and to present the abdication as a service to the war effort, hence its tone. Provenance: A is a report by a conservative who feared revolution, B is an official manifesto written to be read by the whole country, so it hides the pressure behind it. Level 4 explains the differences through this context and judges which is better evidence of the weakness of the government (A shows it from the inside, B shows how the Tsar wanted it to be seen).
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far did Lenin's April Theses change the course of the Bolshevik party in 1917?
H: What did the Bolshevik leaders in Petrograd believe before Lenin arrived?
H: What did the party do differently after April, and what might have happened without Lenin?
A: Change: Lenin ended the policy of conditional support for the Provisional Government and demanded Soviet power; he gave the party its slogans (Peace, Land and Bread, All power to the Soviets); the party grew rapidly after April. Continuity and limits: the party was cautious in July and many leaders resisted a rising in October; the success of the policy depended on the government's failures and on the mood of workers and soldiers, who were already moving to the left; the April Theses were adopted by the party gradually. Level 5 judges, e.g., that the Theses gave the party a clear direction and the opportunity to benefit from the government's failures, but the support came from events more than from the document.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent was the Kornilov Revolt a turning point in 1917?
H: What was the position of the Bolsheviks before and after 27 August?
H: Compare it with other possible turning points: the July Days, the June offensive, Lenin's return.
A: For: the Bolsheviks were released from prison and armed; Kerensky lost the army and the right; the Bolsheviks won majorities in the soviets from 31 August; the Soviet recovered its power. Against: the government was already weakened by the June offensive and by dual power; the July Days showed that workers and soldiers would rise; Lenin's return and April Theses gave direction; the Bolsheviks' organisation made use of the opportunity. Level 5 judges that Kornilov was the turning point for the Bolsheviks (it changed their position from persecuted to dominant in weeks) but not for the revolution as a whole, whose direction had been set by the war.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Some historians see October 1917 as a popular revolution, others as a coup. How far does the evidence of 1917 support one view more than the other?
H: List what supports a coup: planning, small numbers, Lenin's role, the MRC, the Congress being presented with a fait accompli.
H: Then what supports a popular revolution: soviets and the garrison, the Bolshevik majorities, the Congress vote, the Decrees, the lack of defenders.
A: Coup: the party was only a quarter of the vote in November and had no peasant majority; the Central Committee planned the rising on 10 October; the seizure of the city was by a few thousand Red Guards, soldiers and sailors; the Congress was presented with the government as a fact; Kamenev and Zinoviev's objection showed that many Bolsheviks doubted it. Popular: the Bolshevik majorities in Petrograd and Moscow Soviets before the rising; the garrison's support through the MRC; the mood of the capital, which no one defended; the Congress of delegates elected by soviets which approved the transfer of power; the response to the Decrees. A* judgement: October was a planned seizure of power by a minority party, but it was made possible by, and justified by, a popular movement in the cities and the army. The Bolsheviks' skill was to turn a revolution that was popular into the rule of one party.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ John Reed's *Ten Days that Shook the World* (1919) is often used by historians as evidence of the events of October 1917. How far is it reliable evidence of how the Bolsheviks took power?
H: Who was Reed, and where was he in October 1917? What did he want his readers to think?
H: What can an eyewitness see, and what can he not see?
A: Strengths: an eyewitness in Petrograd, with access to Smolny and the Congress; detailed, day-by-day, with many quoted speeches and documents; many details agree with other accounts (the ministers arrested, the vote on peace and land, the Mensheviks' walk-out). Weaknesses: a committed socialist who supported the Bolsheviks and wrote to defend the revolution (Lenin wrote an introduction to the Russian edition); he saw only part of events, and had no access to the Central Committee's discussions, so he says little of the planning; written two years later from notes and the press, in English for an American audience; he speaks of a popular rising when the evidence shows a minority party's planning. Judgement: reliable evidence for what could be seen in public (the atmosphere in the Smolny and the Palace, the speeches, the votes), unreliable on why the Bolsheviks won and on how far they were supported outside the capital.

- **War 1914–16**: Tannenberg (August 1914), the Great Retreat (1915), the Brusilov offensive (June 1916); the Tsar took command on 23 August 1915; Progressive Bloc and suspension of the Duma; the Tsarina, Rasputin (murdered 16–17 December 1916) and ministerial leapfrog.

- **Dual power**: the Provisional Government (legal authority, Lvov, Milyukov, Kerensky) and the Petrograd Soviet (soldiers and railways); Order No. 1 (1 March).
