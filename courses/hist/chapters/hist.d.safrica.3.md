:::why
In 1978 South Africa looked like a strong state: the army was the most powerful in Africa, the economy was rich in gold and the black opposition was banned, in exile or in prison. Sixteen years later, on 10 May 1994, Nelson Mandela, who had spent 27 years in prison, was sworn in as President after an election in which almost 20 million South Africans voted. The change came without civil war and without a military defeat of the white state. Paper 4 asks you to explain why. Was it the pressure of the townships, the weight of sanctions, the end of the Cold War, the courage of Mandela or the realism of de Klerk? No single answer is right, and the best essays rank the causes and say how they worked together. You need the events in order and a way of weighing them.

## What is the question really asking?

"Why did apartheid and minority rule come to an end?" is a causation question over sixteen years, and the paper sets it in several forms: one cause ("international pressure"), two people (de Klerk and Mandela), one policy (Total Strategy) or one stage (the negotiations). Most strong answers sort the causes into four groups.
1. **Resistance inside South Africa.** The township revolt, the UDF, the trade unions, the ANC's underground and armed struggle, and the ANC's moral authority through Mandela.
2. **The failure of the state's own policy.** Botha's Total Strategy tried to reform enough to survive and to repress enough to stay in control, and it did neither well enough.
3. **Pressure from outside.** Sanctions, the loss of bank credit, the sports and cultural boycott, the frontline states, the Cuban and Angolan war, and the end of the Cold War.
4. **Leadership and choice.** Botha's attempts, de Klerk's decision of February 1990, Mandela's decision to negotiate from prison, and the compromises of Ramaphosa, Meyer, Slovo, Buthelezi and others.

A fifth theme runs through the story of 1990–94: **why the end came by negotiation and not by civil war**, in a country with a strong army, rival armies of its own people and a record of political killing.

:::def Total Strategy
P. W. Botha's policy from the late 1970s. It held that South Africa faced a "total onslaught" from communism, which used military, political, economic and ideological means, and that the answer had to be total too: reform to win the support of some Black South Africans and of the world, together with military and police power at home and in the region. It was coordinated through the State Security Council. Its aim was to keep white control under a more modern system, not to end it.
:::

:::def Negotiated revolution
A term often used for the change of 1990–94: a transfer of political power achieved by talks and compromise, without a military victory by either side, but with a revolutionary result, since a white-minority state gave way to majority rule. The word "revolution" is the historian's label, and it hides the fact that the talks ran alongside very serious violence.
:::

## Botha's problems, 1978–83

### From Vorster to Botha

B. J. Vorster's government ended in a scandal. In 1978 an inquiry revealed that the Department of Information had spent secret government money on propaganda abroad, including an attempt to buy a newspaper, the *Washington Star*, and the minister, Connie Mulder, lost the contest to succeed Vorster. **Pieter Willem Botha**, Minister of Defence since 1966 and the leader of the Cape National Party, was chosen as party leader in September 1978 and became Prime Minister in October, while Vorster moved to the largely ceremonial post of State President. Botha was a very different man from the lawyers who had led the party before him: a party organiser who knew the army and the security services, impatient with the National Party's talk and ready to take decisions. His reputation as a man of short temper earned him the nickname "the Great Crocodile".

Botha inherited four problems that he saw as a single threat.

### The townships and Black resistance

The generation of Soweto did not disappear. Thousands of young people had left in 1976–77 and joined the ANC's armed wing, **Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK)**, and after 1977 MK carried out bombings and attacks that had more effect than the sabotage of the 1960s: attacks on oil-from-coal plants at Sasolburg and Secunda in June 1980, on the Koeberg nuclear power station under construction in December 1982, and a car bomb outside the Air Force headquarters in Church Street, Pretoria, on 20 May 1983 that killed 19 people (and injured many more), among them civilians. The ANC's Radio Freedom and its leaflets kept the idea of the movement alive, and Mandela's name became a symbol. MK did not threaten the state militarily, but it showed that the ANC was active and that young Africans would join.

Inside the country, resistance was being rebuilt in a new form, the **civic organisation** of township residents who objected to rents, housing, buses and schools, and the **trade union**. After the Durban strikes of 1973 (a wave of strikes by African workers in Durban over wages, outside the legal union system, which the state could not stop), African workers organised in unions outside the old system, and the state, instead of simply banning them, decided to recognise them (see below).

### The economy

The economy that had boomed in the 1960s was in trouble. The gold price, which peaked in January 1980, fell back, and the cost of the arms industry and of the army grew. The economy suffered from the recession that followed the oil crisis of the 1970s, from inflation, and from a shortage of skilled workers, because the education system for Africans had not produced them. By most estimates growth in the 1980s was slower than population growth, so that the average South African was getting poorer. Business leaders, among them Harry Oppenheimer, the former chairman of the Anglo American Corporation, and the mining houses, were more and more critical of the labour controls that kept the economy from using African skills.

### The region

The state's third problem was the loss of the buffer of friendly territories described in [End of minority rule (2)](#hist.d.safrica.2). Mozambique and Angola had become independent under Marxist governments in 1975. In **April 1980 Zimbabwe** became independent with Robert Mugabe as Prime Minister (see [End of minority rule (4): the end of white minority rule in Zimbabwe, 1965–80](#hist.d.safrica.4)), and South Africa now faced a hostile state along its northern border. The ANC's bases were in Zambia, Angola, Tanzania and Mozambique, and its guerrillas infiltrated through Botswana, Lesotho, Swaziland and Zimbabwe. South West Africa (Namibia), which South Africa ruled in defiance of the UN, was fought over by the SWAPO guerrillas, based in Angola, and the South African army. Botha's government came to see a "total onslaught" that came from the Soviet Union and Cuba and worked through the ANC.

### The world

The fourth problem was the world's view of South Africa. The arms embargo of 1977 was in force, the sports boycott was working and the Anti-Apartheid Movement and campaigns for divestment (selling shares in companies that did business in South Africa) were growing in Britain and the USA. The Reagan administration (1981–89) preferred a policy it called "constructive engagement", which meant talking to Pretoria in the hope of change, and the British government of Margaret Thatcher opposed economic sanctions. Even so, the South African government's weakest point was its need for foreign loans and for the confidence of foreign banks.

## Total Strategy: reform and repression

### The idea

Botha had developed the thinking as Defence Minister, in the **1977 Defence White Paper**, which warned of a "total onslaught" on South Africa. His answer, Total Strategy, combined two things. **Reform** was to be used to remove the most obvious grievances, win allies among Africans (a Black middle class and workers), improve the economy and persuade the West. **Repression**, with the army and the police, was to deal with the rest. The tool of coordination was the **State Security Council**, a committee of ministers, generals and police and intelligence chiefs, over which the Prime Minister presided. Gradually it took over decisions from the cabinet, and a network of local committees (the National Security Management System) was set up to coordinate the work of the state down to the township. Botha's slogan, in 1979, was that his party must **"adapt or die"**. He meant that apartheid in its old form could not last, but that change should be directed by the state and should keep whites in control.

:::key What Total Strategy was trying to do
1. **Keep white control by a different route.** Remove petty apartheid and the cost of the labour system, but keep political power.
2. **Divide the opposition.** Co-opt Coloureds, Indians and an African middle class, and separate them from the "radicals".
3. **Strengthen the state.** The army, the police and the security service were given a large place in decisions.
4. **Defend the borders.** Support anti-communist forces in Angola (UNITA) and Mozambique (Renamo), raid the ANC's bases and press neighbours to expel it.
5. **Win back foreign opinion and money,** by showing that reform was taking place.
:::

### The reforms

- **Black trade unions, 1979.** The **Wiehahn Commission** (appointed in 1977) recommended that African workers should be allowed to join registered unions, and the **Industrial Conciliation Amendment Act of 1979** did this. The government wanted to bring the unions into a system it could regulate and to remove the cause of strikes. The effect was the opposite: legal unions became the strongest organised bodies of Black people in the country. The **Congress of South African Trade Unions (COSATU)** was founded in December 1985 with about 450,000 members (a figure that grew fast), under its general secretary Jay Naidoo, and the National Union of Mineworkers, founded in 1982 under Cyril Ramaphosa, led more than 300,000 miners in the largest strike in South African mining history in August 1987.
- **The Riekert Commission (1979).** It proposed that Africans with legal rights to live in the towns should have better housing and rights, while migrants from the homelands were controlled more tightly. It was a way to split urban Africans from the rest.
- **Local government.** Black Local Authorities (town councils for African townships) were introduced in 1982–83, with little money and no power. Residents distrusted the councillors, whom they saw as collaborators.
- **The tricameral parliament.** A new constitution, approved by white voters on **2 November 1983** (66.3 per cent for), created three chambers: a House of Assembly for whites (178 seats), a House of Representatives for Coloureds (85) and a House of Delegates for Indians (45). The new State President, chosen by an electoral college in which whites had a built-in majority, held the real power, and **Africans were excluded entirely**, on the grounds that they had their political rights in the homelands. The constitution took effect in September 1984, and on 14 September Botha was elected the first executive State President.
- **Other relaxations.** The ban on marriage between races and on sex across the colour line was repealed in 1985. Parts of "petty apartheid" (separate public facilities) were dropped in practice. The **pass laws and influx control were abolished by the Abolition of Influx Control Act, which took effect on 1 July 1986**, after a long review, but Africans were still excluded from the cities by the Group Areas Act and by housing policy.
- **Education and housing.** More money went to African schools and housing, but the gap with white schools remained large.

### The repression

Alongside reform the state used force.
- **The army and the police** were expanded and used in townships from October 1984 (see below). Conscription for white men was increased, and the End Conscription Campaign (1983) opposed it.
- **Regional destabilisation.** South Africa raided or supported rebels in its neighbours. Its forces attacked the ANC in Lesotho (Maseru, December 1982, with 42 killed, most of them ANC members) and later in Botswana and Zimbabwe, backed UNITA in Angola and Renamo in Mozambique, and invaded Angola several times. In March 1984 South Africa and Mozambique signed the **Nkomati Accord**, in which each promised to stop supporting the other's enemies. Mozambique expelled most ANC people, but South Africa's support for Renamo did not end.
- **Detention and murder.** The security police detained, tortured and killed opponents. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (the TRC, set up after 1994 to investigate the human-rights abuses of the apartheid years) later found that the state had killed and attacked opponents at home and abroad in the 1980s, among them the "Cradock Four" (Matthew Goniwe and three others, June 1985) and the activist Dulcie September, shot in Paris in 1988.

:::note Evidence: was Total Strategy "reform" or "repression"?
Historians and politicians disagree about the balance. Defenders of the policy said that Botha was moving a reluctant party and a divided white electorate towards change. Critics, among them the ANC and the UDF, said that every reform was designed to keep white power, and that the extra repression showed that the real policy was control. A top essay avoids choosing a side too early: it asks what each reform *changed* (unions and the end of influx control changed a great deal, the tricameral parliament changed nothing for Africans) and what the repression *cost*.
:::

## The United Democratic Front and the township revolt, 1983–86

### The UDF

Total Strategy's most visible failure was the response to the tricameral parliament. Even before the referendum, a movement began to form against it. On **20 August 1983** the **United Democratic Front (UDF)** was launched in the Rocklands Community Hall, Mitchell's Plain, near Cape Town, with delegates from about 575 organisations (and later many more). It brought together civic associations, church groups, student, youth and women's organisations, and some trade unions, under presidents such as Archie Gumede, Oscar Mpetha and Albertina Sisulu and with the support of Archbishop Desmond Tutu and the clergyman Allan Boesak. The UDF was a front, not a party. It was not formally part of the ANC, but it took the Freedom Charter of 1955 as its programme and called for the release of Mandela, so that in practice the UDF was the ANC's voice inside the country.

The UDF campaigned against the new constitution and against the Black Local Authorities. Its first great success was the **boycott of the 1984 elections** to the Coloured and Indian chambers: turnout was very low, so the new constitution started with weak legitimacy among the very people it was meant to win over. The UDF also built a non-racial image that contrasted with the Black Consciousness movement's, and it became the centre of the movement that organised the 1980s protests.

### The Vaal uprising and the spread of revolt

The revolt began in the townships near Johannesburg. On **3 September 1984**, the day that the new constitution came into force, residents of the Vaal townships (Sebokeng, Sharpeville and Boipatong among them) marched against rent increases imposed by their local councils. The councils were seen as puppets, and a deputy mayor was killed. The police responded with force, and the protest turned into a general revolt that spread over the Transvaal and, in 1985, to the Eastern Cape and the Western Cape. In October 1984 about 7,000 soldiers and police went into Sebokeng, Sharpeville and Boipatong in the first large use of the army in the townships.

The revolt took a number of forms.
- **Rent and consumer boycotts** against the councils and against white shops.
- **School boycotts.** Pupils refused to attend, under the slogan "liberation now, education later". From late 1985 the Soweto Parents' Crisis Committee tried to turn the boycott into "People's Education", a different curriculum, but the schools stayed disrupted and a generation lost years of study.
- **"Ungovernability."** The ANC, broadcasting from Lusaka, called on people to make the townships ungovernable. Councillors and police were attacked, and many resigned. Street committees and "people's courts" took over some areas, so that for a time the state lost control of parts of the townships.
- **Violence among Black people.** Crowds attacked people whom they accused of working with the state, and some killed them in terrible ways. The practice divided opinion, even inside the movement, and the state used it in its propaganda.
- **Police killings.** On 21 March 1985, the twenty-fifth anniversary of Sharpeville, police fired on a funeral procession at Langa near Uitenhage, in the Eastern Cape, and killed about 20 people. Pictures from the townships were shown on television across the world, until the government banned the cameras.

### The emergencies

On **21 July 1985** Botha declared a **state of emergency** in 36 magisterial districts. It was lifted in March 1986, but on **12 June 1986** a **nationwide state of emergency** was declared, which was renewed every year until June 1990. The emergency gave the police and army the power to arrest without warrant, to detain without trial and to ban meetings and funerals, and in 1986 and 1987 rules restricted what newspapers and television could report. Tens of thousands of people were detained over the emergencies, among them children, and many were tortured. In February 1988 the government **restricted 17 organisations**, among them the UDF and, partly, COSATU, so that they could not carry out normal political work. The leaders of the UDF were tried for treason (the Delmas trial, 1985–88).

:::key How effective was the state's repression of the townships?
1. **Militarily and in the short term, very effective.** By 1987 the police and army had retaken the townships, detained thousands of activists and broken the street committees.
2. **Politically, it failed.** The state could stop the revolt but could not make the councils and the tricameral system legitimate, so that Black local government collapsed, the elections to the Coloured and Indian chambers were boycotted and no African leaders of standing would cooperate.
3. **It cost the state its image abroad.** Pictures of troops in the townships, killings and detention of children caused the sanctions of 1985–86.
4. **It fed the movement.** Funerals, detentions and killings kept the anger alive, and the UDF and COSATU became more and more closely linked to the ANC.
5. **It left the state unable to reform without a new idea.** It had the power to keep control for years, but had no policy to offer that Black South Africans would accept, and so the security chiefs began to see that talks were needed.
:::

## Economic crisis and international pressure, 1985–89

### The Rubicon speech and the debt crisis

By mid-1985 the government faced revolt at home and loss of confidence abroad. South African firms and the state had borrowed heavily from foreign banks, many of them American, on short-term loans that were renewed each year. When the loans were not renewed, the economy would be in trouble. Foreign opinion was shocked by the township killings and the emergency, and many people expected the government to announce a great change. On **15 August 1985**, in Durban, Botha made the speech that was expected to announce it. It became known as the **Rubicon speech**.

:::note Evidence: Botha's Rubicon speech, 15 August 1985
*From P. W. Botha, speech to the Natal congress of the National Party, Durban, 15 August 1985 (the speech was broadcast on television). One phrase only; the words are Botha's own, as reported in accounts of the speech.*

"I believe that we are today crossing the Rubicon. There can be no turning back."

*Use.* The phrase suggested a decision of historic importance, and the foreign press and bankers expected an announcement of release for Mandela or of talks with the ANC. Botha instead rejected majority rule and the idea of power-sharing with Africans in the national government, said that he would not release Mandela unless he gave up violence, and blamed communist agitators. As evidence the speech shows how far a government that had begun reform was still unwilling to give up control, and how Botha misjudged his audience. It was a speech to his own party and to white voters, so you should not read it as a plan addressed to the world.
:::

The effect on confidence was immediate. The rand fell sharply and, as it did so, foreign banks, led by the American Chase Manhattan Bank (which had made its decision before the speech), refused to renew loans. At the end of August 1985 the government closed the foreign exchange market and announced a **standstill** on repayment of much of the foreign debt, which meant that South Africa could not borrow abroad normally. The loans had to be renegotiated over years (the last repayment was in 2001), which put the government in the hands of the banks. This was the first sign that economic pressure could bite, and it came from bankers and not from governments.

### Sanctions and disinvestment

The pressure widened in 1985–86.
- **The USA.** Divestment campaigns on American campuses, in cities and in state pension funds grew, and Congress passed the **Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act**. President Reagan vetoed it, saying that sanctions would hurt Black South Africans, but Congress overrode his veto on **2 October 1986**, with votes from both parties. It banned new American investment and new loans, imports of South African iron, steel, coal, uranium, textiles and farm goods, and the landing of South African Airways in the USA. Many American companies, among them General Motors and IBM, sold their South African businesses.
- **The Commonwealth and Europe.** The Commonwealth sent an **Eminent Persons Group**, led by the former Australian Prime Minister Malcolm Fraser and the former Nigerian head of state Olusegun Obasanjo, to try to start talks in 1986. In May, during its visit, South African forces attacked ANC targets in Zimbabwe, Zambia and Botswana, and the group reported that the government was not serious about negotiating. Most Commonwealth members, though not Britain under Thatcher, then voted for stronger sanctions, and the European Community adopted limited ones (a ban on iron, steel and gold coins and on new investment).
- **Business and sport.** More foreign companies pulled out or reduced their stake, the sports and cultural boycott continued, and South African firms found it harder to sell their goods and to attract capital.

### How much did sanctions matter?

This is one of the central arguments of the topic.

:::key The case that sanctions and economic pressure mattered
1. **The banks decided in 1985.** The refusal of credit and the debt standstill hit the government at the most sensitive point, its ability to borrow, and made the economy dependent on repayments.
2. **Growth was slow for a decade.** Isolation, the loss of capital and technology, the cost of the army and of the apartheid system and the fall of the gold price left the average South African poorer, and so threatened the white middle class on which the National Party relied.
3. **Business leaders turned against apartheid.** Many were in contact with the ANC (a business delegation led by Gavin Relly, the head of the Anglo American Corporation, met ANC leaders in Zambia in September 1985) and urged reform.
4. **They hurt morale.** The sports boycott and isolation cut whites off from the world and made them feel that apartheid had no future.
5. **They sent a signal.** The 1986 Act and the Commonwealth's action told the government that the West would not defend it.
:::

:::key The case that sanctions mattered less than claimed
1. **The economy did not collapse.** South Africa traded round them, through third countries, and found new markets (in Asia, for example); gold and minerals were still sold.
2. **Sanctions were partial and late.** Thatcher and Reagan opposed them for most of the decade, and the sanctions of 1986 covered only some goods.
3. **They could also harden attitudes.** Many whites reacted with a "laager" (defensive) mentality, and Black workers who lost jobs suffered first.
4. **The economy was damaged for other reasons,** among them the gold price, the cost of apartheid and the township revolt itself, which made it hard to separate the effects.
5. **They worked together with internal pressure.** Sanctions mattered most because the township revolt and the strikes had already made the country less stable.
:::

### The war in Angola and the Cold War

The war in Angola was also a cause. South African forces had fought in Angola since 1975, to support UNITA against the Marxist government (the MPLA) and to attack SWAPO, and the Cubans in Angola, helped by Soviet weapons, were the other side. In 1987–88 South African forces fought a series of battles around **Cuito Cuanavale** in southern Angola, in which the South Africans failed to take the town, and Cuban and Angolan forces moved towards the Namibian border. The battle remains disputed: South Africa says that it was a stalemate that it chose to end, and Cuba and Angola say that it was a victory. Whichever is right, the war was costly, white casualties were politically sensitive and South Africa agreed to talk. The **Tripartite Accord** of **22 December 1988** (South Africa, Angola, Cuba) led to the withdrawal of Cuban troops, and to independence for **Namibia**, which came on 21 March 1990 after UN Security Council Resolution 435 was put into effect from April 1989. It meant that South Africa lost the war it had fought for fifteen years, and that the last white-ruled territory outside its borders was gone.

The **end of the Cold War** worked on three levels. First, Gorbachev wanted to end regional conflicts (see [The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (3): Gorbachev's response to crisis](#hist.d.soviet.3)), and he pressed the ANC and the Angolan government to find political solutions. Second, the collapse of communism in Eastern Europe in 1989 ([The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (4): the collapse of Soviet control, 1980–91](#hist.d.soviet.4)) took away the government's strongest argument to the West, that it was a bulwark against communism, and made the South African Communist Party and the ANC's Soviet patron seem much less threatening. Third, with the Cold War ending, the USA and Britain had less reason to defend white South Africa. Many historians therefore see the end of the Cold War as a condition that made De Klerk's decision of 1990 easier, rather than the single cause of it.

## The last years of Botha, 1985–89

### Mandela in prison and the secret contacts

Nelson Mandela had been moved from Robben Island to **Pollsmoor Prison** near Cape Town in 1982. In January 1985 Botha offered to release him if he would "unconditionally reject violence as a political instrument". Mandela refused, and his reply was read to a crowd in Soweto by his daughter Zindzi in February 1985: he said that he could not give a promise while the people were not free. Behind the scenes, though, the government had begun to talk to him. From 1985 the Minister of Justice, **Kobie Coetsee**, met Mandela in hospital and in prison, and from 1988 a committee of officials, including the head of the National Intelligence Service **Niël Barnard**, held regular talks with him, after he had moved to a house in the grounds of **Victor Verster Prison** near Paarl in December 1988. Mandela, for his part, began the talks without asking the ANC's leaders in Lusaka, but he told them afterwards. Meanwhile, from 1987, Afrikaner academics and businessmen met ANC leaders such as Thabo Mbeki, in secret, at a house in England (Mells Park). Botha himself met Mandela, at his official residence in Cape Town, on **5 July 1989**, and the two men drank tea and talked politely. It showed that the state's leaders knew that they would have to negotiate with the ANC in the end, and that Mandela was the man to talk to.

### The reforms stall; Botha falls

Botha could not go further. His reforms had not won African support, the right wing of the party was angry and his colleagues were divided between the "securocrats" (generals and police) and the "reformers". He suffered a stroke in January 1989 and, on **2 February 1989**, **F. W. de Klerk**, Minister of National Education and leader of the Transvaal party, was chosen by the party's MPs to replace him as leader of the National Party, narrowly beating Barend du Plessis. In August Botha, who had at first kept the presidency, quarrelled with his cabinet and resigned on **14 August 1989**, and de Klerk became acting president. In the general election of 6 September 1989, which the National Party won but with a smaller majority, with the Conservative Party (the white right-wing breakaway from the National Party) gaining on the right and the Democratic Party (the white liberal party) on the left, de Klerk was elected State President (he took office on 20 September).

:::key Why Total Strategy failed
1. **It misread the problem.** Botha thought that Black South Africans wanted better conditions, and he offered those. They wanted political rights, and in the tricameral system he offered them none.
2. **The reforms created the forces that opposed it.** Legal unions, the new constitution and the Black local councils produced COSATU, the UDF and the boycotts.
3. **Repression was costly.** The emergencies kept control but produced sanctions, bank refusal and isolation, and it made the state dependent on the army.
4. **The regional strategy backfired.** Destabilising neighbours failed to defeat the ANC, led to a costly war in Angola and ended in the loss of Namibia.
5. **It had no end point.** Botha wanted "power-sharing" between racial groups but ruled out majority rule, and so he could not reach agreement with the one body that Africans would accept, the ANC.
6. **It split the white elite.** By 1989 many business leaders, the Afrikaner intellectuals and parts of the party wanted a different course.
:::

## De Klerk's new approach, 1989–90

### The man and the choice

Frederik Willem de Klerk, born in 1936, was a lawyer from a family of National Party politicians. He had a conservative reputation: as Minister of Education he had defended segregated universities, and few had expected him to make the choice that he did. Why he did so is a central question (see "The roles of de Klerk, Mandela and others" below). In his first months as president he made small moves: on **15 October 1989** he freed **Walter Sisulu** and other long-term prisoners, allowed large protest marches in Cape Town (in September 1989 tens of thousands of people marched, led by Archbishop Tutu and Allan Boesak), and the Mass Democratic Movement (the UDF, COSATU and others) pursued a campaign of defying segregation in hospitals and on beaches. The Organisation of African Unity's **Harare Declaration** of August 1989 set out the conditions on which the ANC would negotiate: the release of political prisoners, the lifting of the bans and the emergency, and the end of political trials and executions. The fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 followed.

### 2 February 1990

On **2 February 1990**, at the opening of Parliament in Cape Town, de Klerk made a speech that few in his own party, and no foreign government, had expected. He announced that the bans on the **ANC, the Pan Africanist Congress, the South African Communist Party** and other organisations were lifted, that political prisoners imprisoned for non-violent offences would be freed, that the death penalty was suspended, that some emergency rules were dropped, and that the government would release Mandela.

:::note Evidence: de Klerk's speech of 2 February 1990
*From F. W. de Klerk, speech at the opening of Parliament, Cape Town, 2 February 1990 (text published by South African History Online). Three short extracts; the words are de Klerk's own and every cut is marked with dots.*

"The prohibition of the African National Congress, the Pan Africanist Congress, the South African Communist Party … is being rescinded." "… the Government has taken a firm decision to release Mr Mandela unconditionally." "The time for talking has arrived and whoever still makes excuses does not really wish to talk." He ended by urging parties to "walk through the open door" and "take your place at the negotiating table".

*Use.* The words show the change in policy and the language in which de Klerk presented it: as a decision of the government, from a position of strength, and as an invitation to talk, not a surrender. They do not state the aim. De Klerk did not say that he accepted majority rule, and the speech did not mention the Population Registration Act or the Group Areas Act, the main pillars of apartheid, which went in 1991. You should also ask why a speech to the white Parliament was the vehicle, and what it says about his need to keep his own supporters on his side.
:::

On **11 February 1990** Mandela, aged 71, walked out of Victor Verster Prison after 27 years, with his wife Winnie Mandela beside him. That evening, from the balcony of Cape Town's City Hall, he spoke to a crowd of tens of thousands and to a television audience around the world. He did not call for an end to the armed struggle, but said that he stood before them "as a humble servant". He repeated that the ANC's aim was a non-racial democracy.

### The dismantling of apartheid, 1990–91

The 1990–91 reforms came in stages.
- **May 1990, the Groote Schuur Minute.** The first meeting between the ANC and the government, at the Cape Town residence Groote Schuur (2–4 May), produced an agreement on the release of political prisoners, the return of exiles and a review of security laws.
- **August 1990, the Pretoria Minute.** On 6 August the ANC **suspended the armed struggle**, a concession that was necessary to keep the talks going. It did not give up MK or its weapons.
- **Emergency lifted.** The state of emergency was lifted in June 1990 everywhere except Natal, where it ended in October.
- **The Separate Amenities Act repealed (1990)**, which ended the legal segregation of public facilities.
- **June 1991.** The **Land Acts** (which had divided the land) and the **Group Areas Act** were repealed, and on 17 June the **Population Registration Act** went. These removed the three laws on which apartheid had been built.
- **Sebokeng, March 1990.** On 26 March police shot at a crowd in Sebokeng and killed 11 people, a reminder that violence could not be turned off.

By the end of 1991 the apartheid laws were almost all gone, but power was still in white hands. The Black majority could not yet vote, and the real argument was now about the constitution.

:::key Why de Klerk acted when he did
1. **The state could not govern the townships without force,** and force could not provide a political answer. The security chiefs believed that they could contain the ANC but not defeat it.
2. **The economy was weak** after the debt crisis, sanctions and ten years of slow growth, and business wanted a settlement.
3. **The Cold War was ending,** which removed the Soviet threat and the West's willingness to defend a white state.
4. **Namibia and Angola** showed that the military strategy had reached its limits.
5. **He believed that he could negotiate from strength,** and expected power-sharing, with group protection for whites and an effective veto, rather than majority rule. Most historians say that this was an error of judgement, which the ANC's strength and his own concessions exposed in 1992–93.
6. **The ANC was ready to talk** (the Harare Declaration, Mandela's contacts) and Mandela was a leader that he could do business with.
7. **There was a moral element.** De Klerk was a convinced Christian who came to see that apartheid was wrong and unsustainable. How far it counted compared with the others is a matter of dispute.
:::

## Continuing violence, 1990–94

The years of negotiation were also the years of the worst political violence since the 1980s. Estimates of the number killed between 1990 and 1994 run well above ten thousand (the figure depends on the source and on what is counted). The violence came from three sources, which often overlapped, and understanding them is central to the question of why talks nearly failed.

### Inkatha and the ANC

The **Inkatha Freedom Party (IFP)**, led by **Chief Mangosuthu Buthelezi**, the Chief Minister of the KwaZulu homeland, had been founded in 1975 as a Zulu cultural movement (see [End of minority rule (2)](#hist.d.safrica.2)) and had refused "independence" for KwaZulu. It was strong among Zulu-speaking people, especially migrant workers in hostels, and opposed the ANC. From the mid-1980s fighting between UDF/ANC supporters and Inkatha supporters in the Natal Midlands had killed thousands. After 1990 the fighting spread to the townships of the Witwatersrand (Johannesburg), where Zulu-speaking hostel dwellers and township residents were drawn into attacks and revenge killings. Buthelezi feared that the ANC would take away his power base and that the ANC and the government would settle the future without him, and he put forward the demand for a federal system with strong regions and a role for the Zulu king. The ANC accused him of working with the state.

### The Third Force

:::def Third Force
The name that ANC leaders and other critics gave to a hidden network of members of the security forces, former members and right-wing allies, which they believed was fomenting violence in the townships to weaken the ANC and the negotiations. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission found little evidence of a centrally directed "Third Force", but described a network of present and former security operatives, often working with right-wing groups and parts of the IFP, that stirred up violence and caused gross human rights violations.
:::

Evidence that the state was involved came in stages. In **July 1991** the newspaper *Weekly Mail* revealed that the police had given secret money to Inkatha for rallies (called "Inkathagate"), and de Klerk moved the ministers responsible, Adriaan Vlok and Magnus Malan, to other jobs. A judicial commission under Judge **Richard Goldstone**, set up in 1991, investigated violence and in late 1992 found evidence of a secret unit in military intelligence working against the ANC. After that de Klerk dismissed or suspended some twenty senior officers in December 1992. On the evidence that is now available, the government under de Klerk did not order the violence, but parts of the security forces were involved in it, and it is still hard to say how far they were controlled. The ANC believed that the state was playing a double game: negotiating and at the same time weakening its negotiating partner.

Two massacres show the effect on the talks.
- **Boipatong, 17 June 1992.** Armed men, many of them hostel dwellers, attacked the township of Boipatong near Vanderbijlpark and killed 45 people. Residents said that the police had helped the attackers or stood by. Mandela accused the government of being behind the killings. On **24 June** the ANC withdrew from the talks and turned to a campaign of mass action.
- **Bisho, 7 September 1992.** During an ANC march on Bisho, the capital of the Ciskei homeland, Ciskei soldiers opened fire and killed 28 marchers. The shock persuaded both sides that the talks had to be restarted.

### White extremists

On the far right, those who opposed any change formed groups like the **Afrikaner Weerstandsbeweging (AWB)**, led by Eugène Terre'Blanche, and the Conservative Party (the main white opposition after 1987) and many Afrikaners who thought that de Klerk had betrayed their people. They threatened a revolt, held rallies, attacked black people and, in some cases, planted bombs. On **10 April 1993** a Polish-born extremist, **Janusz Waluś**, shot and killed **Chris Hani**, the General Secretary of the South African Communist Party and one of the most popular leaders after Mandela, outside his home in Boksburg; a Conservative Party MP, Clive Derby-Lewis, supplied the gun. Riots followed, and the country came close to a crisis. Mandela made a televised appeal for calm, which helped to prevent a collapse of the talks, and on 3 June 1993 the negotiators announced that the first democratic election would take place in April 1994. On 25 June the AWB drove an armoured vehicle through the doors of the negotiating venue.

:::key How did the violence affect the end of apartheid?
1. **It nearly derailed the talks,** twice: after Boipatong in 1992 and after the murder of Hani in 1993.
2. **It showed that the state did not control its own forces,** and led the ANC to insist on a transitional government, in which the security forces would come under joint control.
3. **It also pushed the parties together,** because both de Klerk and Mandela could see that failure meant civil war. The assassination of Hani produced the election date.
4. **It strengthened Mandela's moral authority** as the leader who could speak to angry crowds, and weakened the government's claim that it alone could keep order.
5. **It left a deadly legacy** of mistrust, which the Truth and Reconciliation Commission examined after 1995.
:::

## The road to 1994: negotiations, 1991–93

### CODESA

The first formal talks took place at the **Convention for a Democratic South Africa (CODESA)**, at the World Trade Centre in Kempton Park, near Johannesburg. **CODESA 1** met on **20–21 December 1991** with nineteen delegations (the government, the ANC, the National Party or NP, the IFP and many smaller parties and homeland governments); the Conservative Party and the PAC stayed away. All signed a **Declaration of Intent** promising a united, non-racial, democratic South Africa. It was helped by the **National Peace Accord** of September 1991, signed by 26 organisations to reduce violence.

Before CODESA 2, de Klerk faced a problem at home. The National Party had lost by-elections to the Conservative Party, and the right claimed that he had no mandate. On **17 March 1992** he held a **referendum of white voters**, asking: "Do you support continuation of the reform process which the State President began on 2 February 1990 and which is aimed at a new constitution through negotiation?" About 85 per cent of the 3.3 million registered white voters took part, and **68.7 per cent voted yes** (1,924,186 to 875,619). It was the last time that only white South Africans voted, and gave de Klerk the mandate he wanted.

**CODESA 2** (15–16 May 1992) ended in deadlock over how large a majority would be needed to approve a constitution and how powerful the regions would be. The ANC wanted a simple democratic majority, and the National Party wanted a veto for minorities. The ANC returned to mass action, and then came Boipatong.

### The Record of Understanding and the sunset clauses

After Bisho both sides returned to talk. The ANC's secretary-general, **Cyril Ramaphosa**, and the government's Minister of Constitutional Development, **Roelf Meyer**, built a personal relationship in a "back channel" that was crucial. On **26 September 1992** Mandela and de Klerk signed the **Record of Understanding**: the government accepted that the constitution would be written by an elected body and not agreed behind closed doors, and the ANC accepted that a government of national unity would rule for a transition.

The ANC's concession to the whites had a name. In late 1992 **Joe Slovo**, the leader of the South African Communist Party and a leading ANC thinker, proposed the **"sunset clauses"**: a fixed period (five years) of power-sharing in a coalition, guarantees for civil servants' jobs and pensions, and a promise that the process would not become a victory parade for one side. The ANC's National Executive agreed in February 1993. It was a compromise that the ANC's critics inside the movement disliked, but it brought the security forces and the white bureaucracy on board.

### The Multi-Party Negotiating Forum and the interim constitution

The **Multi-Party Negotiating Process** began at Kempton Park on **1 April 1993**, with 26 parties and groups. The ANC and the National Party used a method called "sufficient consensus", under which the larger parties could proceed when the smaller ones disagreed, so critics said that a deal was being done between two parties. Buthelezi's IFP walked out in June over the election date. In October 1993 opponents of the settlement formed a bloc called the **Freedom Alliance**: the Conservative Party and the Afrikaner Volksfront (an alliance of white far-right groups), the IFP, and the governments of the KwaZulu, Ciskei and Bophuthatswana homelands. It demanded a federal system and a "volkstaat" (an Afrikaner homeland). In the early hours of **18 November 1993** the forum approved the **interim constitution**, which gave South Africa a bill of rights, nine provinces to replace the old four provinces and the homelands, a parliament elected by proportional representation and a five-year Government of National Unity that included every party with at least 5 per cent of the vote. The homelands were abolished, and a Constituent Assembly (the elected parliament) was to write a final constitution within two years, by a two-thirds majority. The Tricameral Parliament, in its last act, approved the interim constitution. In 1993 Mandela and de Klerk were jointly awarded the **Nobel Peace Prize**.

:::key Why were the negotiations successful?
1. **Neither side could win by force.** The state could not destroy the ANC, and the ANC could not overthrow the army; the violence showed both of them what failure would cost.
2. **Each gave up something it needed to give up.** The ANC accepted power-sharing for five years, protection for civil servants and a negotiated constitution. The government accepted one person, one vote.
3. **The negotiators built trust,** especially Ramaphosa and Meyer, and Mandela and de Klerk, who disagreed bitterly in public but kept talking.
4. **Outside pressure kept both sides at the table:** the world wanted a settlement and would lift sanctions only when it came.
5. **Smaller parties were bought in, or left out.** The IFP and the white right stayed out until almost the end, which was the greatest danger to the settlement.
:::

## The 1994 election and majority rule

### The last obstacles

In early 1994 the settlement was threatened from both sides. The Freedom Alliance refused to take part. In **March 1994** the leader of the Bophuthatswana homeland, Lucas Mangope, refused to take part in the election, and a strike by civil servants and a mutiny by his own police and army followed; the AWB sent armed men to support him, and in front of television cameras three of them were shot dead by a Bophuthatswana policeman. Mangope was removed, and Ciskei's military ruler resigned too. General **Constand Viljoen**, the former head of the South African Defence Force and leader of the Afrikaner Volksfront, saw that a civil war could not be won, and registered a new party, the **Freedom Front**, to contest the election. At the last moment, on **19 April 1994**, after mediation by an international team, Buthelezi agreed that the IFP would take part; its name was added to the ballot papers by a sticker. Bombs in Johannesburg and nearby in the days around the election, planted by white extremists, killed about twenty people, but they did not stop it.

### The election, 26–29 April 1994

South Africa's first election with universal suffrage was run by a new **Independent Electoral Commission** and watched by thousands of foreign observers. The elderly, sick and some others voted on 26 April; the main polling day was **27 April**, and because of long queues and shortages in some areas, voting was extended to the 29th. Millions of people voted for the first time, and many queued for hours in lines that were described around the world. There were irregularities, especially in KwaZulu-Natal, and problems with counting, and the commission was criticised for the way that some results were settled by agreement among parties; but observers and most parties accepted that the result reflected the way people had voted.

The voters chose a National Assembly of 400 members and the legislatures of the nine new provinces.

Three points matter for essays.
- **The ANC fell just short of two-thirds.** Its 252 of 400 seats was 63 per cent, short of the 267 needed to write the final constitution alone, so that it had to work with others. This was one reason why the election did not mean one-party rule.
- **The National Party did well among minorities.** Its 20 per cent came from white, Coloured and Indian voters (it won the Western Cape), and it was enough to give de Klerk a post in the new government.
- **The IFP won KwaZulu-Natal** (where the ANC and IFP competed) and so gained a part in the government.

### The Government of National Unity

On **10 May 1994**, in a ceremony at the Union Buildings in Pretoria, Nelson Mandela was inaugurated as President. The two Deputy Presidents were **Thabo Mbeki** (ANC) and **F. W. de Klerk** (NP). Buthelezi became Minister of Home Affairs. The Government of National Unity was a coalition of the ANC, the National Party and the IFP, each with seats in the cabinet. The National Party left it in 1996, and the Constituent Assembly adopted the final constitution in 1996 (it came into force in 1997).

:::note Evidence: Mandela's inaugural address, 10 May 1994
*From Nelson Mandela, inaugural address as President, Pretoria, 10 May 1994 (text published by the South African Government news agency). Three short extracts; the words are Mandela's own.*

"The time for the healing of the wounds has come." … "Never, never and never again shall it be that this beautiful land will again experience the oppression of one by another …" He spoke of building "a rainbow nation at peace with itself and the world".

*Use.* The address shows the theme of reconciliation on which the new government rested, and the language in which the first Black president spoke to his white fellow citizens. It was a speech to a crowd, to the world and to a divided country, and it expresses an aim. It cannot show how far reconciliation succeeded, or how much of the old inequality of land, jobs and income remained.
:::

## The roles of de Klerk, Mandela and other leaders

### F. W. de Klerk

- **For:** he took the decision of 2 February 1990, against the advice of some colleagues, at a time when his own supporters were uncertain. He unbanned the ANC, repealed the main laws, took a referendum to the white electorate and kept the security forces broadly under control, so that there was no coup. He accepted one person, one vote.
- **Against:** he did not set out to end white rule and misjudged the balance; he failed to control the security forces; he was slow to respond to the Third Force and to the killings; and he often tried to gain tactical advantage in the negotiations. His fall from power came because the ANC and the Black majority would accept nothing less than majority rule.

### Nelson Mandela

- **For:** he was a prisoner who had become a symbol, with the authority to hold the ANC together and to persuade it to talk; he began secret contacts in 1985 and went on despite criticism; he refused to accept conditional release; he built a personal relationship with de Klerk's government and with Afrikaner leaders; his appeal for calm after Hani's murder and his words about reconciliation prevented a collapse.
- **Against:** the ANC was a collective leadership, and the negotiations depended on Ramaphosa, Mbeki, Slovo and others; Mandela was sometimes bitterly critical of de Klerk (after Boipatong, for example); and his authority depended on a movement that had grown in the townships in the years when he was in prison.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: what ended apartheid?)
**Allister Sparks**, a South African journalist and former editor of the *Rand Daily Mail*, wrote *Tomorrow Is Another Country* (1995), an account of the negotiations based on interviews with the people involved. It stresses the secret talks and the personal roles of Mandela, Coetsee, de Klerk and others, and presents the change as the result of choices that political leaders made when they saw that neither could win. **Hermann Giliomee**, an Afrikaner historian, argues in *The Last Afrikaner Leaders* (2012) that the white government was not defeated, since its army was strong and its economy intact, and that its leaders chose a settlement, as they expected to share power. His account puts a great deal of weight on the choices and the mistakes of the Afrikaner leaders. **Nigel Worden**, in *The Making of Modern South Africa* (several editions from 1994), sets these events in the longer history of South African society, in which the growth of Black organisation and of the Black workforce limited the state's choices. For essay purposes, you can put it like this: historians who focus on **leaders** stress Mandela and de Klerk, historians who focus on **structures** stress the economy, the townships and the world, and the best answers show how the two connect.
:::

## How far did each factor end apartheid?

:::key A judgement you can defend
Apartheid ended because the **state could no longer govern without consent and could not win consent**. Resistance in the townships and the unions made the old system unworkable; Total Strategy failed to replace it; sanctions, the debt crisis, the war in Angola and the end of the Cold War raised the cost and removed the allies; and by 1989 the National Party's leaders were more willing to negotiate than to fight on. **No one factor was sufficient**: without internal resistance the world's pressure would have had little to bite on, without outside pressure the government would have had more time, and without de Klerk's and Mandela's decisions a stalemate might have ended in civil war. A defensible ranking puts **internal resistance and the failure of the state's own strategy** first, **economic and international pressure** second as an accelerator, **the end of the Cold War** third as a condition, and **leadership** as the factor that decided *how* the end came, by negotiation. That is why the end came as a negotiated transition rather than a revolution.
:::

:::eg 'International pressure was the main reason for the end of apartheid.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained and balanced argument, a clear and supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Evaluate this view" needs a verdict on *main reason*, which means ranking international pressure against other causes, not just describing it. Define international pressure (sanctions, bank loans, sport, the frontline states, the Cold War). Criteria: (1) how directly it hurt the state; (2) whether it came before or after the state's own decision to change; (3) how it compares with internal resistance; (4) what leadership added. Support: the 1985 loan refusal and standstill; the US Act of 2 October 1986; the Commonwealth's Eminent Persons Group; the Tripartite Accord (22 December 1988) and Namibia; the Cold War's end in 1989; business in contact with the ANC. Challenge: the Vaal uprising (3 September 1984), the UDF (20 August 1983) and COSATU (1985) came first and caused the emergencies; the state regained control by 1987 and yet de Klerk still chose to negotiate; sanctions were partial and Thatcher resisted; the 2 February 1990 decision was a choice by individuals; the negotiations were won by Ramaphosa and Meyer. Judgement: international pressure was a major accelerator and made 1990 possible, but internal resistance was the main reason, because it created the crisis to which the outside pressure was applied.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "International pressure was important in ending apartheid. Many countries introduced sanctions against South Africa in the 1980s. The USA passed a law in 1986 that stopped trade. Banks stopped lending money and the economy got worse. South Africa was also not allowed to play in international sport. All this made the government change its mind. This shows that international pressure was the main reason why apartheid ended."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with a range of examples, so Table B is Level 3. But the paragraph describes the pressure and then *asserts* that it was the main reason ("All this made the government change its mind"): there is no test of how much each measure did, no comparison with any other cause and no sense of timing. "Many countries" and "stopped trade" are loose (the 1986 Act banned certain imports and new loans, and did not end all trade). Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If 'main reason' means the factor that hurt the state most directly, economic pressure has a strong claim. In August 1985 the Chase Manhattan Bank led American banks in refusing to renew loans, the rand fell, and the government announced a standstill on repayments of foreign debt, which cut it off from normal borrowing. The Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act of October 1986, passed over Reagan's veto, banned new loans and several imports, and many American firms left. But if 'main reason' means the factor that began the process, the sequence is against it. The banks acted after the Vaal uprising of September 1984 and the state of emergency of July 1985, which had shown the world that the government had lost control of the townships. International pressure therefore magnified a crisis that began inside South Africa."

*Commentary.* Two criteria are applied and tested against accurate, dated detail, and the argument is balanced. Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs the Cold War and the leadership factor, an assessment of the limits of sanctions, and a judgement that is sustained to the end.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is only partly valid. International pressure was a major accelerator, and without it de Klerk would have had less reason to act in 1990, but it was not the main reason, which was the internal crisis that the Total Strategy of P. W. Botha could not solve. I shall test the view against four criteria: how directly each cause hurt the state; the order in which they came; how far each depended on the others; and what leadership added.

On the **direct effect on the state**, international pressure has a strong case. South Africa relied on short-term loans from foreign banks, and in August 1985 Chase Manhattan and other American banks refused to roll them over. The rand fell, the government closed the foreign exchange market and announced a standstill on repayment of most foreign debt. The Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act, passed on 2 October 1986 over Reagan's veto, banned new investment and loans and imports of iron, steel, coal and textiles, and American companies such as General Motors and IBM sold their South African businesses. By the late 1980s the economy had grown more slowly than the population for a decade, so the white middle class, which was the National Party's base, was getting poorer, and business leaders such as Gavin Relly of Anglo American were meeting the ANC. International pressure also hit the army: after the stalemate at Cuito Cuanavale, South Africa signed the Tripartite Accord on 22 December 1988, and Namibia became independent in March 1990. These were serious costs.

On the **order of events**, however, the internal crisis came first. The United Democratic Front was launched on 20 August 1983 against the tricameral constitution, the Vaal uprising began on 3 September 1984, and the state declared an emergency on 21 July 1985, three weeks before the Rubicon speech and the loan refusals. The banks did not act in a vacuum: it was the sight of troops in the townships and of the Rubicon speech, in which Botha refused to promise change, that made them fear for their money. COSATU, founded in December 1985, and the strike of more than 300,000 miners in 1987 showed that the labour force itself was organised. The state did regain control of the townships by 1987, with detentions and the emergency, but it could not make its system legitimate: the tricameral elections of 1984 were boycotted, and no African leader would work with it. A state that could contain, but not govern, had to find another way. Sanctions, in addition, were partial. Thatcher and Reagan opposed them for most of the decade, South Africa found new trading partners, and economists still disagree about how much damage they did compared with the gold price and the cost of the war.

The **end of the Cold War** belongs with the international causes but worked differently. It did not hurt the economy. It removed the government's strongest argument, that it was defending the West against communism, and it took away the Soviet backing of the ANC and the Communist Party. The Berlin Wall fell on 9 November 1989, and de Klerk spoke on 2 February 1990. That timing suggests that it made the decision easier and the outcome more hopeful for the government, but it would be wrong to say that it caused it.

Finally, **leadership** decided the form of the change. De Klerk could have continued to use the emergency, as the security chiefs advised. He chose instead to unban the ANC and to release Mandela, and Mandela's decision to negotiate and to call for calm after Chris Hani's murder on 10 April 1993 kept the talks alive. The settlement of 1993, the sunset clauses and the Government of National Unity were made by Ramaphosa, Meyer, Slovo and others, not by foreign governments.

Overall, international pressure was a necessary condition and a powerful accelerator: it narrowed the government's options and made 1990 possible. But it was not the main reason. The main reason was that after 1983 Black South Africans, through the UDF, the unions and the township revolt, made the old system impossible to run, and that Botha's Total Strategy offered nothing to end the crisis. Pressure from outside gave that crisis its force, and leadership gave it its direction."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): four explicit criteria, a balanced argument in which each side is tested, a separate assessment of the Cold War, and a sustained judgement that separates "necessary condition" and "accelerator" from "main reason". Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise, selected and dated detail (August 1985 loans; 2 October 1986; 20 August 1983; 3 September 1984; 21 July 1985; 22 December 1988; 9 November 1989; 2 February 1990; 10 April 1993). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 800 words, 35 to 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the roles of de Klerk and Mandela in ending apartheid. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the roles" asks how much each man contributed to the *end* of apartheid, so you need criteria (what did each do, was it decisive, could it have happened without him) and a judgement about the **extent** of their importance in relation to other causes. A common error is to write two biographies.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) decisions that changed the course of events; (2) how far each acted under pressure from other causes; (3) whether the outcome would have been the same without him; (4) the part of others (Ramaphosa, Meyer, Slovo, Tutu, the UDF). De Klerk: 2 February 1990, the repeals of 1990–91, the referendum of 17 March 1992 (68.7 per cent), the Record of Understanding (26 September 1992), the interim constitution (18 November 1993); but also the Third Force and Boipatong and a misjudged expectation of power-sharing. Mandela: refusal of conditional release in 1985; secret talks from 1985; the meeting with Botha on 5 July 1989; the Pretoria Minute (6 August 1990); his appeal after Hani's murder (10 April 1993); the Nobel Peace Prize (1993); reconciliation in 1994. Judgement: both were necessary to the *manner* of the end, but neither caused the end, which came from the crisis of the state.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "De Klerk and Mandela were very important in ending apartheid. De Klerk released Mandela from prison in 1990 and he got rid of apartheid laws. Mandela was in prison for 27 years and when he came out he worked with de Klerk. They won the Nobel Peace Prize together in 1993. Mandela became the first black president in 1994. So they were both very important in ending apartheid."

*Commentary.* True, but a description of what each man did, and the Nobel Prize and the presidency are results, not causes. No criteria, no comparison with other causes, and "very important" is an assertion. "Released Mandela" and "got rid of apartheid laws" lack dates and detail. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 (4–6).

**Moving to Level 4: judge each against a criterion.** "De Klerk's role was decisive in one sense: only the head of the state could unban the ANC and release Mandela, and on 2 February 1990 he did so, against the advice of some colleagues, and went on to repeal the Population Registration Act in June 1991 and to win a white referendum on 17 March 1992 by 68.7 per cent. But he acted under pressure. The debt crisis, the township revolt and the end of the Cold War had left his government with few choices, and he expected power-sharing, not majority rule. Mandela's role was different: he did not control the state but he controlled the ANC's decision to negotiate. He refused a conditional release in 1985, began talking to ministers from that year and spoke to Botha on 5 July 1989, so that when de Klerk made his move, there was a man with authority to answer it."

*Commentary.* Criteria are applied to both men and tested against other causes. Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs the other leaders and the violence (Boipatong, Hani), a view of what might have happened without each man, and a judgement that weighs their roles against the structural causes.

**A Level 5 conclusion** (after paragraphs on each man and on the other leaders and causes).

"Overall, de Klerk and Mandela were essential to the way in which apartheid ended, but not to the fact that it ended. The causes of the end were the township revolt, the failure of Total Strategy, economic pressure and the end of the Cold War, and these would have forced a change of some kind whoever was in power. A different president, such as the securocrats' choice, might have used the emergency for longer and risked a civil war; a different ANC leader might not have been able to unite the movement behind a suspension of the armed struggle in August 1990 or to calm the crowds after the murder of Chris Hani. In that sense both were *necessary*: de Klerk because only he could open the door, Mandela because only he could lead the ANC through it. De Klerk's role was the more limited, since he had expected something much less than majority rule, and his government's security forces and the Third Force did much to wreck trust; Mandela's was the more constructive, since he turned a prisoner's symbolic authority into practical compromise. But neither could have done it alone. The sunset clauses of Joe Slovo, the back channel of Ramaphosa and Meyer, the moral authority of Tutu and the UDF and the decision of General Viljoen to enter the election all mattered, and so I judge that the roles of the two leaders were crucial in determining that the transition was peaceful, and secondary in determining that it happened."

*Commentary.* A clear judgement on the **extent** of significance, with a distinction (the fact of the change, and its manner; necessary and sufficient) that the paragraphs have earned. Table A Level 5; Table B depends on the detail in the paragraphs before it.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the extent to which Botha's reforms were a response to pressure from within South Africa." [30]

A student wrote:

"P. W. Botha introduced many reforms. He made black trade unions legal in 1979, he created the tricameral parliament and he got rid of the pass laws in 1986. Some of this was because of pressure from the townships, where there was a lot of violence. Botha was also under pressure from other countries. In the end he was removed by his party and F. W. de Klerk became leader. So the reforms were mostly a response to internal pressure."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the paragraph lists reforms, mentions two kinds of pressure and ends with a conclusion ("mostly a response to internal pressure") that nothing in it supports. There are no criteria, nothing is explained (why the unions were recognised, why the pass laws went when they did) and the last-but-one sentence is about something else (Botha's fall). Table B Level 3 (7–9) at best: three reforms with dates are accurate, but the paragraph never uses any evidence to test the question.

**The fix.** Sort the reforms by *what caused them*, and give dates. "The reforms divide into two kinds. Some were a response to pressure from below: the Wiehahn reforms of 1979 followed the Durban strikes of 1973 and the strikes of the later 1970s, when the state saw that it could not ignore African workers, and the abolition of the pass laws on 1 July 1986 came after the Vaal uprising and the emergencies, when the pass system had become unworkable and was a symbol the townships hated. Others owed more to Botha's own plan: the tricameral parliament of 1983–84 was designed to win the support of Coloureds and Indians and to keep Africans in the homelands, and it provoked the very opposition that it was meant to avoid. Pressure from abroad was strongest in 1985–86, after the loan refusals and the 1986 American Act, which fits the timing of the abolition of the pass laws. So the answer depends on the reform: the labour reforms and the pass laws were largely responses to internal pressure, and the constitution was a pre-emptive plan for control." Now the facts support a ranked argument, and the paragraph is on its way to Level 4 or 5.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was Total Strategy, and which two groups of measures did it combine?
A: P. W. Botha's policy from the late 1970s, based on the claim that South Africa faced a "total onslaught" from communism. It combined reform (legal Black trade unions in 1979, the tricameral parliament of 1983–84, the abolition of the pass laws in 1986) with repression (the army and police, the states of emergency of 1985–90, raids on neighbouring states), coordinated by the State Security Council.
Q: What was the United Democratic Front, and when and why was it founded?
A: A non-racial front of about 575 organisations launched on 20 August 1983 at Mitchell's Plain to oppose the tricameral constitution and the Black Local Authorities. It took up the Freedom Charter, called for the release of Mandela and organised boycotts; its leaders were tried for treason (Delmas trial, 1985–88) and in February 1988 it was restricted.
Q: What happened in the townships in 1984–86 and how did the state respond?
A: A revolt began in the Vaal townships on 3 September 1984 over rents, spread through the country, and took the form of boycotts of rent, schools and shops, attacks on councillors and "ungovernability". The army entered the townships in October 1984, a state of emergency was declared on 21 July 1985 (36 districts) and nationwide on 12 June 1986, and tens of thousands were detained.
Q: What was the Rubicon speech and why was it a mistake?
A: Botha's speech in Durban on 15 August 1985, in which he said "we are today crossing the Rubicon". He was expected to announce major reform, but he refused to release Mandela or to concede majority rule. The rand fell, Chase Manhattan and other banks refused to renew loans, and at the end of August the government announced a debt standstill.
Q: What did the US Congress do on 2 October 1986?
A: It overrode President Reagan's veto of the Comprehensive Anti-Apartheid Act, which banned new American investment and loans, banned imports of iron, steel, coal, uranium, textiles and farm goods, and ended South African Airways' landing rights in the USA.
Q: What did de Klerk announce on 2 February 1990, and what happened on 11 February?
A: He lifted the bans on the ANC, PAC and South African Communist Party, announced that political prisoners would be released and that Mandela would be freed unconditionally, and suspended the death penalty. Mandela walked out of Victor Verster Prison on 11 February after 27 years.
Q: Which laws were repealed in 1990–91, and why did they matter?
A: The Reservation of Separate Amenities Act (1990), and in June 1991 the Land Acts, the Group Areas Act and the Population Registration Act. These were the foundation of apartheid, which required classification by race, land by race and separate places to live.
Q: What was the Third Force?
A: The name for a hidden network of present and former security-force members and right-wing allies thought to be stirring up violence in the townships to weaken the ANC. The TRC found little evidence of a centrally directed force, but found a network that fomented killings; evidence included "Inkathagate" (July 1991) and the Goldstone Commission (1992).
Q: What happened at Boipatong and at Bisho in 1992, and how did each affect the talks?
A: Boipatong (17 June 1992): 45 people were killed in an attack by armed men; the ANC withdrew from the talks on 24 June. Bisho (7 September 1992): Ciskei soldiers killed 28 marchers; the shock led to the Record of Understanding on 26 September 1992.
Q: What was CODESA, and why did it fail at first?
A: The Convention for a Democratic South Africa, at Kempton Park. CODESA 1 (20–21 December 1991) signed a Declaration of Intent; CODESA 2 (15–16 May 1992) deadlocked over the size of the majority needed to pass a constitution and the powers of the regions.
Q: What were the "sunset clauses", and who proposed them?
A: Joe Slovo's proposal (late 1992, endorsed by the ANC in February 1993) for a five-year Government of National Unity, with guarantees for civil servants' jobs and pensions, to reassure whites and the security forces and make the move to majority rule possible.
Q: What were the results of the 1994 election, and what was the Government of National Unity?
A: ANC 62.65 per cent and 252 of 400 seats, NP 20.39 per cent (82), IFP 10.54 per cent (43), Freedom Front 2.17 per cent (9), DP 1.73 per cent (7), PAC 1.25 per cent (5), ACDP 0.45 per cent (2). Mandela was inaugurated on 10 May 1994 with Thabo Mbeki and de Klerk as Deputy Presidents in a coalition of the ANC, NP and IFP.

:::recap
- **Botha** (PM from October 1978; State President 14 September 1984–14 August 1989): Total Strategy; State Security Council; Wiehahn reforms (1979); tricameral parliament (referendum 2 November 1983, 66.3 per cent yes); pass laws abolished 1 July 1986; Nkomati Accord (March 1984).
- **Resistance**: UDF launched 20 August 1983; Vaal uprising 3 September 1984; states of emergency 21 July 1985 and 12 June 1986; COSATU (December 1985); UDF restricted February 1988.
- **Pressure**: Rubicon speech 15 August 1985, loan refusals and standstill; US Act 2 October 1986; Eminent Persons Group 1986; Cuito Cuanavale, Tripartite Accord 22 December 1988; Namibia 21 March 1990; Berlin Wall 9 November 1989.
- **De Klerk**: leader of the NP 2 February 1989; speech of 2 February 1990; Mandela freed 11 February 1990; Groote Schuur (May) and Pretoria Minute (6 August 1990); Land Acts, Group Areas Act and Population Registration Act repealed June 1991.
- **Violence**: Inkathagate (July 1991), the Third Force, Goldstone Commission; Boipatong (17 June 1992, 45 dead), Bisho (7 September 1992, 28 dead), Hani (10 April 1993); AWB and the Freedom Alliance; Bophuthatswana (March 1994).
- **Negotiations**: CODESA 1 (20–21 December 1991), referendum (17 March 1992, 68.7 per cent), CODESA 2 (15–16 May 1992), Record of Understanding (26 September 1992), sunset clauses, multi-party forum (1 April 1993), interim constitution (18 November 1993), Nobel Peace Prize (1993).
- **1994**: election 26–29 April; ANC 62.65 per cent and 252 of 400; NP 20.39 per cent (82); IFP 10.54 per cent (43); Mandela inaugurated 10 May; Mbeki and de Klerk Deputy Presidents; Buthelezi Home Affairs.
- **Judgement**: internal resistance and the failure of Total Strategy came first; sanctions, the banks and the end of the Cold War accelerated and enabled change; leadership decided that it came by negotiation.
