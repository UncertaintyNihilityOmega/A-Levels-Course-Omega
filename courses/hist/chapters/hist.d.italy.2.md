## The terror state and coercion

Fascist Italy was a police state, but it was not a state of mass terror. The regime relied on a small, efficient apparatus of surveillance and punishment, and on the knowledge that those who resisted would lose their jobs, their freedom or their lives.

### The police, OVRA and the Militia

The Italian police were headed from 1926 until his death in 1940 by **Arturo Bocchini**, a career official who built a network of informers in every town, workplace and organisation. A section of the political police, which from about 1930 was called **OVRA** (the letters have never been convincingly explained), collected reports and files on tens of thousands of Italians, opened letters and tapped telephones. Informers were paid or pressured. The idea was that no one could be sure who was listening. The **Militia (MVSN)**, several hundred thousand strong in the 1930s, backed the police at rallies and in the countryside, and took over guard duties, railway and forest policing, and pre-military training; some units fought in Ethiopia and Spain.

:::def OVRA
The political police of the Fascist regime, a section of Bocchini's police that ran informers and kept files on suspected opponents. The name appears from about 1930 and its meaning is uncertain. It was effective at finding opponents but was not on the scale of the Gestapo or the NKVD.
:::

### The Special Tribunal and confino

The **Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State** (set up in November 1926) tried political offences. Its judges were army and militia officers, not professional judges. Hearings were rapid and defence rights were limited. Over its life (1927–43) it tried about 5,600 people and convicted about 4,600, with sentences adding up to tens of thousands of years in prison. It passed 42 death sentences, of which 31 were carried out, some of them against Slovene nationalists in the Trieste area (figures from the standard Italian study by Dal Pont and others, 1961). Among its prisoners was the Communist leader **Antonio Gramsci**, arrested in November 1926 and sentenced in 1928 to over twenty years in prison, where his health broke down. He died in 1937, a few days after release.

Many more people were punished without trial. **Confino** allowed the regime to send a person to a remote village in the south or to a small island such as Lipari or Ponza for up to five years (renewable), with a daily check and a ban on leaving. Over the regime's life, over ten thousand people were sent to confino, including Communists, Socialists, Liberals and many people who had simply said something critical. Others were given a formal "warning" (*ammonizione*) or lost their jobs. Opponents who stayed in Italy faced exclusion from work in the civil service, teaching and the professions, for the party card became necessary for public posts in 1933. Opponents abroad (the *fuorusciti*) were watched and sometimes attacked: in 1937 the brothers **Carlo and Nello Rosselli**, leaders of the anti-fascist movement Giustizia e Libertà, were murdered in France by French far-right gunmen with the support of Italian military intelligence.

:::warn A police state, but not a regime of mass murder
Do not write that Mussolini "murdered millions" at home. In Italy itself the regime killed dozens of political opponents by execution before the war, and other deaths came through beatings, prison and confino. That is a very different scale from Nazi Germany or Stalin's USSR, and historians use it to argue that Italian Fascism was less totalitarian. The regime's violence overseas was far greater (see the foreign policy section), and the pressure on Italians took other forms: fear of losing a job, informers, and the loss of free speech.
:::

### The antisemitic laws, 1938

Italy had about 46,000 Jews in 1938, roughly 0.1 per cent of the population, who had been part of Italian life since Roman times. Many had been Fascists: Margherita Sarfatti, Mussolini's biographer and for many years his lover, was Jewish, and several thousand Jews were party members. Until the mid-1930s Mussolini said that Fascism did not persecute Jews, and the early regime's racism was aimed at Africans and Slavs. Then in 1938 he adopted anti-Jewish policy.
- **14 July 1938:** the *Manifesto of Race*, signed by ten scientists, claimed that there was an "Italian race" of Aryan descent and that Jews did not belong to it.
- **22 August 1938:** a census of Jews.
- **September 1938:** Jewish teachers and pupils were banned from state schools (5 September), and foreign Jews who had arrived since 1919 were ordered to leave (7 September).
- **17 November 1938:** a royal decree "for the defence of the Italian race" banned marriage between Jews and non-Jews, barred Jews from the army, the party and state employment, and limited the property they could own.

Why did Mussolini do it? Historians disagree. Some (notably **Renzo De Felice**) argued that Mussolini acted mainly to please Hitler as the alliance grew closer, and that racism was not at the heart of Italian Fascism. Others (notably **Michele Sarfatti**) argue that the laws came from the regime's own racism, which had grown in the Ethiopian war when laws forbade "mixed" relationships with African women, and from Mussolini's wish to make Italians more martial and "pure". The laws were not popular and many Italians quietly helped Jewish neighbours; the king signed the laws with reluctance; the Church objected to the marriage ban because it conflicted with the Lateran agreements. But the laws were carried out, thousands of Jews lost their jobs and thousands emigrated. After the German occupation of northern and central Italy in September 1943, the lists made in 1938 helped the Germans and Italian Fascists to find Jews: on 16 October 1943 more than 1,000 Jews were seized in Rome and sent to Auschwitz, and several thousand more were deported from Italy before 1945, most of them murdered.

:::key The terror state: what it achieved and where it stopped
1. **Machinery.** Police under Bocchini, the OVRA, informers, the Militia, the Special Tribunal and confino, all in place by 1927.
2. **Effect.** Organised opposition inside Italy was crushed: the Communists were driven underground and the Socialists and Liberals into exile.
3. **Limits.** Few executions at home, no mass camps for opponents, and no control of the army, the monarchy or the Church.
4. **Peak of repression.** The racial laws of 1938, which targeted a small minority and made Italy's persecution of Jews part of the regime's record.
:::

## Propaganda and censorship

Mussolini was a journalist, and he believed that opinion could be made. The regime tried to control what Italians read, heard and saw, and to give them a faith in the Duce and in Fascism.

### Organising propaganda: the press, radio and film

The regime's central control of the press began with a Press Office in Mussolini's own office (1922) and became, step by step, a Ministry of Press and Propaganda (1935, under his son-in-law **Galeazzo Ciano**) and, in May 1937, the **Ministry of Popular Culture**, known as **MinCulPop**, under Dino Alfieri. This ministry controlled the press, radio, film, theatre, music and publishing.
- **The press.** From 1925 a journalist had to be on an official register approved by the party, and editors were chosen by the regime. Each day the press office sent editors instructions (*veline*) on what to print, which stories to hide and which headlines to run. Mussolini's own paper, *Il Popolo d'Italia*, set the tone. Foreign newspapers were available only to a few, and the stories of crimes, suicides and accidents were suppressed to give an impression of calm.
- **Radio.** Radio was run by a state monopoly from 1927, named **EIAR**. In the 1930s a Rural Radio body put sets into schools, village squares and party halls. Radios were expensive and in 1939 there were only about a million licence-holders in a population of about 44 million, so radio reached fewer than the party hoped, but speeches were broadcast in squares to crowds.
- **Film.** The state institute **LUCE** (founded 1924, state-controlled from 1925) made newsreels and documentaries, and from 1926 cinemas had to show its newsreels before the main feature. In April 1937 the regime opened **Cinecittà**, a huge studio complex near Rome. Most Italian films were light comedies and melodramas ("white telephone" films) and not overt propaganda, but some, such as *Scipione l'Africano* (1937), an epic about the Roman general who beat Hannibal, were clear parallels with the conquest of Ethiopia. Cinema was the most popular entertainment and the regime watched it closely, and in 1938 it restricted imports of American films.

### Linking Fascist Italy to ancient Rome

The regime presented itself as the heir of the Roman Empire, a theme known as ***romanità***. The *fasces* was the emblem of the party and appeared on coins, stamps and buildings. The raised arm (the "Roman salute", though the Romans did not use it) became compulsory for public occasions and was made the party's standard greeting by its secretary Starace. In 1938 the army was ordered to adopt a goose-step called the "Roman step". Streets and squares were named after Roman figures and the party counted years from the March on Rome (1922 was "Year I"). In 1932, for the tenth anniversary of the March, the government cut a broad avenue, the *Via dei Fori Imperiali*, through the middle of Rome to link the Colosseum with Mussolini's offices in the Palazzo Venezia, uncovering the Roman forums and destroying medieval streets. In 1937–38 the **Mostra Augustea della Romanità**, an exhibition to mark the two-thousandth anniversary of Augustus's birth, displayed plaster casts of Roman monuments and showed the Fascist empire as the third Rome, after that of the Caesars and of the popes. Mussolini was portrayed as a new Caesar or Augustus, and the proclamation of the empire on 9 May 1936 was presented as the rebirth of the Roman Empire.

:::def Romanità
The Fascist cult of ancient Rome ("Romanness"): the claim that Fascist Italy continued the Roman Empire, shown in the fasces, the salute, the "Third Rome" of new monuments, and plans for an empire in the Mediterranean and Africa.
:::

### The personality cult

The **cult of the Duce** was the centre of propaganda. Pictures showed Mussolini as a strong and tireless leader: stripped to the waist threshing wheat, skiing, driving fast cars, flying planes, riding horses, and speaking from the balcony of the Palazzo Venezia to enormous crowds. The slogans on walls said "Mussolini is always right" and "Believe, obey, fight" (*Credere, obbedire, combattere*). Children learned in school that the Duce was the saviour of the nation. Photographs of his birthplace at Predappio became a place of pilgrimage. Party secretary Achille Starace (1931–39) increased the rituals: the salute, the uniform for party members, the Fascist Saturday, mass rallies, and in 1938 a campaign to replace the polite *lei* with *voi* as a form of address. Much of this looked ridiculous to many Italians, and Starace was widely mocked, but the Duce himself stayed popular for years.

### Rallies, youth and leisure

The regime tried to bring all Italians into its organisations from childhood.
- **The Balilla** (*Opera Nazionale Balilla*, 1926) organised boys and girls from the age of eight, with uniforms, drill and sport; in 1937 it was merged into the **Gioventù Italiana del Littorio** (GIL), which all pupils were pressed to join. Teachers in schools were required to be loyal Fascists, and a single approved textbook was introduced for primary schools.
- **The Dopolavoro** (*Opera Nazionale Dopolavoro*, 1925) organised leisure for workers: sports clubs, cheap holidays, libraries, theatres, cinemas, excursions and radio groups. By the late 1930s it had close to four million members. Many joined for the cheap outings rather than for the politics.
- **The "Fascist Saturday"** (1935) gave workers free Saturday afternoons for sports and pre-military training.
- **Rallies and exhibitions.** Mass parades, displays of gymnastics and "oceanic" meetings in Piazza Venezia were staged to show unity. The **Exhibition of the Fascist Revolution** in Rome (opened 1932) attracted millions of visitors.

### Art, architecture and culture

The regime used modern art and architecture for propaganda. It did not impose a single style, as the Nazis did, and tolerated variety so long as artists did not criticise it. Futurists such as Filippo Marinetti praised speed and war; the Novecento movement celebrated classical simplicity; modernist architects such as **Giuseppe Terragni** (the Casa del Fascio at Como, 1936) and the monumental style of **Marcello Piacentini** were all used. Huge projects were built: the Foro Mussolini (later Foro Italico) sports complex in Rome, new towns such as Littoria (1932) and Sabaudia (1934) on the drained Pontine Marshes, and the plan for a new district, EUR, for a world fair in 1942. The universities were under pressure: in 1931 professors were ordered to swear loyalty to the regime, and of about 1,200 only 12 refused and lost their posts. The philosopher **Giovanni Gentile**, Education Minister in 1922–24, led the Fascist Institute of Culture and directed the Italian Encyclopaedia.

### Sporting success

Sport was a powerful propaganda tool because it showed Italians as strong and united. Italy hosted and won the **1934 football World Cup**, beating Czechoslovakia 2–1 in the final in Rome on 10 June 1934; won the Olympic football tournament in Berlin in 1936; and won the World Cup again in France in 1938, beating Hungary 4–2. **Primo Carnera**, a giant from Friuli, became world heavyweight boxing champion in June 1933 when he beat Jack Sharkey in New York, and lost the title a year later. Italian cyclists such as Gino Bartali won the Tour de France (1938), and Italo Balbo led mass flights of seaplanes across the Atlantic (1933). Mussolini attended major events, was photographed as an athlete himself and gave the sporting victories to the regime. The regime built stadiums in most major cities.

### Extent, impact and success of propaganda

Was it effective? The evidence is mixed.
- **Strengths.** It controlled all the major media; it created a ubiquitous image of the Duce; it gave millions of ordinary Italians cheap leisure through the Dopolavoro; Ethiopia in 1936 produced real enthusiasm (when sanctions were imposed, Italians gave gold: on 18 December 1935, the "Day of the Wedding Ring", many donated their wedding rings to the state); and the cult of Mussolini helped make people feel that Italy was respected again. De Felice and others argued that the years 1929–36 were years of *consenso*, or consent.
- **Weaknesses.** Many people were more interested in their daily life than in the politics. Radio ownership was low, literacy was still incomplete in the south, and the peasants who made up a large part of the population were hard to reach. The Church ran its own youth organisation, Catholic Action, and in 1931 the Pope protested at an attack on it, forcing the regime to retreat. The king remained a rival focus of loyalty. The informers' reports show that Italians grumbled about prices, wages, the party and its petty rules, and the Duce's popularity fell after the racial laws, the German alliance and the failures of 1940. Historians such as **R. J. B. Bosworth** and **Paul Corner** (in an article of 2002 asking what had happened to the idea of dictatorship) have argued that "consensus" was shallower than De Felice supposed: public enthusiasm was staged, and it is hard to tell conformity from belief when no one could say no.
- **A fair judgement.** The cult of the Duce was more successful than the cult of Fascism. The regime made Mussolini a figure that many Italians admired, and it won support where it delivered something (cheap leisure, national pride, public works, the Lateran Pacts). But propaganda could not make Italians into a nation of obedient fanatics, and it could not hide the gap between boasts and facts when the war began.

:::exam Using the propaganda evidence
For a question on propaganda, avoid a catalogue (radio, film, sport, and so on). Pick three or four means and for each say what it was designed to do, what it achieved, and what limited it, with one precise fact each (for example, a licence-holders figure of about a million in 1939; close to four million Dopolavoro members; the 12 professors who refused the oath). Then judge whether it created *belief* or only *conformity*.
:::
## The nature of Fascist government

On paper Italy after 1926 was a dictatorship in which every power flowed to the Duce. In practice the picture was more complicated, because the Fascists had taken over an existing state and had not replaced it.

### The Duce

**Mussolini** was "Head of the Government" under the law of December 1925, answerable to the king and not to parliament, and from 1928 the Grand Council could suggest his successor. He took the title ***Duce*** ("leader") and the regime centred on him. He held several ministries himself: at times he was prime minister, foreign minister, minister of the interior, of war, the navy and the air force at once, and he worked from the Palazzo Venezia in Rome. He liked to be seen as the only man who made decisions. In practice this style produced poor government: decisions were made on impulse, he disliked being contradicted, he read little, he distrusted capable subordinates, and ministers competed for his favour. His son-in-law **Galeazzo Ciano**, married to his daughter Edda, was foreign minister from 1936.

:::def Duce
The Italian word for "leader", Mussolini's title as head of the Fascist movement and regime. The regime claimed that the Duce was always right, and that his will was the will of the nation.
:::

### The Fascist Party (PNF) and the Grand Council

The **National Fascist Party** (PNF) had a secretary, appointed by Mussolini, and a network of provincial leaders (*federali*). After the *ras* had been brought under control in 1926, the party's job was to organise, not to rule. Membership was opened up and became necessary for public posts in 1933; by 1939 it had well over two million members. Under **Achille Starace**, secretary from December 1931 to October 1939, it became an organisation of ritual and conformity, with youth sections, women's groups, welfare work and uniforms. Many members joined to get jobs. Party officials were often in conflict with the prefects, who were professional state servants, and Mussolini generally backed the prefects against the party's radicals.

The **Fascist Grand Council**, set up as a party body in 1922, became in 1928 an official organ of state. It could be consulted on major matters of state and could draw up the list of names for the succession. It met irregularly and Mussolini used it to approve decisions. In July 1943 it turned against him: after a night's debate on 24–25 July it voted by 19 to 7 for a motion to ask the king to resume command, and the king dismissed him the next day.

### Central government: the king, cabinet and parliament

The monarchy was never abolished. **Victor Emmanuel III** stayed head of state and commander-in-chief of the armed forces, appointed the prime minister in law, and signed all the laws, including the racial laws. The army, the navy and the officer corps swore loyalty to the king. Mussolini resented this and sometimes talked of a republic, but he did not dare to remove the king; and in 1938, when he and the king were both made *First Marshal of the Empire*, a title of equal rank, the king was offended. Real power, however, lay with Mussolini.

The **cabinet** lost its collective power: ministers reported only to the Duce, and the cabinet rarely met as a body. **Parliament** survived as a decoration. A law of 1928 replaced free elections by a **plebiscite** on a single list of 400 candidates chosen by the Grand Council from names put forward by the Fascist syndicates; voters could only say yes or no to the whole list. In March 1929 about 8.5 million voted yes and about 136,000 voted no (more than 98 per cent yes), and in 1934 about 10 million said yes and 15,000 no. In 1939 the Chamber of Deputies was replaced altogether by the **Chamber of Fasces and Corporations**, whose members sat by virtue of their party or corporate offices. The **Senate**, appointed by the king, continued and included many of the old elite.

### Police, civil service and judiciary

The **police** were under Bocchini and the Interior Ministry and were the main instrument of control. The **civil service** stayed largely as before, staffed by professionals who were required to take the party card in 1933 but whose habits and loyalties did not change. The **judiciary** was bound by new codes (the Rocco penal code of 1930 raised penalties for political crimes and kept the death penalty), and judges in ordinary courts were mostly career lawyers; for political cases the Special Tribunal took over. In this way many parts of the old state were used and preserved, and the Fascists mainly added party organs and a police.

### Prefects and podestà

Local government was brought under the centre. **Prefects**, appointed by the Interior Ministry, ran the provinces and from 1927 were the highest state authority in their province, with power over the local party. The elected mayors and councils of towns were replaced in 1926 by an official called the ***podestà*** (the name came from medieval Italian cities), appointed by the government for five years; by 1928 all towns had one. The podestà were often local landowners, lawyers and professionals, and not young Fascist radicals, so that local power often stayed in the hands of the same notables, now wearing a black shirt.

:::def Prefect
The government's representative in each Italian province, appointed by the Interior Minister, in charge of order and administration. Under Fascism prefects were given authority over the provincial party secretaries, and Mussolini often backed them against the radicals.
:::

:::def Podestà
A mayor appointed by the government from 1926 to replace the elected mayor and council of a town. He served for five years and was usually a respected local person, often a landowner or professional man.
:::

### How totalitarian was Fascist Italy?

Mussolini's slogan was "everything within the state". The reality fell short, and the contrast with Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR is important in answers.

:::key Totalitarian in aim, dual in practice
1. **In aim**: one party, one leader, control of media, youth and leisure, the claim that the individual belongs to the state, and the repression of all rivals.
2. **Limits on the regime's power**: the king kept the army's oath and the right to dismiss the Duce; the Church kept its schools, Catholic Action and its own doctrine through the Lateran Pacts of 1929; big business and the civil service kept their independence; the party itself was divided and corrupt; and ordinary Italians kept their private lives.
3. **Violence**: far less than in Nazi Germany or Stalin's Russia, which also means that Italian Fascism needed less terror because the old elites cooperated.
4. **The result**: historians such as **Emilio Gentile** stress the regime's totalitarian *project*, a political religion that tried to remake Italians; others, such as **Bosworth** and **Corner**, stress how often it fell short and how much of the old state survived. A balanced answer says that Fascist Italy was a one-party dictatorship with totalitarian ambitions that were only partly carried out.
:::

## Mussolini's foreign policy

### Aims

Mussolini had three aims: to restore Italy's **prestige** as a Great Power (the answer to the "mutilated victory"), to control the **Mediterranean**, which Fascists called *mare nostrum* ("our sea") although Britain held Gibraltar, Malta, Cyprus and the Suez Canal, and to build an **empire in Africa**, partly from the colonies Italy already held (Libya, Eritrea, Somalia) and partly by conquering Ethiopia, the one African state that had defeated an Italian army, at Adwa in 1896. He also wanted to make Italians into a warrior people, and believed that war was necessary for a great nation. For most of the 1920s he did not act on this in a way that threatened Britain and France, and he talked of Italy's "decisive weight" between the other powers. After 1935 he took more risks.

### Methods and events, 1922–35

- **Corfu (1923).** After the murder of the Italian General Tellini on the Greek frontier, Mussolini bombarded and occupied Corfu. The Conference of Ambassadors, not the League, settled the matter, and Greece paid 50 million lire. Italian prestige gained, but it was a bullying of a small state (see [International relations, 1919–29 (3)](#hist.int.1919.3)).
- **Fiume (1924).** By the Treaty of Rome of 27 January 1924 Yugoslavia accepted Italian possession of Fiume, which gave the regime a nationalist success.
- **Locarno (1925).** Italy was a guarantor, with Britain, of the Franco-German and Belgian-German frontiers, which showed that Italy was accepted as a Great Power. But Mussolini failed to get the Brenner frontier with Austria guaranteed.
- **Albania.** The Pacts of Tirana (1926 and 1927) made Albania, ruled by King Zog, an Italian protectorate in all but name, with Italian loans, advisers and a twenty-year defensive alliance.
- **Libya.** In 1923–32 Italian forces under Badoglio and Graziani crushed the resistance in Cyrenaica. Tens of thousands of civilians were put in camps in the desert and many died there; estimates of total deaths vary widely and run to tens of thousands. The resistance leader Omar al-Mukhtar was hanged in September 1931. In 1938 about 20,000 Italian settlers were shipped to Libya in a single convoy.
- **Diplomacy.** A friendship treaty with Ethiopia in 1928; a Four-Power Pact proposed by Mussolini in 1933 (Britain, France, Germany, Italy), which was signed but never ratified; and the defence of Austria's independence. After Austrian Nazis murdered Chancellor Dollfuss on 25 July 1934, Mussolini sent troops to the Brenner Pass and Hitler backed down. At **Stresa** (April 1935) Italy joined Britain and France in condemning German rearmament and pledging to defend Austria. At that moment Mussolini seemed to be a defender of the status quo.

### Abyssinia, 1935–36

Mussolini now moved to the conquest of Ethiopia (Abyssinia). He had wanted revenge for Adwa since his youth, needed a success to offset the effects of the Depression (see [the next chapter](#hist.d.italy.3)), and thought that Britain and France, who needed Italy against Hitler, would not stop him. The pretext was a clash at the Wal Wal oasis on 5 December 1934. Italy poured troops and supplies into Eritrea and Somalia and in the French agreement of January 1935 (Laval) Mussolini believed he had been given a free hand. On **3 October 1935** Italian forces invaded without a declaration of war.

Ethiopia's emperor Haile Selassie appealed to the League. On 18 November 1935 the League voted **economic sanctions** against Italy: a ban on arms, loans and imports from Italy and on the export of some metals and rubber to it. Oil, coal and steel were left out, so the sanctions did not stop the war. The British and French foreign ministers, **Hoare and Laval**, secretly agreed in December 1935 to offer Italy two-thirds of Ethiopia; when it leaked, public opinion in Britain forced Hoare to resign, and Mussolini did not accept the plan. Badoglio replaced De Bono as commander in November 1935. Italy used modern weapons including aircraft and, in defiance of the 1925 Geneva Protocol, **mustard gas** against soldiers and civilians. The Italian government denied it until 1996. Addis Ababa fell on **5 May 1936** and on 9 May Mussolini proclaimed the empire, with Victor Emmanuel III as emperor of Ethiopia. After an attempt on the life of the viceroy Graziani in February 1937, Italian troops and Blackshirts killed thousands of people in Addis Ababa. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, and the country was never fully pacified. In July 1936 the League dropped the sanctions.

:::key What the Abyssinian War meant
1. **For Italians**: the high point of Mussolini's popularity. Italians donated gold, and the empire was celebrated as the return of Rome's glory.
2. **For the League**: its sanctions were half-hearted and the invasion succeeded, which destroyed its authority (see [International relations, 1929–39 (1)](#hist.int.1929.1)).
3. **For Italian diplomacy**: it ended the Stresa Front, left Italy isolated from Britain and France and pushed Mussolini towards Hitler, who had not joined the sanctions.
4. **For Italy's strength**: the war cost money that Italy could not afford, used up arms and foreign exchange, and the occupation needed a large garrison.
:::

### The Axis, Spain and the road to war, 1936–40

- **Axis (1936).** In October 1936 Ciano and the German foreign minister agreed on a protocol, and on 1 November Mussolini spoke of a Rome–Berlin "axis" around which other European states could turn. He visited Germany in September 1937, was impressed by the German army and rallies, and joined the Anti-Comintern Pact in November 1937. Italy announced its withdrawal from the League in December 1937.
- **Spain (1936–39).** From the first weeks Mussolini sent aircraft and arms to General Franco, and from December 1936 about 70,000 to 75,000 Italian "volunteers" fought for the Nationalists. They were beaten at Guadalajara in March 1937, a humiliating setback for a regime that boasted of its martial spirit. Franco won in 1939, but Italy had spent huge quantities of arms and money that it needed for its own army.
- **Austria and Munich (1938).** In March 1938 Mussolini let Hitler annex Austria, abandoning the Brenner policy he had defended in 1934. In September 1938 he presented himself as the mediator who saved the peace at Munich, which was popular in Italy.
- **Albania (7 April 1939).** Italian forces invaded and took the country in a few days. King Zog fled, and the crown was offered to Victor Emmanuel III. It was an easy conquest but it persuaded Britain to guarantee Greece and Romania.
- **Pact of Steel (22 May 1939).** Italy bound itself to Germany in a full military alliance, although a memorandum from Mussolini himself told Hitler that Italy would not be ready for a major war before 1942–43.
- **War.** When Germany invaded Poland in September 1939 Italy declared itself "non-belligerent". In June 1940, as France collapsed, Mussolini declared war on Britain and France on **10 June 1940**, expecting a quick victory and a share of the spoils. Italy's army was ill-equipped: its tanks and aircraft were out of date, it lacked oil, and its steel output was about a tenth of Germany's. The boast of "eight million bayonets" (1936) hid these weaknesses. Mussolini's attack on Greece in October 1940 failed, and by early 1941 Italy had lost heavily in North Africa and East Africa and depended on German help.

### Extent of success

| Aim | Successes | Failures |
|---|---|---|
| Prestige | Treaty of Rome (1924), Locarno (1925), the Four-Power Pact idea, mediator at Munich (1938), a Great Power empire in 1936 | Isolation after Abyssinia; dependence on Germany; Hitler, not Mussolini, set the pace; Italy was a junior partner by 1939 |
| Mediterranean | Naval and air bases in Libya, Albania and the Dodecanese; Spanish Nationalists' victory | Britain kept Gibraltar, Malta and Suez; the Italian fleet could not break out of the Mediterranean; Italy needed British and French goodwill |
| Empire | Libya "pacified" (1932) and settled; Ethiopia conquered (1936); Albania (1939) | Ethiopia never fully controlled; costs far above returns; empire lost in 1941 |
| Wider effects | Popularity at home in 1936 | Left the League, split from Britain and France, entered a war for which the army was not ready |

:::key Judging the success of Mussolini's foreign policy
1. **Individual successes**: Fiume (1924), Libya (1932), Ethiopia (1936), Albania (1939), Munich (1938).
2. **Individual failures**: Austria (1938), the cost of Spain, Greece (1940), isolation after Abyssinia.
3. **Overall achievement of aims**: In the short term Mussolini won prestige and territory; in the long term he lost both, for the empire was built on weak foundations and the alliance with Hitler turned Italy into a dependent. The criterion matters: success measured by territory gained and popularity in 1936 gives one answer, success measured by Italy's security and strength in 1940 gives another.
:::

## Consent or coercion? Weighing the regime

The central question of this chapter is how Mussolini ruled: did the regime rest on consent or on force?

| | Evidence of coercion | Evidence of consent |
|---|---|---|
| Politics | Banned parties; Special Tribunal; confino; informers; plebiscites with one list | Plebiscite results of 98 per cent (but not free); no mass resistance before 1943 |
| Society | Press censorship; loyalty oaths; compulsory party card for jobs (1933) | Dopolavoro (close to four million members); youth organisations; sport; the Lateran Pacts; the Church's support |
| Foreign policy | Ethiopia and Libya won by force | Mass enthusiasm in 1936 (gold donations); Munich popular in 1938 |
| Limits | The regime could not impose the racial laws easily or break the Church | Grumbling; apathy; decline of support from 1938 |

:::note The historians' debate: consent in Fascist Italy (stretch: useful for A*)
- **Renzo De Felice**, in his biography of Mussolini (the volume *Mussolini il duce: gli anni del consenso, 1929–1936* appeared in 1974), argued that between the Lateran Pacts and the Ethiopian war Mussolini enjoyed the genuine support of most Italians. Critics said that a dictatorship cannot measure consent, and that he took propaganda for popular opinion.
- **Victoria de Grazia**, *The Culture of Consent* (1981), studied the Dopolavoro and argued that the regime's leisure organisations won real if shallow support by offering what people wanted, while remaining under party control.
- **Emilio Gentile** argued that Fascism was a political religion with a totalitarian project of making a new Italian.
- **R. J. B. Bosworth**, *Mussolini's Italy* (2005), argued from police and diary evidence that most Italians conformed and adapted, but did so for ordinary reasons, neither believing in nor opposing the regime.
- **Philip Morgan**, *Italian Fascism, 1919–1945* (1995), gave a balanced account of the regime's methods and the limits of its power.
A fair judgement: the regime had more consent than a purely coercive dictatorship, but less than it claimed, and its support came mainly in the years 1929–36, when it seemed to be delivering.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'Mussolini's government relied more on consent than on coercion.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9): some analysis; one side argued with the other mentioned; knowledge adequate but with description. Level 4 (10–12): both sides with valid criteria; judgement supported but partly substantiated; good, mostly precise detail. Level 5 (13–15): sustained balance, wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and precise, carefully selected knowledge on every point.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criterion: which of the two did more to keep the regime in power, tested by "what would have happened without it?" and by period (1926–29, 1929–36, 1936–40).
- Coercion: the 1926 laws; Special Tribunal; confino (over 10,000); Bocchini and OVRA; informers; the press; party card for jobs (1933); the oath of 1931 (12 refusals out of about 1,200); the racial laws.
- Consent: the Dopolavoro (close to four million); the Lateran Pacts (1929); youth organisations; the Duce cult; 1929 plebiscite (98 per cent yes, but unfree); Ethiopia (18 December 1935: the wedding rings); Munich.
- Limits of both: few executions at home; the king, Church and army kept power; consent often passive; fall in support after 1938; the 1943 collapse.
- Judgement: coercion created the conditions for consent, by removing every alternative by 1927, but consent in 1929–36 was real in part and stopped the regime from needing mass terror; it was thin and fell away under strain.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Mussolini used a lot of coercion. He had a secret police called OVRA and a Special Tribunal which sent people to prison. He also had censorship so people could only read what he wanted. But he also had consent because he had propaganda and many people liked him, for example because he won the World Cup. So I think consent was more important because people supported him."

*Commentary.* Both sides appear, but the points are general ("a lot of coercion", "many people liked him"), the examples are thin, and the conclusion ("people supported him") asserts what the essay needed to prove. No criteria. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 3 (about 7).

**A Level 4 paragraph (precise, but no criterion).** "The Dopolavoro was the most successful consent organisation. It was set up in 1925 and by the late 1930s had close to four million members. It offered cheap holidays, sports clubs, cinemas and excursions, and many workers joined because it gave them things they could not afford. This gave the regime a favourable image and shows that Mussolini gained real support. However, the Dopolavoro was controlled by the party, and joining was often a way to avoid trouble."

*Commentary.* Precise (the year, the figure, what it offered) and balanced inside the paragraph, so Table B is Level 4 (about 11). But it does not say how much the Dopolavoro mattered to the regime's survival, and it does not compare consent with coercion. Table A: Level 4 (about 10).

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"A regime can be said to rely on something if its survival would have been in doubt without it. On that test the Fascist regime relied on coercion to establish itself and to remove every organised alternative, and on consent, much of it passive, to run Italy without having to use mass terror. The claim that it relied *more* on consent is partly right for the years 1929 to 1936, but wrong about the foundations of the regime.

The case for coercion is that nothing else could have produced the dictatorship of 1926. By November 1926 the opposition parties were banned, the press was controlled by the prefects, 120 deputies had lost their seats, and the Special Tribunal tried political offences before army and militia officers. Arturo Bocchini's police and the political police later called OVRA ran networks of informers, and more than 10,000 people were sent into confino without trial. Gramsci, the leading Communist, was sentenced to over twenty years in prison in 1928 and died in 1937, a few days after his release. Pressure also worked quietly: from 1933 a party card was needed for public employment, and in 1931 only 12 of about 1,200 university professors refused to swear loyalty, which shows how effectively livelihoods were held hostage. Without this machinery the Socialists, Communists and Catholic Popolari might have rebuilt an opposition that, in 1924, still won over a third of the votes.

The case for consent has three parts. First, the regime gave something to many Italians. The Dopolavoro, with close to four million members by the late 1930s, offered cheap leisure; the Lateran Pacts of 1929 settled the quarrel with the papacy that had troubled Catholics since 1870 and won the regime the Church's goodwill; and the cult of the Duce offered national pride. Second, there were moments of real enthusiasm, above all in 1935–36, when Italians gave their gold wedding rings on 18 December 1935 to support the Abyssinian war, and Mussolini's popularity reached its peak when the empire was proclaimed. Third, opposition inside Italy was tiny after 1927: there was no mass resistance before 1943. De Felice called 1929–36 the 'years of consensus'.

But the evidence for consent is weaker than it looks. The plebiscite of March 1929, in which about 8.5 million said yes and about 136,000 said no, was held under a single list with open voting pressure and so measures conformity, not belief. The Dopolavoro's members often joined for the cheap outings, and the police reports of informers record grumbling about prices, wages and the party. Radio, the most modern means of propaganda, reached only about a million licence-holders in a population of 44 million in 1939. And consent was conditional: support fell after the racial laws of 1938, the German alliance and the defeats of 1940–41, and in July 1943 the regime collapsed almost without resistance when the Grand Council voted 19 to 7 against the Duce and the king dismissed him. A regime that rested on firm consent might have defended itself.

Two further points show why the question is more complicated than a choice between the two. First, the regime used less violence than Nazi Germany or Stalin's Russia, executing dozens of opponents before the war rather than thousands, and this suggests that it needed less coercion, because the king, the army, the Church and big business cooperated with it and the opposition had been broken early. Second, the line between consent and coercion is blurred: a worker who joined the Dopolavoro, the party or the youth movement did so partly because refusal could cost a job.

Overall, the regime relied on coercion for its foundation and on consent, mostly passive, for its day-to-day running. Coercion was the more fundamental: without the laws of 1925–26 and the police there would have been no one-party state, and no consent could have been measured, because no alternative could have been expressed. Consent was real in 1929–36 and spared the regime from needing mass terror, but it was shallow and conditional, and it collapsed when the regime failed in war. The view that the government relied *more* on consent is therefore only half true: it describes the dictatorship at its best, not what held it up."

*Commentary.* The essay sets a **criterion** in the first sentence (what the regime's survival depended on) and uses period to organise the argument. Every point has a precise fact (the years, the numbers, the names). The evidence for consent is itself tested (the plebiscite, the Dopolavoro members' motives, the radio figure), which is a mark of Level 5 evaluation. The conclusion compares the two and gives a limited judgement. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 14). Total about 28/30.

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the success of Mussolini's foreign policy in the years 1922–40.
**What the levels want.** "Assess" asks for an informed judgement on a long period. Level 5 needs criteria (here, the regime's own aims) and an overall judgement that weighs short-term against long-term results, not a list of events.

**Plan.**
- Criteria: the three aims (prestige, control of the Mediterranean, empire in Africa), and for each ask short-term and long-term.
- Prestige: Corfu (1923), Fiume (1924), Locarno (1925), Four-Power Pact (1933), Brenner (1934), Stresa (1935) as successes; Abyssinia (1935–36) as a short-term success but long-term isolation; the League's sanctions; Austria and the Axis; Munich (1938).
- Mediterranean: Albania (1939), bases in Libya, Spain; but Britain held Gibraltar, Malta and Suez; the Italian fleet was confined; the war of 1940.
- Empire: Libya consolidated; Ethiopia taken (1936) but never pacified; lost by 1941.
- Judgement: success in the short term, failure in the long term, because the means (alliance with Hitler, war) destroyed the ends.

**A Level 5 paragraph (the aim of prestige, tested over time).** "Judged by prestige, Mussolini's foreign policy was more successful in the 1920s than in the 1930s. At Locarno in 1925 Italy joined Britain as guarantor of the Rhine frontier, which treated it as a Great Power, and in 1934 Mussolini's troops on the Brenner Pass checked Hitler's first move on Austria. The Stresa conference of April 1935 seemed to make him a pillar of the western order. Yet the war against Abyssinia reversed this: sanctions in November 1935 estranged Britain and France, ended the Stresa Front and drove Italy towards Germany. The proclamation of the empire on 9 May 1936 brought huge popularity at home but little respect abroad, and by 1938, when Mussolini allowed Hitler to annex Austria without protest, the Duce was acting as a follower and not as an equal. Prestige peaked in 1935–36 and was lost in the process of getting it."

**A Level 5 conclusion.** "Overall, Mussolini achieved real but short-lived successes, in Fiume in 1924, Libya in 1932, Ethiopia in 1936 and Albania in 1939, and the territory looked impressive on a map. But the three aims were not achieved. Italy did not control the Mediterranean, because Britain's bases and fleet, and the lack of oil and steel, made 'our sea' a boast. Italy's prestige fell, because by 1939 the Pact of Steel made it a junior partner of Germany. And the empire was lost within five years. The most revealing fact is that in June 1940, after eighteen years, Mussolini still needed Hitler's victories to enter the war and Hitler's armies to avoid defeat. The policy was successful in tactics and unsuccessful in strategy."

*Commentary.* The first paragraph uses the regime's aim as a criterion and dates every claim; the conclusion distinguishes short term from long term and tactics from strategy. With a similar paragraph on the Mediterranean and on empire, and an opening that defines the criteria, this is Level 5 on both tables.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the extent and success of Fascist propaganda." [30]

A student wrote:

"Fascist propaganda was very extensive. Mussolini controlled the press, radio and cinema, and there were posters of him everywhere saying 'Mussolini is always right'. There were also youth groups like the Balilla and a lot of sport, and Italy won the World Cup in 1934 and 1938. Mussolini also tried to link Italy to ancient Rome. All this shows that propaganda was very successful because Italians supported Mussolini."

**The level.** Level 2 on Table A and Level 3 on Table B (about 5 and 7). It lists many accurate points but does not explain any of them, gives no evidence of effects, and ends with the claim it was supposed to prove. "Extent" is covered by a list, and "success" is covered only by an assertion.

**The fix.** Pick three means, give a fact and a limit for each, and then judge. "Propaganda was extensive in the media: from 1925 journalists were on an official register and editors received daily instructions, and from 1926 cinemas had to show LUCE newsreels. But its reach was limited: in 1939 only about a million Italians held radio licences in a population of about 44 million. It was most successful where it offered something, as the Dopolavoro did with cheap leisure for close to four million members, and where it matched an event, as in 1936, when the empire made the cult of the Duce real. It failed where it demanded belief: Starace's rituals were mocked, the Pope protected Catholic Action in 1931, and support fell away after 1938. So propaganda secured conformity widely and belief narrowly." Now each fact proves a point, and the answer reaches a judgement.

Q: What were OVRA, the Special Tribunal and confino?
A: OVRA was the political police, a section of Bocchini's police that ran informers and kept files on opponents. The Special Tribunal (November 1926) tried political offences before army and militia officers. Confino was internal exile, without trial, to a remote village or island for up to five years.
Q: What did the racial laws of 1938 do, and what are two views of why they were introduced?
A: They banned marriages between Jews and non-Jews, removed Jews from state jobs, the party and the army, and limited their property (the decree of 17 November 1938, after the Manifesto of Race of 14 July). One view (De Felice) is that Mussolini acted mainly to please Hitler; another (Sarfatti) is that they came out of the regime's own racism, which grew in the Ethiopian war.
Q: Name four means of Fascist propaganda and one limit on the effect of propaganda.
A: Press control (the register of journalists, daily instructions), radio (EIAR), film (LUCE newsreels and Cinecittà), sport (the World Cups of 1934 and 1938) and the cult of the Duce; also the Dopolavoro and the Balilla. A limit: only about a million radio licences in 1939; the Pope's defence of Catholic Action; falling support after 1938.
Q: What was *romanità*?
A: The cult of ancient Rome: the fasces, the Roman salute, the "Third Rome" of new monuments and avenues (the Via dei Fori Imperiali, 1932), the Mostra Augustea of 1937, and the claim that Fascist Italy was the heir of the Roman Empire.
Q: Who held real power in Fascist government, and who kept some independence?
A: The Duce, who was Head of Government, controlled the ministries, the police, the Militia and the prefects, and the party served him. The king (commander of the army, with the right to dismiss him), the Church (through the Lateran Pacts), the army and big business kept some independence.
Q: What was the role of prefects and podestà?
A: Prefects, appointed by the Interior Ministry, ran the provinces and were the highest state authority there (from 1927). Podestà, appointed by the government from 1926, replaced elected mayors; they were usually local notables.
Q: What were the main events of the Abyssinian War, and what were its results for Mussolini?
A: Wal Wal incident (5 December 1934); invasion on 3 October 1935; League sanctions (18 November 1935) that left out oil; Hoare–Laval plan; mustard gas; Addis Ababa taken 5 May 1936; empire proclaimed 9 May. Results: high popularity at home; end of the Stresa Front; isolation from Britain and France; rapprochement with Germany; heavy costs.
Q: Give two successes and two failures of Mussolini's foreign policy.
A: Successes: Fiume (1924); Locarno (1925); Libya (1932); Ethiopia (1936); Munich (1938); Albania (1939). Failures: isolation after Abyssinia; letting Hitler annex Austria (1938); the cost and the defeat at Guadalajara in Spain (1937); dependence on Germany; the failed attack on Greece (1940).

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which Fascist Italy was a totalitarian state.
A: Support: one party and one leader; control of media, youth and leisure; secret police; Special Tribunal; the party card for jobs; the 1925 slogan 'everything within the state'; the racial laws; the cult. Challenge: the king, army, Church (Lateran Pacts, Catholic Action) and big business kept power; the civil service and prefects remained; coercion was less violent than in Germany or the USSR; the party was divided; many Italians kept private lives. Level 5 defines "totalitarian" (aim against reality) and judges, e.g., that Italy was totalitarian in ambition, authoritarian in practice, because the regime compromised with the old elites.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the racial laws of 1938 were introduced mainly to please Hitler.
A: Support: the timing (Axis 1936, the Pact of Steel talks, Mussolini's visit to Germany in 1937 and Hitler's to Italy in May 1938); no tradition of state antisemitism in Italy; about 46,000 Jews, mostly integrated; De Felice's argument; unpopularity of the laws. Challenge: the regime's racism in Africa (the 1937 laws against mixed relationships); the Manifesto of Race (July 1938) with Italian scientists; Mussolini's wish to form a martial, 'pure' people; Michele Sarfatti's argument that the laws had Italian roots; the speed and thoroughness of the laws. Level 5 weighs timing and context against the Italian roots, and judges, e.g., that the alliance created the occasion but that the regime's own racism and the Ethiopian conquest made the laws possible.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The Fascist government of Italy was inefficient and chaotic.' Evaluate this view.
A: Support: Mussolini held several ministries and made impulsive decisions; ministers competed for his favour; party and prefects quarrelled; the ras and federali; Starace's absurd rituals; rivalry between Ciano and others; poor planning for war (1940). Challenge: the civil service, police and prefects kept the state running; effective repression; large public works; the Lateran Pacts; the regime survived for twenty years; the chaos was a method of rule (divide and rule) as well as a weakness. Level 5 judges that the government was inefficient in policy-making but effective in keeping power, and that these were connected.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons why Mussolini invaded Abyssinia in 1935.
H: Sort reasons into aims (prestige, empire, revenge for Adwa), circumstances (Britain and France needed Italy against Hitler, the Laval agreement of January 1935) and domestic motives (the Depression, popularity).
H: Say which were necessary and which were decisive.
A: Prestige and empire (the third aim of the regime); revenge for Adwa (1896); the example of Libya; the Depression and the need for a popular success at home (see [the next chapter](#hist.d.italy.3)); Mussolini's view of war as a test of a nation; the chance: Britain and France were concerned about Germany (Stresa, April 1935), the Laval agreement of January 1935 gave a free hand as Mussolini understood it, and the League seemed weak; the Wal Wal incident as pretext; the aim of absorbing a state to settle Italians. Level 5 uses criteria (necessary and sufficient, long-term and short-term) and judges that aims and opportunity combined, with the domestic need decisive in the timing.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that Mussolini's foreign policy brought Italy more harm than benefit.
A: Harm: isolation after Abyssinia; sanctions; closeness to Hitler; the cost of Ethiopia and Spain; weakness in 1940; the loss of the empire; the wars of 1940–43. Benefit: prestige in the 1920s (Locarno, Fiume); Libya; Ethiopia (briefly); popularity at home in 1936; Albania; Munich. Level 5 judges by time and by criteria such as security, wealth and prestige, and says harm outweighed benefit overall because the benefits were short-lived and the policy ended in defeat, though the question asks about the policy up to 1940.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the success of the Balilla and the Dopolavoro in winning the support of young people and workers.
A: Success: close to four million Dopolavoro members; cheap leisure and holidays; sport; Balilla and GIL membership in the millions; the Fascist Saturday (1935). Limits: many joined for the benefits or to keep a job; the Church's Catholic Action competed (1931); teachers and parents were not always loyal; the organisations were run by the party and did not reach all the south or women evenly; support fell after 1938. Level 5 judges the difference between membership and commitment, e.g., that the organisations won wide conformity and some real attachment (above all from the leisure they offered) but not the ideological conversion the regime wanted.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Fascist regime was a compromise with the old elites and never a Fascist revolution.' Evaluate this view.
H: Make a list of the old institutions that survived: the monarchy, the army, the Church, the civil service, the judiciary, big business.
H: Then list what was new: the party, the Militia, the Grand Council, the Special Tribunal, the corporations and the youth organisations. Which list is stronger?
A: Support: the king stayed head of state and dismissed Mussolini in 1943; the army swore loyalty to him; the Church won the Lateran Pacts and kept its schools and Catholic Action; the prefects (state servants) were set above the party; the podestà were local notables; big business kept its independence (Vidoni agreement); the civil service stayed; the *ras* were curbed; the 1943 coup came from within the elite. Challenge: the party, Militia, Grand Council, Special Tribunal, the youth organisations and the Dopolavoro were new; the state was reshaped by the Head of Government law and the corporative system; the regime claimed to create a new Italian; Emilio Gentile's totalitarian project; the racial laws and the Axis showed radicalisation. A* judgement: a compromise in structure and a revolution in aspiration, with the compromise winning in practice until the war showed that the radical aims could not be met.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Mussolini's foreign policy in the 1930s was driven by ideology, not by opportunism.' Evaluate this view.
H: Choose three decisions (Abyssinia, Spain, the Axis or Pact of Steel) and ask of each whether it served a settled aim or a short-term chance.
H: Remember that the rhetoric of 'eight million bayonets' and the actual policy of 'non-belligerence' in 1939 did not match.
A: Ideology: war as the test of a nation; empire and the Roman myth; Mussolini's talk of a new Fascist civilisation; the racial laws; the Spanish war as a crusade against communism. Opportunism: the sudden switch from the Brenner policy (1934) to the Axis; Abyssinia timed to the weakness of Britain and France; non-belligerence in 1939; entry to the war in June 1940 only when France was falling; Albania in 1939 as a reply to the German occupation of Prague. A* judgement: the goals were ideological (prestige, empire, war), the timing and the methods opportunist, and the two combined to make Mussolini follow Hitler.
:::

:::recap
- **Terror state**: Bocchini (police chief 1926–40), OVRA, informers; Militia; Special Tribunal (1926); confino (over 10,000); economic pressure (party card 1933; oath of 1931, 12 refusals); murder of the Rosselli brothers (1937); fewer killings at home than in Germany or the USSR.
- **Racial laws**: Manifesto of Race (14 July 1938); decree of 17 November 1938; about 46,000 Jews; explanations (to please Hitler, or from the regime's own racism).
- **Propaganda**: press register (1925) and daily instructions; EIAR radio (about a million licences in 1939); LUCE newsreels and Cinecittà (1937); MinCulPop (1937); romanità (Via dei Fori Imperiali 1932; Mostra Augustea 1937); cult of the Duce; Balilla (1926), Dopolavoro (1925, close to four million), Fascist Saturday (1935); sport (World Cups 1934, 1938; Carnera 1933).
- **Limits of propaganda**: low radio ownership, the Church's Catholic Action (1931), the king, mockery of Starace, falling support after 1938.
- **Government**: Duce as Head of Government; PNF (Starace 1931–39); Grand Council (state organ 1928; 19–7 vote 24–25 July 1943); king; cabinet; parliament by plebiscite (1929: about 8.5 million yes, about 136,000 no); Chamber of Fasces and Corporations (1939); prefects and podestà; dual power, less totalitarian than Germany or the USSR.
- **Foreign policy**: aims of prestige, the Mediterranean and empire. Corfu (1923); Fiume (1924); Locarno (1925); Tirana pacts; Libya (1923–32); Brenner (1934); Stresa (April 1935); Abyssinia (3 October 1935 to 5 May 1936); Axis (1936); Spain (about 70,000–75,000 men); Munich; Albania (7 April 1939); Pact of Steel (22 May 1939); war (10 June 1940).
- **Judgement**: foreign-policy successes were short-term, and the alliance with Hitler turned them into long-term failure; the regime combined coercion and, in 1929–36, a real but shallow consent.
:::
