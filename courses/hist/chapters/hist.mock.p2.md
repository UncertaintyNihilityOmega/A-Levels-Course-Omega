Q: [30 marks] (#hist.eu.germany.3) **Liberalism and nationalism in Germany, 1815–71.**

(a) Explain why Austria and Prussia went to war in 1866. [10]

(b) "Liberalism was the main threat to the German Confederation in the years 1815–48." How far do you agree with this view? [20]

A: **Mark scheme, Question 1.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [10]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO2).**

- **The rivalry for leadership of Germany.** Austria presided in the Federal Diet from 1815, but Prussia had gained the Rhineland and Westphalia and, through the Zollverein (1 January 1834), led an economic union that excluded Austria. Prussia had been forced to give way to Austria at Olmütz in 1850; Bismarck, Prussia's envoy to the Diet from 1851 to 1859, had learned that Austria would not treat Prussia as an equal. Once he became Minister-President in 1862 he aimed to push Austria out of German affairs.
- **The duchies.** After the war against Denmark, the Treaty of Vienna (30 October 1864) gave Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg to Austria and Prussia jointly. The Convention of Gastein (14 August 1865) divided the administration (Prussia took Schleswig, Austria Holstein) but left the question open; Holstein was surrounded by Prussian land, so Prussia could always interfere. On 1 June 1866 Austria referred the question to the Diet, which Bismarck called a breach of Gastein; Prussian troops entered Holstein.
- **Bismarck's preparation.** He secured the goodwill of Napoleon III at Biarritz (October 1865) and signed an alliance with Italy on 8 April 1866 (Italy would join if Prussia went to war within three months, which also pushed Bismarck to act). On 9 April he proposed to the Diet a national parliament elected by universal manhood suffrage, to embarrass Austria and divide his liberal opponents.
- **Austria's mistakes.** The Diet voted on 14 June 1866 for mobilisation against Prussia; Bismarck declared the Confederation dissolved and war began. Austria had let the war come on his terms.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–2).** Describes the topic without causation ("Prussia beat Austria at Königgrätz in 1866").
- **Level 2 (3–5).** Addresses causation but mainly describes: it narrates the Danish war and the quarrel over the duchies; one reason is named with little support.
- **Level 3 (6–8).** Explains one or two factors with relevant information, for example the duchies and Bismarck's aim, each supported with dates and detail.
- **Level 4 (9–10).** Connects the factors to reach a reasoned conclusion: the long rivalry made a clash likely, the duchies supplied the occasion, Bismarck's diplomacy made a war winnable and gave him a pretext, and Austria's appeal to the Diet let him blame her. The conclusion says which cause mattered most or how they combined.

*A Level 4 answer.* "War broke out because Prussia and Austria were rivals for leadership of Germany and because Bismarck wanted a war on favourable terms. The rivalry was old: Austria presided over the Confederation, but Prussia led the Zollverein, which excluded Austria, and Bismarck, who had represented Prussia at Frankfurt, believed that Austria would never accept Prussian equality. The duchies gave the rivalry a quarrel. Denmark had given up Schleswig and Holstein to both powers in 1864, and the Convention of Gastein in August 1865 shared them out without settling who owned them; Bismarck called it papering over the cracks. In 1866 he made sure that the war would be fought on his terms: Napoleon III's goodwill at Biarritz, the alliance with Italy on 8 April, and a proposal for a parliament elected by universal manhood suffrage, which wrong-footed Austria in front of German opinion. Austria then made his task easy by taking the duchies to the Diet on 1 June, which he called a breach of Gastein, and by voting for mobilisation on 14 June. The deepest cause was the rivalry, but Bismarck's diplomacy and Austria's mistakes decided that it ended in war in 1866 rather than later."

**Part (b) [20]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO2).**

*Support: liberalism as the main threat.* The Confederation's own reaction shows what its rulers feared. The Burschenschaften (1815) and the Wartburg Festival (18 October 1817) were liberal and national in aim; after the murder of the writer Kotzebue (23 March 1819) the Diet approved the Carlsbad Decrees (20 September 1819), which set curators over the universities, imposed press censorship and created an investigation commission. Liberal constitutions existed in Baden and Bavaria (1818), Württemberg (1819) and Hesse-Darmstadt (1820), where liberal deputies challenged their rulers over budgets and rights. After the 1830 revolution in Paris, new constitutions followed in Brunswick, Saxony, Hesse-Kassel and Hanover (1831–33), the Hambach Festival (27 May 1832, 20,000 to 30,000 people) demanded a free and united Germany, and the Diet answered with the Six and Ten Articles (June and July 1832), the investigation office in Frankfurt (1833, after the failed attack on the Frankfurt guardhouse) and a ban on Young Germany (1835). Liberalism also gave nationalism its programme: a constitution and a parliament for a united Germany.

*Challenge: other threats.* **Nationalism** can be separated from liberalism: the Rhine crisis of 1840 produced "The Watch on the Rhine" and "The German Rhine", the "Song of the Germans" (1841) put the nation above the states, and the Schleswig-Holstein question (1846) united Germans against Denmark; these movements threatened a Confederation of sovereign princes as much as constitutional demands did. **Economic change** did more to weaken Austria's position than any liberal movement: the Zollverein (18 states and about 23 million people by 1834) tied the states to Prussia, railways (the first line opened in 1835) knitted the market together, and the middle class grew. The **structure** of the Confederation was a weakness in itself: the unanimity rule made change almost impossible, and the rivalry of Austria and Prussia meant that neither would let the other lead. The **crisis of 1846–47** (potato blight and harvest failures, high food prices and unemployment) turned a movement of educated men into a mass revolt in March 1848.

*Judgement.* **Level 5** (17–20) defines "threat" (to the survival of the Confederation and the rule of the princes) and compares the sources of danger on that test. Liberalism was the threat the rulers saw and the one they suppressed most successfully: between 1819 and 1847 the Carlsbad Decrees and later measures kept it in check, since the liberals were few, divided between moderates and radicals, and mostly wanted reform by agreement. The threats that proved more lasting were economic (the Zollverein shifted power towards Prussia), national (it gave the liberals their aim) and, in 1848, social (distress gave them a mass following). A strong answer concludes, for example, that liberalism was the main ideological threat before 1840 but that it became dangerous only when joined to nationalism and to economic crisis, so the claim is partly true. A **Level 3** answer describes liberalism in detail (Wartburg, Carlsbad, Hambach) and mentions other factors without weighing them; a **Level 2** answer lists events.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–4).** General information about Germany before 1848, with little link to the question.
- **Level 2 (5–8).** Describes liberal and national events with few explicit links to "threat".
- **Level 3 (9–12).** Explains one perspective (for example, how liberalism threatened the Confederation, with evidence); alternatives are mentioned rather than explained.
- **Level 4 (13–16).** Explains liberalism and at least one alternative (nationalism, the economy), with mostly relevant detail; the conclusion may lack a clear comparison.
- **Level 5 (17–20).** A comparative judgement based on analysis of the alternatives: well-focused, consistently supported by detail, with a clear comparison of the strength of the perspectives.

*A Level 5 conclusion.* "Liberalism was the threat that the princes feared and the one they fought hardest, and the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819 and the Six Articles of 1832 show it. But a threat that can be put down by decrees for nearly thirty years cannot have been the main one. What the decrees could not stop was the Zollverein, which by 1834 had made Prussia the head of an economic union that Austria could not join, or the national feeling, shown in the Rhine crisis of 1840, which gave German liberals an aim that the princes could not forbid. Liberalism became dangerous only in 1848, and then it was the economic crisis of 1846–47, not the liberals' arguments, that put crowds in the streets. So liberalism was the main threat in the sense of the main enemy, but it needed nationalism and economic change to threaten the Confederation's survival."

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.eu.russia.4) **Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924.**

(a) Explain why the Bolsheviks introduced the New Economic Policy in 1921. [10]

(b) "Nicholas II's weaknesses as a ruler were the main reason why the Tsarist regime collapsed in February 1917." How far do you agree with this view? [20]

A: **Mark scheme, Question 2.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response. Dates are Old Style unless marked.

**Part (a) [10]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO2).**

- **The collapse of the economy under War Communism.** The state had requisitioned grain (prodrazverstka, formalised in January 1919), nationalised industry and banned private trade to feed the Red Army. Peasants sowed less when the state took what they grew: the grain harvest fell from about 80 million tonnes in 1913 to less than 50 million in 1920. Industry and transport were in ruins.
- **Peasant and worker unrest.** Peasants rose against the requisitions in 1920–21 (in Tambov province and in Siberia); strikes broke out in Petrograd in February 1921 as rations were cut and factories closed for lack of fuel.
- **Kronstadt.** The sailors of the Kronstadt naval base, once among the Bolsheviks' strongest supporters, rose from 28 February 1921 demanding free elections to the soviets and an end to War Communism. The Red Army stormed the fortress across the ice on 16–18 March. The rising showed that the regime had lost the loyalty of the people who had made the revolution.
- **The Tenth Party Congress (8–16 March 1921).** Lenin proposed replacing requisitioning with a tax in kind; the Congress agreed on 15 March and a decree followed on 21 March. The famine of 1921–22 (drought on top of requisitioning and war; estimates of 3 million to 5 million dead) added to the pressure. Lenin called the policy a "retreat" to save the regime: free trade in grain, small private business and a stable currency, while the state kept the "commanding heights" of the economy and the party its monopoly of power.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–2).** Describes NEP or War Communism with no reference to causation.
- **Level 2 (3–5).** Addresses causation but mostly describes ("there was a famine and sailors rebelled"); little explanation of how these led to the policy.
- **Level 3 (6–8).** Explains one or two factors (for example the peasants' resistance, or Kronstadt) with relevant detail.
- **Level 4 (9–10).** Connects the factors: War Communism caused the economic collapse and peasant resistance; the unrest reached the cities and the navy; Kronstadt convinced Lenin that the regime's survival, not the principle of requisitioning, was at stake; the tax in kind was a retreat to save power.

*A Level 4 answer.* "The Bolsheviks introduced the New Economic Policy because War Communism had brought them to the edge of losing power. Requisitioning had kept the Red Army fed during the Civil War, but it destroyed the incentive to grow food: the harvest fell from about 80 million tonnes in 1913 to under 50 million in 1920, and peasants rose in Tambov and Siberia. By the winter of 1920–21 there were strikes in Petrograd, and at the end of February the sailors at Kronstadt, who had been among the revolution's strongest supporters, demanded free elections to the soviets and an end to requisitioning. The rising was crushed on 18 March, but it persuaded Lenin that force alone could not hold the peasants, who were most of the country. He had already asked the Tenth Party Congress, meeting from 8 March, to replace requisitioning by a tax in kind, and it agreed on 15 March. The famine of 1921–22 then showed how much was at stake. So the policy was a political retreat to save the regime, made when economic failure had turned into open revolt; Kronstadt did not cause it, but it decided that it could not wait."

**Part (b) [20]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO2).**

*Support: the Tsar's weaknesses.* In August 1915 Nicholas II dismissed his cousin, the Grand Duke Nicholas, and took command of the army himself, so that every defeat after that was his; he left Petrograd to the Tsarina Alexandra, who was distrusted as a German-born princess and who chose ministers with the advice of Rasputin. Between the start of 1916 and the revolution there were four Prime Ministers (Goremykin, Stürmer, Trepov and Golitsyn), the "ministerial leapfrog" that made government seem incompetent. He refused the Progressive Bloc's request in August 1915 for a "ministry of public confidence" and suspended the Duma on 3 September, pushing the property owners and the middle class into opposition. The murder of Rasputin (16–17 December 1916) by members of the elite showed that even they had lost faith. In February 1917 he was at headquarters at Mogilev, took the strikes for a riot (the Tsarina called it a "hooligan movement"), ordered the commander of the Petrograd garrison to end the disorders by force, and did not understand the crisis until the generals told him to abdicate (1–2 March).

*Challenge: other causes.* **The war** was a burden that no regime had managed: Tannenberg (26–30 August 1914), the Great Retreat of 1915, about 15 million men taken from the villages, and losses of millions killed, wounded and captured by the end of 1916. **Economic breakdown**: the government paid for the war by printing money (the money supply roughly quintupled between 1914 and 1917), so that prices outran wages; the railways, choked with military traffic and cold weather, failed to bring grain to the cities, and bread queues in the winter of 1916–17 brought women textile workers onto the streets on 23 February. **Long-term weakness**: the autocracy had been reformed so little after 1905 (the Fundamental Laws of 1906 kept the Tsar's "supreme autocratic power") that the regime had no way to absorb the shock. **The collapse of loyalty**: the Petrograd garrison mutinied on 27 February, and the army's leaders, General Ruzsky and General Alekseev with the front commanders, advised him to go. **No revolutionary plan**: the socialist parties were surprised, so the revolution came from below, from hunger.

*Judgement.* **Level 5** (17–20) tests the claim by asking what would have happened with a stronger ruler. The war and the economy were necessary causes: without defeat, inflation and bread shortages there would have been no crisis. But the regime fell because nobody who might have defended it (the Duma, the elite, the generals, the garrison) was willing to do so, and the Tsar's decisions had lost him each of them: the command of the army in 1915, the quarrel with the Duma, the Tsarina's government. A strong answer concludes, for example, that the war created the crisis and the Tsar's weaknesses decided that it became a revolution, so the claim is largely, though not wholly, true. A **Level 3** answer describes the Tsar's failings, or the war, in detail and refers to the rest without comparing; a **Level 2** answer narrates events from 1914 to March 1917.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–4).** General information about Russia in 1917 loosely linked to the question.
- **Level 2 (5–8).** Descriptions of the Tsar, the war or the shortages, with few explicit links to "main reason".
- **Level 3 (9–12).** Explains one perspective (the Tsar's weaknesses, or the war) with relevant detail; other factors are described rather than explained.
- **Level 4 (13–16).** Explains the Tsar's part and at least one alternative with mostly relevant detail; the conclusion may lack a clear comparison.
- **Level 5 (17–20).** A comparative judgement based on analysis of the alternatives, consistently supported by detail, with a clear comparison.

*A Level 5 conclusion.* "The war and the economy produced the crisis, but the Tsar's weaknesses explain why the crisis ended the monarchy. Other rulers fought the war and survived hunger: what was fatal in 1917 was that, by February, the Duma, the nobles, the generals and the Petrograd garrison had each been alienated by Nicholas himself, by taking command of the army in 1915, by suspending the Duma, and by leaving the government to the Tsarina, Rasputin and a series of ministers. When the bread strikes began on 23 February he was far away and thought it a riot, and when the army chiefs told him on 1 March that only abdication could save the war effort, no one was left to defend him. The war was a necessary cause; the Tsar's weaknesses were the cause without which it would probably not have been enough."

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.us.civilwar.4) **The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77.**

(a) Explain why Reconstruction came to an end in 1877. [10]

(b) To what extent did the Emancipation Proclamation change the nature of the Civil War? [20]

A: **Mark scheme, Question 3.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [10]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO2).**

- **Southern white resistance and the Redeemers.** The Ku Klux Klan (1866–72), then the White League and the Red Shirts and the "Mississippi Plan" of 1875, used violence and intimidation to win elections; the Democrats ("Redeemers") retook the Southern states one by one between 1869 and 1877. At Colfax, Louisiana (13 April 1873) a white militia killed dozens of Black men (estimates of the dead range from about 60 to 150); at Hamburg, South Carolina (July 1876) Black militiamen were killed after surrendering.
- **Northern fatigue and other priorities.** Many Northerners felt the war's aims had been achieved by 1870, were tired of intervention and had never believed in Black equality. The Panic of 1873 (from 18 September) turned attention to the economy; scandals such as Crédit Mobilier damaged Grant and the Republican Party's reputation, and gave credibility to the claim that Republican governments were corrupt; the Democrats won control of the House in 1874. In 1875 Grant refused the Republican governor of Mississippi, Adelbert Ames, troops to stop intimidation, after his Attorney General said the public was tired of the "autumnal outbreaks".
- **The courts.** The *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) and *United States v. Cruikshank* (1876) limited the federal government's power to protect individuals.
- **The disputed election of 1876 and the Compromise of 1877.** Tilden led in the popular vote and in the electoral college by 184 to 165, with 20 votes disputed in Florida, Louisiana and South Carolina. An Electoral Commission voted 8–7 along party lines for Hayes. The understanding reached in the negotiations (never written down as one agreement) was that Southern Democrats would accept Hayes if federal troops left the South; Hayes withdrew them from Louisiana and South Carolina in April 1877, and the last Republican state governments fell.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–2).** Describes Reconstruction in general, with no causation ("the South was rebuilt after the war").
- **Level 2 (3–5).** Addresses causation but mainly narrates ("Hayes became President and withdrew the troops").
- **Level 3 (6–8).** Explains one or two factors with relevant detail, for example the Compromise of 1877 or Southern violence.
- **Level 4 (9–10).** Connects the factors: violence showed that Reconstruction could be undone; Northern fatigue, scandal and the Panic of 1873 meant that the North would not pay the price of stopping it; the courts removed legal tools; the Compromise of 1877 only confirmed what had already happened. The conclusion says which cause mattered most.

*A Level 4 answer.* "Reconstruction ended in 1877 because the white South had already won it back and the North was no longer willing to stop it. From 1869 the Redeemers retook the Southern states one by one, using violence and intimidation (the White League, the Red Shirts, the Mississippi Plan of 1875) to win elections. Many Northerners had never believed in Black equality, felt that the war's aims were achieved, and were tired of intervention; the Panic of 1873 and scandals such as Crédit Mobilier made Republicans seem corrupt, and the Democrats won the House in 1874. The Supreme Court narrowed the federal government's power in the Slaughter-House Cases and in Cruikshank. The turning point came in 1875, when Grant refused Governor Ames troops in Mississippi, which told the South that violence would not be punished. The disputed election of 1876 and the Compromise of 1877, by which Hayes withdrew the troops from Louisiana and South Carolina, gave the end a date, but it confirmed what Southern violence and Northern fatigue had already done."

**Part (b) [20]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO2).**

*Support: a change in the nature of the war.* **From a war for the Union to a war for freedom.** In August 1862 Lincoln told Horace Greeley that his "paramount object" was to save the Union and not "either to save or to destroy slavery"; the preliminary Emancipation Proclamation (22 September 1862, after Antietam) and the Proclamation itself (1 January 1863) made the end of slavery a war aim, and at Gettysburg (19 November 1863) Lincoln described a nation "conceived in Liberty". **Black soldiers.** About 180,000 Black men served in the Union army (the United States Colored Troops) and about 20,000 in the navy, a major addition to Union numbers in 1864–65. **The Confederacy's labour.** Enslaved people ran to Union lines as armies advanced, and slavery, which supplied the Confederate economy with labour, was now a target; the Proclamation made a compromise peace that kept slavery almost impossible. **Foreign policy.** After 1 January 1863 it was harder for Britain and France to recognise a Confederacy fighting to keep slavery.

*Challenge: limits and other changes.* **Its limits.** It applied only to areas in rebellion, left slavery legal in the loyal Border States and in the exempted areas, and freed no one where Union armies did not reach; the 13th Amendment (ratified December 1865) ended slavery. It was justified as a war measure, an act of the commander-in-chief. **Northern opposition**: the Democrats gained heavily in the autumn 1862 elections and the draft riots of July 1863 had an anti-Black element, so for many the war's purpose remained the Union. **Other changes** made the war harder and more total: the Confiscation Acts (August 1861 and July 1862) had already treated enslaved people as a resource; conscription (the Confederacy in April 1862, the Union in March 1863), taxation and paper money; the growth of the federal government; and the hard war of Grant and Sherman in 1864, which was a military decision, not a result of the Proclamation. The war had also changed in character before the Proclamation: Antietam (17 September 1862, about 23,000 casualties in a day) and the failure of the early hope of a quick, limited campaign.

*Judgement.* **Level 5** (17–20) separates the aspects of "nature": purpose, who fought, and how it was fought. The Proclamation changed the purpose decisively (it made the war a war to end slavery, which made compromise impossible) and the make-up of the Union army; it changed the conduct of the war much less, since the move to total war came from the failure of limited war and from Grant and Sherman. It also changed it unevenly: freedom came as Union armies advanced. A strong answer concludes, for example, that it changed what the war was about and who fought it, but not how it was fought. A **Level 3** answer explains one side (what the Proclamation did) in detail; a **Level 2** answer describes the Proclamation and the war.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–4).** General information about the Civil War loosely linked to the question.
- **Level 2 (5–8).** Describes the Proclamation and the war, with few explicit links to "nature of the war".
- **Level 3 (9–12).** Explains one perspective (what the Proclamation changed, or what it did not) with relevant detail; others are described.
- **Level 4 (13–16).** Explains both change and limits, with mostly relevant detail; the conclusion may lack a clear comparison.
- **Level 5 (17–20).** A comparative judgement on the extent of change, separating purpose, participants and conduct, consistently supported by detail.

*A Level 5 conclusion.* "The Emancipation Proclamation changed what the war was for and who fought it, but not mainly how it was fought. Before it Lincoln could say that his 'paramount object' was the Union; afterwards slavery was a target of Union policy, a Confederacy that fought for it could not be recognised by Britain and France, and about 180,000 Black men joined the army. It freed no one in the Border States and freed people elsewhere only as the army arrived, and the harder war of 1864 came from Grant and Sherman and from the failure of limited war. So the change was real but selective: it transformed the purpose and the manpower of the Union war effort, which is what makes it a turning point, while the conduct of the war was changing for other reasons."

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.us.depression.4) **The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41.**

(a) Explain why many Americans did not share in the prosperity of the 1920s. [10]

(b) To what extent did the New Deal change the role of the federal government in American life between 1933 and 1941? [20]

A: **Mark scheme, Question 4.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [10]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO2).**

- **Farmers.** The war had encouraged farmers to plough more land and borrow; after it, European farms recovered and prices collapsed: between 1920 and 1921 the wholesale prices of farm products fell by about 53 per cent. Debts fixed in dollars became harder to pay, foreclosures stayed high all decade, and high tariffs (the Fordney–McCumber Tariff of 1922) hurt exports because other countries could not sell to the USA and so could not buy American wheat and cotton. The McNary–Haugen bills for government purchase of surpluses were passed by Congress in 1927 and 1928 but vetoed by Coolidge. About a quarter of Americans still lived on farms in 1929.
- **Wages and unions.** Output per worker grew much faster than pay: one study finds that real weekly earnings rose by about 5 per cent for skilled and semi-skilled men, and about 9 per cent for unskilled men, between 1923 and 1929. Trade unions were weak (employers broke strikes, offered company unions and "welfare capitalism", and the courts were hostile).
- **The distribution of wealth.** The richest 1 per cent of Americans took about 19.6 per cent of all income in 1928 (excluding capital gains; Piketty and Saez). Tax cuts under Mellon lowered the top rate of income tax from 73 per cent in 1921 to 25 per cent in 1925 and left more money in rich hands.
- **Particular groups and places.** Black sharecroppers in the South, miners in West Virginia and workers in the older industries had little share in the consumer boom (cars, radios, electric appliances, hire purchase), which was largely an urban and middle-class affair.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–2).** Describes the 1920s boom, with no causation ("some people were rich and some were poor").
- **Level 2 (3–5).** Addresses causation but mainly describes, naming groups who were poor (farmers, Black Americans) without explaining why.
- **Level 3 (6–8).** Explains one or two factors with relevant information, for example the farm depression, with figures.
- **Level 4 (9–10).** Connects the factors: government policy (tax cuts, tariffs, no help for farmers, weak unions) and the way new industries gave jobs and pay to some and not others, so that wealth was concentrated and large groups were left out. The conclusion says why the boom was narrower than it seemed.

*A Level 4 answer.* "Many Americans missed the prosperity of the 1920s because the boom rested on policies and industries that helped some groups and left others behind. Farmers did worst: after the war prices collapsed (farm prices fell by about 53 per cent between 1920 and 1921), their debts stayed fixed in dollars, and the high tariff of 1922 cost them export markets; when Congress passed the McNary–Haugen bills in 1927 and 1928, Coolidge vetoed them. Industrial workers did better, but their pay rose by only 5 to 9 per cent between 1923 and 1929 while output per worker grew faster, because unions were weak and the courts and employers were against them. At the top, the richest 1 per cent took about a fifth of all income by 1928, helped by tax cuts that took the top rate from 73 per cent to 25 per cent in four years. The consumer boom in cars, radios and appliances was largely a middle-class and urban one; a Black sharecropper in Mississippi or a miner in West Virginia could not buy them. So government policy, weak labour and the geography of the new industries together made the prosperity narrow."

**Part (b) [20]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO2).**

*Change: a larger role.* **Banking and finance.** The Emergency Banking Act (9 March 1933) and the Banking Act of June 1933 (which created federal deposit insurance) and the Securities Exchange Act of 6 June 1934 (which created the Securities and Exchange Commission) made the federal government a regulator of banks and the stock market. **Relief and jobs.** The federal government took on responsibility for relief: the CCC (31 March 1933), the CWA and FERA, and then the WPA (1935), which employed about 8.5 million people between 1935 and 1943. **Welfare.** The Social Security Act (14 August 1935) created national old-age insurance and a federal-state system of unemployment insurance and aid to dependent children, "the permanent American welfare state in outline". **Labour and the economy.** The Wagner Act (5 July 1935) gave workers the right to organise and set up the National Labor Relations Board; the Fair Labor Standards Act (25 June 1938) set a minimum wage of 25 cents an hour and a maximum week; the TVA (18 May 1933) and the AAA showed federal intervention in regions and farming. **The Constitution.** In 1937 the Supreme Court upheld the Wagner Act (*NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin*, 12 April) and a minimum wage (*West Coast Hotel v. Parrish*, 29 March) and in 1941 the Fair Labor Standards Act (*United States v. Darby*), giving the commerce clause a broad meaning.

*Limits and continuity.* The New Deal did not end the Depression: unemployment, 24.9 per cent in 1933 on Lebergott's figures, was still 14.6 per cent in 1940 and fell to 9.9 per cent only in 1941, as defence spending grew; the recession of 1937–38 showed how fragile recovery was. The Court struck down the NRA (*Schechter*, 1935) and the first AAA (1936) and many measures were temporary. The role of the states remained: Social Security's unemployment insurance was run by the states, and the Act left out many agricultural and domestic workers, who included many Black Americans; national health insurance was dropped. Roosevelt remained cautious about spending (the cuts of 1937 helped to cause the recession), and after 1938 the pace of reform slowed. Business and Southern Democrats limited the reach of reform. The wartime economy, not the New Deal, brought full employment.

*Judgement.* **Level 5** (17–20) sets a test: what the government was expected to do (responsibilities), what it did in practice (programmes and spending) and what stayed. On the first, the change was large and lasting: the federal government became responsible for relief, for bank deposits, for the right to bargain collectively and for old-age pensions, and the Court's decisions of 1937–41 removed the constitutional barrier. On the second, the change was more limited, since the programmes were partial and some ended; and the Depression did not end until the war. A strong answer concludes that the New Deal changed the role of the federal government more than it changed the American economy: it set up the principle of federal responsibility and the agencies to carry it out, but left much to the states and to business. A **Level 3** answer lists the New Deal's laws and agencies without judging how far they changed the role of government; a **Level 2** answer describes the New Deal.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–4).** General information about Roosevelt and the Depression loosely linked to the question.
- **Level 2 (5–8).** Describes New Deal programmes, with few explicit links to the "role of the federal government".
- **Level 3 (9–12).** Explains one perspective (what changed, or what did not) with relevant detail; the other is described.
- **Level 4 (13–16).** Explains change and limits with mostly relevant detail; the conclusion may lack a clear comparison.
- **Level 5 (17–20).** A comparative judgement on the extent of change, using a clear test, consistently supported by detail.

*A Level 5 conclusion.* "The New Deal changed the role of the federal government more than it changed the American economy. After 1933 the federal government insured bank deposits, regulated the stock market, paid millions to work on public projects, guaranteed the right to join a union and, in the Social Security Act of 1935, took on responsibility for the old and the unemployed; and the Supreme Court's decisions of 1937 and 1941 gave the commerce clause a width that made such laws safe. Against this, unemployment was still 14.6 per cent in 1940 and fell only with the war, Social Security left out many farm and domestic workers and left unemployment insurance to the states, and Roosevelt cut spending in 1937 with disastrous results. But what the government was expected to do, as distinct from what it achieved, was permanently changed, and that is the sense in which the change was fundamental."

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.int.1919.4) **International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation.**

(a) Explain why the Geneva Protocol of 1924 was never put into effect. [10]

(b) "The improvement in international relations in the years 1924–29 depended more on the USA than on the European powers." How far do you agree with this view? [20]

A: **Mark scheme, Question 5.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [10]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO2).**

- **What the Protocol was.** The League Assembly adopted it on 2 October 1924, on the initiative of the French and British prime ministers (Herriot and MacDonald). It had three parts: compulsory arbitration of disputes; a definition of the aggressor (a state that refused arbitration or went to war), against which the League could apply economic and military sanctions; and a disarmament conference, to be held in 1925, without whose success the Protocol would not come into force.
- **The change of British government.** MacDonald's Labour government supported it in principle, but it fell in November 1924. Baldwin's Conservative government, with Austen Chamberlain as Foreign Secretary, would not accept it, and on 12 March 1925 Chamberlain told the League Council that Britain would not ratify. Without Britain, the leading naval power and a permanent member of the Council, it could not work.
- **Britain's reasons.** The Protocol would commit the Royal Navy to enforcing sanctions anywhere in the world, perhaps against the USA, which was outside the League and rejected any restriction of its trade at sea; the dominions (Canada, Australia and others) did not want to be drawn into European quarrels; and Chamberlain preferred specific, limited agreements to a general promise.
- **Other opposition.** The USA disapproved, and Germany, not yet a League member, disliked it because it seemed to freeze the post-war frontiers. Its consequence was Locarno: Britain would guarantee one frontier where its interest was clear, not give a general promise.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–2).** Describes the League or the Protocol with no causation.
- **Level 2 (3–5).** Addresses causation but mainly describes ("it was rejected by Britain in 1925").
- **Level 3 (6–8).** Explains one or two factors, for example Britain's fear of naval commitments, with relevant detail.
- **Level 4 (9–10).** Connects the factors: the Protocol depended on Britain; the change of government brought a government that preferred limited commitments; its reasons (the navy, the USA, the dominions) all came from Britain's global position; and the other opponents made it harder to rescue.

*A Level 4 answer.* "The Geneva Protocol was never put into effect because Britain would not ratify it, and Britain's refusal followed from the change of government and from Britain's position in the world. The Protocol, adopted by the Assembly on 2 October 1924, offered France the automatic security it wanted: compulsory arbitration, a definition of the aggressor, and sanctions against him. MacDonald's Labour government had supported it, but it fell in November 1924, and Baldwin's government, with Austen Chamberlain as Foreign Secretary, objected that it would commit the Royal Navy to enforcing sanctions anywhere in the world, perhaps against the USA, which was outside the League; the dominions did not want to be dragged into European quarrels; and Chamberlain preferred a limited commitment. On 12 March 1925 he told the Council that Britain would not ratify. Without Britain the League's sanctions could not work, and the USA and Germany were also opposed. The effect was to turn security towards Locarno, where Britain would guarantee one frontier instead of making a general promise."

**Part (b) [20]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO2).**

*Support: the USA.* **The Dawes Plan** (committee chaired by the American banker Charles Dawes; report 9 April 1924; in force 1 September 1924) rescheduled reparations (1 billion marks in the first year, rising to 2.5 billion by 1928–29), reorganised the Reichsbank, and gave Germany a foreign loan of 800 million gold marks, about half raised on the American market. **American loans** poured into Germany after 1924, roughly 25 billion marks in 1924–29 (estimates run higher) against about 10 billion marks of reparations paid, creating a circle of payments: American loans to Germany, German reparations to Britain and France, and British and French war debts to the USA. The Agent-General for Reparations was an American, Seymour Parker Gilbert. The **Young Plan** (Owen Young; report 7 June 1929) cut and fixed the German burden and ended the occupation of the Rhineland (completed 30 June 1930). The **Kellogg–Briand Pact** (27 August 1928, fifteen states at first) was the widest commitment against war, and Kellogg turned Briand's offer into a pact open to every country.

*Challenge: the European powers.* **Stresemann** ended passive resistance in the Ruhr (26 September 1923), chose "fulfilment" over confrontation, and was the key figure in Locarno (October 1925; the treaties signed in London on 1 December 1925) and in Germany's entry to the League (1926). **France**: Herriot's government accepted the Dawes Plan and promised to leave the Ruhr (it ended in 1925), and Briand worked with Stresemann for conciliation. **Britain**: MacDonald and then Austen Chamberlain brokered Locarno, with Britain and Italy as guarantors, and MacDonald hosted the London Conference of July and August 1924 that put the Dawes Plan into effect. The political and security agreements of the period were European: Locarno, the League's admission of Germany and the Franco-German reconciliation. The Kellogg–Briand Pact had no machinery to enforce it, and the USA refused to join the League or to cancel war debts, so its part was financial and moral, not political. Locarno was a settlement of European frontiers made by Europeans.

*Judgement.* **Level 5** (17–20) distinguishes economic from political improvement and tests both. The USA made the economic basis possible: without the Dawes loan and American capital, German recovery and the end of the quarrel over reparations are hard to imagine, and the Ruhr crisis of 1923 showed what happened without them. But the political improvement needed Europeans to take decisions that Americans could not take for them: Stresemann's policy of fulfilment, Herriot's concession on the Ruhr and the guarantees at Locarno. A strong answer also sees the weakness of the American part: it depended on private loans that could be recalled, and the improvement was fragile once the Wall Street Crash (October 1929) ended them. So the claim is half right: the USA was more important for recovery, the Europeans for cooperation. A **Level 3** answer explains the American role in detail and describes the European one; a **Level 2** answer lists the agreements of 1924–29.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–4).** General information about the 1920s loosely linked to the question.
- **Level 2 (5–8).** Describes the Dawes Plan, Locarno and the Kellogg–Briand Pact with few explicit links to the question.
- **Level 3 (9–12).** Explains one perspective (the American role, or the European one) with relevant detail; the other is described.
- **Level 4 (13–16).** Explains both the American and the European contribution with mostly relevant detail; the conclusion may lack a clear comparison.
- **Level 5 (17–20).** A comparative judgement on the relative importance of the USA and the Europeans, based on a clear test and consistently supported.

*A Level 5 conclusion.* "The improvement after 1924 depended more on the USA for its money and more on the Europeans for its politics. American capital paid for the recovery: the Dawes Plan's loan of 800 million gold marks and the roughly 25 billion marks that followed made it possible for Germany to pay reparations while rebuilding, and so for Britain and France to repay their war debts. But the agreements that made Europe feel safer, the end of the Ruhr occupation, Locarno and Germany's entry to the League, were made by Stresemann, Briand, Chamberlain and Herriot, and no American was at Locarno. The American share was also the most fragile: it rested on short-term loans, which stopped after the Crash. If improvement means prosperity, the USA mattered more; if it means trust between the powers, the Europeans did."

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.int.1929.2) **International history, 1929–39: the rise of extremism and the road to war.**

(a) Explain why Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931. [10]

(b) "The Spanish Civil War was a turning point in international relations in the 1930s." How far do you agree with this view? [20]

A: **Mark scheme, Question 6.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [10]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO2).**

- **The Depression.** Japan depended on foreign trade and was a crowded country with few raw materials (about 64 million people in 1930). The USA, which bought most of Japan's raw silk, stopped buying, and silk prices roughly halved between 1929 and 1931; deflation and rural poverty discredited the party governments that ran Japan in the 1920s, and the army told the public that the parties had served the rich and obeyed the Western powers (the London Naval Treaty of 1930 was a target). The prime minister was shot in November 1930.
- **Manchuria's value.** Japan held a lease on the South Manchurian Railway and a large share of the investment in the region, which the army called Japan's "lifeline": a source of coal, iron and soya, a market, a land for settlers and a buffer against the Soviet Union.
- **Chinese nationalism and weakness.** The Chinese were building their own railways and ports and boycotting Japanese goods, which threatened Japanese interests. China was divided between Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists and regional warlords and could not resist.
- **The army's initiative and a weak government.** Officers of the Kwantung Army, Japan's force in the region, planned the Mukden incident (18 September 1931), blaming Chinese saboteurs for a small explosion on the railway; the government in Tokyo could not or would not control them. The Western powers were absorbed in the Depression, and the League acted slowly (the Lytton Commission was sent in December 1931, and Manchukuo was proclaimed on 1 March 1932), so Japan had little to fear.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–2).** Describes the invasion with no causation ("Japan took Manchuria and left the League").
- **Level 2 (3–5).** Addresses causation but mainly describes, naming reasons without development ("Japan wanted raw materials").
- **Level 3 (6–8).** Explains one or two factors with relevant information, for example the Depression, or the army's view of Manchuria.
- **Level 4 (9–10).** Connects the factors: the Depression discredited the civilian government and strengthened the army's case for Manchuria; Manchuria's resources and railway interests supplied the aim; the weak government, divided China and the League's slowness supplied the opportunity. The conclusion judges which mattered most.

*A Level 4 answer.* "Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931 because the Depression strengthened the army's case, and because the army had both the aim and the opportunity. The slump hit Japan hard: the USA stopped buying silk, whose price roughly halved between 1929 and 1931, and the party governments were blamed for the hardship and for yielding to the Western powers, as in the London Naval Treaty of 1930. The army argued that Japan needed security and resources, and Manchuria, where Japan held the South Manchurian Railway and large investments, was its 'lifeline': coal, iron and soya, a market and land for settlers, and a buffer against the Soviet Union. China, divided between Chiang Kai-shek and the warlords, was weak, but its railways and boycotts threatened Japanese interests. Then the Kwantung Army staged the Mukden incident on 18 September 1931 without orders from Tokyo, and the weak government could not stop it. Since the Western powers were absorbed in the Depression, the League acted slowly. The deepest cause was the army's belief in Manchuria; the Depression gave the army a public that would listen, and the opportunity made it possible."

**Part (b) [20]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO2).**

*Support: a turning point.* **The Axis.** In October 1936, after the Spanish war began (17–18 July), Germany and Italy agreed the protocols that Mussolini named the "Rome–Berlin Axis" on 1 November; both were helping Franco, and the partnership grew from it. Germany and Japan signed the Anti-Comintern Pact on 25 November 1936 (Italy joined on 6 November 1937). **The weakness of Britain and France.** The Non-Intervention Agreement (August 1936, 27 states, with a committee in London from 9 September) was ignored by Germany, Italy and the USSR, and Britain and France did not inspect cargoes or punish breaches: the dictators learned that the democracies would not act. **Ideology.** The Soviet Union sent arms and advisers, and the Comintern organised the International Brigades (about 32,000 volunteers); the war divided opinion in Britain and France between left and right and made many conservatives fear communism more than fascism. **Testing.** The German Condor Legion (from November 1936) tested aircraft and tactics, and with Italian aircraft bombed Guernica on 26 April 1937. **The League.** The League was not even asked to act: after Abyssinia it had lost its authority.

*Challenge: not a turning point.* **Earlier turning points.** The Manchurian crisis (1931–33) had already shown that a great power could defy the League; Hitler's coming to power (January 1933) and the failure of the Disarmament Conference (1932–34) began the change in the balance; Abyssinia (1935–36) broke the Stresa Front and drove Mussolini towards Germany, and German troops entered the Rhineland on 7 March 1936, four months before the Spanish war began. Mussolini had already told the German ambassador in January 1936 that he would no longer oppose German influence in Austria. **Continuity of policy.** Britain and France had already chosen appeasement and non-intervention; Spain confirmed their policy and did not change it. **Limited effects.** The war did not decide the later crises (the Anschluss, Munich, Prague); Franco stayed out of the Second World War; and the Anti-Comintern Pact was not a result of Spain. **Other later events** such as the Anschluss (March 1938), the Munich Agreement (29–30 September 1938) and the occupation of Prague (March 1939) were more decisive in turning appeasement into war.

*Judgement.* **Level 5** (17–20) sets a test (did the war change the direction of events, or only accelerate trends already in motion?) and weighs the evidence. Spain brought Germany and Italy together and showed the dictators the democracies' reluctance, which made later gambles seem safe, but most of what it revealed was already visible after Manchuria, Abyssinia and the Rhineland. A strong answer concludes that it was a significant stage rather than a turning point: it confirmed and hastened the division of Europe into two blocs, but did not begin it. A **Level 3** answer describes the war and its international effects without comparing them to earlier events; a **Level 2** answer narrates the war.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–4).** General information about the 1930s or the Spanish war, loosely linked to the question.
- **Level 2 (5–8).** Describes the war and foreign involvement with few explicit links to "turning point".
- **Level 3 (9–12).** Explains one perspective (why it was a turning point, or why it was not) with relevant detail.
- **Level 4 (13–16).** Explains why it might be a turning point and at least one alternative (an earlier event, or a later one) with mostly relevant detail.
- **Level 5 (17–20).** A comparative judgement based on a clear test of "turning point", consistently supported, with a clear comparison of the perspectives.

*A Level 5 conclusion.* "The Spanish Civil War accelerated trends in international relations more than it turned them. It made the Rome–Berlin Axis (October to November 1936) because both dictators were helping Franco, it let Germany test the Condor Legion, and it showed that Britain and France would not act, since Non-Intervention was ignored by Germany, Italy and the USSR from the start. But the League had already failed over Manchuria and Abyssinia, Hitler had already entered the Rhineland in March 1936, and Mussolini had already begun to move towards Germany. The war was a stage in a process that the Depression and the dictators' ambitions began; the real turning points were earlier, in 1931 and 1935–36, and later, in 1938–39, when appeasement ended."

## After the paper: marking yourself

**Mark each part separately, using the levels in the mark scheme.** Part (a) is out of 10 and part (b) out of 20. First decide the level that best fits the whole answer, then place it within the level: the top mark if the answer meets the level convincingly, the middle if adequately, the lowest if it only just meets it. Do not give a mark for each fact: the real mark scheme rewards the quality of the explanation and the argument. Then enter your marks for the two questions you answered (the page adds them up). Cambridge sets its grade boundaries after every exam series, so this course gives no fixed grade for a mark: use the level of each part instead.

| Part (a) level | What it tells you | What to practise next |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (1–2) | You described the topic and did not say why | Start every paragraph with a reason ("One reason was …, because …") |
| 2 (3–5) | You named reasons or told the story, with little explanation | Take each reason and add the evidence that proves it, and the link to the outcome |
| 3 (6–8) | You explained one or two reasons well | Add a link between reasons ("this led to …", "and this made … possible") and a final sentence on which mattered most |
| 4 (9–10) | You connected the reasons and reached a conclusion | Check the time: this should take 14 minutes, no more |

| Part (b) level | What it tells you | What to practise next |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (1–4) | You wrote about the topic, not the question | Underline the key word of the question (for example "main", "threat", "change") and use it in every paragraph |
| 2 (5–8) | You described events with few links to the question | End every paragraph with a sentence that says what the evidence shows about the question |
| 3 (9–12) | You explained one side and described the other | Give the other perspective the same weight: two pieces of evidence, explained |
| 4 (13–16) | You explained both sides but did not weigh them | Write a conclusion that sets a test and says which side is stronger and why |
| 5 (17–20) | You compared the perspectives and reached a judgement | Check the detail: dates, names and numbers should prove each point |

:::warn The five mistakes that cost the most marks on this paper
1. **Telling the story in part (a).** "Explain why" asks for reasons, not an account of events. A paragraph of narrative scores at Level 2 however accurate.
2. **Writing only the case for the statement in part (b).** The mark scheme's higher levels need an alternative perspective, explained. Plan a case for and a case against before you write.
3. **Ending part (b) without a judgement.** "How far", "to what extent" and "main" all ask for a decision. A conclusion that repeats the paragraphs scores no more than the paragraphs.
4. **Spending too long on part (a).** Part (a) is worth a third of the question: 14 minutes. Time lost on it cannot be found for the argument that carries 20 marks.
5. **Detail with no link.** Facts that do not prove the point do not earn marks. After every fact ask "so what?", and say so.
:::
