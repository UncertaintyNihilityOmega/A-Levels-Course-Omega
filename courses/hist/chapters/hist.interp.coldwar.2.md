:::why
Few historical arguments have been shaped so closely by the world in which historians lived. The first accounts were written by Americans who had served in the government and who saw a dangerous Soviet Union. A generation later, in the years of the Vietnam War, young historians asked whether the United States had been the aggressor. In the 1970s the archives began to open and a more balanced picture appeared. After 1991 the archives of the Soviet Union itself were opened for a time, and new books asked again what Stalin had wanted. Each answer tells you about the question the historian was asking, and about the time and place that made that question seem important. Paper 3 asks you to read an unseen extract from this argument and say what it shows about its author's interpretation and approach. This chapter gives you the argument, school by school, and the events (from [Origins of the Cold War (1): the breakdown of the wartime alliance, 1941–49](#hist.interp.coldwar.1)) that each school uses.
:::

## Were East–West tensions bound to resurface in 1945?

The first syllabus question is about the long term. The wartime alliance was a partnership of two systems that had been enemies since 1917. When the common enemy was beaten, was a quarrel certain?

**The case for "yes".** The Bolsheviks believed that capitalism would end in revolution and war; the capitalist states believed that Bolshevism threatened their societies. In 1918–20 Britain, France, the USA and Japan sent troops into the Russian Civil War; the United States did not recognise the Soviet government until 1933; Stalin made the Nazi–Soviet Pact in 1939 and took half of Poland; Soviet distrust of the West after Munich and Western distrust of Stalin after 1939 were deep. Soviet historians of the 1950s and 1960s, and some revisionists, drew a straight line from 1918 to 1945 and blamed Western hostility. Traditionalists, and some later writers, drew a different straight line from 1917 and blamed Soviet ideology. Both start from the idea that the alliance of 1941–45 was an interruption.

**The case for "no".** The war showed that the three powers could cooperate. They fought together from 1941, shared intelligence and supplies, and agreed at Tehran, Yalta and Potsdam on how to run Germany, to set up the United Nations and to bring the Soviet Union into the war on Japan. Roosevelt believed that the USSR could be treated as a partner, and in his last message to Churchill, in April 1945, he advised him to play down the Soviet problem. Revisionists such as Fleming (1961) and Alperovitz (1965) argued that the break came after Roosevelt, when Truman changed American policy; if so, the conflict was a choice, not a fate.

**The case for "it was likely, but not fixed".** Alexis de Tocqueville had predicted in 1835 that the United States and Russia would each hold sway over half the world. The war destroyed Germany, weakened Britain and France, and left two superpowers with armies and economies far larger than anyone else's. Where Germany had been there was a power vacuum, and each side feared that the other would fill it. This is a structural argument: it does not say that Stalin or Truman wanted a Cold War, but that the system produced rivalry. Leffler and Westad lean this way, though with differences, and so do many post-revisionists.

:::key Three answers to "was a quarrel bound to come?"
- **Yes, because of ideology and history since 1917.** Used to blame the Soviet Union (the traditionalist) or the West's intervention and hostility (the Soviet historian).
- **No, cooperation was possible and was thrown away.** Used by revisionists to blame American choices after April 1945, and by some post-revisionists to say that both sides made choices.
- **Likely, because of the power vacuum, but not fated.** Used by post-revisionists and the New Cold War historians. The test: which decisions of 1945–48 could have gone differently, and what did the actors know and fear when they made them?
:::

:::def Inherent tensions
The conflicts of ideology, system and interest between the Soviet Union and the capitalist powers that existed before 1941 and that the wartime alliance covered but did not remove. The phrase is used in the syllabus; historians differ on how much weight to give them compared with the decisions of 1945–49.
:::

## The personalities of the leaders

A second group of historians asks what difference particular people made.

**Stalin.** He had ruled since the late 1920s, had killed or imprisoned millions in the 1930s and was deeply suspicious. He was also a realist, careful to avoid war with the West: he stayed out of Greece in 1944–46, withdrew from Iran in 1946 and Bornholm and northern Norway in 1945–46 and did not shoot at the Airlift. Historians read these facts in different ways. Schlesinger (1967) wrote of "the madness of Stalin" among the causes; Gaddis (1997) described a leader whose suspicion and need for control made cooperation impossible; Zubok and Pleshakov (1996) described a Marxist who saw the world through ideology and calculated power; Geoffrey Roberts (*Stalin's Wars*, 2006) argued from the Soviet archives that Stalin was a cautious pragmatist who hoped to keep cooperating with the West for some time after the war.

**Roosevelt and Truman.** Roosevelt hoped to charm Stalin and to build a world order through the United Nations; he was ill at Yalta and died on 12 April 1945. Truman, who had been Vice-President for 82 days, was inexperienced, blunt and ready to take his advisers' hard line. Revisionists argue for a sharp break in April 1945; post-revisionists such as Gaddis find more continuity, because Roosevelt had begun to harden his views on Poland before he died. Yergin (1977) described two ways of seeing the Soviet Union: the "Yalta axiom" (the Soviet Union acts from security fears and can be bargained with) and the "Riga axiom" (the Soviet Union is driven by an ideology of expansion and cannot be trusted), named after the American diplomatic post in Riga where a school of Soviet experts had trained in the 1920s and 1930s. He argued that Truman's circle moved from the first to the second between 1945 and 1947.

**Churchill and Bevin.** Churchill proposed the Percentages Agreement and the Iron Curtain speech. For the Soviet side the second showed that he was a warmonger; for the traditionalists it showed that he saw the danger sooner than others. Bevin's work for the Marshall Plan and the Brussels Treaty shows the part played by Britain in drawing the Americans into Europe.

:::key What the argument about personalities tells you
- **A "great man" explanation** (Stalin's paranoia, Truman's toughness) puts blame on a person and a choice. It is strongest for traditionalists (Stalin) and for revisionists (Truman).
- **A structural explanation** (the power vacuum, the weight of institutions) puts blame on circumstances. It is strongest for post-revisionists.
- **The test**: could a different leader have made a different decision, and did the system leave room for it? An extract that stresses personalities rarely mentions this question.
:::

## Ideology, security and economics

The syllabus names three kinds of motive that historians weigh against each other.

**Ideology.** Soviet leaders held Marxism-Leninism, which taught that capitalism produced war and that it would be replaced by socialism. American leaders held a liberal creed, of free elections, free trade and free speech, which they believed should apply everywhere. Traditionalists put ideology first on the Soviet side (Schlesinger wrote of "the intransigence of Leninist ideology, the sinister dynamics of a totalitarian society, and the madness of Stalin" as factors without which an account of the origins is "obviously incomplete"). Post-1991 writers such as Zubok and Gaddis return to Stalin's ideology as a real cause. Revisionists treat ideology as a cover for interest.

**Security.** The Soviet Union had been invaded in 1914, 1918 and 1941; Poland was the road by which Germany had come twice. It lost about 27 million people. After 1945 its leaders wanted a belt of friendly states and, because of the atomic bomb, a counterweight to American power. The United States had been attacked at Pearl Harbor in 1941; its officials had learned that air power and the atomic bomb had made distance a poor defence, and they decided that no hostile power must dominate Europe or Asia. Leffler's *A Preponderance of Power* (1992) makes this the centre of his account of American policy, and Mastny and Zubok do the same for Soviet policy.

**Economics.** The United States emerged from the war with an economy that produced roughly half of the world's manufactured goods, and its leaders believed that the 1930s slump could return unless there were markets abroad. Williams, LaFeber and Kolko (revisionists) placed this "Open Door" motive at the centre: American policy-makers wanted open markets for American trade and investment. Evidence they cite: the Bretton Woods agreements of 1944 (the Soviet Union decided in December 1945 not to join the IMF and World Bank); Will Clayton's memorandum of May 1947 warning that European collapse would hurt American exports; the terms of the Marshall Plan. The Soviet Union was ruined and wanted reparations and credit: Molotov in January 1945 asked for a 6 billion dollar American loan for reconstruction, which was not granted (historians dispute whether this was deliberate pressure or neglect and the unwillingness of Congress). Economic historians such as Alan Milward (1984) argued that European recovery was already under way and that the Plan was less decisive than American writers claimed.

:::key Ideology, security, economics: how the schools weight them
| Motive | Traditionalist | Revisionist | Post-revisionist and New Cold War |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ideology | Soviet Marxism-Leninism drove expansion | A cover for interest; American ideology of the Open Door counts | Both sides; for Stalin it shaped how he read events |
| Security | Soviet claim is a pretext; American security was threatened | Soviet security was real; American "security" was often about markets | The centre of the story: each side's search for safety made the other feel less safe |
| Economics | Marginal | The main American motive: the Open Door | An important American concern, but not the only one |
:::

## Traditionalism, 1940s–1960s

:::def Traditionalism
The interpretation of the Cold War, dominant in the West in the 1940s and 1950s, that the Soviet Union was mainly responsible: Stalin and Soviet Communism were expansionist, broke the agreements of 1945, and imposed Communist rule on Eastern Europe, and the American response (the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, NATO) was necessary and defensive. Also called "orthodox".
:::

The traditional view began with the participants. Truman's *Memoirs* (1955–56) and Dean Acheson's memoirs told the story from Washington. Kennan's Long Telegram and his "X" article (*Foreign Affairs*, July 1947) are contemporary documents rather than histories, but they shaped the view: Kennan wrote that "the main element of any United States policy toward the Soviet Union must be that of a long-term, patient but firm and vigilant containment of Russian expansive tendencies". Among historians, **William H. McNeill**, *America, Britain and Russia* (1953), and above all **Herbert Feis** (who had served in the State Department) wrote with documents from the American side: *Churchill, Roosevelt, Stalin* (1957), *Between War and Peace: The Potsdam Conference* (1960) and *From Trust to Terror* (1970). Feis showed the Western leaders wanting cooperation, Stalin breaking promises in Poland and Romania, and the West forced into resistance.

**Arthur Schlesinger Jr** wrote "The Origins of the Cold War" in *Foreign Affairs* (October 1967) in reply to the revisionists. A historian of the New Deal and an adviser to President Kennedy, he defended the main traditional case: the roots of the conflict lay in Soviet ideology and in the Soviet system, and a Western policy of firmness was the right response. But he allowed that American policy had made mistakes, and that the Cold War had become a struggle in which each side's fears fed the other's.

**What the traditionalists rest on.** The breaches of the Declaration on Liberated Europe in Romania (March 1945) and Poland (1945–47); the Iran and Turkey crises of 1946; the Czech coup of 1948; Stalin's speech of February 1946; the Berlin Blockade. **Their approach.** They focus on the Soviet side's actions and on ideology; they write as people who had lived through the Cold War and for whom the Soviet threat was real; they rely mostly on Western documents. **Their limits.** Little access to Soviet material; they treat American actions as responses and so seldom ask what American leaders wanted.

:::key The traditionalist case in four claims
1. Soviet ideology and the nature of Stalin's regime made a lasting partnership with the West unlikely.
2. Stalin broke the Yalta promises (the Declaration on Liberated Europe) and imposed Communist governments.
3. The Western response (Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, NATO) was defensive and came after Soviet moves.
4. The West should be seen as reluctant: it demobilised, offered aid to Russia too, and acted only when the danger was clear.
:::

## Revisionism, 1959–early 1970s

:::def Revisionism
The interpretation of the Cold War, developed from the late 1950s, that the United States bore the main responsibility, because of its economic expansion, its atomic monopoly and the tougher policy Truman adopted after Roosevelt, while Soviet policy was cautious and mainly defensive. Many revisionists came from the "New Left". The word revises the traditional view; in the debate on the First World War the same label meant something else.
:::

The pioneer was **William Appleman Williams**, *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959). He argued that since the 1890s American foreign policy had been guided by the **Open Door**: the belief that American prosperity and freedom depended on access to markets abroad. Its tragedy was that a nation that believed itself generous had pursued, in effect, an informal empire. After 1945 this pursuit, not Soviet aggression, set the course. **D.F. Fleming**, *The Cold War and Its Origins, 1917–1960* (1961), traced the Cold War to the Western intervention of 1918 and described Truman's reversal of Roosevelt. **Gar Alperovitz**, *Atomic Diplomacy* (1965), argued that Truman used the atomic bomb, and delayed his meeting with Stalin until it was ready, in order to strengthen the American hand in Europe. **Walter LaFeber**, *America, Russia, and the Cold War, 1945–1966* (1967), set the postwar story within the Open Door tradition. **Gabriel Kolko**, *The Politics of War* (1968), argued that American leaders aimed at a capitalist world order under American leadership, that the United States had enormous strength and that Soviet policy was conservative and cautious. The Kolkos' *The Limits of Power* (1972) carried the argument to 1954.

**What the revisionists rest on.** The atomic bomb and Potsdam; the end of Lend-Lease; the loan that was not granted; the Truman Doctrine's universal language; the Marshall Plan; the policy on Germany (the end of reparations, Bizonia, currency reform). **Their approach.** They look at American documents (the opening of the archives and the *Foreign Relations of the United States* series gave them material) and ask what the strongest power wanted; they stress economic motives and long-term patterns; they treat the Soviet Union as weaker and defensive. **Why they arose.** The Vietnam War (American troops in large numbers from 1965), the civil rights movement and the "New Left" made many young historians doubt that American motives were always benevolent. The Pentagon Papers, published in 1971, appeared to show that American policy-makers had pursued aims they did not state publicly.

**Criticisms.** Robert James Maddox, *The New Left and the Origins of the Cold War* (1973), accused revisionists of misreading or selecting documents. Other critics say that they gave too little attention to Soviet actions (Romania, Poland, Czechoslovakia), made the American economy the decisive factor when American officials spoke chiefly of security, and had no Soviet sources. The Soviet archives after 1991 showed Stalin as more active and more ideological than the revisionists had allowed.

:::key The revisionist case in four claims
1. American economic power and the Open Door tradition shaped postwar policy; American leaders wanted a world open to American trade.
2. Truman broke with Roosevelt's policy of cooperation and used the atomic bomb and economic leverage against Moscow.
3. Soviet actions in Eastern Europe were mainly defensive, a reaction to invasion and to American power; Stalin was cautious.
4. American policies (the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan, the German currency reform) made the division of Europe, which the Soviets then answered.
:::

## Post-revisionism, 1970s–1980s

:::def Post-revisionism
The interpretation, developed from the 1970s, that neither side alone was responsible: the Cold War came from the interaction of the two powers, their fears and misperceptions, their domestic politics and the power vacuum left by the war. The label covers writers who differ about which side was more responsible, and does not simply mean that "both were to blame".
:::

Three works show the variety.

**John Lewis Gaddis, *The United States and the Origins of the Cold War, 1941–1947* (1972).** Gaddis used the American archives that had recently opened. He accepted some revisionist points (the importance of American power and of domestic politics) but rejected the claim that American policy was a plan for economic domination. He stressed the limits on American leaders: the President had to answer to Congress, to the press and to a public that wanted the troops home and distrusted Soviet rule in Eastern Europe. Stalin, he argued, faced no such domestic checks, so he had more room to choose a compromise than Truman had, and in that sense Gaddis placed a larger share of responsibility on the Soviet leader even while rejecting the idea that one side alone was to blame. American officials, he argued, came to see the Soviet Union as a threat in the period 1945–47 because of what the Soviets did, and each side's acts looked more aggressive to the other than they were meant to be.

**Daniel Yergin, *Shattered Peace: The Origins of the Cold War and the National Security State* (1977).** Yergin set out the two ways of thinking about the Soviet Union, the "Yalta axiom" and the "Riga axiom" (see above), and showed how the second won in Washington. He also traced the growth of what he called the American "national security state". He did not blame the American leaders alone; he explained how they came to see a world in which every Soviet move was a threat.

**Melvyn Leffler, *A Preponderance of Power: National Security, the Truman Administration, and the Cold War* (1992).** Leffler argued that American policy was driven by officials' definition of national security. They believed that, after the war, the United States had to prevent any hostile power from controlling the industrial resources of Europe and Asia. This required a "preponderance of power": control of key regions, bases, the atomic monopoly and the rebuilding of Germany and Japan. Soviet intentions, he thought, were more cautious than American planners believed; the planners' fear of Soviet *capabilities* and of social instability in Europe drove them. Leffler is closer to the revisionists than Gaddis in blaming American overreach; he differs from them in making security, not capitalism, the motive.

**Other post-revisionist work.** Geir Lundestad's article "Empire by Invitation?" (1986) argued that the American presence in Western Europe came partly because the Europeans asked for it. Michael Hogan (*The Marshall Plan*, 1987) and Alan Milward (1984) wrote on the Plan's economics. Norman Naimark's *The Russians in Germany* (1995) used Soviet records of the occupation.

:::key Do not say "post-revisionist" means "both to blame"
Gaddis (1972) and Leffler (1992) are both called post-revisionist, but they weight things differently. **Gaddis** stresses constraints on both sides and, increasingly, Stalin's character and system. **Leffler** stresses American officials' definition of security and the structure of the postwar world. A good answer says "post-revisionist" only when the extract shows a balanced argument (both sides, or perceptions and fear) and adds *because*: "the extract says that each side's defensive acts looked aggressive to the other".
:::

## Russian and Soviet perspectives

**The official Soviet view (1940s–1980s).** Soviet histories presented the Soviet Union as the chief victor over fascism, anxious to keep the wartime cooperation, and the West as the aggressor. In their account the "imperialist circles" of the USA and Britain abandoned the principles of Yalta and Potsdam, used the atomic bomb to blackmail, imposed the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan to subordinate Europe to American capital, and set up military blocs against the socialist states. Churchill's Fulton speech was a declaration of war. The Cold War was a continuation of the intervention of 1918. This is, in its main claim, the revisionist argument in a Marxist form, though the sources and the purpose were different: the Soviet account came from a state that controlled its archives and told a story that served the government. Soviet historians could not publish a different view; some in private were more critical.

**Glasnost and the 1990s.** Under Gorbachev, from 1987, historians began to reopen subjects the state had closed, such as the secret protocols of the Nazi–Soviet Pact (declared void by the Congress of People's Deputies in December 1989) and the Katyn massacre, which Moscow admitted in 1990. After 1991 the archives of the Soviet Presidential, Foreign Ministry and Party were opened in part to researchers. Russian and Russian-born historians such as **Vladislav Zubok** and **Vladimir Pechatnov** published documents and studies. The picture that emerged did not simply confirm the old Soviet story or the Western one: Stalin's regime appeared to be driven by security fear, by ideology and by a wish to extend Soviet power.

**Today.** Since the early 2000s access to some key Russian archives has been restricted again, and public discussion in Russia of the war and of Stalin's policy has been closely linked to official views. Historians inside and outside Russia continue to work with the sources that are open, and with the archives of former Soviet-bloc states, which stayed open.

:::key How Russian and Western perspectives differ
- **Different questions.** Soviet histories asked how the West broke the alliance; Western ones asked how Stalin or the United States caused the division.
- **Different sources.** Western historians relied on American and British archives; Soviet historians on a controlled selection of Soviet documents; after 1991, historians could use both, but unevenly.
- **Different experience.** For Russians the war meant about 27 million dead and a long fear of Western encirclement; for Americans it meant Pearl Harbor and the lesson of Munich.
- **The test for an extract:** does the writer assume that the Soviet Union acted from fear and in response (a Soviet or revisionist stance), or that it acted from ideology and ambition (a traditionalist stance), or does the writer use Soviet documents to argue that it did both?
:::

## New archives and the New Cold War history

The end of the Soviet Union in 1991 gave historians access to papers they had never seen. The **Cold War International History Project**, founded in 1991 at the Woodrow Wilson International Center for Scholars in Washington, collected and translated documents from Russia, Eastern Europe and China, and made them available. The new evidence changed the debate in several ways.

:::def New Cold War history
The work, from the 1990s, that uses the archives of the former Communist states (and others) to study the Cold War from both sides and from outside the USA and USSR. It is sometimes called post-post-revisionism. It does not form a single school: some of its writers return to the traditionalist stress on Stalin's responsibility, and some do not.
:::

**Zubok and Pleshakov, *Inside the Kremlin's Cold War: From Stalin to Khrushchev* (1996).** They argued that Soviet policy came from a "revolutionary-imperial paradigm". Stalin was a revolutionary who believed that Soviet victory opened the way to world socialism, and an imperialist who wished to hold and extend the Russian empire; these two aims combined to make him seek security by expansion, which in turn made the West afraid. They showed a Kremlin less cautious than the revisionists had said, but not planning a war.

**Vojtech Mastny, *The Cold War and Soviet Insecurity: The Stalin Years* (1996).** Mastny stressed the deep insecurity of Stalin and his circle: a man who expected Western hostility, who could be cautious when he met strength, but who saw conflict as part of the Soviet system's nature.

**John Lewis Gaddis, *We Now Know: Rethinking Cold War History* (1997).** Gaddis argued that the new sources supported a view that placed more responsibility on Stalin: his personality, ideology and authoritarian rule made him need an enemy and made a stable settlement unlikely, whereas the United States was, in Lundestad's phrase, an "empire by invitation" whose allies asked it to stay. Many saw this as a return to a modified traditionalism; critics said that it made American policy too innocent and used the new documents selectively.

**Odd Arne Westad, *The Global Cold War* (2005).** Westad looked at the Cold War as a worldwide contest, especially in the Third World. He argued that the roots of the conflict lay in the ideologies of the two powers (American belief in a mission of freedom and Soviet belief in a mission of socialist progress), which each applied to the rest of the world. His emphasis is broad and long-term: not on the question "who started it in Europe" but on "what kind of conflict it was".

**Leffler, *For the Soul of Mankind* (2007), and Roberts, *Stalin's Wars* (2006).** Leffler concluded that fear and misperception on both sides had made the conflict worse, and that there were opportunities for compromise that leaders missed. Roberts, using the Soviet archives, argued that Stalin's policy was more flexible and defensive than Gaddis said.

**Anne Applebaum, *Iron Curtain: The Crushing of Eastern Europe, 1944–1956* (2012),** used Polish, Hungarian and East German archives to show how Communist control was planned and carried out, which supports the traditionalist claim that Sovietisation was deliberate, though she does not claim that this alone made the Cold War.

:::key What the archives changed
- **Stalin's role is clearer.** His ideology, his control of policy and his methods in Eastern Europe are better documented. This weakens the revisionist claim of a purely defensive Soviet Union.
- **Soviet fear is also clearer.** Novikov's telegram, Zhdanov's report and Stalin's cautious responses show fear of Western power. This weakens the traditionalist picture of a Soviet Union simply on the offensive.
- **The result is more argument, not less.** Gaddis (1997) and Roberts (2006) read the same archives and reach different conclusions about Stalin.
- **The test for an extract:** does the writer cite Soviet or Eastern European documents, or the "new evidence", to support the claim? If so, the approach is the New Cold War history.
:::

## How the time and place of writing shaped each interpretation

The syllabus asks you to see that historians' views are shaped by the time and place in which they work. Here the link is unusually clear.

| Period and place | What was happening | Effect on the historians |
|---|---|---|
| 1945–55, USA | Berlin, Korea, McCarthyism; the Soviet Union seemed a threat; historians were often former officials | Traditionalist view: Soviet responsibility; documents from the American side; little doubt about American motives |
| Late 1950s–70s, USA | The Vietnam War, civil rights, the New Left; Cuban Missile Crisis (1962); the Pentagon Papers (1971) | Revisionism: doubt about American motives; Williams, Alperovitz, LaFeber, Kolko; the Wisconsin school of Williams |
| 1970s, USA and Britain | Détente, SALT I (1972); the American and British archives opened under the thirty-year rule | Post-revisionism: more documents, less moral certainty; Gaddis 1972; Yergin 1977 |
| 1980s | The "Second Cold War" (Soviet invasion of Afghanistan 1979; Reagan); Gorbachev from 1985 | Leffler 1992 written as the Cold War ended; debate over whether to see the USSR as a threat |
| 1989–2000s | The fall of the Berlin Wall; the end of the USSR in 1991; archives open | New Cold War history: Zubok, Mastny, Gaddis 1997; a sense of knowing the end of the story |
| 2000s onwards | The wars of the 2000s; a shift to global history | Westad 2005; Leffler 2007; Applebaum 2012; attention to the Third World and to Eastern Europe |
| Soviet Union, 1945–85 | A controlled press and archives; the war as the great national achievement | Official histories: Western aggression; no space for other views |

:::warn Do not guess about motives
A historian's time and place can help to explain an approach, but you must show it from the extract, not assume it. "He wrote in the Vietnam era, so he blamed America" is a guess. "The extract says that American leaders wanted markets, which is an economic explanation typical of revisionists writing when American motives were being questioned" is an argument.
:::

## Reading an extract: signposts for each school

You cannot know which historian wrote a Paper 3 extract, but you can tell which tradition it belongs to from what it stresses and what it leaves out.

|---|---|---|
| Soviet ideology, Stalin's expansion, broken promises, a reluctant West | Soviet responsibility (traditionalist) | Romania March 1945, Poland 1945–47, Czech coup, Iran, Berlin; test with the Percentages Agreement and US economic power |

## Worked example: a full answer on a practice extract

:::eg Paper 3 [40]: What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Cold War to explain your answer.
**Practice extract M** (about 180 words)

American policy after 1945 cannot be understood as a reaction to Soviet moves. For half a century American leaders had believed that the nation's prosperity and freedom depended on open markets abroad, and by 1945 the United States was the one great power whose economy had grown during the war. Its leaders feared that without foreign markets the slump of the 1930s would return. In Eastern Europe, where the Soviet Union closed doors, and in Western Europe, where aid could be made to open them, they saw their opportunity. Policy-makers talked of the danger from Moscow, but their planning shows that their concern was the shape of the world economy. The Soviet Union, ruined by invasion, had neither the means nor the wish to challenge this. If the Cold War was a tragedy, it was a tragedy in which the stronger power chose a course that the weaker could only resist, and the roots of that choice lay in the American idea of how a prosperous world should be arranged.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of William Appleman Williams, The Tragedy of American Diplomacy (1959). The words are not Williams's.*

**Plan (read, find, test).**
- Contrast and judgement words: "cannot be understood as a reaction"; "half a century"; "feared"; "talked of … but their planning shows"; "neither the means nor the wish"; "the stronger power chose"; "the roots … lay in".
- **Main interpretation (two linked aspects):** (1) American policy after 1945 was driven by a long-standing need for open markets, not by fear of Soviet aggression; (2) the Soviet Union was weak and defensive, so the Cold War came from the American choices of the stronger power.
- Sub-messages: the 1930s slump; Eastern Europe as a closed door; Western Europe and aid; the word "tragedy".
- Approach: economic motives; continuity over fifty years; American policy-making documents and planning; an American critic of American policy; a reply to an older "reaction" view; sympathy for the weaker power.
- Knowledge to use: Bretton Woods (July 1944) and the Soviet decision not to join; Molotov's request for a 6 billion dollar loan (January 1945); Clayton's memorandum of May 1947; the Marshall Plan's conditions and the sum; the Truman Doctrine's language; Soviet actions in Romania and Poland (to test); the size of Soviet losses (27 million) and of the Red Army (to test).

**A Level 3 answer (about 20 of 40).**
"The extract says that the Soviet Union was ruined by invasion and had neither the means nor the wish to challenge the United States. So the historian thinks that the Soviet Union was innocent and did nothing wrong. This is not true because in 1945 Stalin took over Eastern Europe, including Poland and Romania, and in 1948 there was a coup in Czechoslovakia and the Berlin Blockade. The Cold War started because of Stalin."

*Commentary.* The answer picks up one sub-message (the Soviet Union's weakness) and builds a **different interpretation** (the Soviet Union was innocent) that the whole extract does not make: the extract says the stronger power *chose* a course, and the cause lay in American economic aims. The knowledge is accurate (Poland, Romania, Czechoslovakia, Berlin) but is used to argue against the extract and not to explain it. **Level 3** in Table A (10) and Table B (10), about 20 of 40.

**A Level 5 answer (about 32 of 40).**
"The main message is that the United States, not the Soviet Union, made the Cold War, because American leaders needed open markets. The historian says that 'for half a century American leaders had believed' that prosperity depended on 'open markets abroad'. This is supported by the Bretton Woods conference of 1944, which set up the International Monetary Fund and World Bank on American lines, and by the Marshall Plan, which gave about 13 billion dollars to western Europe between 1948 and 1952 and was intended, as Will Clayton wrote in 1947, to prevent a collapse of European demand for American goods. The historian says that in Eastern Europe 'the Soviet Union closed doors' and that in Western Europe 'aid could be made to open them'. This fits the way the Soviet Union rejected the Marshall Plan at Paris on 2 July 1947 and created Comecon in 1949.

The extract also says that the Soviet Union was 'ruined by invasion' and had 'neither the means nor the wish' to challenge the United States. This fits the Soviet loss of about 27 million people and the destruction of its industry. The historian's last sentence uses the word 'tragedy' to suggest that the Cold War was an outcome that no one wanted but that the stronger power could have avoided. The approach is to look at American economic motives and at long-term patterns rather than at Stalin."

*Commentary.* A detailed, accurate explanation of the first aspect (the Open Door motive) with precise knowledge, and a good start on the second (the weak USSR). But the answer does not explain how the two aspects form one argument, does not test the claims with evidence that cuts the other way, does not name the label, and gives the approach only a sentence. **Level 5** in Table A (16) and Table B (16): about 32 of 40.

**A Level 6 answer (about 37 of 40).**
"The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. Its main interpretation is that the United States, driven by a long-standing need for open markets abroad, chose the policies that made the Cold War, against a Soviet Union that was too ruined and too cautious to threaten it.

The first aspect is about American motive: 'for half a century American leaders had believed that the nation's prosperity and freedom depended on open markets abroad'. The evidence the historian could use is the Bretton Woods agreements of July 1944, which created an international economy on American terms and which the Soviet Union decided in December 1945 not to join; the American refusal to grant the 6 billion dollar loan that Molotov had sought in January 1945; Will Clayton's memorandum of May 1947 on the effect of a European collapse on American exports; and the Marshall Plan itself, about 13 billion dollars of aid tied to a revival of western European, and German, trade. The phrase 'policy-makers talked of the danger from Moscow, but their planning shows' that the real concern was economic is the historian's boldest claim and needs testing. Against it, American planners such as George Kennan, in the Long Telegram of February 1946, wrote of Soviet insecurity and ideology and not of markets, and the Truman Doctrine of March 1947 was presented in terms of free peoples threatened by armed minorities. The extract can answer that public language is not planning, but it must then show planning documents, and what it offers is a general statement.

The second aspect is about the weaker power: the Soviet Union, 'ruined by invasion', had 'neither the means nor the wish to challenge this'. The fact of ruin is not in doubt: about 27 million Soviet citizens died, and the Soviet armed forces were cut from about 11 million men to under 3 million by 1948. But the claim about 'wish' meets evidence the extract leaves out. The Groza government was imposed in Romania in March 1945 and sixteen Polish leaders were tried in Moscow in June 1945, both before the Truman Doctrine or the Marshall Plan; the Red Army remained the largest army in Europe; and Stalin used the Cominform and the Czech coup of February 1948 to impose Soviet control. These facts suggest that Soviet policy was not only a response to American pressure. The extract's silence on them is a clue to its approach.

The approach is to write the history from the point of view of American decisions. The historian asks what the strongest power wanted, finds the answer in the economic ideas of its leaders over fifty years, and treats Soviet policy as a reaction. 'Tragedy' shows the moral tone: the writer is critical of American policy, but regrets and does not condemn it. The extract belongs to the revisionist school, because it revises the orthodox view of the 1950s that the Soviet Union was the aggressor, by placing the cause in American economic policy. A writer in the late 1950s and 1960s, when many Americans began to doubt that their country's motives were always generous, would find this argument natural; but the extract itself does not say so, and the label needs only its *because*. The two aspects fit together: if American economic need was the cause, then Soviet weakness is what makes American choices responsible."

*Commentary.* The opening sentence states the **whole** interpretation and its two aspects. Each aspect is explained with the extract's own words and tested with precise knowledge (Bretton Woods, the loan, Clayton's memorandum, the Marshall Plan, the Long Telegram, Romania and Poland in 1945, the Czech coup). The approach is explained from the extract, shown to fit the interpretation, and the label "revisionist" is used with its *because*. **Level 6** in Table A (19) and Table B (18): about 37 of 40. About 700 words, which is a little long for 55 minutes: a 550-word version that keeps the opening, one test of each aspect and the approach would earn the same level.
:::

A student wrote this about Practice extract P (printed in the box under "Practice" below):

"The historian is post-revisionist, which means that both sides were equally to blame for the Cold War. Stalin took over Eastern Europe and Truman made the Truman Doctrine. The Marshall Plan was in 1947. This shows that both sides were to blame."

The extract's phrase 'not equally at every moment' shows that the historian does not mean a simple equal blame." Now the label has a *because*, the claim is not distorted, and the knowledge is matched to the extract.

:::eg Writing about the approach: three versions of one paragraph (Practice extract N)
Practice extract N is printed in the box under "Practice" below. Suppose your plan for it says: *approach: American documents, decisions of American leaders, diaries; atomic bomb as a means to influence the USSR.* Here are three ways to write it.

**A Level 3 paragraph (Table A: explains a sub-message or asserts).** "The historian was writing in 1965 so he was against America and the Vietnam War. The extract says Truman wanted to delay meeting Stalin. That shows that he was biased."

*Commentary.* "Biased" is an assertion and a verdict on the historian, which the question does not ask for. The date is a guess, since the extract does not give one. The sub-message about delaying the meeting is noticed but not explained. Level 3 at best.

**A Level 5 paragraph (Table A: sound, but does not explain the whole).** "The historian's approach is to look at the bomb as an instrument of American policy in Europe. The extract says that Truman and his advisers 'hoped it would make the Russians more manageable' and that Japan was 'already close to defeat'. It also says that the Soviets 'understood the message' and began their own bomb. The approach is therefore based on American decisions and on the idea that the bomb was used for diplomatic reasons."

*Commentary.* Accurate, close to the text and sound on the approach. But it does not say **why** the approach fits the interpretation, or what in the context shaped it, and it does not test the claim. Level 5.

**A Level 6 paragraph (Table A: complete understanding of interpretation and approach).** "The approach is to read the use of the atomic bomb as part of American planning for Europe, using the diaries and papers of American leaders. The extract says that Truman 'wanted to postpone any meeting with Stalin until the bomb was ready' and that the bomb was used 'also as a demonstration'. This fits the interpretation exactly. If the bomb was meant to impress Moscow, then American leaders began the rivalry in 1945, and the Soviet bomb programme, begun in earnest in August 1945, was a reply. The extract also shows what the historian cares about: the sequence of American decisions from the spring to the summer of 1945 (the delay to the Potsdam meeting; the test of 16 July; the notice to Stalin on 24 July; the bombing of 6 and 9 August) and not Soviet actions in Eastern Europe. This is a choice of evidence that makes the American side the active one. The approach can be tested: other historians point to the military need to end the war and to the Soviet declaration of war on 8 August, which the extract passes over, and to the fact that Soviet work on the bomb began in 1943. Knowing this, I can say that the approach is revisionist (it revises the view that the bomb was only a military decision), and that its strength is its use of American papers, while its limit is that it infers intention from timing."

*Commentary.* The approach is tied to the interpretation by a sentence of explanation ("If … then …"), supported by the extract's own words, placed among accurate dates and given a label with its *because* and a test. That is complete understanding: Level 6.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give the three answers to "were East–West tensions bound to resurface in 1945?"
A: Yes (ideology and history since 1917); no (cooperation was possible, and was thrown away by American choices after Roosevelt); likely but not fixed (a power vacuum and two superpowers, in a structural argument).
Q: What were Yergin's "Yalta axiom" and "Riga axiom"?
A: Two ways of seeing the Soviet Union. The Yalta axiom: the USSR acts from security fears and can be bargained with. The Riga axiom: the USSR is driven by an ideology of expansion and cannot be trusted. Yergin (*Shattered Peace*, 1977) argued that Truman's circle moved from the first to the second between 1945 and 1947.
Q: Who were the main traditionalists, and what were the main points of the traditional case?
A: Herbert Feis (1957–70), William McNeill (1953), Arthur Schlesinger Jr (1967); Kennan's "X" article (1947) is a contemporary statement. Points: Soviet ideology and Stalin's expansion; broken promises at Yalta; a defensive Western response.
Q: What did Williams argue in *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959)?
A: That American foreign policy since the 1890s had been shaped by the Open Door, the belief that prosperity depended on markets abroad; that after 1945 this, and not Soviet aggression, set the course; and that its tragedy was that a nation that thought itself generous had built an informal empire.
Q: What was Alperovitz's thesis in *Atomic Diplomacy* (1965)?
A: That Truman used the atomic bomb, and delayed his meeting with Stalin until it was ready, partly to strengthen the American hand against the Soviet Union in Europe.
Q: Why did revisionism arise in the 1960s?
A: The Vietnam War (large-scale American involvement from 1965), the civil rights movement and the New Left led young historians to doubt American motives; American archives opened; the Pentagon Papers (1971) seemed to confirm that officials had acted on unstated aims.
Q: What were the main criticisms of revisionism?
A: Maddox (1973) said that they misused documents; they gave little attention to Soviet actions; they made economics the main motive although officials spoke of security; they lacked Soviet sources; the Soviet archives after 1991 showed a more active Stalin.
Q: How do Gaddis (1972) and Leffler (1992) differ, though both are called post-revisionist?
A: Gaddis stressed constraints on both sides (public opinion and Congress; Stalin's system) and that each side's defensive acts seemed aggressive; Leffler stressed American officials' definition of national security and the pursuit of a "preponderance of power", blaming American overreach more, but because of security and not capitalism.
Q: How did official Soviet histories explain the origins of the Cold War?
A: As the work of imperialist circles in the USA and Britain, who abandoned Yalta and Potsdam, used the bomb to blackmail, and used the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan and NATO to subordinate Europe and encircle the socialist states; the continuation of 1918.
Q: What did Zubok and Pleshakov mean by the "revolutionary-imperial paradigm"?
A: Stalin was both a revolutionary (believing Soviet victory opened the way to world socialism) and an imperialist (wishing to hold and extend the Russian empire). Together they made him seek security by expansion.
Q: What was the Cold War International History Project and why does it matter?
A: A project founded in 1991 at the Woodrow Wilson Center in Washington that collected and translated documents from Russia, Eastern Europe and China; it made possible the New Cold War history.
Q: What is meant by "empire by invitation", and who used it?
A: Geir Lundestad (1986) argued that the American presence in Western Europe came partly because the Europeans asked for it; Gaddis (1997) used the idea to contrast the American with the Soviet empire.

**Practice extract N** (about 150 words)

The decision to use the atomic bomb on Japan must be seen together with American plans for Europe. **Practice extract P** (about 160 words)

The origins of the Cold War are not to be found in the villainy of either side. American leaders held fast to the principle that the world should be open and self-governing, and could not accept a closed Soviet sphere; Soviet leaders, shaped by a revolutionary doctrine, by their country's history of invasion and by the personal suspicion of Stalin, could not accept an open one. **Practice extract Q** (about 150 words)

American officials did not act in 1945–48 because they feared that Soviet armies would march west; they were cautious about Soviet intentions. Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Explain how the time and place of writing shaped the approach of **Alperovitz** in 1965 and of **Gaddis** in 1997, and say how each might have used the Soviet atomic project.
H: Think of the United States in the Vietnam years.

A: Alperovitz wrote when the Vietnam War was beginning and the civil rights movement was challenging the American self-image; American archives were opening; his question (what did Truman want?) and his judgement (that the bomb was a diplomatic act) fit this world. Gaddis wrote in 1997 after the end of the USSR, with new sources on Stalin; his question (what did Stalin want?) and his judgement (that Stalin's character and system made a settlement unlikely) fit a world in which the Cold War was over and the Soviet side could be read for the first time. On the atomic project: Alperovitz would see it as a reply to American atomic diplomacy; Gaddis would see it as part of a Soviet drive that Stalin's regime would have pursued anyway (the work began in 1943). A* work says that the link between time and approach is a claim to be shown from the extract, and not a guess about motives.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Take Practice extracts L, M, Q and R and say what each historian would make of the Marshall Plan and why.
H: Which question does each ask?
A: L (traditionalist): an act of generosity and defence, and Stalin's refusal was a choice to keep his sphere closed. :::recap
- **Shape of the debate**: contemporary views (Kennan, Truman Doctrine; Novikov, Zhdanov) → traditionalism (Feis 1957–70, Schlesinger 1967) → revisionism (Williams 1959, Fleming 1961, Alperovitz 1965, LaFeber 1967, Kolko 1968) → post-revisionism (Gaddis 1972, Yergin 1977, Leffler 1992) → the New Cold War history (Zubok and Pleshakov 1996, Mastny 1996, Gaddis 1997, Westad 2005).
- **Traditionalism**: Soviet ideology and expansion; broken promises at Yalta; a defensive West.
- **Revisionism**: American economic expansion (the Open Door); the atomic bomb; a weaker, defensive USSR. It arose in the Vietnam years; Maddox (1973) criticised it.
- **Post-revisionism**: both sides, fear and misperception, domestic constraints; Gaddis stresses constraints and Stalin's system; Leffler stresses American national security.
- **Soviet view**: the West's imperialism broke the alliance; the archives after 1991 changed the picture, but access has narrowed again.
- **New Cold War history**: Soviet sources; Stalin's ideology and aims; some return to traditional emphasis, some (Roberts) stress Stalin's caution; Westad widens the view to the world.
- **Time and place** shape every approach: McCarthy-era America, the Vietnam years, détente, the end of the USSR.
- **Labels**: use only when the extract supports them, with a *because*; "post-revisionist" is not simply "both are to blame".
- **Method**: main interpretation (two aspects); sub-messages; knowledge to explain and test; approach; time and place.
:::
