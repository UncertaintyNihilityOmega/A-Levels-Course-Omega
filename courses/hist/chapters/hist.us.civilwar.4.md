:::why
In 1865 about four million people who had been property became citizens, and the country had to decide what that meant. Within twelve years Black men voted, sat in Congress and wrote the constitutions of Southern states; within twenty-five years most had lost the vote and the protection of the law. Reconstruction is the story of one of the most ambitious attempts in American history to change a society by law, and of why it was not carried through. It teaches a central skill of this course: judging success and failure by asking "success at what?" and "by whose standard?". It is also a topic on which historians once wrote a version of the story that was wrong, and learning why shows how evidence and prejudice interact.
:::

## The problems facing the United States in 1865

### A devastated South and a divided North

The war ended in April 1865 with a country that had to answer questions no government had faced before.

**The cost.** About 620,000 to 750,000 soldiers had died (see the note in the previous chapter). In the South, roughly one in five white men of military age had been killed, and many more were wounded. Much of the fighting had taken place there: railways, bridges, mills, farms and towns in Virginia, Georgia, South Carolina and the Mississippi valley were wrecked. Confederate money and bonds were worthless, banks had failed, and the South's largest form of wealth, the enslaved people who had been worth very roughly three billion dollars in 1860, had vanished from the owners' balance sheets. Cotton output in the first postwar years was far below the prewar level (roughly four and a half million bales in 1860, and under half that in 1865–66), and it took years to recover.

**The freed people.** About four million people had been enslaved in 1860, about 3.5 million of them in the Confederate states, where they were nearly 4 in 10 of the people. Most were now free, with no land, no money, and in many cases no education: laws in most Southern states had forbidden teaching enslaved people to read. They wanted what freedom meant to them: to live as families, to control their own labour, to own land, to educate their children, to worship in their own churches, and to vote. Many believed that the government would give them "forty acres and a mule" (a phrase from General Sherman's Field Order No. 15 of 16 January 1865, which set aside confiscated land on the Georgia and South Carolina coast for freed families; tens of thousands settled there, and President Johnson later returned most of the land to its former owners).

**Politics.** The government in Washington had to decide **how to readmit the Southern states**, and on what terms. The Constitution did not say. Had the Southern states ever really left the Union (Lincoln's view: no, a rebellion by individuals) or had they become conquered provinces (the Radicals' view)? And who should decide: the president, under his war powers, or Congress, which has the power to admit states and "guarantee to every State in this Union a Republican Form of Government"? Behind these legal questions lay political ones: what rights should Black Americans have? Should the Confederate leaders be punished? Who would govern the South, and what would that mean for the Republican Party?

:::def Reconstruction
The period from 1863 to 1877 during which the federal government tried to restore the Southern states to the Union, to end slavery and decide the rights of the freed people, and to rebuild the South. It is also used in a narrower sense for the policy of Congress after 1867. Historians divide it into **Presidential Reconstruction** (1865–67), **Radical** or **Congressional Reconstruction** (1867–c.1872) and the **Redemption** of the South by white Democrats (c.1870–77).
:::

### The conflicting views: Radicals, Conservatives and Copperheads

Northern opinion was divided into at least four camps. Which one had power at a given time explains most of this chapter.

| Group | Who | What they wanted |
|---|---|---|
| **Radical Republicans** | Thaddeus Stevens (Pennsylvania, House), Charles Sumner (Massachusetts, Senate), Benjamin Wade (Ohio, Senate), George Julian (Indiana) | Equal civil and political rights for Black Americans, including the vote; punishment of the Confederate leaders; permanent change in Southern society, in Stevens's case by confiscating the large estates of rebels and dividing the land among freed families; a strong role for Congress |
| **Moderate Republicans** | Lyman Trumbull (Illinois), William Pitt Fessenden (Maine), John Sherman (Ohio) | The largest group in Congress. They wanted the end of slavery, civil rights for freed people and loyal governments in the South, but with less change and less federal power than the Radicals; they would act when Johnson refused to compromise |
| **Conservatives** | Andrew Johnson, most Northern Democrats, and some conservative Republicans such as William Seward | A quick return of the Southern states, with their own governments run by whites; limited federal power; no Black voting (or none imposed by Washington); mercy for the former rebels |
| **Copperheads** | Peace Democrats such as Clement Vallandigham and Fernando Wood | In the war they had wanted an early peace. After it they rejected emancipation's consequences as well, were against Black rights and condemned Reconstruction as unconstitutional military rule. They were a minority but merged into the Democratic Party's opposition to the Republicans |

:::def Radical Republicans
A group of Republicans in Congress who wanted the war to end slavery and to give Black Americans equal rights and the vote, to punish Confederate leaders and to remake Southern society, using federal power. Their leaders were Thaddeus Stevens and Charles Sumner.
:::

:::def Copperheads
Northern Democrats who opposed the war and Lincoln's policies, especially emancipation and the draft, and sought a negotiated peace. The name, used by their opponents, came from the poisonous snake. After 1865 many were hostile to Black rights and to Reconstruction.
:::

The Radicals were a minority in 1865, and the moderates held the balance. Four things pushed the moderates towards the Radicals: Johnson's refusal to compromise, the Black Codes, violence against Black Southerners, and the election of ex-Confederates to Congress in 1865. Remember this when you judge why Congress took over Reconstruction in 1867: it was not because the moderates became Radicals, but because Johnson's conduct drove them to act.

### Economic recovery and the reintegration of the South

Reconstruction was also an economic question.
- **Land and labour.** Planters had land but no money and no enslaved workforce; freed people had labour but no land. Whether land would be redistributed, and who would control the Black labour force, was the most important economic question of the period. Stevens proposed to confiscate the lands of the richest 70,000 or so Southerners (his figures, which Congress never accepted) and give 40 acres to each freed family. Few Northerners were prepared to break the principle of private property, so land redistribution failed.
- **Credit and cotton.** The South had little capital. Banks had failed and the cotton price fell after the war ended and the war boom. Farmers needed credit, which merchants gave in return for a lien on the crop.
- **Reintegration.** Southern states had to elect governments, rejoin Congress, and become partners in the federal system again. Northern investors were interested in Southern railways and industry, and Republican governments in the South spent heavily on railways, which raised debts and taxes.
- **The role of the federal government.** Never before had the federal government taken on so large a role in the states: the Freedmen's Bureau, military districts, federal courts for civil rights. Many Northerners were uneasy about this power, and it is one reason why support for it faded.

:::key Why Reconstruction was so hard in 1865
1. **No agreed plan**: the Constitution did not say who controlled the process, and Lincoln had died.
2. **Conflicting aims**: restoring the Union quickly, protecting freed people, punishing rebels, and rebuilding the Southern economy pulled in different directions.
3. **A divided North**: Radicals, moderates, conservatives and Copperheads wanted different things, and most white Northerners did not support Black equality as an aim of the war.
4. **Poverty, landlessness and war damage** in the South made the freed people dependent on the people who had owned them.
:::

## Lincoln: the Ten Percent Plan, the Wade–Davis Bill and his legacy

### The Ten Percent Plan, December 1863

Lincoln began to plan for the end of the war while it was still being fought. His aim was **speed and reconciliation**: to encourage Southern Unionists and weaken the Confederacy by showing that there was an easy way back. On 8 December 1863 he issued the **Proclamation of Amnesty and Reconstruction**, known as the **Ten Percent Plan**. It offered a **pardon** to most Confederates who took an oath of loyalty to the Union and accepted the abolition of slavery (high Confederate officials and army and navy officers were excluded). When the number of voters in a state who had taken the oath reached **ten per cent of the votes cast in 1860**, they could set up a state government, which the president would recognise. The plan left the freed people's civil and political rights to the states.

Governments formed under the plan in Louisiana, Arkansas and Tennessee, and Louisiana's new constitution of 1864 abolished slavery. But Congress refused to seat their representatives. Radical Republicans said the plan was too easy on the rebels, left power with the old planter class, and did not protect freed people. Lincoln, in turn, hoped to bring the Confederacy to an end sooner by making surrender easy.

### The Wade–Davis Bill, July 1864

Congress's answer was the **Wade–Davis Bill**, sponsored by Senator Benjamin Wade and Representative Henry Winter Davis, which passed on 2 July 1864. It was tougher:
- A **majority** (not 10 per cent) of the white male citizens of a state had to take an "ironclad oath" that they had never voluntarily supported the Confederacy before a state government could be set up.
- Only those who could take the oath could vote for the state convention, so Confederate supporters were excluded.
- The new state constitution had to abolish slavery, ban Confederate debt and bar high Confederate officials from office.

Lincoln refused to sign it, and let it die by **pocket veto** on 4 July 1864 (he did not sign it before Congress adjourned). On 8 July he explained that he was not "inflexibly committed to any single plan" and that he would be glad to see states follow it if they chose. Wade and Davis published a furious reply, the **Wade–Davis Manifesto**, on 5 August 1864. They accused the president of "a more studied outrage on the legislative authority of the people" than ever before, and warned that "our support is of a cause and not of a man" and that "the authority of Congress is paramount and must be respected".

:::def Pocket veto
A veto is the president's refusal to sign a bill into law; Congress can override an ordinary veto by passing the bill again by a two-thirds vote in both houses. A pocket veto is a veto by inaction: the president does not sign a bill after Congress has adjourned, and so it fails without Congress being able to override. Lincoln used it on the Wade–Davis Bill on 4 July 1864.
:::

### Lincoln's legacy

Lincoln's own ideas were still developing when he died. He pushed the **13th Amendment** through Congress (the House passed it on 31 January 1865 by 119 votes to 56, after he lobbied Democrats), supported the creation of the **Freedmen's Bureau** (3 March 1865), and in his Second Inaugural Address on 4 March 1865 asked the nation to proceed "with malice toward none; with charity for all". In his last public speech, on 11 April 1865, he said of the vote for Black men in Louisiana that he would prefer it "were now conferred on the very intelligent, and on those who serve our cause as soldiers", the first time a president had suggested Black suffrage in public. Three days later, on **14 April**, the actor John Wilkes Booth shot him at Ford's Theatre in Washington; he died on 15 April.

Historians argue about what Lincoln would have done. Some suggest he would have worked with the moderates and avoided the confrontation that Johnson provoked; his political skills and prestige might have held the Republicans together. Others point out that he too put reconciliation first, that the Ten Percent Plan left Southern whites in charge, and that his changing views on Black voting would have met the same Northern racism and Southern resistance. The honest answer is that we cannot know. What the evidence does show is that **Lincoln's legacy was a framework** (emancipation, the 13th Amendment, the Bureau, a willingness to move on voting) and not a finished policy, and that his death put the decision in the hands of a president who did not share his flexibility.

## Johnson and Presidential Reconstruction, 1865–67

### Andrew Johnson

**Andrew Johnson** was born in poverty in North Carolina, worked as a tailor, and rose in Tennessee politics as a champion of small white farmers against the planter class. He was the only Southern senator who stayed in the Senate when his state seceded, and Lincoln made him military governor of Tennessee. He was a Democrat on the National Union ticket in 1864. Early on, his language about "traitors" and the planters made Radicals think he would be harsh. Johnson himself hated the planter aristocracy; but he was a white supremacist, a believer in states' rights and a stubborn, quarrelsome man who did not like to compromise. He opposed Black suffrage and wanted the former Confederate states returned quickly, with white men in charge.

### Johnson's plan

While Congress was not in session (it did not meet until December 1865), Johnson acted alone:
- On **29 May 1865** he issued a **Proclamation of Amnesty**: pardon for most Confederates who swore loyalty, with exceptions for officials and for those with taxable property worth more than $20,000, who could apply to him personally. He then granted thousands of such pardons.
- He appointed **provisional governors** and told each Southern state to hold a convention. The conventions had to void secession, repudiate the Confederate debt and ratify the 13th Amendment. The voters were white men who had taken the oath.
- Black Southerners had no vote in this process.
- He ordered the return of most land seized during the war to its former owners, ending hopes of land for freed people.

By the end of 1865 all the Southern states except Texas had met his terms and elected governments and members of Congress. The results alarmed Northerners: voters chose many leading Confederates, including **Alexander Stephens**, the Confederate vice-president, who was elected US senator from Georgia, several Confederate generals and dozens of other former Confederate officials. Several states adopted only grudging versions of the required changes.

:::key Features of Presidential Reconstruction
1. **Speed and leniency**: pardons for most ex-Confederates and quick readmission.
2. **White control**: only white men voted for the new governments; Black men had no say.
3. **Executive power**: Johnson acted without Congress, using his powers as commander-in-chief.
4. **No land reform**: the confiscated lands were returned.
5. **Little protection for freed people** beyond the end of slavery.
:::

### The Black Codes

In the autumn and winter of 1865–66 the new Southern legislatures passed **Black Codes**: laws that defined the rights of the freed people and restricted them.

:::def Black Codes
Laws passed by the Southern states in 1865–66 which gave freed people some legal rights (to marry, own personal property, sue and make contracts) but restricted their freedom of work, movement, land ownership and public life, to keep a cheap, controlled Black labour force.
:::

What the codes did varied, but the pattern was the same.
- **Labour contracts.** In **Mississippi**, the law of November 1865 required freedmen to show written proof of "a lawful home or employment", such as a labour contract or a licence, and let them rent land only in towns. Breaking a contract could be punished as a crime.
- **Vagrancy.** Mississippi's amended vagrant law defined as vagrants "idle and disorderly persons" and also freedmen without lawful employment. The penalty was a fine (up to $50 for a freedman). A companion act said that a freedman who did not pay a fine within five days was to be **hired out** by the sheriff "to any white person who will pay said fine and all costs".
- **Apprenticeship.** Officials could bind Black children whose parents were thought unable to support them as apprentices to white employers, with the former owner given preference. The master could use "moderate corporeal chastisement".
- **Other limits.** Curfews, restrictions on carrying guns, bans on Black preachers or on interracial marriage, and separate punishments for crimes.
- **Some rights.** Most codes made marriages between freed people legal, allowed them to own personal property and to sue, though not to serve on juries or vote.

For Black Southerners, the codes looked like slavery under a new name. For Northerners, they showed that the white South had not accepted that slavery was over. They helped turn many moderates against Johnson. It is also worth noting that the codes were not all alike: South Carolina's and Mississippi's were harsh, and Virginia's and Tennessee's were milder; some states repealed or softened them when Northern opinion reacted. Even so, they were the work of legislatures chosen under Johnson's plan.

### Congress and Johnson clash, 1865–66

When Congress met in December 1865, it refused to seat the Southern representatives and set up a **Joint Committee on Reconstruction** of fifteen members, led by moderate William Pitt Fessenden, to investigate conditions in the South. Moderates then tried to find a compromise by passing two bills:
- A bill to extend the life of the **Freedmen's Bureau**, which Johnson vetoed on 19 February 1866. The Senate sustained the veto.
- The **Civil Rights Act of 1866**, drafted by Lyman Trumbull, which made all persons born in the United States (except untaxed Native Americans) **citizens**, and gave them equal rights to make contracts, own property, sue and be protected by law. Johnson vetoed it on **27 March 1866**, arguing that it was an unconstitutional extension of federal power, that it gave citizenship to Black Americans when eleven states were unrepresented in Congress, and that "the distinction of race and color is by the bill made to operate in favor of the colored and against the white race". On **9 April 1866**, Congress overrode the veto (passed the bill again by a two-thirds vote in both houses), the first time it had overridden a veto on a major law. In July it also passed the Freedmen's Bureau bill over a second veto.

Violence made the case for the moderates. In **Memphis**, Tennessee, on 1–3 May 1866, white mobs, led by police and city officials, attacked Black residents and Black soldiers after a street clash: 46 Black people were killed, and women were raped, and homes, schools and churches burned. In **New Orleans** on 30 July 1866, police and a white crowd attacked a convention of Black and white Republicans who wanted to give Black men the vote; dozens were killed, most of them Black. Johnson was widely blamed because he had encouraged Southern whites to resist Congress.

Congress then wrote the **14th Amendment** (see the section on the amendments below) and sent it to the states in June 1866. Johnson told Southern states to reject it, and all but Tennessee did. In August and September 1866 Johnson toured the North in a "Swing Around the Circle" to win support in the midterm elections (the elections for Congress held halfway through a president's term), and his angry exchanges with hecklers damaged him. The elections of November 1866 gave the Republicans more than two-thirds of the seats in both houses. That majority was enough to override any veto, and it meant that Johnson's plan was dead and that Congress would take control.

:::key Why Presidential Reconstruction failed
1. **Southern whites did not change**: they elected ex-Confederates and passed Black Codes.
2. **Johnson would not compromise**: he vetoed the Freedmen's Bureau and Civil Rights bills and told the South to reject the 14th Amendment.
3. **Violence** (Memphis, New Orleans) showed that Black Southerners needed protection.
4. **Northern opinion** turned against the president, and the 1866 elections gave the Republicans a veto-proof majority.
:::

## Congress and Radical Reconstruction, 1867–c.1872

### The Reconstruction Acts

With its two-thirds majority, Congress took over. The **First Reconstruction Act** (2 March 1867, passed over Johnson's veto) set aside the governments formed under Johnson in the ten states that had not been readmitted (Tennessee, which had ratified the 14th Amendment, was readmitted in July 1866 and was not covered). The acts:
- divided the ten states into **five military districts**, each under a general, with troops to keep order and supervise the process;
- required each state to hold a **new constitutional convention**, elected by all adult male citizens **including Black men**, with leading ex-Confederates barred;
- required the new constitutions to guarantee **Black male suffrage**, and the state to **ratify the 14th Amendment**, before being readmitted to Congress.

Three supplementary acts (March, July 1867 and March 1868) tightened the rules and gave the generals power to register voters and remove officials. Johnson vetoed them all, and Congress overrode him each time. By July 1868 Arkansas and six other states (Alabama, Florida, Louisiana, North Carolina, South Carolina and, for a time, Georgia) were readmitted, and by 1870 all ten were back, Virginia, Mississippi, Texas and Georgia last.

:::def Military Reconstruction
The system created by the Reconstruction Acts of 1867–68, under which ten Southern states were governed in five military districts by army generals until they wrote new constitutions with Black male suffrage and ratified the 14th Amendment. The army's role was to supervise registration and elections, not to rule day to day.
:::

The result was a political revolution. In 1867 about 700,000 Black men registered to vote in the South, and in five states (Alabama, Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi and South Carolina) they were a **majority** of registered voters. Alongside them voted white Southerners who supported the new order. The new state constitutions of 1868–69 went beyond the minimum. They gave Black and poor white men the vote, ended property qualifications for office, created state **public schools** for the first time in much of the South, reformed local government and courts, and expanded the legal rights of women in some states, for example the property rights of married women.

### The Tenure of Office Act and the impeachment of Johnson

The conflict between Johnson and Congress continued. Congress passed the **Tenure of Office Act** (2 March 1867) over his veto. It said that the president could not remove officials whose appointment had needed Senate approval without Senate consent. The aim was to protect **Edwin Stanton**, the Secretary of War, who supported Radical Reconstruction and was the Radicals' ally in the cabinet. Another law, the Command of the Army Act, said that the president's orders to the army had to pass through the General of the Army, Grant.

Johnson saw both as unconstitutional and set out to test them. He suspended Stanton in August 1867 and put Grant in his place. When the Senate refused to agree in January 1868, Grant stepped aside, and on **21 February 1868** Johnson dismissed Stanton and named another general. Stanton barricaded himself in his office. Three days later, on **24 February 1868**, the House voted 126 to 47 to **impeach** the president (to bring formal charges against him; the Senate then tries him and needs a two-thirds vote to remove him). The trial in the Senate followed in March–May: the House managers presented eleven articles: nine about the removal of Stanton and the Tenure of Office Act, one about his speeches attacking Congress, and a last one (Article 11) that gathered several charges together.

Why did Congress impeach Johnson?
1. **The immediate trigger** was his dismissal of Stanton, which Republicans said broke the Tenure of Office Act.
2. **The deeper cause** was a conflict over who ran Reconstruction. Johnson had vetoed every major Reconstruction law, urged Southerners to reject the 14th Amendment, used his powers to undermine the Reconstruction Acts (by replacing generals who enforced them and by pardoning ex-Confederates) and made inflammatory speeches.
3. **The Radicals' political aim** was to remove an obstacle: with Johnson gone, the Senate president pro tempore, Benjamin Wade, a Radical, would become president (there was no vice-president, so the next in line was the Senate's presiding officer).
4. **Principle**: many Republicans believed that Johnson had violated the Constitution and the law, though others thought the Tenure of Office Act was itself of doubtful validity.

The Senate needed a two-thirds majority (36 of 54 votes) to convict. On 16 May 1868 (Article 11) and 26 May (Articles 2 and 3), the vote was **35 for conviction and 19 against**, one vote short. Seven Republican senators, including William Pitt Fessenden, Lyman Trumbull and Edmund Ross of Kansas, voted for acquittal. They gave different reasons: that the charges were weak, that the Tenure of Office Act did not apply to Stanton (who had been appointed by Lincoln, not Johnson), or that removing a president on political grounds would damage the Constitution and set a precedent that Congress could remove any president it disliked. Johnson stayed in office until March 1869, but his power over Reconstruction was gone.

:::key Johnson's impeachment
1. **Why**: his dismissal of Stanton in defiance of the Tenure of Office Act, set against a long conflict over Reconstruction.
2. **Result**: acquitted by one vote (35–19) on 16 and 26 May 1868.
3. **Significance**: Johnson survived, but Congress and the Republican Party controlled Reconstruction, and Grant, the Republican candidate, won the election in November 1868.
:::

## The 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments

The three **Reconstruction Amendments** changed the Constitution more than anything since the Bill of Rights. The table gives the date of each, what it did, why it was passed, and how it was later weakened.

| Amendment | Passed by Congress / ratified | What it did | Why | How it was undermined |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **13th** | Passed 31 January 1865; ratified 6 December 1865 (proclaimed 18 December) | Abolished slavery and involuntary servitude "except as a punishment for crime" throughout the United States | The Emancipation Proclamation was a war measure that left slavery legal in the Border States and might be challenged in court; Lincoln and the Republicans wanted permanent abolition | The exception for crime: Southern states used vagrancy and other laws to convict Black men and **lease** the convicts to employers (convict leasing); debt and sharecropping bound many Black farmers to the land |
| **14th** | Passed June 1866; ratified 9 July 1868 | **Section 1**: all persons born or naturalised in the US are citizens (overturning *Dred Scott*); no state may abridge the "privileges or immunities" of citizens, deprive anyone of "life, liberty, or property, without due process of law", or deny "the equal protection of the laws". **Section 2**: states that deny Black men the vote lose seats in the House. **Section 3**: bars ex-Confederate officials from office unless Congress pardons them. **Section 4**: Confederate debt void | The Black Codes; the need to protect the Civil Rights Act of 1866 from repeal or challenge; to give Congress a basis for acting against states; and to set terms for readmission | The Supreme Court narrowed it: the *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) read the "privileges or immunities" clause narrowly; *United States v. Cruikshank* (1876) said the federal government could not punish private violence; the *Civil Rights Cases* (1883) struck down the Civil Rights Act of 1875; *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) allowed "separate but equal". Section 2 was never enforced |
| **15th** | Passed 26 February 1869; ratified 3 February 1870 | The right to vote "shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude" | Republicans wanted to secure Black votes in the South once troops left, to guarantee them in the North (where several states still barred Black men from voting), and to make Black suffrage safe from changes by Congress or the states | It said nothing about literacy tests (reading tests for voters), poll taxes (a fee to vote) or property qualifications, so from the 1890s Southern states used these and the "grandfather clause" (which let a man vote without passing the tests if his grandfather had voted, so it spared poor white men) to disfranchise (take the vote from) most Black voters while keeping to the letter of the law; Mississippi's constitution of 1890 was the first; women were not given the vote |

:::key What the amendments achieved, and why they did not secure equality
1. **The 13th Amendment** ended slavery for good, but its exception clause allowed forced labour of convicts.
2. **The 14th Amendment** defined citizenship and created the principle of equal protection; its text was later the basis of the civil rights movement. But the Supreme Court, between 1873 and 1896, narrowed it so that it protected little.
3. **The 15th Amendment** gave Black men the vote, but left the states free to find other ways to restrict it.
4. **Together**, they changed the Constitution, but depended on Congress, presidents and courts to enforce them. When Northern support faded, enforcement stopped.
:::

:::warn Do not say the 15th Amendment gave the vote to all Black Americans
It prevented denial of the vote "on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude" and applied to **men**. Women of every race were not given the vote nationally until the 19th Amendment (1920), and Southern states soon found ways to keep most Black men from voting that did not mention race. The amendment also divided the women's movement: Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Susan B. Anthony opposed it because it did not include women, while Frederick Douglass and others supported it.
:::

## Freedom in practice: the changing position of former slaves

### The Freedmen's Bureau

The **Bureau of Refugees, Freedmen, and Abandoned Lands** was set up by Congress on 3 March 1865 under General Oliver Otis Howard. It was the first federal welfare and civil-rights agency. It provided food and medical help to hundreds of thousands, negotiated labour contracts between freed people and employers, set up courts to hear complaints, and, above all, helped to build **schools**. With Northern missionary societies and Black communities, it helped to found hundreds of schools and colleges, including Fisk (1866), Howard (1867) and Hampton (1868). Black families paid for teachers and buildings out of very small incomes. Bureau agents also reunited families and registered marriages.

Its limits were large. It never had more than about 900 agents for the whole South, and it was always short of money. Johnson's vetoes and his pardons undermined its main land policy: it had controlled abandoned and confiscated land, but most of it was returned to white owners in 1865–66. The Bureau helped to protect freed people, but many agents sided with planters, and the Bureau was wound up in 1872 (its schools were handed over to the states from 1870).

:::def Freedmen's Bureau
The federal agency created on 3 March 1865 to help freed people with food, medical care, schools, labour contracts and legal protection in the South. Led by General Oliver O. Howard, it was always short of staff and money and ended in 1872.
:::

### Land, labour and sharecropping

Most freed people wanted land. Without it they had to work for those who owned it. At first planters tried to use gangs of workers on wages, as under the Black Codes. Freed people disliked this because it looked like slavery: they preferred to work as families on their own plots, with their own hours. Planters had little money to pay wages and a shortage of labour. The result, which developed after 1865 and spread in the 1870s, was **sharecropping**.

:::def Sharecropping
A system in which a landowner lets a farming family use a plot of land and provides tools and seed, in return for a share of the crop (often half). The family chooses how to work, but usually has to buy food and supplies on credit from a local merchant, repaid at the harvest.
:::

Sharecropping gave Black families more independence than slavery had: they worked as families, away from overseers, and the women and children could choose to work less in the fields. But it gave very little security. The share was often small; merchants charged high prices and took a **crop lien** (a claim on the harvest) for credit; the landlord and the merchant kept the accounts, which the cropper often could not check; and the cotton price fell. Many families ended each year in debt and could not leave. Sharecropping bound many Black and many poor white farmers to cotton in the South for generations. Some Black families did acquire land, but they were a small minority. Eric Foner, the leading modern historian of the period, stresses that the failure to give freed people land was one of the main reasons the "unfinished revolution" stopped: freedom without land left them dependent.

### Education, churches and families

The first things freed people did with freedom were personal and community ones.
- They sought out **family members** sold away under slavery, and legally married.
- They left white-controlled churches and built their own, such as the African Methodist Episcopal (AME) and Baptist churches, which became centres of community and political life.
- They demanded **schools** for their children and adults. By the 1870s hundreds of thousands of Black children and adults were attending school, which was a major change.
- They organised politically, through the **Union Leagues** and churches.

### Black political participation

Black men took part in politics on a scale unknown before. They voted in huge numbers, joined the Republican Party, which they saw as the party of Lincoln and freedom, and held office at every level. Around **1,500 Black men** held public office in the Southern states during Reconstruction, from local posts to state legislatures and Congress (the exact figure depends on what is counted; some estimates are higher). About sixteen served in the US Congress between 1869 and 1877, including **Hiram Revels**, a minister born free in North Carolina, who was seated on 25 February 1870 as the first Black senator, in the Mississippi seat once held by Jefferson Davis, and **Blanche K. Bruce**, who served a full Senate term for Mississippi from 1875 to 1881. In South Carolina Black legislators formed a majority in the lower house in 1868–74; **Pinckney B. S. Pinchback** briefly served as governor of Louisiana in 1872–73.

These officials were mostly well-educated or experienced men, many of them ministers, teachers, artisans and former soldiers. Their record was not one of "Black rule": in most states Black officeholders were a minority of the total, Black officials were often moderate, and they supported public education, equal access and the protection of civil rights.

## The response of the white South

### Carpetbaggers and scalawags

White Southern Democrats used insulting nicknames for the Republican coalition, and the names stuck.

:::def Carpetbaggers
A hostile nickname used by white Southerners for Northerners who moved to the South after the war, supposedly with all their belongings in a carpet-bag, to profit from Reconstruction. In fact they were a mixed group: Union veterans who bought land, teachers and missionaries, officials and business people. Some were corrupt, many were idealistic, and they were never more than a small share of Southern Republican office-holders.
:::

:::def Scalawags
A hostile nickname used by white Southern Democrats for white Southerners who supported the Republican Party, such as wartime Unionists in the hill country, some businessmen and a few former Confederates (the general James Longstreet among them). They were often more important in numbers than the carpetbaggers.
:::

The two groups, together with Black voters, were the basis of the Republican governments in the South. White Southern Democrats attacked them for **corruption** and heavy taxes. There was real corruption in some states (for example in South Carolina and Louisiana), and taxes and debts did rise because the new governments spent on schools, roads, railways and hospitals, and because the war had left the South poor. But corruption was just as great in the North in the same years (the Tweed Ring in New York City; the Crédit Mobilier scandal in 1872 in the Grant administration) and was not the real reason for white opposition. The main objection of most white Democrats was to **Black political power** and to the Republican Party that depended on it.

### The Ku Klux Klan and the rise in violence

White violence was both a response to Reconstruction and an attempt to reverse it.

The **Ku Klux Klan** was founded in Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1865–66 by Confederate veterans, at first as a social club. By 1868 it had become a network of local groups across the South, with robes and masks, and with ex-Confederate leaders, including General Nathan Bedford Forrest, associated with it. Its purposes were political: to stop Black men voting, to destroy Republican organisation, to intimidate Black teachers, landowners and officials, and to restore white Democratic rule. Its methods were night raids, whippings, arson and murder.

Some examples show what lay behind the numbers.
- In 1868, in the months before the presidential election, hundreds of Republicans, most of them Black, were killed in Louisiana, Arkansas and Georgia.
- In North Carolina in 1870, Klansmen lynched **Wyatt Outlaw**, a Black town commissioner of Graham and head of the local Union League, and murdered State Senator **John W. Stephens**, a white Republican.
- In York County, South Carolina, in 1870–71, the Klan terrorised Black communities. A Black preacher, **Elias Hill**, who was physically disabled, was dragged from his home and beaten, and later told Congress about it.
- At **Colfax**, Louisiana, on 13 April 1873, after a disputed election, a white militia attacked Black men who were defending the courthouse. Historians' estimates of the Black dead range from about 60 to 150 (the most careful recent work suggests 62 to 80); three white men also died. No one was convicted in the end, and the Supreme Court's decision in *United States v. Cruikshank* (1876) made prosecution of private violence harder.
- In 1874–76 paramilitary groups such as the **White League** in Louisiana and the **Red Shirts** in South Carolina, and the "Mississippi Plan" of 1875, used open violence and intimidation to win elections for the Democrats. At Hamburg, South Carolina, in July 1876, Black militiamen were killed after surrendering.

These attacks were aimed at Black voters, Black officials, Black schools and churches, and Republicans of both races. They show why Black Southerners asked the federal government for protection, and why the question of how long the government would give it decided the outcome of Reconstruction.

:::key White resistance to Reconstruction
1. **Law**: Black Codes (1865–66), then, after 1869, attempts to regain control of state legislatures.
2. **Violence and intimidation**: the Klan (1866–72), then the White League, Red Shirts and the Mississippi Plan (1873–76).
3. **Politics**: the Democrats appealed to white solidarity, claimed that Republican governments were corrupt and "Negro-ruled", and called themselves **Redeemers** (men who would "redeem" the South).
4. **Economics**: planters and merchants used sharecropping, credit and the threat of dismissal to control Black voters and workers.
:::

By 1877 Reconstruction governments survived only in South Carolina, Louisiana and Florida, and those were held up by federal troops, which the president was about to withdraw.

## Grant and the end of Reconstruction, 1869–77

### Ulysses S. Grant

Grant, the hero of the Union armies, was elected president in November 1868 as a Republican on the slogan "Let us have peace", defeating the Democrat Horatio Seymour. He had no political experience and a modest, honest personality, which made him popular but also vulnerable to scandals among his friends and appointees. He believed in protecting Black rights and supported the 15th Amendment. He was also a practical politician who wanted to keep the Republican coalition together, and who in the end gave in to the argument that the North was tired of the South's troubles.

### Enforcement: the Force Acts

Grant's government used federal law, for the first time, against violence and fraud. There were three **Enforcement Acts**, also called the **Force Acts**.
- **First Enforcement Act (31 May 1870):** made it a federal crime to use force, bribery or intimidation to stop citizens voting on grounds of race, and to conspire to interfere with civil rights. Federal courts could try offenders. The Department of Justice was created later in 1870 and, under Attorney General Amos Akerman, began to prosecute Klansmen.
- **Second Enforcement Act (28 February 1871):** placed congressional elections in large cities under federal supervision.
- **Third Enforcement Act, or Ku Klux Klan Act (20 April 1871):** made it a federal crime for individuals to conspire to deprive citizens of equal protection, and let the president use troops and suspend the writ of habeas corpus against the Klan.

Grant used the Klan Act in October 1871 in nine South Carolina counties, where federal troops arrested hundreds of suspected Klansmen. Congress held hearings, which produced thirteen volumes of testimony about Klan violence, taken from victims such as Elias Hill. Thousands of cases were brought in the federal courts, and hundreds ended in convictions, mostly in 1871–72, in South Carolina, Mississippi and North Carolina. The Klan as an organisation largely collapsed by 1872. This is the clearest evidence that the federal government could defeat organised violence when it was prepared to use its power.

### The Amnesty Act and the Liberal Republicans

The second strand of Grant's policy was **conciliation**. The **Amnesty Act** of **22 May 1872** removed the political disabilities placed on former Confederates by the 14th Amendment from all but about 500 of the most prominent. The reasons were that many Republicans, including the Liberal Republicans who broke with Grant in 1872 and nominated Horace Greeley, argued that continuing to punish the South was unjust, and that Grant wanted to be seen as reconciling. The effect was to let ex-Confederate leaders return to Southern politics, and strengthen the Democrats.

### The Civil Rights Act of 1875 and the Supreme Court

The **Civil Rights Act** of **1 March 1875** guaranteed all citizens equal access to inns, public transport, theatres and other places of public amusement, and the right to serve on juries, whatever their race. It was passed in memory of Charles Sumner, who had proposed it and who died in March 1874. It had very little effect: enforcement was weak, and in the **Civil Rights Cases** of 15 October 1883 the Supreme Court declared it unconstitutional, saying that the 14th Amendment applied to the actions of states, not of private businesses.

### Why the North lost interest

By 1873–75 Northern support for Reconstruction had weakened.
- **The Panic of 1873.** The failure of the banking house of Jay Cooke on 18 September 1873 began a long depression. Northern voters worried about jobs and prices, and not about the South.
- **Scandals.** The Crédit Mobilier scandal and later cases damaged Grant and the Republican Party's reputation for honesty, and gave credibility to the claim that Republican governments were corrupt.
- **Fatigue and racism.** Many white Northerners had never supported Black equality, felt the war's aims had been achieved by 1870, and were tired of intervention; some accepted the Redeemers' claim that Black voters were unfit for self-government.
- **The Democrats' recovery.** In the 1874 elections the Democrats won control of the House of Representatives for the first time since the war, gaining nearly 100 seats.
- **The Supreme Court.** The *Slaughter-House Cases* (1873) and *United States v. Cruikshank* (1876) reduced the power of the federal government to protect individuals.

The turning point came in 1875 in Mississippi. The Republican governor, Adelbert Ames, asked Grant for troops to stop violent intimidation before the elections. Grant's Attorney General, Edwards Pierrepont, replied that the public was tired of the "autumnal outbreaks" in the South, and that Ames should deal with it himself. Grant refused to send troops. The Democrats won Mississippi, and Ames resigned in 1876. This was the signal to other Southern Democrats that the federal government would not intervene.

:::key Why support for Reconstruction collapsed in the North
1. **Economic depression** after 1873 turned attention to the economy.
2. **Scandals** in the Grant administration and in Southern governments.
3. **Racism and fatigue**: the war's aims seemed to be achieved and many Northerners did not support Black equality.
4. **Democratic gains** in 1874, and Redeemers' success showed that violence worked.
5. **The courts** narrowed federal power to act.
:::

### The disputed election of 1876 and the Compromise of 1877

The Republican candidate in 1876 was **Rutherford B. Hayes**, governor of Ohio, who had a reputation for honesty. The Democrat was **Samuel J. Tilden**, governor of New York, who campaigned against corruption. Tilden won the popular vote by about a quarter of a million votes and led in the electoral college by 184 to 165, one short of the 185 needed. Twenty electoral votes were disputed: **Florida, Louisiana and South Carolina** (where both parties claimed victory, with violence and fraud on both sides) and one in Oregon. If Hayes won all twenty, he would win by one vote, 185 to 184.

The Constitution did not say who should decide. In January 1877 Congress created an **Electoral Commission** of fifteen members (five senators, five representatives and five Supreme Court justices) to judge the disputed returns. It had eight Republicans and seven Democrats and voted 8–7, along party lines, for Hayes in every case. Democrats in the House threatened to delay the count past 4 March and force a crisis.

The crisis was settled by negotiations. Historians call the result the **Compromise of 1877**. The terms were never written down in a single agreement, but Southern Democratic leaders, Hayes's representatives and railway interests are thought to have reached an understanding, in meetings at the Wormley Hotel in Washington and elsewhere, that the Democrats would accept Hayes if:
- Hayes withdrew federal troops from the South (the last garrisons in the state houses of South Carolina and Louisiana);
- at least one Southerner was appointed to the cabinet (Hayes named David M. Key of Tennessee, a former Confederate, Postmaster General);
- the South would get federal money for internal improvements, including railways (the Texas and Pacific Railroad); and
- the Southern Democrats would respect the civil and political rights of Black citizens (a promise that was not kept).

Congress declared Hayes the winner in the early hours of **2 March 1877** and he was inaugurated on 5 March. In April he ordered the troops out of the South Carolina and Louisiana state houses, and the Republican governments there collapsed. By then every Southern state was in the hands of Democrats.

:::key The Compromise of 1877: reasons and results
1. **Reasons**: the electoral deadlock; Hayes's wish to be president; Southern Democrats' wish for withdrawal of troops and for federal money for railways; Northern fatigue.
2. **Results**: troops left the state houses and the federal government stopped intervening; Redeemer rule was confirmed across the South; Black political power collapsed and Black Southerners were left to white state governments. Most of the economic promises to the South were not kept.
3. **Significance**: it marks the formal end of Reconstruction, but the real end had come earlier, with Northern retreat in 1873–75.
:::

:::note Did the Compromise of 1877 end Reconstruction?
The historian **C. Vann Woodward**, in *Reunion and Reaction* (1951), argued that the bargain of 1877 traded Black rights for Southern support for Hayes and for Northern business interests, and ended Reconstruction. Later historians, notably **Eric Foner**, argue that Reconstruction was already largely over by 1877: Redeemers controlled most of the South, the Supreme Court had curbed federal power, and Northern support had faded. On this view the withdrawal of the troops from the last three states recognised what had already happened. For an AS essay, say that the Compromise was the **final act**, not the sole cause.
:::

## Was Reconstruction a success or a failure?

The question "how successful was it?" cannot be answered until you decide what Reconstruction was **for**. It had several aims, held by different people, and it did better on some than on others.

| Aim | Evidence of success | Evidence of failure | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Restore the Union** | All eleven Confederate states were readmitted by 1870; no further secession; the country stayed one | The South was reunited on terms that left the white South in control of its own affairs | Success |
| **End slavery and give freed people civil rights** | 13th Amendment (1865); Civil Rights Acts of 1866 and 1875; 14th Amendment citizenship and equal protection | Black Codes; convict leasing; the courts narrowed the 14th Amendment (1873–83); the Civil Rights Act of 1875 struck down (1883) | Success in law, failure in practice |
| **Give Black men political power** | Black suffrage from 1867; about 1,500 officeholders; sixteen in Congress; state constitutions that built public schools | Violence and fraud; Redeemers in control of every state by 1877; disfranchisement from the 1890s | Success for a decade, then reversed |
| **Change Southern society and its economy** | Public schools; Black churches, schools and families; railways and some industry | No land reform; sharecropping and debt; the planter class kept its land and returned to power; cotton dependence | Largely a failure |
| **Protect Black Southerners from violence** | The Enforcement Acts and the Klan Act (1870–71) broke the first Klan by 1872 | The Colfax massacre (1873); White League and Red Shirts (1874–76); withdrawal of troops in 1877 | Success for a short time, then withdrawn |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Reconstruction **succeeded in changing the law and the Constitution**: it ended slavery, defined citizenship and equal protection, and gave Black men the vote. For a short time it created a real biracial democracy in the South. It **failed to make those rights secure**, because it did not give freed people land and an economic base, because it relied on Northern will that faded after 1873, and because the white South used violence to retake control. Its legacy is double: the amendments became the basis of the civil rights movement of the 1950s and 1960s (sometimes called the "Second Reconstruction"), but for nearly a century Black Southerners were denied the rights they had briefly held. The best AS answers use the aims above as criteria.
:::

:::key Why Reconstruction ended: causes to weigh
1. **Southern white resistance**: Black Codes, the Klan, the White League and the Mississippi Plan used violence and economic pressure to retake control.
2. **Northern loss of will**: the Panic of 1873, scandals, racism and fatigue, the Democrats' gains in 1874 and Grant's refusal in 1875 to send troops.
3. **Weaknesses of Reconstruction itself**: no land reform; a reliance on military force, a small Freedmen's Bureau and the ballot alone; divisions among Republicans (the Liberal Republicans, the Amnesty Act); corruption charges.
4. **Johnson's obstruction (1865–69)**: wasted the best chance to build a lasting settlement while Northern support was strongest.
5. **The Supreme Court**: narrowed federal power (1873–76).
6. **The Compromise of 1877**: the final withdrawal of troops.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **The Dunning School.** For the first half of the twentieth century the standard account came from **William A. Dunning** of Columbia University and his students, in *Reconstruction, Political and Economic, 1865–1877* (1907), and from popular writers such as **Claude Bowers**, *The Tragic Era* (1929). They described Reconstruction as a "tragic era" in which vengeful Northerners, corrupt "carpetbaggers" and ignorant Black voters imposed misrule on a defeated South, until honest white Southerners "redeemed" it. This version shaped textbooks and films for decades.
- **Why it was discredited.** Its authors accepted the racist assumption that Black Americans could not govern; they relied on the accounts of white Southern opponents of Reconstruction and ignored Black voices; and the records show that the Reconstruction governments, despite real corruption, built public schools and new rights, that the Klan's violence was deliberate, and that corruption was also common in the North. Historians now treat the Dunning account as evidence of the racial attitudes of its time.
- **W. E. B. Du Bois**, *Black Reconstruction in America* (1935), the first major challenge, argued that Black Southerners were active agents in the period, that they were the main force for democracy in the South, and that the failure came from the combined resistance of Southern planters and Northern capital. Academic historians at the time largely ignored him.
- **Eric Foner**, *Reconstruction: America's Unfinished Revolution, 1863–1877* (1988), the standard modern account, describes a "remarkable experiment in interracial democracy" that was overthrown by violence and by Northern retreat. The title gives his verdict: it was a revolution that was not finished.
You can use this at AS to explain why the question of "success" changed over time, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Congress impeached Johnson in 1868.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Andrew Johnson became president after Lincoln was killed in 1865. He did not agree with Congress about Reconstruction and vetoed the Civil Rights Act. Congress passed the Tenure of Office Act in 1867. Johnson sacked Stanton, who was Secretary of War. So Congress voted to impeach him in February 1868, but the Senate voted not guilty by one vote."

Q: What were the main terms of Lincoln's Ten Percent Plan (1863), and why did Radicals oppose it?
A: A pardon for most Confederates who swore loyalty, and a new state government once ten per cent of the 1860 voters had taken the oath and accepted the end of slavery. The Radicals thought it too lenient: it left power with the old white élite, gave Black people no rights, and put the Southern governments in place without Congress's say.
Q: What was the Wade–Davis Bill and what happened to it?
A: Congress's plan of 1864, which required a majority of white men to swear they had never supported the Confederacy before a state could be reorganised. Lincoln did not sign it, and it died by pocket veto on 4 July 1864. The authors replied with the Wade–Davis Manifesto.
Q: Give three features of Johnson's Presidential Reconstruction.
A: Pardons for most ex-Confederates (29 May 1865); provisional governors and conventions elected by white men only; no land redistribution; readmission of states with governments that passed Black Codes and elected ex-Confederates. (Also: it was done while Congress was not sitting.)
Q: What did the Civil Rights Act of 1866 do and how did it become law?
A: It made all persons born in the United States (except untaxed Native Americans) citizens with equal rights to make contracts, own property and be protected by law. Johnson vetoed it on 27 March 1866; Congress overrode the veto on 9 April, the first major law passed over a veto.
Q: What did the Reconstruction Acts of 1867 require of the Southern states?
A: Ten states were put under five military districts. Each had to hold a convention elected by all adult male citizens, including Black men, write a constitution guaranteeing Black male suffrage, and ratify the 14th Amendment before readmission.
Q: Why did the Senate fail to remove Johnson in 1868?
A: The vote was 35 to 19, one short of the two-thirds needed. Seven Republican senators voted for acquittal, saying that the Tenure of Office Act was doubtful and that removing a president for political disagreement would set a dangerous precedent.
Q: Name the three Reconstruction Amendments and what each did.
A: 13th (1865): abolished slavery except as punishment for crime. 14th (1868): citizenship, due process and equal protection. 15th (1870): the right to vote not to be denied because of race, colour or previous condition of servitude.
Q: What was sharecropping, and why did it trap many Black farmers?
A: Families farmed a plot owned by a landlord for a share (often half) of the crop, buying supplies on credit from a merchant who took a lien on the harvest. Low cotton prices, high prices at the store and unfair accounting left many in debt and unable to leave.
Q: What did the Enforcement Acts (1870–71) allow the federal government to do, and with what result?
A: They made interference with voting and conspiracy against civil rights federal crimes, put elections under federal supervision, and allowed the president to use troops and suspend habeas corpus against the Klan. Grant used them in South Carolina in 1871; hundreds were convicted, and the first Klan was broken by 1872.
Q: What was agreed in the Compromise of 1877?
A: After the disputed election of 1876, Southern Democrats accepted Hayes as president in return for the withdrawal of federal troops from the South, a Southerner in the cabinet, federal money for Southern railways and a promise to respect Black rights (which was not kept). The troops left the last state houses in April 1877.

H: Split the Dunning account into claims: Black voters were ignorant and ruled; governments were uniquely corrupt; the Klan was a response; Redemption was a restoration of honest government.
H: Test each claim against the evidence in this chapter.
A: Challenge to Dunning: Black officials were a minority in most states and mostly able and moderate; the governments built public schools and extended rights; corruption was also widespread in the North (Tweed, Crédit Mobilier) and in the South under Redeemers; the Klan and White League used terror to remove elected governments; the 1890s disfranchisement showed what Redemption meant. Some support for parts: there was real corruption in South Carolina and Louisiana, taxes and debts rose, and white Southerners had real grievances about military rule and the ex-Confederate disqualifications. A* judgement: the Dunning account is wrong in its central claims, and was based on racist assumptions and one-sided evidence, though it recorded real faults in some state governments.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Eric Foner calls Reconstruction "America's Unfinished Revolution". How far does the evidence in this chapter and your own knowledge support that description?
H: What would make it a revolution? Consider the amendments, Black suffrage and the end of slavery.
H: What would make it unfinished? Consider land, violence, the courts and the withdrawal of troops.
A: Support for "revolution": the end of slavery without compensation and the 13th–15th Amendments; Black citizenship and suffrage within a decade of slavery; about 1,500 Black officeholders; new state constitutions and public schools; a constitutional shift of power from states to the federal government. Support for "unfinished": no land reform; sharecropping; violence; the courts' narrow readings; Redeemers; disfranchisement from the 1890s; the civil rights movement (the "Second Reconstruction") had to finish the work in the 1950s–60s. Challenge: some historians say "revolution" overstates a movement that Northern whites accepted only partially and that never aimed at economic equality; others say the term "failure" fits better. A* judgement: "revolution" fits the constitutional and political change; "unfinished" fits what happened to it. The description is supported by the evidence, provided "unfinished" is read as "overturned in practice" as well as "incomplete".

:::recap
- **Problems in 1865**: a devastated South, about four million freed people, no agreed plan; Radicals (Stevens, Sumner), moderates, Conservatives (Johnson, most Democrats) and Copperheads disagreed.
- **Lincoln**: Ten Percent Plan (8 December 1863); Wade–Davis Bill (pocket veto 4 July 1864) and the Wade–Davis Manifesto; 13th Amendment through the House 31 January 1865; Freedmen's Bureau 3 March 1865; shot 14 April, died 15 April 1865.
- **Johnson**: Amnesty (29 May 1865), provisional governors, white-only conventions; Black Codes; vetoes of the Freedmen's Bureau and Civil Rights bills; Civil Rights Act over his veto (9 April 1866); 1866 elections gave the Republicans two-thirds.
- **Radical Reconstruction**: Reconstruction Acts (2 March 1867), five military districts, Black male suffrage; Tenure of Office Act; impeachment (24 February 1868; acquitted 35–19).
- **Amendments**: 13th (6 December 1865), 14th (9 July 1868), 15th (3 February 1870), each later undermined (convict leasing; *Slaughter-House*, *Cruikshank*, *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896, which held that "separate but equal" segregation is lawful); disfranchisement from the 1890s).
- **Freed people**: Freedmen's Bureau (1865–72), schools and churches, sharecropping, about 1,500 officeholders, Revels (1870).
- **White resistance**: Black Codes, Klan (from 1866), White League and Red Shirts, Colfax (13 April 1873), Mississippi Plan (1875); carpetbaggers and scalawags; Redeemers controlled every Southern state by 1877.
- **Grant**: 15th Amendment; Enforcement Acts and Klan Act (1870–71); Amnesty Act (22 May 1872); Civil Rights Act (1 March 1875); Panic of 1873; refusal of troops in Mississippi (1875).
- **1877**: disputed election, Electoral Commission 8–7, Hayes president (2 March), troops out of the last state houses in April.
- **Judgement**: success in law and for a decade in politics, failure to secure rights, land and protection; resistance was the means, Northern withdrawal the reason it won.
