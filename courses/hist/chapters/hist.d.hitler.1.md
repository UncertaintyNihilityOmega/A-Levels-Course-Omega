:::why
On 30 January 1933 Adolf Hitler became Chancellor of Germany. Eighteen months later he was Führer, with no parliament, no rival party and no limit on his power, and within eleven years he had led Europe into the worst war and the worst genocide in its history. ## The Weimar Republic in 1929

To understand why the Republic broke, you need to know how it was built. The German Empire ended with defeat in November 1918, and the new republic was named after the city of Weimar, where the National Assembly met in 1919 to write its constitution. By 1929 it had survived a revolution, a hyperinflation and an occupation of the Ruhr, and from 1924 it had enjoyed five years of calmer times under the Dawes Plan and the diplomacy of Gustav Stresemann. The Nazi Party, a small party led by Hitler since 1921, had been banned after the failed **Beer Hall Putsch** of 8–9 November 1923, and Hitler had served just over a year of a five-year prison sentence for treason (he wrote the first volume of *Mein Kampf* there). After his release he re-founded the party and decided to win power through elections. In the Reichstag election of May 1928 the Nazis won **2.6 per cent** of the vote and 12 seats out of 491. In 1929 few people thought the party mattered.

Four features of the constitution matter for this chapter.

- **Proportional representation.** Seats went to parties in proportion to their votes. This gave every party a voice, but it produced a Reichstag of many parties, none with a majority, and governments were coalitions that often broke apart.
- **The President.** The Reich President was elected directly for seven years. He appointed and dismissed the Chancellor (Article 53) and could dissolve the Reichstag (Article 25). Since 1925 the President had been **Paul von Hindenburg**, the field marshal of the First World War, who was 77 when elected and 85 when he appointed Hitler.
- **Article 48.** The President could, "if public safety and order are seriously disturbed", take the measures needed to restore them, including suspending basic rights and using the army. The Reichstag could demand that the measures be cancelled, but the President could answer by dissolving it.
- **The Chancellor's two masters.** A Chancellor needed the Reichstag's confidence, but he also needed the President's trust, and in the Depression the second counted for more than the first.

:::def Article 48
The article of the Weimar constitution that let the Reich President issue emergency decrees with the force of law, and suspend basic rights, when public safety and order were seriously disturbed. From 1930 it was used to govern without a Reichstag majority, which taught Germans to accept rule by decree before Hitler took office.
:::

:::def Presidential government
Rule by a Chancellor who depended on the confidence of the Reich President and on Article 48 emergency decrees instead of on a majority in the Reichstag. It began with Brüning in March 1930 and continued under Papen and Schleicher until Hitler's appointment.
:::

## Problems facing Weimar governments

The syllabus names three problems: the impact of the Great Depression, the unpopularity of the governments of Müller and Brüning, and the rise of communism. Each helped the Nazis, but none of them made Hitler Chancellor by itself.

### The impact of the Great Depression

Germany was vulnerable because it had recovered after 1924 on borrowed money. American banks lent large sums to German cities and companies, and the money paid for reparations and for investment. In 1929 American lenders began to pull back to invest in the Wall Street boom, and after the Crash of 24–29 October they called in short-term loans. German firms and banks that had relied on the loans lost their credit, and the economy, already weak in agriculture and in some industries, fell.

The scale of the fall can be shown in a few figures. Registered unemployment averaged about **1.9 million** in 1929, about **3.1 million** in 1930, about **4.5 million** in 1931 and about **5.6 million** in 1932, with more than six million out of work in the worst winter months. Industrial production in 1932 was about 40 per cent below its 1929 level, and wages and prices fell with it. In 1933 the average figure fell to about 4.8 million, because the slump had passed its worst point and, from the spring, the new government began its work-creation schemes (see [Hitler's Germany (3)](#hist.d.hitler.3)).

- **Industrial workers** lost jobs or hours, and unemployment benefit (Germany's insurance system, set up in 1927) ran out after a limited period, after which people had to rely on poor relief from the towns, whose finances were collapsing. Young men with no job and no prospect were the easiest to recruit to the paramilitary groups of the left and right.
- **The middle classes** (shopkeepers, craftsmen, clerks, civil servants) feared a repeat of 1923, when inflation had destroyed their savings. They saw the Depression as a second blow and blamed a Republic that seemed unable to protect them.
- **Farmers** had been suffering from low prices since the mid-1920s and were heavily in debt; many lost their land to forced sales in 1930–32, and rural Protestant areas became some of the Nazis' strongest.
- **Business** was hit by the collapse of credit. In July 1931 the Danat Bank and other major banks failed, and the government closed the banks for a time.

The Depression did two things. It destroyed faith in the Republic's ability to deliver prosperity, and it made voters ready to try something different. It did not make a Nazi government certain, since other countries (Britain, France, the USA) suffered badly and kept their democracies. The Depression is best seen as a **necessary condition**, because without it the Nazis would have remained a small party, and not a **sufficient** one.

### The unpopularity of Müller's and Brüning's governments

**Hermann Müller**, a Social Democrat (SPD), led a "grand coalition" from June 1928 that included the SPD, the Centre Party (the Catholic party), the liberal parties and the Bavarian People's Party, and it had a majority in the Reichstag. It broke up over the cost of the unemployment insurance fund, which began to run into deficit as unemployment rose. The SPD wanted higher contributions from workers and employers, and the German People's Party (DVP), the party of business, wanted to cut benefits instead. The parties could not agree, and Müller resigned on **27 March 1930**. He had asked Hindenburg for the right to use Article 48, and Hindenburg had refused. It was the last government to rest on a majority in the Reichstag before 1933. Its fall showed how far party self-interest could damage a democracy in a crisis, and many Germans concluded that parliamentary parties were bickering while the country sank.

**Heinrich Brüning** of the Centre Party became Chancellor on **30 March 1930**, chosen by Hindenburg and the circle around him (including General Kurt von Schleicher of the Reichswehr ministry; the Reichswehr was the German army of 100,000 men allowed by the Treaty of Versailles) to govern without the SPD. He had no majority and did not want one: he aimed to rule by presidential decree and to end reparations by showing that Germany could not pay them. His policy was **deflation**: to balance the budget he cut public spending, wages and benefits and raised taxes, which would restore confidence and exports but, in the middle of a slump, made unemployment worse. Critics called him the "hunger chancellor". When the Reichstag rejected his budget in July 1930, he issued it by decree, the Reichstag voted to cancel the decree, and Brüning dissolved it and called the election of 14 September 1930.

The result was a disaster for the Republic. The Nazis rose from 12 seats to 107 and the Communists from 54 to 77. From then on Brüning relied on the SPD, which "tolerated" his government because it feared that a new election would favour the Nazis. The use of Article 48 rose from 5 decrees in 1930 to 44 in 1931. Brüning's other measures brought him no popularity: a decree of December 1931 ordered further cuts in wages and prices, and he was widely blamed for the suffering. He fell, not from a vote in the Reichstag, but because Hindenburg withdrew his trust. After Hindenburg's re-election in April 1932 (below), the President, who had been angered by Brüning's ban on the SA and SS (the Nazi Party's Brownshirt army, and its black-uniformed guard under Heinrich Himmler, then still part of the SA) on 13 April and by his plan to break up bankrupt estates in eastern Germany, which the landowners attacked as "agrarian Bolshevism", dismissed him on **30 May 1932**.

:::key Why the governments of 1930–32 helped the Nazis
1. **Democracy without a majority.** After March 1930 no Chancellor had a Reichstag majority, and the Reichstag was used less and less; Germans grew used to rule by decree.
2. **Suffering without relief.** Brüning's deflation deepened the slump, and the Republic's leaders seemed indifferent.
3. **Party quarrels.** The fall of Müller's coalition over a small sum showed the parties putting interests before the country.
4. **A President with the real power.** The key decisions were taken by an old man and his advisers, which meant the future depended on their choices and not on the voters.
:::

### The rise of communism

The Communist Party (KPD), led by **Ernst Thälmann**, grew with unemployment. It had 10.6 per cent of the vote in 1928, 13.1 per cent in 1930, 14.3 per cent in July 1932 and **16.9 per cent** in November 1932, when it had 100 seats. It was strongest among the unemployed in the big cities, since the SPD, tied to the trade unions, had few members among the jobless. Moscow's policy was that the SPD was the main enemy, a party of "social fascists", and so the KPD refused to work with the SPD, even against the Nazis. In Berlin in November 1932 the KPD and the Nazis even joined a strike against the city's transport company.

The growth of communism helped Hitler in three ways.

1. **Fear.** Many middle-class and business people, and the army and landowning elite, feared a Communist revolution after the Soviet example, and the Nazis, who promised to crush the KPD, could present themselves as the shield against it. The Nazis and the right-wing press used street fights, which the KPD's Red Front Fighters also fought, as evidence of a threat.
2. **A divided left.** The SPD and the KPD together won more votes than the Nazis in November 1932 (20.4 plus 16.9 per cent against 33.1), but they would not act together, and the Communists helped weaken the democratic defence.
3. **A negative majority.** In July 1932 the Nazis (230 seats) and the Communists (89), both enemies of the Republic, together held 319 of 608 seats, more than half. In November the figure was 296 of 584. No government could be formed that had a majority in the Reichstag unless it included one of them, so the Reichstag could not work.

:::warn The Depression does not explain everything
Do not write "the Depression caused Hitler" as if it were the whole answer. The Nazis were a party with a leader and a programme from the 1920s; they won few votes when times were good (2.6 per cent in 1928). Other democracies survived the slump. The Depression explains why so many Germans abandoned the Republic, but not why Hindenburg appointed Hitler in January 1933, when the Nazi vote had been falling and the worst of the slump was passing.
:::

### The appeal of Hitler

Hitler's greatest asset was himself. The Nazi Party's programme of 1920 was vague, and it was Hitler's personal image that held its supporters together. Ian Kershaw, in *The 'Hitler Myth'* (1987) and in his biography *Hitler* (1998, 2000), has argued that a "Führer myth" was created in these years: Hitler was presented as a man above party and above class, a simple soldier and a saviour who stood outside the squabbling of the Weimar parties.

Several things made it work.

- **His speaking.** Hitler was a powerful speaker who could suit his words to an audience, with long pauses, rising passion and slogans that blamed the "November criminals" (those who had signed the Armistice of November 1918), the Jews, the Communists and the Treaty of Versailles. He spoke of national renewal, "work and bread" and the end of the humiliation of 1919.
- **A new kind of campaign.** In 1932 he flew from city to city, addressing several huge rallies in a day, in the campaign called "Hitler over Germany". The aeroplane gave him an air of modernity and energy, and let him reach many more voters than the other leaders could.
- **Simple answers.** He offered one cause for every misery (the Jews, Marxists and the "system") and one remedy (a strong leader and a united people), at a time when the democratic parties could only offer cuts.
- **The "legal path".** After 1923 Hitler said that the party would win power by voting. On 25 September 1930 he gave evidence at the trial of three army officers for Nazi activity at Leipzig and swore that the party would come to power by legal means. This reassured conservatives and army officers, who wanted a movement of order and not a street rebellion. The party's violence continued, so the promise was partly a screen.
- **The Young Plan referendum.** In 1929 Hitler joined Alfred Hugenberg, the leader of the nationalist DNVP, in a campaign against the Young Plan, which reduced reparations but extended them until 1988. The referendum of 22 December 1929 failed, as only 13.8 per cent of voters supported it (50 per cent were needed), but it gave Hitler something he lacked, national publicity and respectability in the nationalist camp.
- **Citizenship.** Hitler, who was Austrian by birth, became a German citizen in February 1932, by appointment to a minor post in Brunswick. This allowed him to stand in the presidential election.

But the appeal has limits. Hitler's personal rating was never the same as his party's; many who voted for him did so for his promises. He lost the presidential run-off in April 1932, and the Nazi vote fell in November. Catholics and the working class, the groups least touched by his appeal, remained largely loyal to the Centre, the BVP, the SPD and the KPD.

### The role of the SA

The **SA** (*Sturmabteilung*, "Storm Detachment"; the Brownshirts) was the party's paramilitary wing. It protected Nazi meetings, broke up rivals' meetings, marched through working-class districts and fought the Communist Red Front Fighters and the SPD's Reichsbanner. Its leader from 1931 was **Ernst Röhm**. It grew with the Depression, because it fed, clothed and housed unemployed young men in its "SA kitchens" and hostels. Its membership was about 60,000 in 1930, 77,000 in January 1931, 221,000 by November 1931 and **445,000 by August 1932**.

It helped the Nazis in three ways.

1. **Strength and discipline.** Its uniforms, marches and bands gave the movement a look of order, force and comradeship, and it attracted jobless young men who wanted a place to belong.
2. **Violence as a tool.** It intimidated opponents and made the streets of many cities unsafe for the left. On 17 July 1932 an SA march into the Communist district of Altona, in Hamburg, led to a battle in which eighteen people died. The Nazis could present such fighting as a defence against Communist threats, and the Papen government used Altona to justify its removal of the Prussian government three days later.
3. **A reserve of power.** Conservative leaders such as Papen believed that the SA could be used or controlled, and feared what it might do if the Nazis were shut out of office.

The SA also cost the Nazis votes, since its violence appalled many middle-class and Catholic voters, and Brüning's government banned it on 13 April 1932. Papen lifted the ban on 16 June 1932. Röhm's wish to make the SA the basis of a new army was to become a problem for Hitler in 1934 (below).

### Nazi propaganda and promises

**Joseph Goebbels**, who became Gauleiter (party leader) of Berlin in 1926 and the party's national director of propaganda in 1930, ran the most modern campaigns in Germany. The Nazis used mass rallies, torchlight marches, posters, leaflets, films and sound trucks, and edited their own newspapers (in Berlin, *Der Angriff*). Goebbels understood that propaganda works by simple slogans repeated and by emotion. One famous 1932 poster read "Our last hope: Hitler".

The Nazis' great trick was to give different groups different promises. The 1920 programme of "25 points" contained a mix of nationalism, anti-Jewish measures and socialist-sounding demands. From 1930 the party tailored its message.

| Group | What the Nazis promised |
|---|---|
| Unemployed workers | "Work and bread"; jobs through public works and rearmament |
| Farmers | Protection from debt, higher prices, security on the land |
| Small business and the middle class | Protection against big stores and big business, and against Communism |
| Nationalists and veterans | An end to Versailles and reparations; a strong army; national pride |
| Big business and the elite | Destruction of the Marxist left; order |
| Everyone | A "people's community" (*Volksgemeinschaft*) in which class conflict would end |

The promises were contradictory, which did not matter in opposition. Their effect was to make the Nazis a **catch-all party of protest**, as the American historian Thomas Childers argued in *The Nazi Voter* (1983). Childers found that the Nazis' strongest support was among Protestants, farmers and the lower middle class of the small towns, but that they also gained support among workers and the young; the Catholic parties and the left kept most of their voters. The scapegoats, above all the Jews, also gave the propaganda a target. It is worth noting that the party did not win elections on antisemitism alone: voters seem to have been attracted by promises of national revival and work, and many put up with the antisemitism.

### Elite support

The Nazis were helped by powerful men, though not as much as some have claimed. It is helpful to separate three kinds of elite support.

- **Press and politics.** Alfred Hugenberg, the leader of the DNVP and owner of a large press and film empire, joined the Nazis in the Young Plan campaign in 1929 and in the "Harzburg Front" of October 1931, giving Hitler access to a national audience and to the respectable right.
- **Business.** Some businessmen, among them the former Reichsbank president Hjalmar Schacht and the industrialist Fritz Thyssen, supported Hitler, and a "Keppler Circle" of business supporters was formed in 1932 to gather support. In November 1932 about twenty businessmen, bankers and landowners signed a petition to Hindenburg, dated 19 November, which asked him to make Hitler Chancellor. On 20 February 1933, after Hitler had taken office, he and Göring met leading industrialists, and about three million marks were pledged for the Nazi election campaign (the figure comes from evidence at the Nuremberg trials). Historians disagree about how much it mattered. Older, mostly Marxist accounts saw Hitler as the tool of big business. Henry Turner, in *German Big Business and the Rise of Hitler* (1985), argued that big business gave the Nazis little money before 1933, that most of it went to other parties, and that most business leaders preferred Papen. On Turner's view the elite's importance lay in its political influence and not in its cash.
- **The army, the landowners and the President's circle.** The Reichswehr, the old Prussian landowners (the Junkers) and the advisers around Hindenburg distrusted democracy and wanted an authoritarian government. They saw the Nazis as a mass movement that could give such a government popular support, and they also feared Communism. Their decision, in January 1933, to bring Hitler in was the decisive act of elite support.

:::key Four reasons for the growth of Nazi support, 1929–32
1. **Hitler**: a new type of leader above party, a great speaker and campaigner, who promised a legal road to power and national renewal.
2. **The SA**: a mass of 445,000 by August 1932 that gave the party force, uniforms and a means of intimidation, but also cost it some votes.
3. **Propaganda and promises**: modern campaigns under Goebbels, with different promises for each group, which made a protest party into a catch-all one.
4. **Elite support**: the press of Hugenberg, the money and prestige of some business leaders, and the conservatives' belief that they could use the Nazis.
Behind all four was the **Depression**: without mass unemployment and fear, none of them would have worked.
:::

## Hitler's appointment as Chancellor, 1933

By the summer of 1932 the Nazis were the largest party but had no majority, and they could come to power only if the President appointed Hitler Chancellor. The story of 1932 is how that happened.

### The elections of 1932

1932 was a year of four national votes: two rounds of a presidential election and two Reichstag elections. Together they show how the Nazi vote rose and then fell.

**The presidential election.** Hindenburg's seven-year term was to end in 1932, and Brüning, who feared that a contest would be a Nazi triumph, tried to extend it by a vote of the Reichstag, which failed. Hindenburg, aged 84, stood again, supported by the Centre, the SPD and the liberals, who saw him as the barrier against Hitler. The first round on 13 March gave Hindenburg about 49.6 per cent (a shade short of a majority), Hitler about 30.1 per cent, Thälmann about 13.2 per cent and the nationalist Theodor Duesterberg about 6.8 per cent. In the second round on **10 April** Hindenburg won **53.0 per cent** and Hitler **36.8 per cent**, with Thälmann on 10.2 per cent. Hitler lost, but the vote of over 13 million for him showed the strength of the movement. The SPD voters who backed Hindenburg were then dismayed when he dismissed Brüning, the man they had supported, in May.

**July 1932.** The new election on **31 July 1932** gave the Nazis **37.3 per cent** and **230 seats** in a Reichstag of 608, the largest party, with the SPD second at 133. It was the Nazis' best result in a free election. Hitler demanded the Chancellorship on 13 August and was offered only the Vice-Chancellorship, which he refused: Hindenburg refused to hand the government to a party that, he feared, would rule as a one-party dictatorship. Hitler left the meeting humiliated.

**November 1932.** The Reichstag was dissolved again on 12 September after a vote of no confidence in Papen by 512 votes to 42. In the election of **6 November 1932** the Nazis fell to **33.1 per cent** and **196 seats**, a loss of about 2 million votes (from 13.7 million to 11.7 million) and 34 seats. Voters seem to have been tired of elections, put off by the SA's violence and by the party's strike with the Communists in Berlin, and encouraged by signs that the economy was bottoming out. The Communists gained and reached 100 seats. The party's funds were low, and its leaders were despondent. Many observers concluded that the Nazi tide had turned.

:::key The significance of the 1932 elections
1. **The Nazis were the largest party but never held a majority.** The highest figure in a free election was 37.3 per cent.
2. **The tide turned in November 1932.** This matters because it made Hitler's appointment less obviously the result of popular pressure, and more the result of a decision by the elite.
3. **Elections could not solve the crisis.** A Reichstag with a hostile majority of Nazis and Communists could not support any government, so the President ruled through his chosen Chancellors.
:::

### Papen's government

After Brüning's dismissal Hindenburg appointed **Franz von Papen**, a Catholic aristocrat and former cavalry officer who had been a member of the Centre Party and had hardly any following in it (he left the party under pressure when it would not support his appointment). He took office on **1 June 1932**. His cabinet was nicknamed the "cabinet of barons", because most of its ministers were nobles or businessmen. Papen had no majority in the Reichstag and did not seek one; he wanted a new, authoritarian constitution with a reduced role for parliament. He had been recommended to Hindenburg by General **Kurt von Schleicher**, the Reichswehr minister, who thought that a conservative government would be able to win the Nazis' support.

Papen's government took three steps that helped Hitler.

1. **It lifted the ban on the SA and SS (16 June 1932)**, a concession to the Nazis, and the result was a wave of street violence in the summer.
2. **It removed the Prussian government on 20 July 1932.** Papen used an emergency decree to dismiss the Social Democratic government of Prussia, the largest German state, and made himself Reich Commissioner for Prussia, claiming that it could not keep order after Altona. The SPD and the trade unions did not resist, which showed their weakness. Hitler would later control the Prussian police under Göring, which helped his consolidation.
3. **It failed to win support.** The Reichstag showed its contempt in September by a vote of no confidence that Papen's side lost 512 to 42.

By November Papen had called two elections and had no more to show. Hindenburg liked him, but Schleicher now concluded that the Papen government could not last, and that the army would be unable to defeat a Nazi and Communist insurrection together, a view that was supported by a staff exercise in December.

### Schleicher's government

On **3 December 1932** Schleicher himself became Chancellor, the only general to hold the post in the Republic. He tried a different tactic, which the press called the **Querfront** ("cross-front"): to build a broad base that would include the trade unions and the left wing of the Nazi Party. He offered the Vice-Chancellorship to **Gregor Strasser**, a senior Nazi who favoured cooperation and who had broken with Hitler's all-or-nothing tactics. Strasser, however, was undermined by Hitler and resigned from all his party posts on **8 December 1932**, and the scheme failed. The trade unions and the SPD distrusted Schleicher, and the industrialists and landowners disliked his plans for public works and for agrarian reform. He had no majority, and when on **28 January 1933** he asked Hindenburg for an emergency decree to dissolve the Reichstag and rule without it, Hindenburg refused, and he resigned the same day.

### The deal of January 1933

Meanwhile Papen, who was angry at Schleicher for his dismissal, set out to bring him down. On **4 January 1933** he met Hitler at the Cologne home of the banker Kurt von Schröder. Hitler wanted the Chancellorship; Papen, as Vice-Chancellor, hoped to control him. The talks were kept secret at first, but they were in the press within a day. Over the following weeks Papen won the support of Hugenberg and of Hindenburg's son Oskar and the head of the President's office, Otto Meissner, who were close to the President. The landowners, who were angry at Schleicher's plans, lobbied Hindenburg. Hindenburg, who had refused Hitler's demands in August, now accepted a coalition in which the Nazis would be a minority, the Reichswehr minister would be General Werner von Blomberg (chosen by Hindenburg) and Papen would be Vice-Chancellor.

At about midday on **30 January 1933** Hitler took the oath as Chancellor. Of the eleven ministers in the cabinet, only three were Nazis: Hitler himself, **Wilhelm Frick** (Interior) and **Hermann Göring** (minister without portfolio, and Prussian Interior Minister, which gave him control of the Prussian police). The rest were conservatives: Papen, Hugenberg (economics and agriculture), Blomberg and others. Papen and his colleagues were confident that they had boxed Hitler in and would use his popularity for their own ends. That evening, the SA and the party marched in torchlight processions through Berlin.

:::def Machtergreifung
The Nazis' own word (the "seizure of power") for 30 January 1933. Many historians prefer to speak of a "handover" of power, because Hitler was appointed by Hindenburg in a legal way and not by a coup or an election victory.
:::

Four men took the decisive steps: Hindenburg, Papen, Schleicher and Hitler. Their ages in January 1933 are worth remembering: Hindenburg was 85, Papen 53, Schleicher 50 and Hitler 43.

:::key Why was Hitler appointed Chancellor in January 1933?
1. **The Depression** created mass support for extremist parties and made democratic government unworkable.
2. **No majority was possible.** The Nazis and Communists together held more than half the seats, so the Reichstag could not support any government, and the President's circle controlled the choice.
3. **The choices of the elite.** Hindenburg and his advisers feared communism, disliked democracy and wanted a government with mass support. Papen thought that he could control Hitler. The deal, not an election win, put him in office.
4. **The failure of Papen's and Schleicher's governments.** Each lacked support, and Schleicher made enemies of the landowners and of Papen.
5. **Hitler's own tactics.** He refused to join any government unless he led it. In August 1932, when he was offered less, he held out, and it paid off.
:::

:::warn Appointed, not elected
Never write that Hitler "was elected" in 1933, or that the German people "voted him in". He never won a majority in a free election (his best was 37.3 per cent in July 1932), his vote had fallen in November 1932, and he was **appointed** by the President under the constitution. The people who appointed him were Hindenburg and a small circle. The election of March 1933 took place after he was in office, and it was not free.
:::

## Consolidation of power and the creation of a dictatorship, 1933–34

Hitler had been given a coalition government of conservatives and a minority of Nazis, in a Reichstag with no Nazi majority. Within eighteen months he had become dictator. The sequence is the key: each step used the previous one.

### The Reichstag Fire and subsequent repression

Hitler at once asked Hindenburg to dissolve the Reichstag and called a new election for **5 March 1933**. In February, Göring, as Prussian Interior Minister, dismissed many police chiefs and on 22 February made 50,000 members of the SA, SS and the nationalist Stahlhelm (an ex-servicemen's league) "auxiliary police" in Prussia. The Nazis campaigned with the whole machinery of the state behind them.

On the night of **27 February 1933** the Reichstag building in Berlin was set on fire. A young Dutch Communist, **Marinus van der Lubbe**, was found inside the building. The Nazis said it was the start of a Communist uprising. Most historians, among them Kershaw and Evans, accept that van der Lubbe set the fire himself and alone, though a minority has argued that the Nazis may have been involved, and the debate has continued. Whatever the cause, Hitler's government used it at once. Van der Lubbe was tried in Leipzig, found guilty and executed in January 1934; the Communist leaders tried with him were acquitted.

On **28 February** Hindenburg signed, on Hitler's advice, the **Decree for the Protection of People and State**, known as the **Reichstag Fire Decree**.

:::note The Reichstag Fire Decree: provenance and use
*Adapted from the Decree of the Reich President for the Protection of the People and State ("Reichstag Fire Decree"), 28 February 1933, section 1, in the English translation from the Nuremberg trial documents (1390-PS), as printed by German History in Documents and Images.*

"Articles 114, 115, 117, 118, 123, 124, and 153 of the Constitution of the German Reich are suspended until further notice. Thus, restrictions on personal liberty, on the right to free expression of opinion including freedom of the press, on the right to assembly and the right to association; interventions into the privacy of communications by post, telegraph, and telephone; warrants for house searches, orders for confiscations, and restrictions on property, also beyond the legal limits otherwise prescribed, are permitted."

**How to use it as evidence:** it is an official text, signed by the President, so it shows the legal form that the repression took, and it shows how wide the powers were: it suspended seven articles of the constitution "until further notice", and the Nazi regime never lifted it. It does not tell you who started the fire, and it is no evidence of popular feeling. It was the legal basis of the "protective custody" (arrest without trial) that sent thousands of Communists, Social Democrats and others to prison and to the first camps.
:::

The decree was used at once. In the days before the election the police and SA arrested about 4,000 Communist leaders and members, including Thälmann, and closed the left-wing press, and the Nazis broke up the meetings of other parties. The KPD was not banned formally, but its leaders were in custody or in flight. At the same time Hitler's government was not yet free of its conservative partners, and the decree was in the name of the President.

### The March 1933 election

The election of **5 March 1933** was held in an atmosphere of fear, with the left silenced and the Nazis in control of the radio. The Nazis won **43.9 per cent** (17.3 million votes) and **288 seats** out of 647. It was their best result, but it was not a majority: the party was still 36 seats short of the 324 needed, and the SPD, the Centre and the BVP held their vote (the SPD got 18.3 per cent and the Centre 11.3 per cent). The Nazis needed the DNVP's 52 seats to have a majority (288 plus 52 is 340). The Communists won 81 seats, but their deputies were arrested or in hiding and the seats were never taken up. Hitler could rule in the Reichstag with his conservative allies, but he wanted more: the power to make laws without the Reichstag.

### The Enabling Act

The **Enabling Act**, formally the *Law to Remedy the Distress of People and Reich*, gave the cabinet the right to pass laws, including laws that broke the constitution, without the Reichstag. A change in the constitution needed a two-thirds majority, with at least two-thirds of the members present. The Nazis, even with the DNVP, did not have this, and Hitler had to win the votes of other parties, or stop them voting.

The Reichstag met on **23 March 1933** in the Kroll Opera House in Berlin, because the Reichstag building had been burned. The hall was ringed by SA and SS men in uniform, and the Nazi deputies were reported to have shouted threats. Hitler used three methods.

1. **Removing opponents.** The 81 Communist deputies could not attend. Some SPD deputies (26) were in custody or in hiding. Göring, as President of the Reichstag, ruled that the 81 Communist seats need not be counted when working out the two-thirds requirement, which lowered it from 432 to 378.
2. **Persuasion and promises.** Hitler promised that the Act would be used only "to the extent necessary", that it would not threaten the position of the Reichstag, the Reichsrat (the upper house, which represented the states) or the President, and that the churches and their schools would be respected. The **Centre Party**, led by Monsignor Ludwig Kaas, was reassured by these assurances, by the hope of protecting its Catholic supporters and by a promised guarantee in writing that never came.
3. **Fear.** The SA and the arrests in the previous weeks showed what opposition might cost.

The vote was **444 in favour and 94 against**, and all 94 against came from the **Social Democrats**. Their leader **Otto Wels** made a brave speech, defending the Republic, in which he said that the Nazis might take their lives and their freedom but could not take their honour. Even with the 81 Communist seats counted, 444 votes were more than two-thirds of all 647 members (432), so the Act passed whichever way the count is made, though only because so many opponents were absent.

:::note The Enabling Act: provenance and use
*Adapted from the Law to Remove the Distress of the People and the State (the Enabling Act), promulgated 24 March 1933, Articles 1, 2 and 5, in the English translation (U.S. Department of State, 1943) printed by German History in Documents and Images.*

"Article 1. National laws can be enacted by the Reich Cabinet as well as in accordance with the procedure established in the Constitution." … "Article 2. The national laws enacted by the Reich Cabinet may deviate from the Constitution as long as they do not affect the institution of the Reichstag and the Reichsrat. The powers of the President remain unaffected." … "Article 5. … It becomes invalid on April 1, 1937; it also becomes invalid if the present Reich Cabinet is replaced by another."

**How to use it as evidence:** the text shows the "legal" form of the revolution: laws were made by the cabinet, not by the Reichstag, and could overturn the constitution, with two limits that look like safeguards (the Reichstag and the President). Both limits disappeared in practice. Note that it was valid for four years, and the Nazis renewed it in 1937 and 1939, and in 1943 Hitler simply extended it without a vote. The text does not show the pressure in the room, and it cannot tell you how many deputies voted freely.
:::

The consequences were large. From now on Hitler's cabinet could pass laws without the Reichstag, so the Reichstag no longer mattered, and Hitler's government could pass laws that broke the constitution. Hindenburg's emergency powers were no longer needed. The Act was the legal base of the dictatorship, and it served until 1945.

### Gleichschaltung

**Gleichschaltung** ("coordination", or bringing into line) was the process by which the Nazis took control of every part of political and social life in 1933–34.

:::def Gleichschaltung
The Nazi policy of "coordination" in 1933–34: bringing the states, the civil service, the trade unions, political parties, professional bodies and clubs into line with Nazi control, by laws, pressure and force, so that no independent organisation was left.
:::

- **The states (Länder).** The Law of 31 March 1933 dissolved the state parliaments and reconstituted them according to the Reichstag vote of 5 March, which gave the Nazis control. A second law of 7 April 1933 appointed Reich Governors (*Reichsstatthalter*), nearly all Nazi Gauleiter, to run each state. In January 1934 the state parliaments were abolished and the states lost their sovereignty.
- **The civil service and the courts.** The **Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service** of 7 April 1933 allowed the dismissal of civil servants who were Jewish or considered politically unreliable (with exceptions for veterans of the war, which Hindenburg had insisted on). It purged the state of its critics and began the legal persecution of the Jews.
- **The trade unions.** On 1 May 1933 the government made May Day a paid national holiday, the "Day of National Labour", and celebrated it with huge rallies, which lulled the unions. The next morning, on **2 May**, SA and SS men occupied union offices across Germany and arrested union leaders. The unions' funds were seized, and on 10 May all workers were enrolled in the Nazi German Labour Front (DAF) under Robert Ley.
- **The parties.** The KPD was already crushed. The SPD was banned on **22 June 1933**. The other parties, under pressure, dissolved themselves in the next days: the DNVP on 27 June, the liberal parties in late June and early July, and the Centre Party on **5 July 1933**. On 20 July the Vatican signed a Concordat with Hitler that guaranteed the Catholic Church's rights in exchange for its withdrawal from politics. A law of **14 July 1933** made the Nazi Party the only legal party in Germany.
- **Other bodies.** Churches, youth groups, sports clubs, professional associations and the press were taken over or closed (see [Hitler's Germany (2)](#hist.d.hitler.2) for propaganda and control).

By the end of 1933, Germany was a one-party state. The use of violence was selective but enough: in the first months of 1933 tens of thousands of people were held in "protective custody" in early camps run by the SA and SS, and Dachau, near Munich, opened on 22 March 1933.

### The Night of the Long Knives

The Nazi revolution still had a conflict at its centre. The SA, now about three million strong (it had absorbed other groups such as the Stahlhelm), included many who had expected a "second revolution": jobs, power and the end of the old elites. **Röhm**, the SA's chief of staff and a minister in the government since December 1933, wanted the SA to become a people's army that would absorb the Reichswehr of 100,000 men. This alarmed the army's leaders, who were Hitler's allies, and Hitler's plans for rearmament needed their skills. It also disturbed conservatives and businessmen, and it threatened Hitler's own plan to succeed the ailing Hindenburg as head of state with the army's support. (According to later accounts, in April 1934 Hitler and the Reichswehr leaders reached an understanding that the army would back his succession if he curbed the SA.) On 17 June 1934 Papen, in a speech at Marburg University, warned of a "second revolution" and of terror, and the army's leaders, led by Blomberg, put pressure on Hitler.

On **30 June 1934** Hitler acted. He flew to Munich, arrested SA leaders at a hotel at Bad Wiessee and had several of them shot that day; in Berlin, Göring and the SS under Himmler, with Himmler's security chief Reinhard Heydrich, killed others according to lists drawn up beforehand. **Röhm** was held until 1 July and then shot in Stadelheim prison after refusing to kill himself. The killing went on until 2 July. The victims were not only SA leaders: they included General Kurt von Schleicher and his wife, Gregor Strasser, the former Bavarian state commissioner Gustav von Kahr, the leader of Catholic Action Erich Klausener, and two of Papen's close colleagues, **Herbert von Bose** and **Edgar Jung** (the author of the Marburg speech). Papen himself was held under house arrest. Estimates of the dead differ: the names of at least **85** victims are known, and historians' estimates run higher (Kershaw gives 150–200). The event is called the **Night of the Long Knives**, a phrase borrowed from a Nazi song.

The regime made the murders legal after the event. On **3 July 1934** the cabinet passed a law that declared the measures of 30 June to 2 July "legal as acts of state self-defence". In a Reichstag speech on 13 July Hitler called himself the "supreme judge" of the German people, and the jurist Carl Schmitt wrote in August that "the Führer protects the law". The Reichswehr, delighted that the SA had been crushed, said nothing about the murder of two of its generals (Schleicher and Ferdinand von Bredow). Blomberg congratulated Hitler, and a telegram of thanks, in Hindenburg's name, was published. (Göring later admitted at Nuremberg that Hindenburg never saw it.)

The consequences were great.

1. **The SA lost its power.** It remained as a minor body, while the **SS**, formerly part of the SA, became independent on 20 July 1934 and grew under Himmler.
2. **Hitler gained the army.** The Reichswehr leaders had a stake in his success.
3. **Murder was legalised.** Hitler showed that the head of state could kill without trial and make it lawful, and the state's judges and officials did not protest. This also removed most conservative critics: Papen's circle was warned off.
4. **Hitler became the one source of authority.** After the purge no one in the party could challenge him. The "second revolution" was over.

### Hindenburg's death and the army oath

Hindenburg, aged 86, died on **2 August 1934** at his estate at Neudeck. The day before, the cabinet had passed a **Law on the Head of State of the German Reich**, which combined the offices of President and Chancellor on Hindenburg's death in Hitler's person. Hitler took the title **Führer and Reich Chancellor** and the powers of the head of state, including the command of the armed forces. This was against the constitution, and it was done by a law of the cabinet under the Enabling Act. On the same day the army was sworn to Hitler by name. This was a change: the old oath was to the people and the country; the new one was to a man, and the initiative came from the Reichswehr minister Blomberg and his colleagues, and not from Hitler.

:::note The army oath of 1934: provenance and use
*Adapted from the oath of the German armed forces sworn from 2 August 1934, with the earlier oath, in the English translation given by the Jewish Virtual Library (which cites the Joric Center). The German phrase is "unbedingten Gehorsam".*

The earlier oath: "I will at all times loyally and honestly serve my people and country …"

The oath of 2 August 1934: "I will render unconditional obedience to the Fuehrer of the German Reich and people, Adolf Hitler, Supreme Commander of the Wehrmacht, and, as a brave soldier, I will be ready at any time to stake my life for this oath."

(The Reichswehr was renamed the Wehrmacht, "defence force", in 1935; the oath text uses the later name, and the same army is meant.)

**How to use it as evidence:** it shows how the Reichswehr's loyalty was tied to Hitler as a person. A soldier who broke it could be punished as a traitor, which later made resistance harder, since officers who disliked Hitler's policies felt bound by the oath. It does not prove that the army leaders liked Nazi ideas; many wished to avoid trouble and to rearm. The translation comes from a secondary website, and the German original is in the official records of the armed forces.
:::

On **19 August 1934** a plebiscite asked voters to approve Hitler's new position, and about 90 per cent (38.4 million) voted yes. It was not free, but it was a sign that many Germans, as well as the regime, thought that the new order was legitimate. By August 1934 Hitler was dictator of a one-party state, with the army's oath, the SA broken and the SS rising, in just eighteen months.

:::def Führer
"Leader": the title Hitler took on 2 August 1934, as *Führer und Reichskanzler*, which joined the powers of the head of state and of the head of government and was based on the idea that his will was the highest law.
:::

### Was it a "legal revolution"?

The Nazis used the forms of law throughout. Hitler was appointed under the constitution; the Fire Decree and the Enabling Act were passed by legal procedures; the Law against the Formation of New Parties, the Reich Governors and the law of 3 July 1934 were all laws. Historians sometimes call this a **legal revolution**. The term is only partly fair. The laws were passed under terror: the SA and police arrests of thousands, the Communist deputies' absence and the threats in the Kroll Opera House. They were also used to destroy the legal order itself. So the better view is that Hitler used legal forms to carry out a revolution, with violence at each key step, and with the help of the conservatives who opened the door to him and of the army that stood aside.

| Step | Date | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Reichstag Fire Decree | 28 Feb 1933 | Suspended civil rights; the basis of arrests and terror |

| Gleichschaltung | Mar–Jul 1933 | States, civil service, unions and parties brought into line; one-party state by 14 Jul |
| Night of the Long Knives | 30 Jun–2 Jul 1934 | Broke the SA, won the army, killed critics, made murder lawful |
| Death of Hindenburg and oath | 2 Aug 1934 | Hitler became Führer; the army swore loyalty to him personally |
:::

## Why did Hitler gain control? Weighing the reasons

Questions on this topic ask you to rank reasons. Here are the main ones and a way to compare them.

| Reason | The case that it was decisive | The case against |
|---|---|---|
| The Depression | Mass unemployment (5.6 million in 1932) gave the Nazis their vote: 2.6 per cent in 1928, 37.3 per cent in July 1932 | The Nazi vote fell in November 1932 as the slump eased; other democracies survived; it does not explain the January 1933 appointment |
| Weakness of Weimar democracy | Proportional representation, Article 48 and the President's powers made rule by decree normal; no Chancellor had a majority after March 1930 | The Republic survived the crises of 1923; the structural flaws mattered because of the slump and the elite |
| The appeal of Hitler, the SA and propaganda | Hitler's leadership image, the modern campaigns, and a mass paramilitary that gave the movement strength | The Nazis never won a majority; Hitler lost the presidency; many Germans resisted the appeal |
| Elite support and the deal of 1933 | Hindenburg, Papen and the landowners and army chose Hitler; without them the Nazis could not have reached office | The elite did not make the Nazi vote; they chose Hitler only after the mass movement existed |
| The fear of communism | The KPD reached 16.9 per cent; many conservatives and voters feared revolution and saw the Nazis as the shield | The KPD was weaker than it seemed and was disunited; fear explains the elite's choice more than voters' |
| Mistakes of opponents | The SPD tolerated Brüning and did not resist the Prussian coup; the KPD refused to cooperate with the SPD; Papen and Schleicher quarrelled | The left had little room for manoeuvre against a hostile President and the army |

A strong answer separates **long-term conditions** (the defeat of 1918, the weak constitution, the legacy of inflation), **the trigger** (the Depression), **the Nazis' own strengths** (Hitler, the SA, propaganda) and **the decisions of 1932–33** (Hindenburg's and Papen's). It then argues which mattered most for *the question asked*. For the question "why was Hitler appointed Chancellor?" a defensible judgement is that the decisions of the elite, in the autumn and winter of 1932–33, were the decisive reason, because they were the last link in the chain and because the Nazis' electoral support had already peaked; but that the elite would not have made this choice without the mass movement the Depression had created, and so the two reasons depend on each other.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
Historians have given different weights to these reasons.
- **Older Marxist accounts** treated Nazism as the tool of capitalists who wanted to destroy the labour movement. **Henry Turner**, *German Big Business and the Rise of Hitler* (1985), argued from business records that big business gave the Nazis only limited help, and that Hitler's rise cannot be explained by capital.
- **Thomas Childers**, *The Nazi Voter* (1983), argued that the Nazis became a catch-all party of protest, drawing votes across classes but above all from Protestants and the lower middle class, and not from the Catholics and the organised working class.
- **Ian Kershaw**, *Hitler* (vol. 1, *Hubris*, 1998), stressed Hitler's appeal as a leader and the "Hitler myth", but also the choices of the conservative elite in the last weeks, which, on his account, were the decisive ones.
- **Richard Evans**, *The Coming of the Third Reich* (2003), gave a long account of the Republic's collapse in which the Depression, the elite's rejection of democracy and the Nazis' skill each had a place, and he emphasised how much the outcome depended on the decisions of 1932–33.
:::

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 3.1
1. **Define the criteria first.** For "Why did Hitler become Chancellor?" criteria could be *necessity* (would it have happened without this?), *contribution* (how much did it add?) and *timing* (did it matter in January 1933, or only earlier?).
2. **Separate the stages.** The question may be about the rise of the Nazi vote (1929–32), the appointment (January 1933) or the dictatorship (1933–34). Do not mix them: the Depression explains the first, the elite explains the second.
3. **Use precise evidence**: 2.6 per cent in 1928, 18.3 per cent in 1930, 37.3 per cent on 31 July 1932, 33.1 per cent on 6 November 1932, 444 to 94 on 23 March 1933, 2 May 1933, 30 June–2 July 1934, 2 August 1934.
4. **Beware of hindsight.** Do not write as if 1933 was bound to happen; in November 1932 observers thought the Nazis were declining.
5. **Do not narrate.** Use events as evidence for a point, not as a story in order.
6. **Judge.** End with a view that says which reason mattered most and why, and what would have been different without it.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, and the total is out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'The Depression was the main reason why Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement partly substantiated; a good range of mostly precise points with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criterion: which reason, if absent, would most probably have prevented Hitler's appointment on 30 January 1933?
- The case for: unemployment 1.9 million (1929) to 5.6 million (1932); the Nazi vote from 2.6 per cent (1928) to 37.3 per cent (July 1932); Brüning's deflation; the collapse of the grand coalition.
- Against: the Nazi vote fell to 33.1 per cent in November 1932 as the slump eased; the Nazis never had a majority; Hindenburg's refusal in August 1932 and then his reversal; Papen's deal of 4 January; Schleicher's failure; the elite's fear of communism; Hitler's appeal and the SA; the weakness of the constitution.
- Judgement: the Depression was the necessary condition but the decisions of the elite in 1932–33 were the decisive reason for the appointment.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Depression was the main reason why Hitler became Chancellor. Millions of people lost their jobs and they were angry with the government. They voted for Hitler because he promised work and bread. The Nazis went from a few seats to the largest party. Hitler was also a good speaker and he had the SA to help him. So the Depression was the main reason he became Chancellor."

*Commentary.* Relevant and broadly accurate, but general ("millions", "a few seats") and with no figures, dates or mechanisms. It assumes that the votes automatically produced the Chancellorship, and it adds other reasons only in passing, so the argument lacks balance. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 2 to 3 (about 6). To move up: use figures, show *how* the Depression helped (not just that it did), and test it against another reason.

**A Level 4 paragraph (one side well explained, but no real test).** "The Depression was a very important reason for Hitler's appointment, because it transformed the Nazis from a small party into a mass movement. Registered unemployment averaged about 1.9 million in 1929 and about 5.6 million in 1932, and the Nazi vote rose from 2.6 per cent in May 1928 to 18.3 per cent in September 1930 and 37.3 per cent in July 1932. Brüning's deflation, which cut wages and benefits, deepened the suffering, and middle-class Germans who had lost their savings in 1923 feared a second ruin. These people turned to Hitler, who promised work and an end to the Treaty of Versailles. The Depression therefore gave the Nazis their votes and made it impossible for the democratic parties to form a majority."

*Commentary.* Precise, focused and well explained, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). But it treats votes as if they were enough to produce a Chancellor, and it does not say what else was needed. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs a **test of the view** against other reasons, and a **judgement about the appointment**, not just the vote.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The view can be tested by asking which reason, if removed, would most probably have prevented Hitler's appointment on 30 January 1933. On that test the Depression was a necessary condition but it was not the main reason. It created the mass movement, but it was the decisions of a few men in the winter of 1932–33 that turned that movement into a government.

The case for the Depression is strong. Before it the Nazis were marginal: in May 1928 they won 2.6 per cent of the vote and 12 seats. As registered unemployment grew from about 1.9 million in 1929 to about 5.6 million in 1932, their share rose to 18.3 per cent in September 1930 and 37.3 per cent on 31 July 1932, which made them the largest party. The slump also destroyed the political basis of the Republic. The grand coalition broke up in March 1930 over how to finance unemployment insurance, and Brüning's deflation deepened the misery while he governed by Article 48 without a majority. The Depression also supplied Hitler's recruits (the SA grew from about 60,000 in 1930 to 445,000 by August 1932, largely from the jobless) and made Germans ready to listen to a leader who offered work and national revival. Without the Depression, the Nazis would probably have stayed at the margins, as in 1928.

But the Depression was not sufficient, and it does not explain the timing or the form of Hitler's appointment. First, by the time of the appointment the slump was past its worst and the Nazi tide had turned: on 6 November 1932 the party lost about two million votes and 34 seats, falling to 33.1 per cent, and its funds were short. If voters had decided the matter, Hitler would have been less likely to be Chancellor in January than in August. Second, the Nazis never had a majority, and a party with 33 per cent could not form a government without the President. In August 1932 Hindenburg refused to appoint Hitler Chancellor even after the Nazis won 37.3 per cent. It was the change of mind between August 1932 and January 1933 that made the difference, and that change owed little to the slump. It came from the scheming of Papen, who wished to return to power after Schleicher had removed him, from the access of Hindenburg's son Oskar and the head of his office, Otto Meissner, from the landowners' anger at Schleicher, and from the failure of Schleicher's Querfront once Strasser resigned on 8 December. Hindenburg appointed Hitler to a cabinet in which only three of the eleven ministers were Nazis because the people around him believed that they could control him and that this was the only way to get a government with mass support.

Other reasons also had a place. Fear of communism, with the KPD at 16.9 per cent in November 1932, explains why the army and the landowners preferred Hitler to a left-wing alternative. The weakness of the constitution, in which the President could appoint a Chancellor without a majority, made the elite's choice possible. Hitler's own refusal to take second place, which he maintained in August and which paid off, also counted.

Overall, the Depression was the deepest cause of the Nazi rise, and without it nothing would have happened. But the question is about the appointment, and for that the decisive reason was the decisions of Hindenburg, Papen and Schleicher in the winter of 1932–33. The Depression made Hitler a candidate; the elite made him Chancellor."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** (the counterfactual test) and uses it to separate the stages of the question (the Nazi vote and the appointment). The first paragraph gives the case for the view with precise figures (1.9 and 5.6 million; 2.6, 18.3 and 37.3 per cent; the SA); the second tests it against the fall in November 1932, the lack of a majority, and the change of mind between August and January; the third adds the other reasons briefly, and the conclusion states how the reasons connect (Table B, Level 5: about 14). It reaches a clear judgement that rests on the question's wording (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30. The facts are chosen to prove points, and not listed.
:::

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the importance of the Night of the Long Knives in Hitler's consolidation of power in 1933–34.
**What the levels want.** This is an "Assess the importance" question with one named event. Level 5 asks for criteria and a comparison: not "what happened?" but *how much it changed* and *compared with what*, with a judgement that places the event among the other steps.

**Plan.**
- Criterion: what did Hitler still lack on 29 June 1934 that he had gained by 2 August 1934, and how much of it was due to the purge?
- For its importance: it broke the SA (about three million) as a rival; it won the army, which wanted the SA curbed and which swore the oath on 2 August; it removed conservative critics (Papen's circle) and Hitler's old rivals (Strasser, Schleicher); it legalised murder (3 July law) and set a precedent; it let the SS grow (independent from 20 July).
- Against: by June 1934 the main institutions had already been destroyed (the Enabling Act of March 1933, Gleichschaltung, the one-party law of July 1933); Hitler already had the cabinet; the death of Hindenburg and the merging of offices were separate steps; the army's oath came from the army's own initiative; the SA's power was already less than its size suggested.
- Judgement: the purge was the most important step for the *security* of the dictatorship (it removed the one rival force and secured the army), but it was less important than the Enabling Act for its legal basis and than Hindenburg's death for its completion.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"To assess the importance of the Night of the Long Knives, one should ask what Hitler lacked in June 1934 and how far the purge supplied it. On that test it was the most important step in securing the dictatorship, because it removed the only rival force and bound the army to him, but it was not the step that created the dictatorship, which was already largely built by then.

By June 1934 Hitler held much of what a dictator needs. The Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 had given the cabinet the power to make laws without the Reichstag; the states, the civil service, the trade unions and the parties had been brought into line by Gleichschaltung, and the law of 14 July 1933 made the Nazi Party the only legal party. In that sense the purge changed less than the earlier steps. What he still lacked was control of the party's armed wing and the loyalty of the army, and above all a safe succession to the ailing Hindenburg, aged 86.

On these points the purge was decisive. The SA had grown to about three million men, many of whom wanted a 'second revolution', and its chief, Röhm, wanted it to absorb the Reichswehr of 100,000. That threatened the army's leaders, whose skills Hitler needed for rearmament, and whose support he needed to take Hindenburg's place as head of state. By killing Röhm and the SA's leaders on 30 June–2 July, Hitler removed the one force that might have rivalled him and gained the army's gratitude. The effect was seen at once: Blomberg congratulated him, the army said nothing about the murder of two of its own generals, Schleicher and Bredow, and on 2 August, the day Hindenburg died, the armed forces swore an oath of unconditional obedience to Hitler himself. Without the purge it is unlikely that the army would have given him that oath so readily.

The purge also mattered in other ways. It removed critics: Papen's circle was warned off, with his aides Bose and Jung murdered, and Strasser and Schleicher, the symbols of the alternatives of 1932, were killed. It made murder lawful, because the cabinet passed a law on 3 July declaring the killings legal state self-defence, and Hitler said in the Reichstag on 13 July that he had been the supreme judge of the nation; no judge or minister resisted. And it raised the SS under Himmler, which became independent of the SA on 20 July 1934 and later ran the police and the camps.

Yet its importance must not be exaggerated. It did not destroy the Reichstag or the opposition parties, which had gone already. Hindenburg's death on 2 August, and the law merging the offices of President and Chancellor, were needed to complete the dictatorship, and the purge was only a precondition of that. And the army's loyalty had been shaped by its own interests (the Reichswehr wanted to rearm and to curb the SA, and it had come to terms with Hitler in the spring) as well as by the purge.

In conclusion, the Night of the Long Knives was the most important step for the security of Hitler's regime, because it removed the SA and secured the army and so made a safe succession possible. But the legal foundation of the dictatorship lay in the Enabling Act, and its completion in the events of 2 August. The purge turned a dictatorship in the making into one that was safe."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** (what he lacked, and what the purge supplied) that lets it compare the event with others, which is what "assess the importance" requires. It uses precise evidence (23 March 1933; 14 July 1933; about three million; 100,000; 3 July; 13 July; 20 July; 2 August) to support each claim (Table B, Level 5: about 14). It explains the mechanisms (SA, army, critics, law, SS), then limits its claim by comparing the purge with the Enabling Act and Hindenburg's death, and reaches a supported judgement (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the importance of the Enabling Act in Hitler's consolidation of power." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Enabling Act was passed in 1933. It gave Hitler the power to make laws without the Reichstag. This was very important because he could now do what he wanted. The Centre Party voted for it and so did other parties, and only the SPD voted against. After this, Hitler was a dictator and ruled until 1945. So the Enabling Act was the most important step in Hitler's rise."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are broadly right, but the paragraph **describes** and does not analyse. It says what the Act did but not *how* it was passed or *why that mattered*: there is no date, no vote, no mention of the pressure in the hall and the arrests that came before it. "Very important" is an assertion. The claim that it made him a dictator is too large, since it does not say what else had to happen (Gleichschaltung, the purge, Hindenburg's death), and it gives no comparison with other steps.

**The fix.** Make a claim, use the detail as proof and set a limit. "The Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 was important because it gave the dictatorship a legal base. It allowed the cabinet to make laws, even laws that broke the constitution, without the Reichstag for four years, and it passed by 444 votes to 94 because Hitler had made sure of a two-thirds majority: the 81 Communist deputies could not sit, 26 Social Democrats were in custody or in hiding, the hall was ringed by the SA, and the Centre Party was won over by promises that were not kept. Only the SPD, led by Otto Wels, voted against. But the Act was not enough by itself. The SA, the army, the trade unions and the Länder still had to be dealt with, which took until the summer of 1934, so the Act should be seen as the legal key that opened the door and not the building of the dictatorship." Now each fact proves a point, and there is a limit and a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What were the main features of the Weimar constitution that Hitler's rise exploited?
A: Proportional representation (many parties, no majorities); the directly elected President who appointed the Chancellor (Article 53) and could dissolve the Reichstag (Article 25); and Article 48, which let the President rule by emergency decree. From 1930 these made presidential government possible, and the choice of Chancellor depended on Hindenburg and his advisers.
Q: Why did Müller's government fall, and why does it matter?
A: The grand coalition broke up on 27 March 1930 over how to finance unemployment insurance as unemployment rose; Hindenburg refused Müller emergency powers. It was the last government with a Reichstag majority before 1933 and showed that the parties would not compromise in a crisis.
Q: What was Brüning's policy, and what were its results?
A: Deflation: cuts in public spending, wages and benefits and higher taxes, governing by Article 48 decrees from 1930. It deepened the slump (the "hunger chancellor"), pushed voters to extremes (the Nazis won 18.3 per cent in September 1930), and ended when Hindenburg dismissed him on 30 May 1932.
Q: How did the Communist Party's growth help Hitler?
A: The KPD rose to 16.9 per cent in November 1932; its growth frightened the middle class and the elite, who saw the Nazis as a shield, and its refusal to cooperate with the SPD split the left. Together the Nazis and KPD held more than half the seats in 1932, which made a Reichstag majority impossible.
Q: What was the SA and how did it help and harm the Nazis?
A: The party's paramilitary wing, led by Röhm from 1931, which grew to 445,000 by August 1932. It gave the party strength and intimidated opponents; but its violence repelled some voters (Brüning banned it in April 1932) and Röhm's ambitions later became a problem.
Q: What did the Nazis offer different groups, and why did it work?
A: Work and bread to the unemployed, debt relief to farmers, protection to the middle class, an end to Versailles to nationalists, and a crushing of the left to business and the elite. The promises were contradictory but made the Nazis a catch-all party of protest (Childers).
Q: Why did the Nazi vote fall in November 1932 and why was Hitler still appointed?
A: It fell from 37.3 to 33.1 per cent (about 2 million votes) because voters were tired of elections, the economy was improving slightly, and the SA's violence and the Berlin strike alienated some. Hitler was still appointed because Hindenburg, Papen and others decided in January 1933 that a coalition with Hitler as Chancellor, and conservatives in the cabinet, was the best way to get a government with mass support.
Q: What did the Reichstag Fire Decree do?
A: It suspended seven articles of the constitution that protected civil rights, "until further notice", and allowed searches, arrests and confiscations beyond the normal limits. It was the basis of the mass arrests of Communists and others in 1933 and of the early camps.
Q: How was the Enabling Act passed?
A: On 23 March 1933 by 444 votes to 94, in the Kroll Opera House ringed by SA and SS men, after the 81 Communist deputies were prevented from attending, 26 Social Democrats were in custody or hiding, and the Centre was won over by promises. Only the SPD voted against.
Q: Why did Hitler order the Night of the Long Knives?
A: To remove the SA as a rival and a threat to the army, to win the army's support for his succession to Hindenburg, and to strike at conservative critics such as Papen's circle and at old rivals such as Schleicher and Strasser.

:::recap
- **Weimar in 1929**: proportional representation; a President who appointed Chancellors and could use Article 48; the Nazis at 2.6 per cent and 12 seats in May 1928.
- **Depression**: unemployed about 1.9 million (1929), 3.1 (1930), 4.5 (1931), 5.6 (1932), about 4.8 (1933); American loans withdrawn; industrial production about 40 per cent down.
- **Weak governments**: Müller's coalition fell 27 March 1930; Brüning (30 March 1930–30 May 1932) ruled by Article 48 and deflation; rise of the KPD to 16.9 per cent (November 1932); Nazis and KPD held a majority of seats.
- **Nazi support**: Hitler's appeal and the "legal path"; the SA (445,000 by August 1932); Goebbels's propaganda and tailored promises; elite support (Hugenberg, some businessmen, the Hindenburg circle).
- **Elections**: 18.3 per cent (14 September 1930); Hitler 36.8 per cent in the second round on 10 April 1932; 37.3 per cent (230 seats) on 31 July 1932; 33.1 per cent (196 seats) on 6 November 1932.
- **Appointment**: Papen (1 June–3 December 1932), Schleicher (3 December 1932–28 January 1933), the Cologne meeting of 4 January, appointment on 30 January 1933 with three Nazis in a cabinet of eleven.
- **Consolidation**: Reichstag Fire (27 February) and Decree (28 February); election of 5 March (43.9 per cent); Enabling Act (23 March, 444 to 94); Gleichschaltung (unions 2 May, parties banned by 14 July); Night of the Long Knives (30 June–2 July 1934); Hindenburg's death and the army oath (2 August 1934).
- **Judgement**: the Depression was the necessary condition, but the decisions of the elite in 1932–33 made the appointment; the dictatorship was built by terror in legal form.
:::
