:::why
Jimmy Carter took office in January 1977 promising a foreign policy "as good as the American people". Four years later he had brought Egypt and Israel to a peace treaty that has lasted for more than forty years, given the Panama Canal back to Panama and recognised China, and he had also lost Iran, a key ally, watched fifty-two Americans held prisoner for 444 days, and been beaten by a landslide. Both stories are true, and a Paper 4 essay on his foreign policy needs you to hold them together. The question "how effective?" has no single answer: it depends on what you measure (aims achieved, lasting results, or the cost to America's standing), and this chapter trains you to choose and defend your measure.

## Carter, his team and his aims

### Who made foreign policy

James Earl Carter, a former Governor of Georgia, had no experience of foreign affairs when he won the 1976 election, and he said so as a strength: he would bring honesty and decency to a policy that voters thought Vietnam and Watergate had stained. Three things shaped what he could do.

- **A suspicious public and Congress.** The Vietnam War, the Watergate scandal and the investigations into the CIA in 1975–76 (the Church Committee) had left Congress determined to watch the President's foreign policy. Congress wrote human rights tests into aid laws, banned some covert operations and, as the Panama Canal and arms control debates show, could block treaties.
- **A new agenda.** Carter wanted to move American policy away from the single contest with the Soviet Union. He talked of human rights, nuclear arms control, cutting arms sales, stopping the spread of nuclear weapons, and dealing with poorer countries as partners.
- **A divided team.** His advisers disagreed from the start, and the disagreement is a main theme of the key question.

Historians describe the Vance–Brzezinski split as a struggle between "reason" and "power". Gaddis Smith, in *Morality, Reason, and Power* (1986), organised his account of the Carter years around three approaches, the moral (Carter's own), the reasoned (Vance's) and the power-based (Brzezinski's), and argued that moral concerns shaped the first two years while the power-based approach came to dominate the last two. Later writers, such as Nancy Mitchell in *Jimmy Carter in Africa* (2016), have argued that Carter was less torn between the two than this picture suggests, because he held both a concern for rights and a Cold War outlook at once. Keep both views in mind: they are a way of explaining why the record looks so mixed.

:::key What "effective" can mean
When an essay asks how effective a policy was, choose your tests and use them on every policy:
1. **Aims achieved.** Did it do what Carter said he wanted?
2. **Lasting results.** Did the result survive his presidency?
3. **Cost.** What did it cost America in standing, money, lives or other policies?
4. **Alternatives.** Could another approach have done better in the circumstances?
A policy can pass one test and fail another: the Camp David Accords passed the first two but did little for the Palestinians; the human rights policy succeeded as a statement of principle but was applied unevenly.
:::

## Human rights as foreign policy

### Carter's view of human rights

Carter put human rights at the front of his inaugural address on 20 January 1977. "Our commitment to human rights must be absolute," he said, and "because we are free we can never be indifferent to the fate of freedom elsewhere." He meant it as a break with the policy of Nixon and Kissinger, who had dealt with other governments as they found them and thought that a country's treatment of its own people was not America's business. Carter's reasons were a mixture:

- **Moral conviction.** He was a deeply religious man, and he saw rights as a duty.
- **Repairing America's image.** After Vietnam and Watergate, a policy of principle would restore, he believed, American leadership in the world. He told students at Notre Dame on 22 May 1977 that to ignore the world's demand for human rights "would be to lose influence and moral authority in the world".
- **Practical politics.** Congress and the public, including liberals in his own party, wanted a policy that did not support dictators. A rights policy also gave the United States something to say in the Cold War that the Soviet Union could not match, because the Helsinki Accords of 1975 had made rights a subject of international bargaining (see [The USA, 1974–93 (4)](#hist.d.usa74.4)).

:::note Evidence: Carter at Notre Dame, 22 May 1977
*Adapted from President Carter's address at the commencement of the University of Notre Dame, 22 May 1977. Shortened; the phrases are the speech's own.*

"… we are now free of that inordinate fear of communism which once led us to embrace any dictator who joined us in that fear. … we have reaffirmed America's commitment to human rights as a fundamental tenet of our foreign policy."

*How to use it.* Nature: a major set-piece speech by the President to a university audience, written to explain a new policy. Purpose: to persuade Americans that the end of the Vietnam era freed them to put values first. Value: shows what Carter meant to change (support for anti-communist dictators). Limit: it is a statement of intention; to judge effectiveness you need the record, and critics said it ignored how much the United States needed such allies. In the speech he also said he believed in détente with the Soviet Union, which shows the human rights policy and the arms control policy were meant to go together.
:::

:::def Human rights policy
The Carter administration's attempt to make a government's treatment of its own people a factor in American aid, arms sales and diplomacy. It was applied by cutting or conditioning aid, by public criticism and by quiet pressure for the release of prisoners.
:::

### The machinery of the policy

The policy was written into the State Department. By the end of 1977 a Bureau of Human Rights and Humanitarian Affairs existed, with Patricia Derian, a civil rights campaigner from Mississippi, as its head (an Assistant Secretary). It produced the annual country reports on human rights, which at first covered countries receiving American aid and later every country. Presidential Directive 30 of February 1978 set out the policy: economic and military aid was to be tied to the recipient's rights record, with good or improving records favoured. An interagency group reviewed aid decisions one by one, and Congress added laws of its own, such as the ban on military aid to Argentina in 1978. The system made a human rights test a regular part of decisions, which was new.

Two features of the policy matter in an essay. It was **case by case**, not automatic, because Vance said it must be flexible, and so it could be applied strictly to weak countries and gently to important ones. And it relied on **leverage**, which the United States had in some countries (those that wanted aid or arms) and lacked in others (those that did not).

### Nicaragua and the Somoza regime

Nicaragua had been ruled since the 1930s by the Somoza family, first Anastasio Somoza García, then his sons. The third Somoza, Anastasio Somoza Debayle, ruled through the National Guard, and after the Managua earthquake of 1972 his associates were accused of diverting relief money. Carter's aim was to press him to reform without helping the Sandinista National Liberation Front (FSLN), a guerrilla movement with Marxist leaders and links to Cuba.

- **1977.** The administration criticised Somoza's abuses and made military aid depend on improvement. Somoza lifted some restrictions, and US aid went on.
- **January 1978.** The murder of Pedro Joaquín Chamorro, the editor of the opposition newspaper *La Prensa*, caused protests across the country. Somoza's role was never proved.
- **August–September 1978.** Sandinista guerrillas seized the National Palace and held hundreds of hostages until the government paid a ransom and freed prisoners, and in September attacks broke out in several cities.
- **October 1978 to January 1979.** The United States backed mediation by the Organization of American States (OAS), with an American diplomat, William Bowdler, as the main negotiator. Somoza refused to accept a plebiscite (a vote on a single question, here whether he should stay in power) under international supervision, and the effort failed in January 1979. In February the United States cut military aid to the National Guard.
- **June–July 1979.** On 21 June Vance called for Somoza to be replaced by a broadly based government, and on 23 June the OAS passed a resolution to the same effect. A plan for an orderly transfer of power collapsed when Somoza's chosen successor refused to give way. Somoza left for the United States on 17 July and the Sandinistas entered Managua on 19 July 1979.
- **After the revolution.** Carter did not break with the new government. In September 1979 he met its leaders at the White House and urged moderation, and in November he asked Congress for $75 million in aid for Nicaragua (part of an $80 million request for Central America). Congress approved aid in 1980 but required regular reports on human rights in Nicaragua and let the President stop the aid if Nicaragua supported guerrillas elsewhere.

**Assessment.** The policy failed at its main task: the United States neither saved a moderate Nicaragua nor kept the Sandinistas friendly for long. Carter has been criticised from both sides: conservatives said he had helped "lose" Nicaragua by weakening an ally, and liberals said he had backed Somoza for too long. His defenders reply that he refused to use force to prop up a dictator, ended military aid and then tried to keep a channel to the new government. What is certain is that the revolution became a central issue of the Reagan years (see [The USA, 1974–93 (4)](#hist.d.usa74.4)).

### Chile: the Pinochet regime

General Augusto Pinochet had taken power in Chile in the military coup of 11 September 1973 and ruled by repression. In September 1976 agents of his secret police murdered the exiled former ambassador Orlando Letelier and a young American colleague, Ronni Moffitt, with a car bomb in Washington, a crime on American soil that the FBI traced to Chilean intelligence. Congress had already banned military aid to Chile in 1976 (the Kennedy amendment), so Carter inherited a policy of pressure.

Carter kept it. His administration opposed loans to Chile in international banks, cut other aid and criticised Pinochet in public. Pinochet came to Washington in September 1977 for the signing of the Panama Canal treaties, and Carter told him that Chile still had a human rights problem. Yet Pinochet stayed in power until 1990, and his government did not extradite the officers named in the Letelier case. The pressure had effect on small points (some prisoners were released and the secret police was renamed and restructured) but not on the regime itself. For an essay, the Chilean case shows the limits of US leverage over a government that did not depend on American aid.

### Brazil and Argentina

**Brazil** was a military dictatorship under General Ernesto Geisel, and it was a regional power that did not want American advice. Carter's human rights policy clashed with his anti-proliferation policy at once: Brazil had signed a deal in 1975 to buy nuclear reactors and fuel technology from West Germany, and Carter wanted it stopped because the technology could be used for weapons. Brazil reacted in early 1977 by rejecting American military aid after the State Department's human rights report criticised it. Rosalynn Carter visited Brazil in June 1977 and Carter himself visited in March 1978 and spoke of rights, but the nuclear programme went on. The result was a cooler relationship with a large and important country, and little change in how it ruled.

**Argentina** was a more serious case. The military took power in March 1976 and fought what it called a "dirty war" against its opponents, with kidnappings, secret prison camps and "disappearances". A commission in 1984 documented nearly 9,000 disappeared people, and human rights organisations believe the true number was far higher. Derian pressed the junta hard, and Congress banned arms sales and military training in 1978 (the Humphrey–Kennedy amendment). The United States also cut credit and voted against loans. Pressure had some results: the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights was allowed to visit in 1979, and some prisoners, among them the journalist Jacobo Timerman, were released. But the junta turned elsewhere for trade, in particular to the Soviet Union, and it refused to join Carter's grain embargo (a ban on selling American grain to the Soviet Union, his answer to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979) in 1980, which showed how little leverage a sanctions policy had over a government that had other buyers.

### How consistent was the policy?

The strongest criticism of Carter's human rights policy was that it was **inconsistent**. The United States criticised Argentina and Chile but not governments that mattered more to its strategy: Iran, South Korea, the Philippines, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia and, after January 1979, China. Carter had toasted the Shah in Tehran on 31 December 1977, calling Iran "an island of stability", while the Shah's secret police tortured opponents. Critics gave three explanations:

- **Security came first.** Oil, bases and the Cold War overrode principle where the stakes were high.
- **Leverage varied.** Rights policy worked on countries that needed American aid and failed on those that did not.
- **The "double standard" charge from the right.** In an essay in *Commentary* in November 1979, Jeane Kirkpatrick, later Reagan's ambassador to the UN, argued that the United States should distinguish between authoritarian regimes, which might reform, and Marxist "totalitarian" ones, which never did, and that Carter had helped to bring down friendly authoritarian governments (the Shah, Somoza) that were replaced by worse ones.

His defenders reply that the policy shifted the terms of debate: the question whether a government respects rights became a normal part of American diplomacy and was taken up by Reagan's administration, whatever its first rhetoric, and by Congress. Human rights, in short, were established as a subject, if not as a rule.

:::key Judging the human rights policy
- **For:** it restored the idea that American power should serve principle; it created a permanent bureau, annual reports and aid conditions; it freed some prisoners and slowed some abuses (Argentina in 1979); it linked the United States with campaigners such as the Soviet dissidents; it was admired abroad.
- **Against:** it was applied unevenly (Iran, South Korea, the Philippines, China); it had little leverage over Chile or Brazil; in Nicaragua it did not save a moderate outcome; it strained relations with allies in Latin America and set the policy against anti-proliferation and Cold War aims; its critics on the right said it weakened friendly governments.
- **Judgement to test:** the policy was more effective as a change in how Americans argued about foreign policy than as a way of changing other governments.
:::

## Carter and the Middle East: the Camp David Accords

### American aims in the Middle East

In 1977 the United States had four interests in the region, and every Arab–Israeli policy had to serve them at once.
1. **The security of Israel.** A long-standing commitment backed by public opinion and Congress.
2. **Oil.** The Arab oil embargo after the war of October 1973 had shown how much the American economy depended on Gulf supplies.
3. **Keeping the Soviet Union out.** Soviet arms and advisers had been in Egypt and Syria. Each war risked drawing the superpowers in.
4. **Stability.** A war between Israel and its neighbours, or a collapse in one of America's friends, could damage all of the first three.

### From Kissinger's shuttle diplomacy to a comprehensive settlement

After the 1973 war Henry Kissinger, Nixon's and Ford's Secretary of State, had flown between capitals to arrange limited steps. These "shuttles" produced the Israeli–Egyptian disengagement of January 1974, the Israeli–Syrian disengagement of May 1974 and a second Sinai agreement in September 1975, under which Israel withdrew further in the Sinai Peninsula and American civilians staffed an early-warning station in the passes. The method was **step by step**: leave the hardest questions (Jerusalem, the West Bank, the Palestinians) for later, and give each side a gain at each stage. (Israel captured the West Bank from Jordan, and Gaza and the Sinai Peninsula from Egypt, in the war of June 1967; a Palestinian homeland would lie in the first two.) It worked in the sense that it kept the ceasefire and moved Egypt towards the United States. It had two weaknesses: it brought no final settlement, and it left the Palestinians out.

:::def Shuttle diplomacy
Negotiation by a mediator who travels between the capitals of the parties to a dispute, carrying proposals, instead of bringing them together. Kissinger used it in 1973–75 to arrange disengagement agreements between Israel and Egypt and Israel and Syria.
:::

Carter and Vance wanted something larger. They had been influenced by a report published in 1975 by the Brookings Institution, a Washington research body, which argued for a **comprehensive settlement**: one conference, covering all the parties, that would trade peace and recognition for withdrawal from the territories Israel took in 1967 and a solution for the Palestinians. The place for this was the **Geneva Peace Conference**, a meeting set up in December 1973 under joint American and Soviet chairmanship, which had met once and then lapsed. In spring 1977 Carter met the leaders of Israel, Egypt, Jordan and Saudi Arabia in turn, and on 16 March 1977, at Clinton, Massachusetts, he said that a settlement needed three things: real peace, with open borders and trade; Israeli withdrawal to something near the lines of 1967, with minor adjustments; and a "homeland" for the Palestinians. The last phrase caused outrage in Israel and in parts of the American Jewish community.

Then events went against him.
- On **17 May 1977** the Likud party won the Israeli election and Menachem Begin, a man who believed that the West Bank was part of the land of Israel and who rejected a Palestinian state, became Prime Minister. A conference at Geneva with the Palestine Liberation Organization (the PLO) at the table was now nearly impossible.
- On **1 October 1977** Carter agreed a joint statement with the Soviet Union that called for a Geneva conference by the end of the year and referred to "the legitimate rights of the Palestinian people". The statement involved the Soviet Union as an equal partner, which was exactly what Kissinger had worked to prevent. Israel and many members of Congress reacted angrily, and within days Carter had to agree with Israel's foreign minister, Moshe Dayan, a different basis for a conference. It was a bruising lesson in what a comprehensive approach would cost at home.

Carter's approach had changed policy in three ways: it was **comprehensive** rather than step by step, it spoke openly about the Palestinians, and it treated the Soviet Union as a possible partner. By the autumn it had stalled.

### Reactions to Sadat's diplomacy

The deadlock was broken by Egypt's President, Anwar Sadat, who had his own reasons to act. He wanted the Sinai back; he needed to relieve an economy that could not carry the cost of war and of subsidised food (riots had broken out in January 1977 when subsidies were cut); and he had already turned away from the Soviet Union, whose advisers he sent home in 1972 and whose treaty with Egypt he cancelled in 1976. He believed that only the United States could deliver Israel.

On **9 November 1977** he told the Egyptian Assembly that he was ready to go to the ends of the earth for peace, even to the Israeli Knesset. Begin invited him, and on **19 November** Sadat landed at Tel Aviv. The next day he spoke to the Knesset in Jerusalem, the first Arab leader to visit Israel, and said that the real barrier to peace was psychological. The reactions show the shape of what followed:

| Who | Reaction |
|---|---|
| **Israel** | Welcomed him warmly; the visit broke the taboo of non-recognition. Begin, however, would offer the return of Sinai but not much more |
| **Carter and the United States** | Surprised, since Sadat had not consulted them, but delighted; Carter dropped the Geneva idea and put the weight of the United States behind direct talks |
| **Most Arab states** | Hostile. Libya, Syria, Algeria, South Yemen and the PLO formed a "Steadfastness and Confrontation Front" at Tripoli in December 1977; Saudi Arabia and Jordan, whom Carter hoped would back Sadat, stayed aloof |
| **The Soviet Union** | Angry at being excluded and at losing a former client |
| **Egyptians** | Divided: relief at the prospect of an end to war, but also unease at making peace alone; Sadat's foreign minister resigned before the trip |

Sadat's visit solved one problem and created another. By going alone, he had moved the talks from the comprehensive Geneva model to a **bilateral Egypt–Israel track**. That made a treaty possible, and it also meant the Palestinian question might be left for later, as in Kissinger's day.

The direct talks that followed, at Ismailia in December 1977 and later in Jerusalem and at Leeds Castle in England in July 1978, bogged down over two things: whether Israel would withdraw from the Sinai settlements it had built, and what Egypt could say about the Palestinians. By the summer of 1978 the process was close to collapse, and on 8 August Carter invited Sadat and Begin to meet him at Camp David, the presidential retreat in Maryland.

### The Camp David summit, 5–17 September 1978

Carter took a big risk. A failed summit would harm all three leaders, and he had the midterm elections to think of. He prepared by reading about each man's character and history, and in the thirteen days that followed he did more of the work than any American President has done at a summit. The format was unusual: the delegations lived in cabins in the woods with no press. Carter and his advisers (Vance, Brzezinski and the National Security Council's Middle East specialist William Quandt) worked on a single American text, which was redrafted many times. Begin and Sadat, who disliked each other, met together only in the first days. After that Carter went from cabin to cabin.

- **The difficulties.** Sadat wanted Israeli withdrawal from all of Sinai and a link between his treaty and progress for the Palestinians. Begin would give up the peninsula, including its settlements and air bases, but not the West Bank or Gaza. The meeting nearly collapsed in the second week, when Sadat prepared to leave; Carter told him that leaving would do grave damage to the friendship between their countries, and he stayed.
- **The last obstacles.** In the final days the problems were Jerusalem, where Begin would not change Israel's claim to the whole city, and the Sinai settlements, which Begin finally agreed to leave to a vote of the Knesset. The parties exchanged letters on Jerusalem and on the Israeli settlements instead of putting them in the texts, and each side later read the letters differently.

:::note Evidence: the wording of the Frameworks
*Adapted from the Framework for Peace in the Middle East and the Framework for the Conclusion of a Peace Treaty between Egypt and Israel, signed at Washington on 17 September 1978. Short phrases quoted from the documents and joined by dots.*

"… transitional arrangements for the West Bank and Gaza for a period not exceeding five years … the legitimate right of the Palestinian peoples and their just requirements … full recognition, including diplomatic, economic and cultural relations … normal relations will be established between Egypt and Israel."

*How to use it.* The Frameworks are official documents agreed by both leaders, so they are strong evidence of what each side accepted. They are also evidence of what was left vague: "a period not exceeding five years" and "just requirements" give no date for a Palestinian authority and say nothing about a state, and the dispute over Israeli settlements was handled outside the text. The sections on Egypt and Israel are precise (withdrawal, recognition, normal relations); those on the Palestinians are loose. That difference is the key to judging the Accords.
:::

**What was agreed.** The summit produced two documents, signed at the White House on 17 September 1978.

1. **A Framework for Peace in the Middle East.** It set up a transitional arrangement for the West Bank and Gaza, lasting not more than five years, during which the inhabitants would elect a self-governing authority to replace Israel's military government. Talks on the final status of the territories would follow, with representatives of the Palestinians involved. Egypt, Israel and Jordan were to take part. No Palestinian representatives had been at Camp David, and Jordan refused to join.
2. **A Framework for the Conclusion of a Peace Treaty between Egypt and Israel.** Israel would withdraw from Sinai, including its air bases, and Egypt would establish full diplomatic and normal relations with Israel. Both would guarantee passage through the Suez Canal and the Straits of Tiran. A treaty was to be signed within three months.

### The treaty and its consequences

The three months stretched to six. Disputes about the settlements, about how Egypt's treaty duties related to its duties to other Arab states, and about the timetable delayed the treaty, and in March 1979 Carter flew to Cairo and Jerusalem himself to save it. The **Egypt–Israel peace treaty** was signed at the White House on **26 March 1979**. The United States promised aid to both and guaranteed Israel's oil supply; Israel agreed to withdraw from Sinai in stages, completing the move in April 1982.

:::key The results of Camp David
1. **The first peace treaty between Israel and an Arab state**, which has lasted ever since.
2. **Egypt moved into the American camp** and out of the Soviet one. It became one of the largest recipients of American aid, along with Israel.
3. **Egypt, the strongest Arab military power, left the war against Israel**, which made another general Arab–Israeli war far less likely.
4. **The cost to the Arab world's unity.** The Arab League suspended Egypt in 1979 and moved its headquarters from Cairo; Sadat was murdered by Islamist officers on 6 October 1981.
5. **Nothing for the Palestinians.** The self-government talks went nowhere: Israel and Egypt read the autonomy plan differently, the Palestinians and Jordan refused to take part, and Israeli settlement in the West Bank grew.
6. **A Nobel Peace Prize for Sadat and Begin** in 1978, and a political benefit for Carter that did not last.
:::

### How effective was the Middle East policy?

Judged by the first two tests (aims achieved; lasting results), Camp David was Carter's greatest success: he took an Egyptian–Israeli quarrel that had produced four wars, and turned it into a treaty that has outlasted its makers. His personal stubbornness mattered, because without his presence at Camp David the two leaders would have gone home. But three limits belong in any answer.

- **Sadat made the opening.** The visit to Jerusalem was his decision, not Carter's, and it came after Carter's own method had stalled. Carter's contribution was to turn that opening into a treaty.
- **It was not the settlement Carter wanted.** He had begun by wanting a comprehensive peace including the Palestinians and ended with a bilateral treaty. A historian who values the aim ("a comprehensive peace") will judge Camp David less successful than one who values the result ("peace between Egypt and Israel").
- **The costs.** The treaty depended on American aid, divided the Arab world and angered Egyptian Islamists, one of whom murdered Sadat. It also may have made Israel more ready to act against Lebanon in 1982, once its southern border was safe, a point historians debate.

A fair judgement is that Camp David was a **major but limited success**: it achieved the achievable (Egypt and Israel) and failed at the hard part (the Palestinians), and the same Carter who persisted at Camp David had begun by raising hopes that he could not meet.

## Iran and the hostage crisis

### US relations with Iran since 1975

The United States and Iran were allies for twenty-five years. After the CIA-backed overthrow of Prime Minister Mohammad Mosaddegh in 1953, Mohammad Reza Shah Pahlavi ruled as a strong monarch with American help. He supplied oil, a barrier against Soviet influence on the Gulf and Iran's long border with the Soviet Union, and later a friendly neighbour to Israel. Under the **Nixon Doctrine** the United States had asked regional allies to guard their own regions, and in May 1972 Nixon and Kissinger agreed that the Shah could buy almost any non-nuclear American weapon he wanted. After the oil price rise of 1973 the Shah had money to spend, and Iran became the largest customer for American arms. By 1975 the Shah was at the height of his power. He signed the Algiers agreement with Iraq in March 1975, settling a border dispute and ending his support for the Kurds, which the United States had also helped to fund. Tens of thousands of Americans worked in Iran.

Behind the splendour the regime was fragile.
- **Repression.** The secret police, SAVAK, jailed and tortured opponents. The one-party system left no legal way to oppose the regime.
- **Rapid change and inequality.** Oil money produced inflation, a boom in building and a gap between a rich elite and the poor and the bazaar merchants (the traders of the old covered markets), while the Shah's modernising programme offended the Shia clergy (the religious leaders of the branch of Islam followed by most Iranians) and many traditional Iranians.
- **Western dependence.** Many Iranians saw the Shah as an American puppet, a view that went back to 1953.

Carter inherited the policy and kept it. His human rights policy hardly touched Iran, and on **31 December 1977** he visited Tehran and called Iran "an island of stability" in a troubled region. The speech was seen afterwards as a sign that the United States had not understood what was happening beneath the surface.

### The Iranian Revolution, 1978–79

Protests began in January 1978 in Qom, a centre of Shia learning, after a newspaper attacked the exiled cleric **Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini**. Marches followed at forty-day intervals, the traditional time for mourning, and each round of deaths produced the next. On **8 September 1978** ("Black Friday") troops fired on a crowd in Tehran after martial law (rule by the army, with curfews and a ban on gatherings) had been declared. Strikes, including strikes in the oil industry, paralysed the country in the autumn. The opposition was a loose coalition of religious leaders, liberals and the left, united by the demand that the Shah go, and Khomeini, who had been exiled since 1964 and spoke to Iran by cassette tapes and telephone from Paris, became its symbol.

The United States was caught by surprise. As late as the autumn of 1978 an American intelligence assessment said that Iran was not even in a "pre-revolutionary" situation. Inside the administration the old split appeared: Brzezinski, the National Security Adviser, urged the Shah and the army to take a firm line and hoped for a military solution, while Vance and the ambassador in Tehran, William Sullivan, wanted the Shah to accept compromise with the opposition. Carter went one way and then the other. He sent a general, Robert Huyser, to Tehran in January 1979 to hold the army together, but the army was losing heart. On **16 January 1979** the Shah left Iran for what was meant to be a short stay, and on **1 February** Khomeini landed in Tehran to a vast welcome. The army declared itself neutral on 11 February and the government the Shah had left behind collapsed. Iran was proclaimed an Islamic Republic, a state governed under Islamic law with clerics holding the highest power, after a referendum (a public vote on a single question) at the end of March.

For the United States this was a defeat on several fronts: it had lost its strongest ally in the Gulf, an arms market and electronic listening posts that monitored Soviet missile tests, and the new government was hostile. Carter's critics, led by Brzezinski and later by Reagan, said he had failed to back a friend. His defenders said no American policy could have saved a regime that most Iranians had rejected.

### The Shah in America and the seizure of the embassy

The Shah went into exile in Egypt, Morocco, the Bahamas and then Mexico. In the autumn of 1979 he was ill with cancer. Kissinger, David Rockefeller and Brzezinski pressed Carter to let him enter the United States for treatment, and Carter at last agreed, although American diplomats in Tehran warned of danger to the embassy. On **22 October 1979** the Shah flew to New York for medical care. Many Iranians took this as proof that the United States would restore him, as it had in 1953, and the embassy in Tehran had already been briefly overrun by armed activists in February 1979.

On **4 November 1979**, a crowd of students calling themselves the Muslim Student Followers of the Imam's Line climbed the walls of the embassy compound in Tehran and seized it. They took sixty-six Americans captive. Khomeini approved the action and called it a "second revolution", and the interim prime minister, Mehdi Bazargan, who wanted good relations with the United States, resigned two days later. The students released thirteen women and African American hostages on 19–20 November and one man who was ill in July 1980, leaving **fifty-two**. They demanded the return of the Shah and of the wealth they said he had taken.

:::def Hostage crisis
The 444 days, from 4 November 1979 to 20 January 1981, during which fifty-two Americans from the US Embassy in Tehran were held by Iranian students with the support of Iran's revolutionary government.
:::

**Why it mattered.** The crisis was important in four ways.
1. **It broke the rules of diplomacy.** Embassies are protected by the Vienna Convention; the UN Security Council called for the hostages' release in December 1979 and the International Court of Justice ruled against Iran in May 1980. The governments that did not like Khomeini were unable to act.
2. **It helped Khomeini.** He used the crisis to push aside moderates such as Bazargan and to unite the country behind the revolution against an "American enemy".
3. **It dominated American politics.** For fourteen months the television news counted the days; yellow ribbons appeared across the country. At first the crisis rallied Americans behind Carter, but as it dragged on it came to symbolise weakness.
4. **It made Iran America's enemy for the long term.** There have been no normal diplomatic relations since 1980.

### Carter's policy and its outcome

Carter's first aim was to bring the hostages home alive. He rejected the use of force at first because it might lead to their deaths, and because the United States needed to keep the anger of the world behind it.

- **Pressure.** He ended oil imports from Iran on 12 November 1979 and froze Iranian assets in American banks, about $8 billion, on 14 November. In April 1980 he broke diplomatic relations and tightened sanctions. Allies cooperated reluctantly.
- **Diplomacy.** Messages through the UN and many go-betweens produced nothing, since there was no one in Iran who could both make a promise and keep it. Six American diplomats who had avoided capture on 4 November left Iran in January 1980 with Canadian help (the "Canadian Caper"), which lifted American spirits.
- **The "Rose Garden strategy".** Carter stayed at the White House during the early primary campaign and said that he must attend to the crisis. It protected him from Senator Edward Kennedy's challenge for the Democratic nomination for a time but it made him look trapped.
- **Rescue: Operation Eagle Claw, 24–25 April 1980.** After five months Carter ordered an attempt to land helicopters in the desert at a place called Desert One, southeast of Tehran, to fly the force to the capital. Three of the eight helicopters broke down, leaving too few, and the mission was abandoned. While the force was leaving, a helicopter hit a transport plane and **eight American servicemen were killed**. Carter accepted responsibility on television. Vance, who had opposed the raid, resigned on 28 April and was succeeded by Edmund Muskie.
- **Settlement.** The Shah died in Egypt on 27 July 1980. Iraq's invasion of Iran on 22 September 1980 gave Iran a reason to settle, since it needed money and weapons. Negotiations through Algeria went on through the autumn, and Carter's team, led by Deputy Secretary of State Warren Christopher, finished the **Algiers Accords** on **19 January 1981**. The United States promised not to interfere in Iran's internal affairs and to release Iranian assets, and the hostages were freed the next day.

**The timing.** The fifty-two were handed over on **20 January 1981**, minutes after Ronald Reagan took the oath of office. Many Americans assumed Iran had punished Carter and rewarded Reagan, and there was a claim, known as the "October Surprise", that Reagan's campaign had secretly asked Iran to delay the release until after the election. Two congressional investigations in 1992–93 found no credible evidence for it, and most historians do not accept it. The more likely reasons for the timing are that the Accords were signed only on 19 January after months of haggling, that Iran gained nothing more by waiting once its demands (assets, a pledge not to interfere) were met, and that many Iranian leaders enjoyed denying Carter the credit. Be careful in an essay: the release at the moment of Reagan's inauguration is a fact, but "Reagan's toughness freed the hostages" is a claim that needs evidence, and Carter's negotiators had done most of the work.

### The significance of the crisis

:::key Why the hostage crisis was significant
- **For Carter's presidency.** It filled the news for fourteen months, ended his hopes of a second term and made him look weak, alongside inflation and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan. Reagan beat him on 4 November 1980, the first anniversary of the seizure, by 489 electoral votes to 49 and by about 51 per cent of the vote to 41 per cent (the independent John Anderson took about 7 per cent).
- **For American foreign policy.** It led to the Carter Doctrine of January 1980, which said the United States would use force to defend the Persian Gulf, and a Rapid Deployment Force; it started the long hostility with Iran; and it began the American problem of hostage-taking by groups in the Middle East.
- **For the Middle East.** It consolidated the Islamic Republic and showed the strength of political Islam.
- **For judging Carter.** All fifty-two hostages came home alive, which his supporters count as a success of patience, but his critics point to the Shah's admission, the failed rescue and the humiliation as signs of poor judgement.
:::

### What historians say

Three books give you three viewpoints, and each is best used for one kind of claim.
- **Gaddis Smith, *Morality, Reason, and Power: American Diplomacy in the Carter Years* (1986)**: an early, full account, written by a Yale diplomatic historian. His three-part scheme (the moral, the reasoned and the power-based approach) explains why the record looks contradictory, and his argument that the power-based approach came to dominate in the last two years fits the move from human rights to the Carter Doctrine.
- **Nancy Mitchell, *Jimmy Carter in Africa: Race and the Cold War* (2016)**: based on a wide range of archives, she shows that Carter's policy in Africa was driven by his concern for racial justice and by Cold War rivalry together, and that the picture of a President torn between two advisers is too simple. It is the best source for the claim that Carter's outlook was more consistent than his critics said.
- **Stuart Eizenstat, *President Carter: The White House Years* (2018)**: written by Carter's chief domestic policy adviser, who was a participant. It is candid about mistakes, including over Iran, but argues that Carter was a more consequential President than his reputation suggests. It is useful as inside evidence, but its author worked for Carter, so treat its verdicts as those of a defender.

:::key Three verdicts to test
1. **"A failure."** The Shah was lost and the hostage crisis humiliated the country; détente collapsed; SALT II was never ratified; he lost in a landslide; the human rights policy was inconsistent.
2. **"A success on its own terms."** Camp David, the Panama treaties, China and the human rights bureau were lasting achievements; no hostage died in captivity; his policies set the agenda that later Presidents inherited.
3. **"Effective in some places, not in others."** The best answers split the record by test: strong on the aims achieved in the Middle East, Panama and China, weak where he faced revolutions he did not understand (Iran, Nicaragua) and where outcomes depended on a rival (the USSR).
:::

:::note Debate: was Carter's record a failure because of Carter or because of the times?
Defenders say the revolutions in Iran and Nicaragua came from forces inside those countries that no American policy could have controlled, that Congress limited the policy, and that the 1979 oil shock and inflation made every problem look worse. Critics say the choices were Carter's: he sent mixed signals on Iran, admitted the Shah without protecting the embassy, and chose an undefined approach to the Soviet Union. Neither side denies that America's power looked weaker in 1980 than in 1977. The question for you is how much of that was policy and how much was circumstance.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'Carter's foreign policy was a failure.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** Criteria: (1) *aims achieved*; (2) *lasting results*; (3) *what he could control* (revolutions in Iran and Nicaragua; Congress; the USSR). For "failure": Iran (Shah lost, 444 days, Eagle Claw), Nicaragua, détente and SALT II, the 1980 landslide, uneven human rights policy. Against: Camp David and the treaty, Panama, China, human rights as a standing issue. Judgement: not a failure overall, but a policy that succeeded where the other side wanted a deal and failed where events were outside America's control; the verdict depends on the test.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Carter's foreign policy was a failure in some ways. He lost Iran, where the Shah was overthrown and fifty-two Americans were held hostage for 444 days. The rescue mission failed and eight men died. He also failed over the Soviet Union because the SALT II treaty was not ratified and Russia invaded Afghanistan. But Carter also had successes. He got Egypt and Israel to sign a peace treaty at Camp David and gave the Panama Canal back to Panama. So Carter's foreign policy was partly a failure and partly a success."

*Commentary.* Accurate and balanced in layout, so Table B reaches Level 3. But it is a **list**: failures, then successes, then "partly both". There is no criterion for deciding what counts as failure, and no weighing of the two lists. Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: set a criterion and test the evidence against it.** "If a policy is judged by whether it achieved what Carter set out to do, the record is mixed. His central aim in the Middle East was a settlement, and he got a treaty between Egypt and Israel signed on 26 March 1979. His aim on human rights was to make them a test of American policy, and a permanent Bureau of Human Rights was created in 1977. But if a policy is judged by what it cost, the picture is darker: the policy towards Iran failed to foresee the revolution, and the decision to admit the Shah on 22 October 1979 led within two weeks to the seizure of the embassy."

*Commentary.* A criterion is applied to evidence from both sides, and the conclusion follows from it. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs more than two criteria, a test of the claim that events were beyond his control, and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"'Failure' is a harsh word for a presidency that produced the Camp David Accords, but it describes the result that most Americans saw in 1980. To decide whether the view is valid I shall use three tests: whether Carter achieved his aims, whether the results lasted, and how far the failures lay within his control. My argument is that the view is partly valid: the record was one of real and lasting success where the parties wanted a deal and of failure where he faced forces he did not understand, but that the failures were the ones the voters remembered.

By the test of aims achieved, the view fails in the Middle East. Carter set out in 1977 to achieve a comprehensive settlement at Geneva, and when that stalled he gave his own time to the summit at Camp David from 5 to 17 September 1978 and, in March 1979, to a journey to Cairo and Jerusalem to rescue the treaty. The treaty of 26 March 1979 ended a quarrel that had produced four wars, and it has survived the murder of Sadat in 1981 and every crisis since. Two other policies worked in the same way: the Panama Canal treaties, which Carter pushed through the Senate by 68 votes to 32 in 1978 against fierce opposition, and full relations with China from 1 January 1979. These are lasting results by any test.

By the test of lasting results the view is also weak for human rights, though for a different reason. The policy was applied unevenly: it criticised Argentina and Chile while Carter toasted the Shah of Iran as the ruler of 'an island of stability' on 31 December 1977, and it had no effect on General Pinochet, who ruled until 1990. Even so, the Bureau of Human Rights and the annual reports stayed, and Argentina released some prisoners under pressure in 1979. The policy changed the language of American diplomacy more than it changed other countries, but that change has lasted.

Where the view is strongest is on the test of how far events were within Carter's control, because here the record is of failure. In Iran, Carter's government did not foresee the revolution: it kept the Shah as its partner in 1977, was divided between Brzezinski's firmness and Vance's compromise in 1978, and let the Shah into the United States in October 1979 knowing that the embassy was at risk. The hostage crisis then lasted 444 days, and Operation Eagle Claw ended in the death of eight servicemen at Desert One on 24 April 1980 and the resignation of Vance. The 1980 election, in which Reagan won 489 electoral votes to 49, shows what voters thought. In the same way, the Sandinistas took Managua on 19 July 1979 after Carter had failed to find a moderate alternative to Somoza, and détente collapsed after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979, so that SALT II, signed on 18 June 1979, was never ratified.

Yet it is important to separate what Carter did wrong from what he could not prevent. No American policy could have kept a Shah who had lost the support of most of his own people, and the hostage crisis ended with all fifty-two alive, which was Carter's first aim. Congress, hostile after Vietnam, limited his room for action on Nicaragua, and the Soviet decision to invade Afghanistan was Moscow's, not Washington's. What was his own fault was the lack of a clear strategy: the choice of the moral approach in 1977 and the power-based approach in 1980 left both friends and rivals uncertain what the United States wanted, and the contrast of success in the Middle East with failure in Iran came partly from that.

In conclusion, the view is not valid as a verdict on the whole presidency, because Camp David, Panama and China were lasting successes and the human rights policy changed the terms of debate. It is valid as a verdict on the Gulf and the Soviet Union, where Carter lost an ally, saw détente collapse and gave Reagan his campaign. A fair judgement is that Carter's foreign policy was effective where he could negotiate with rulers who wanted agreement, and ineffective where the main forces were revolutions, and that the public's judgement in 1980 was a judgement on the second category."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (13–14): three explicit criteria, an argument that tests both sides against each, a distinction between failure of policy and failure of circumstance, and a judgement that is clear and qualified. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates and figures chosen to prove the points (5–17 September 1978; 68–32; 1 January 1979; 489–49; 24 April 1980; 18 June 1979) and not a recital. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 850 words, 35 to 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the significance of the Camp David Accords. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the significance" asks you to weigh how much difference the Accords made, to whom and for how long, and to reach a judgement about the size and kind of significance.

**Plan.** Criteria: significance (1) for Egypt and Israel; (2) for the Palestinians; (3) for the Middle East; (4) for the United States and for Carter; (5) how long the effects lasted. For: treaty on 26 March 1979; Sinai returned by April 1982; first Arab recognition of Israel; Egypt moved to the American side; the war front against Israel narrowed. Against or limits: the Palestinian framework not carried out; Arab League suspension; Sadat murdered; Jordan and the Palestinians refused to join; settlements grew; Carter's gain was short-lived. Judgement: highly significant for the states it covered, much less for the question it failed to settle.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "The Camp David Accords were very significant. In September 1978 President Carter invited President Sadat of Egypt and Prime Minister Begin of Israel to Camp David and they talked for thirteen days. They signed a peace agreement and later a treaty in 1979. They both won the Nobel Peace Prize. This was very important because there was peace between Egypt and Israel."

*Commentary.* Correct but descriptive, with an assertion at the end ("very important because there was peace") that does not explain why or for whom. No criteria and no limits. Table A Level 2 (4–6), Table B Level 2 (5–6).

**Moving to Level 4: apply criteria and add limits.** "The Accords were most significant for Egypt and Israel. Egypt, the strongest Arab military power, made peace with Israel and recovered Sinai, with Israel completing its withdrawal in April 1982, and this removed the possibility of the kind of Egyptian-led attack that had begun the war of 1973. For the Palestinians the significance was much smaller. The first Framework promised a self-governing authority for the West Bank and Gaza for a period not exceeding five years, but Jordan and the Palestinians refused to take part, Israel and Egypt read the autonomy plan differently, and Israeli settlement in the territories went on. So the significance of the Accords differs sharply according to whose interests are considered."

*Commentary.* Two criteria applied, with accurate evidence on both sides. This is Level 4 for analysis. It needs a wider range of criteria (the region, the United States, time) and a final judgement.

**A Level 5 judgement (the closing paragraphs).**

"By the criterion of the states directly involved, the Accords were of the highest significance: a war that had been fought four times did not recur, and Egypt's change of side, with American aid and with the end of the Soviet role in Egyptian affairs, altered the Cold War in the Middle East. By the criterion of the region as a whole, the effect was double-edged. The Arab League suspended Egypt in 1979 and moved its headquarters from Cairo, and Sadat's murder on 6 October 1981 showed the anger that the treaty had caused, yet no general war between Israel and a coalition of Arab states followed, partly because the strongest had left the field. By the criterion of the Palestinians, the Accords failed to provide what the first Framework promised, and the failure helped to make the Palestinian question the unsolved centre of Middle East politics.

For the United States, the Accords brought great influence but also a heavy commitment: aid to Egypt and Israel at a level that continued for decades. For Carter they were the high point of his foreign policy, and they did little for him in the election of 1980, which was decided by Iran and the economy. The significance of the Accords therefore lay in what they showed as well as in what they achieved: that an American President could bring enemies to agreement if the parties had a reason to deal, and that the hardest question, the Palestinians, could not be settled by two leaders who did not represent them.

I conclude that the Accords were highly significant but limited in what they settled. They were more important as a precedent and as a change in the balance between states than as a solution, and their significance lies in the Egypt–Israel treaty, not in the Framework for Peace in the Middle East."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (13–14): wide-ranging criteria (parties, region, Palestinians, United States, time), balanced, with a judgement about the kind of significance. Table B Level 4–5 (12–13): precise evidence in the paragraph and plan; more detail on the settlements and the treaty's terms would secure Level 5. With the full essay this is a Level 5 answer.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the effectiveness of Carter's human rights policy." [30]

A student wrote:

"Carter made human rights an important part of his foreign policy. In his inaugural address in 1977 he said that America's commitment to human rights must be absolute. He set up a Bureau of Human Rights in the State Department and put Patricia Derian in charge. He cut aid to Chile, Argentina and Brazil. In Nicaragua he tried to get Somoza to change but there was a revolution in 1979. Some people said that his policy was not consistent because he did not criticise the Shah of Iran or South Korea. He also made a deal over the Panama Canal. Overall his human rights policy was quite effective because he did a lot of things."

**The level.** Table B: accurate and relevant detail (Level 3), though without the dates and figures a higher level would need. Table A: it is a **list of actions** with one sentence of criticism, no criterion for "effective", no explanation of how the examples bear on effectiveness, and a conclusion ("he did a lot of things") that confuses activity with effect. The Panama Canal sentence is not about human rights. Table A Level 2 (4–6).

**The fix.** State a criterion, then use the facts as evidence for it. "A human rights policy is effective if it changes what other governments do, or changes American policy for good. By the first test the record is poor: aid cuts did not shake Pinochet, who ruled Chile until 1990, and the Argentine junta found other buyers, such as the Soviet Union, and ignored the grain embargo of 1980; in Nicaragua the pressure on Somoza came too late to produce a moderate successor before the Sandinistas took Managua on 19 July 1979. By the second test it is stronger: the Bureau of Human Rights, the annual country reports and the aid conditions of 1977–78 made rights a standing question in American diplomacy. The policy was therefore more effective at changing the United States than at changing the countries it criticised." Now every fact proves a point, and a judgement follows.

Q: [30 marks] ★ Assess the reasons why Carter's human rights policy was applied unevenly.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: how important each reason was and how well it explains particular cases. Reasons: security and oil (Iran, Saudi Arabia, South Korea, the Philippines); leverage (countries that needed US aid, such as Argentina, were pressed; countries that did not, such as Chile in the end, were not moved); competing aims (anti-proliferation against Brazil; arms control with the USSR; the China opening); Vance's "case by case" flexibility; Congress's role (aid laws applied to some states only); the divisions between Vance, Derian and Brzezinski. Level 5 ranks the reasons, e.g. that the Cold War and oil interests set the limits, and leverage decided where the policy could work, and judges whether "unevenness" was a flaw of the policy or a necessary feature of any policy that must serve several aims.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of Sadat's visit to Jerusalem in the making of peace between Egypt and Israel.
A: Indicative content. Importance: it broke the taboo and opened direct talks; it removed the Geneva idea; it put pressure on Begin through public opinion; it showed Sadat's willingness to make peace on his own. Limits: the talks stalled in 1978 until Carter called the Camp David summit; Carter's personal work was needed over thirteen days and again in March 1979; Begin's concessions came from the Sinai bargain and American promises (aid, oil guarantees), not from the visit alone; the visit also had costs, because it isolated Egypt in the Arab world. Level 5 sets a criterion, e.g. "could the treaty have been made without it?", and judges that the visit was necessary but not sufficient.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that Carter's policy towards Latin America was a success.
A: Indicative content. For: the Panama Canal treaties (7 September 1977; 68–32 votes; handover on 31 December 1999), improved relations with many governments, pressure on Argentina and Brazil, the move away from backing dictators. Against: Nicaragua (the Sandinistas took power in July 1979 and became the focus of Reagan's policy), no effect on Pinochet, strains with Brazil over nuclear policy and rights, domestic anger over Panama, and El Salvador's crisis in 1980. Criteria: aims achieved; lasting results; handling of revolution. Level 5 judges that success was clearest where the issue could be settled by treaty (Panama) and weakest where revolution was under way (Nicaragua, El Salvador).
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Operation Eagle Claw was a mistake.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. For: its plan was complex (eight helicopters, three failures, a collision at Desert One); Vance opposed it and resigned; eight servicemen died and the failure showed weakness; the hostages were not in danger of dying while diplomacy continued (all survived). Against: months of sanctions and diplomacy had failed; Carter was under pressure from Brzezinski and public opinion; a rescue was not an unreasonable last resort; a successful raid could have saved lives and the presidency; the dispersal of the hostages after the raid may have made later talks easier (a claim to test). Level 5 distinguishes the decision to try from the way it was carried out.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The hostage crisis was the main reason for Carter's defeat in the 1980 election.' Evaluate this view.
H: Think about what other problems a voter in 1980 would have named.
H: What did Kennedy's challenge for the nomination reveal about Carter's weakness?
H: Compare Reagan's message with Carter's record.
A: Indicative content. For: fourteen months of daily coverage; Eagle Claw; the sense that the United States was weak; the link with Iran and oil. Against: inflation and unemployment (see [The USA, 1974–93 (1)](#hist.d.usa74.1) on the late 1970s); the energy crisis of 1979; the "crisis of confidence" speech of 15 July 1979, which critics called the "malaise" speech; Kennedy's challenge in the primaries; Afghanistan and the end of détente; Reagan's skill and his appeal on taxes and defence; John Anderson's independent candidacy; the electoral result (489 to 49). Level 5 sets criteria, e.g. which problem most affected how people voted, and judges that the crisis was an important part of a wider picture of weakness, which the economy made worse.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Carter's foreign policy was more successful in its first two years than in its last two.' Evaluate this view.
H: List the main successes and failures by date.
H: Remember that the Panama treaties were ratified in 1978 but the Camp David treaty was signed in 1979.
A: Indicative content. For: 1977–78: human rights agenda, the Panama treaties, Camp David (September 1978). 1979–80: the fall of the Shah, the Sandinistas, the hostage crisis, Afghanistan, SALT II failing, the Carter Doctrine, Eagle Claw. Against: the treaty with Egypt (26 March 1979), China (1 January 1979) and SALT II's signing (18 June 1979) fall in 1979; the first two years also saw the failure of the Geneva plan (1977), the quarrel over the 1 October statement, and the Shah's "island of stability". Level 5 notes that the results of policy lagged behind the decisions, and judges that the second half was worse in outcomes, but not in effort.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Carter's human rights policy conflicted with America's other interests more than it served them.
H: Which other interests: oil, bases, arms control, anti-communism, nuclear non-proliferation?
H: In which cases did the policy serve those interests?
A: Indicative content. Conflict: Brazil (non-proliferation and rights); Argentina (loss of influence and grain sales to the USSR); Iran and Nicaragua (critics said it weakened friends); the Soviet Union (rights criticism upset Brezhnev while Carter wanted arms control). Service: it improved the United States' image after Vietnam; it won support in Congress and Latin America; the Panama treaties were helped by its stress on fairness; it matched the Helsinki Accords and put the USSR on the defensive; later Presidents kept rights as part of policy. Level 5 uses a criterion such as "did the cost to other interests exceed the gain in standing?" and judges by case.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The disagreements between Vance and Brzezinski were the main reason that Carter's foreign policy was ineffective.' Evaluate this view.
H: Match each major decision (Iran, the Soviet Union, Africa) to who advised what.
H: Think of problems that no adviser could have solved, such as the Iranian Revolution or Congress.
H: Use Gaddis Smith's "morality, reason, power" scheme and Mitchell's challenge to it.
A: Support: the split over Iran (Brzezinski for firmness and military options, Vance and Sullivan for compromise); the contrasting signals to Moscow (arms control against the Horn of Africa and Afghanistan); Vance's resignation on 28 April 1980. Challenge: Carter made the final choices and set both aims (Mitchell argues his outlook was more consistent than the picture of a President torn between two men); Iran, Nicaragua and Afghanistan were events driven by others; Congress limited policy; the economy undermined confidence at home. Level 5 would judge that the split made policy look incoherent but that the main reasons for failure lay in Carter's own style and in events he could not control.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Camp David Accords were more the achievement of Sadat than of Carter.' Evaluate this view.
H: Who took the first step, and who completed the work?
H: Which of the hardest decisions (Sinai settlements, Jerusalem, the Palestinian text) did Carter influence?
A: Support: Sadat's decision to go to Jerusalem; his concession to a separate treaty with Israel at the price of isolation in the Arab world; his decision to stay at Camp David. Challenge: Carter's decision to hold the summit; his drafting of the American text; his shuttle between the cabins; the promise of American aid and oil guarantees; his personal visit in March 1979. Level 5 uses a criterion (the difference each made to the result) and judges that Sadat created the opportunity and Carter turned it into an agreement, so each was necessary and neither sufficient.
:::

:::recap
- **Aims and team**: Carter wanted a policy of principle after Vietnam and Watergate; Vance (negotiation) and Brzezinski (power) disagreed; Congress was watchful.
- **Human rights**: inaugural address (20 January 1977), Notre Dame (22 May 1977), a Bureau of Human Rights (Derian), country reports, PD-30 (February 1978). Uneven in application: Argentina and Chile pressed; Iran, South Korea, the Philippines and China not. Little leverage over Pinochet or Brazil.
- **Nicaragua**: aid cuts, OAS mediation (1978–79), Somoza left on 17 July 1979, Sandinistas in Managua on 19 July, aid to the new government (1980).
- **Middle East**: comprehensive settlement and Geneva (1977), the 1 October statement, Sadat in Jerusalem (19–21 November 1977), Camp David (5–17 September 1978), Egypt–Israel treaty (26 March 1979), Sinai returned by April 1982; nothing for the Palestinians; Arab League suspended Egypt; Sadat murdered on 6 October 1981.
- **Iran**: ally since 1953; "island of stability" (31 December 1977); revolution in 1978–79; Shah left 16 January 1979; Khomeini returned 1 February; Shah in New York 22 October; embassy seized 4 November (66 taken, 52 held for 444 days); sanctions; Eagle Claw (24–25 April 1980, eight dead); Algiers Accords (19 January 1981); release on 20 January 1981.
- **Other**: Panama treaties (1977–78), China (1 January 1979), SALT II (signed 18 June 1979; not ratified), Afghanistan (December 1979).
- **Judgement**: a major success where leaders wanted a deal (Egypt–Israel, Panama, China), a failure where revolution was beyond his control (Iran, Nicaragua) and where the USSR was involved; the hostage crisis and the economy decided 1980 (Reagan 489, Carter 49).
