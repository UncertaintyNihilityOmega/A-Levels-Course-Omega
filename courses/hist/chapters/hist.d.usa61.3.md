:::why
In 1961 millions of Black Americans in the South could not vote, eat at a lunch counter, use a public library or send their children to a school with white pupils. Eight years later segregation was illegal, Black voters were registering in their hundreds of thousands, and the nation had passed more civil rights law than in the whole of the century before. Yet in 1968 the leader of the movement was murdered, cities burned, and most Black families in the North still lived in poorer homes, worse schools and lower-paid jobs than white families. "How much progress?" is a question about both stories at once, and a Paper 4 essay on it needs you to weigh law against life, the South against the North, and the movement against the government.
:::

## What "progress" means: four tests

The question asks *how much* progress was made, so you need a way of measuring it. This chapter uses four tests:
1. **Law:** did the rights exist on paper, and could they be enforced?
2. **Power:** did Black Americans gain the vote and a share in political decisions?
3. **Daily life:** did jobs, housing, schools, safety and income actually change?
4. **Reach:** did change reach the whole country, North and West as well as the South, and all Black Americans, not just the educated and the lucky?

A second question runs through the chapter: *who* made the change? The movement (local people, SNCC, CORE, SCLC, the NAACP), the federal government (the President, Congress, the courts) and the opposition (whose violence sometimes helped the cause it meant to stop) all shaped events. A Level 5 essay does not pick one hero. It explains how they worked together and which was necessary for which result.

The short answer to "how much progress?" is that the progress in **law and political power was very large**, in **daily life in the South it was real**, and in **economic equality and in the North it was limited**. The rest of the chapter gives you the evidence for each part.

## The starting point in 1961

:::def Jim Crow
The system of laws, customs and violence that kept Black Americans separate and subordinate, especially in the South: separate schools, buses, restrooms and restaurants, together with barriers to voting. It takes its name from a minstrel-show character of the 1830s and was built after Reconstruction (see [Civil War and Reconstruction (4)](#hist.us.civilwar.4)).
:::

By 1961 the Supreme Court had struck at the legal basis of segregation, in *Brown v. Board of Education* (17 May 1954), and Black Southerners had shown what they could do in the Montgomery bus boycott of 1955–56, which ended when the Supreme Court, in *Browder v. Gayle* (1956), ruled bus segregation unconstitutional (see [Post-war USA (2)](#hist.d.usa45.2)). But the effect on everyday life was small. Southern states resisted *Brown* by delay, by closing schools and by a campaign called massive resistance, and in the autumn of 1964, ten years after the ruling, only about 2.25 per cent of Black schoolchildren in the South attended a school with any white pupils. The Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 had done little for the vote. In Mississippi, in March 1965, only about 6.7 per cent of voting-age Black residents were registered. The movement of 1961–68 was therefore a campaign to turn court rulings into daily reality.

The campaign began in earnest on 1 February 1960, when four Black students from North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College sat down at a whites-only lunch counter in a Woolworth's store in Greensboro, North Carolina. They were refused service and stayed. Within weeks, sit-ins had spread across the South, involving thousands of students. In April 1960, Ella Baker, a veteran organiser, helped the students found the **Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC)**, which stayed independent of King's organisation. A new generation was ready to act.

## The ideas and methods of King and the civil rights organisations

### Martin Luther King and nonviolent direct action

**Martin Luther King** was born in Atlanta on 15 January 1929, the son and grandson of Baptist ministers, and trained at Morehouse College and Boston University, where he earned a doctorate in 1955. He was twenty-six when the Montgomery boycott began and thirty-five when he received the Nobel Peace Prize in December 1964. His ideas came from three sources: the Christian gospel, which taught him to love his enemies and to see suffering as redemptive; the example of Mahatma Gandhi, whose campaigns in India showed that nonviolent resistance could defeat an empire; and the American tradition of liberty, in the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, which he appealed to constantly.

:::def Nonviolent direct action
A method of protest in which people break an unjust law or custom in public, accept the punishment without violence, and use the moral shock of the response to win public opinion. It differs from simple protest because it makes the opponent act: a sit-in, march or boycott creates a crisis that cannot be ignored.
:::

In the "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (16 April 1963) King set out four steps of a nonviolent campaign: collect the facts to see whether injustice exists, negotiate, purify yourselves, and then take direct action. He argued that **tension is not the enemy but the way to progress**, because only a crisis forces a community "to confront the issue".

:::note Evidence: King on why Black Americans could not wait
"We have waited for more than 340 years for our constitutional and God-given rights."

*From Martin Luther King, "Letter from Birmingham Jail", 16 April 1963, an open letter to eight white Alabama clergymen who had called the Birmingham protests "unwise and untimely". The extract is quoted exactly; King wrote it in a jail cell and published it afterwards, so it was meant for a national audience, and it explains the purpose of the campaign as he saw it.*
:::

King's role was that of a **symbolic and strategic leader**. He led through his speeches, which could move millions; through his willingness to go to jail (he was arrested more than twenty times); through his strategy of choosing places where segregation was most violent and most visible; and through his ability to talk both to Black communities and to the White House. He founded the **Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)** in 1957 with other Black ministers, using the Black church as a base of organisation, money and volunteers. His limits matter too: the Albany Movement (Georgia, December 1961 to summer 1962), which he joined, failed, because Police Chief Laurie Pritchett avoided public violence, arrested thousands quietly and moved the prisoners to jails in other counties. King learned from Albany that a campaign needed a clear goal and an opponent who would lose his temper, and he chose Birmingham for that reason.

### The organisations and the people who did the work

King was one voice in a movement of many organisations with different methods.
- **NAACP** (founded 1909, led by Roy Wilkins): the oldest and largest organisation, with local branches. It worked through the courts and through lobbying, and won *Brown*. It sometimes distrusted direct action.
- **CORE** (Congress of Racial Equality, founded 1942, led by James Farmer): committed to nonviolent direct action, famous for the Freedom Rides.
- **SCLC** (1957): King's organisation, based on Black churches, which ran mass campaigns in selected cities.
- **SNCC** (1960): a student organisation that did slow, dangerous work in the rural Deep South, living with local families and helping them to register. Its young organisers, such as John Lewis, Diane Nash, Bob Moses and later Stokely Carmichael, were often more radical than the older leaders.

Local people were the movement's core. **Ella Baker**, who worked for the NAACP and the SCLC before helping to found SNCC, argued that a movement should be built by ordinary people and not by a few famous leaders, and she criticised the leader-centred style of King's campaigns. **Fannie Lou Hamer**, the daughter of Mississippi sharecroppers, was thrown off her plantation and beaten in a jail after she tried to register in 1962, and became the voice of the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party. **Septima Clark** ran the citizenship schools that taught adults to read well enough to pass registration tests. **Diane Nash** led the Nashville sit-ins and organised the Freedom Rides after the first riders were attacked. Women did much of the day-to-day organising, yet few spoke from the platform. This is a point your essays should make: a King-only story misses much of what happened.

The historians differ about who mattered most. **Taylor Branch**, in his three-volume history *America in the King Years* (1988, 1998 and 2006), follows King and the movement's leaders closely and shows how nonviolent protest and violent reaction together forced the federal government to act. **Adam Fairclough**, in *To Redeem the Soul of America* (1987), studies the SCLC and King as a political and strategic leader. **Clayborne Carson**, in *In Struggle: SNCC and the Black Awakening of the 1960s* (1981), puts the young organisers and the grassroots at the centre. **Charles Payne**, in *I've Got the Light of Freedom* (1995), studied Mississippi and argued that the movement grew from patient, local "organising" more than from large national "mobilising". The best answers use these views to test the claim that King alone made the movement.

:::key Judging the ideas and methods
**Strengths of nonviolent direct action:** it won sympathy from white Northerners and the media; it made the use of violence by opponents visible; it made use of the Black church's money and numbers; its choice of targets (Birmingham, Selma) forced the federal government to act.
**Limits:** it needed opponents who would use violence in public (Albany shows what happens when they do not); it struggled in the North, where discrimination was unwritten; it relied on a few leaders and on the press; and it split in the mid-1960s as younger activists lost faith in nonviolence (see [A time of challenges (4)](#hist.d.usa61.4)).
**Judgement to defend:** the movement succeeded because it joined **local organising** (the registration drives, the church networks) to **national drama** (marches, jail, the cameras), and King's leadership was most important in the second.
:::

## The Freedom Riders, 1961

In *Boynton v. Virginia* (5 December 1960) the Supreme Court ruled that segregation in bus terminals serving interstate buses was illegal. Southern terminals ignored it. CORE decided to test the ruling. On **4 May 1961** thirteen riders, Black and white, left Washington on two buses bound for New Orleans. They met little trouble until Alabama. On **14 May**, Mother's Day, a mob in Anniston slashed the tyres of one bus and set it on fire, and the riders were beaten as they escaped. At Birmingham the second bus was attacked, and the police were slow to intervene. CORE decided to end the ride, but Diane Nash and the Nashville students insisted that it must continue so that violence would not stop it. On **20 May** a mob attacked the riders at the Montgomery bus station, and the Governor of Alabama's refusal to protect them led Attorney General Robert Kennedy to send federal marshals. Riders were then arrested in Jackson, Mississippi, where the police protected them from mobs but jailed them for breach of the peace; hundreds more riders came through the summer.

The Rides forced the federal government to act. On 29 May Robert Kennedy petitioned the Interstate Commerce Commission, which on **22 September 1961** ordered the removal of "whites only" signs from interstate bus terminals, effective 1 November. The Rides were a success for direct action: it made the government enforce an existing ruling. They also showed the pattern that would recur: a local mob, a national audience, a reluctant federal response. The limits were also clear: the Kennedy administration had at first asked for a "cooling-off period", and the ICC order, though it opened the bus stations, did nothing to give Black Southerners the vote.

## The Birmingham campaign, 1963 and its consequences

### Why Birmingham, and what happened

Birmingham, Alabama, was in King's words one of the most thoroughly segregated cities in the United States, and it had a history of bombings of Black homes and churches, so that it was nicknamed "Bombingham". Its Commissioner of Public Safety, **Eugene "Bull" Connor**, was a committed segregationist. The local movement was led by the Reverend **Fred Shuttlesworth**, who invited SCLC to join in. The campaign, called Project C (for "confrontation"), began on **3 April 1963** with sit-ins, marches and a boycott of downtown stores aimed at desegregating shops, restrooms and jobs. A state court issued an injunction (a court order forbidding them) against the protests. King decided to break it, and on **12 April**, Good Friday, he was arrested. In his cell he wrote the "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (16 April), a reply to white clergymen who had urged him to wait.

Numbers fell, because many adults feared losing their jobs. Then James Bevel, SCLC's director of direct action, proposed using schoolchildren. On **2 May** more than a thousand children, some very young, marched; hundreds were arrested. On **3 May** Connor ordered fire hoses and police dogs to be used on the children and other marchers. Photographs and film appeared in newspapers and on television across the world. On **10 May** the city's business leaders agreed to desegregate lunch counters, restrooms and fitting rooms in downtown stores, to hire some Black workers and to release those in jail. The next night bombs exploded at the A. G. Gaston Motel, where King had stayed, and at the home of his brother, and rioting followed. President Kennedy sent troops to bases near the city.

### The consequences

Birmingham changed the whole struggle.
- **It brought federal action.** Kennedy had called for caution, but the pictures from Birmingham changed his mind. After Governor Wallace's stand at the University of Alabama on 11 June, Kennedy went on television and said that the nation was "confronted primarily with a moral issue". On 19 June he sent Congress the bill that became the Civil Rights Act.
- **It spread the movement.** In the weeks after Birmingham there were hundreds of demonstrations in cities across the country, including the North and West, and thousands more Americans, Black and white, joined the cause.
- **It provoked a violent backlash.** Medgar Evers, the NAACP field secretary in Mississippi, was shot dead outside his home on 12 June, the day after Kennedy's speech. On **15 September 1963** a bomb planted by Klansmen exploded at the 16th Street Baptist Church in Birmingham on a Sunday morning. It killed four girls, Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley and Carole Robertson, all fourteen, and Denise McNair, eleven. No one was convicted for fourteen years; the first conviction was in 1977, the second in 2001 and the third in 2002.
- **It showed the strategy.** SCLC had chosen Birmingham because it expected Connor to answer with force. The Birmingham agreement itself was modest, and its terms were slow to be carried out, but the effect was on opinion in the North and on Congress.

## The March on Washington, 1963

A march on Washington was an old idea. **A. Philip Randolph**, the president of the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, had threatened one in 1941 to win jobs in defence factories, and called it off when Roosevelt issued an order banning discrimination in war industries. In 1963 Randolph, with his organiser **Bayard Rustin**, planned a "March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom". The "Big Six" leaders who backed it were Randolph, King, Roy Wilkins of the NAACP, Whitney Young of the National Urban League, James Farmer of CORE and John Lewis of SNCC, joined by white trade unionists and church leaders. President Kennedy tried at first to discourage it, fearing violence and the loss of votes for his bill, but then helped the organisers.

On **28 August 1963** about 250,000 people, perhaps a quarter of them white, assembled at the Lincoln Memorial, in an orderly crowd watched live by millions. The demands were a strong civil rights law, school desegregation, a federal jobs programme and a higher minimum wage. King's closing speech, with its repeated "I have a dream", became the most famous address of the movement. The main speakers were all men; Daisy Bates, who had advised the Little Rock students, was given only a brief tribute to Black women. John Lewis was asked to tone down his own speech, which had criticised the Kennedy bill as too weak.

The importance of the March lay in its message and its timing. It was peaceful, it was televised, and it showed that Black Americans and many white allies would back the bill. But it did not itself pass a law. It is often remembered as the climax of the movement, yet at the time the bill was still stuck in committee, and less than three weeks later the Birmingham church bombing showed how far the opposition would go. Malcolm X, a Black nationalist leader who rejected integration and nonviolence, dismissed the March as a "farce". It made the case for the bill; the law came only after Kennedy's assassination and Johnson's political skill.

## Freedom Summer, 1964 and the reaction to it

### The plan

By 1964 SNCC's organisers in Mississippi had worked for three years under threat and had registered very few voters. **Bob Moses**, the SNCC organiser who led the Mississippi project, decided to bring in outsiders. The **Council of Federated Organizations (COFO)**, an alliance of the civil rights groups in the state, planned the **Mississippi Summer Project**, known as Freedom Summer. More than 700 volunteers, mostly white college students from the North, came to teach in "Freedom Schools", to run community centres and to help Black residents register. The aim was twofold: to register voters and to make the country look at Mississippi. Moses and his colleagues knew that the nation cared more about white students than about Black Mississippians, and used this to bring the cameras.

The project also set up the **Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP)**, an alternative to the all-white state Democratic Party, which let Black Mississippians vote in a mock election and elect delegates to the national convention. Fannie Lou Hamer became its best-known figure.

### The reaction

Violence began at once. On **21 June 1964**, the day after the first volunteers had left their training in Ohio, three civil rights workers disappeared in Neshoba County: **James Chaney**, a 21-year-old Black Mississippian from Meridian, and two white New Yorkers, **Andrew Goodman**, 20, and **Michael Schwerner**, 24. They had gone to investigate the burning of a Black church, Mount Zion, that had been due to host a Freedom School. A deputy sheriff, Cecil Price, arrested them for speeding, released them that night, and they were stopped by Klansmen and shot. Their bodies were found on **4 August**, buried in an earthen dam. President Johnson sent agents of the FBI, and sailors from a nearby base joined the search. Across Mississippi that summer there were about a thousand arrests, and about seventy bombings and fires, including the burning of Black churches. The search also turned up the bodies of other Black men who had gone missing, and, as SNCC workers pointed out bitterly, their deaths had won far less attention. In 1967, in a federal trial, an all-white jury convicted seven men, including Price and Klan leaders, of conspiring to deprive the three of their civil rights. Mississippi itself did not charge anyone with the murders until 2005.

:::note Evidence: Fannie Lou Hamer to the credentials committee
"Is this America, the land of the free and the home of the brave"

*From the testimony of Fannie Lou Hamer to the Credentials Committee of the Democratic National Convention, Atlantic City, 22 August 1964. The extract is the opening of a sentence, quoted exactly; the question went on to ask whether a Black woman could be beaten in jail and threatened for trying to vote. Hamer spoke as a delegate of the MFDP, which was challenging the right of Mississippi's all-white delegation to the convention.*
:::

Hamer's account of her beating in a Mississippi jail was broadcast to the nation. The party leaders feared that a floor fight would split the Democrats before the election, and President Johnson, afraid of losing the South, called a press conference to draw the cameras away. The networks showed her testimony that evening anyway. The convention offered the MFDP two at-large seats, to be filled by men the party leaders named, and a promise of reform in 1968, and the MFDP refused. Most of the Mississippi white delegation walked out.

### The results

Freedom Summer's gains were real and its costs high. About 17,000 Black Mississippians tried to register that summer and only about one in ten, around 1,600, were accepted by the white registrars, so the local goal failed. But the Freedom Schools gave thousands of young people a political education, the MFDP built a network that could be used later, and national attention to Mississippi helped to carry the Civil Rights Act (signed on 2 July, while the search for the three men continued) and prepared the way for the Voting Rights Act. For the activists the summer was a loss of faith: the refusal of the Democratic Party to seat the MFDP persuaded many in SNCC that the liberal alliance would not give Black Americans power, which fed the turn to Black Power (see [A time of challenges (4)](#hist.d.usa61.4)).

## Selma and the Voting Rights Act campaign, 1965

### The campaign

After the Civil Rights Act, the vote was the unfinished business. Selma, Alabama, the seat of Dallas County, was chosen by SCLC for a voting rights campaign at the start of 1965. The county had a Black majority of residents but almost no Black voters, SNCC had worked there since 1963, and the Sheriff, **Jim Clark**, was known for violence. King and SCLC arrived in January 1965, and mass arrests followed, with hundreds of schoolchildren and adults jailed. In nearby Marion, Alabama, a state trooper shot **Jimmie Lee Jackson**, a young Black man, during a night march on 18 February; he died on 26 February. The marchers decided to carry their protest to the state capital, Montgomery, 54 miles away.

### Bloody Sunday

On **Sunday 7 March 1965** about 600 marchers, led by John Lewis of SNCC and Hosea Williams of SCLC, left Selma and crossed the Edmund Pettus Bridge over the Alabama River. On the far side state troopers and Sheriff Clark's men, some on horseback, ordered them to turn back, then attacked with clubs and tear gas. More than a hundred people were injured, and Lewis suffered a fractured skull. Film of the attack was shown on national television that evening, and the response was immediate: sympathy marches in many cities, and messages to Congress. King led a second symbolic march on **9 March**, turned back at the bridge, and that night a white Unitarian minister from Boston, James Reeb, who had come to support the movement, was beaten by white men; he died on 11 March.

On **15 March** Johnson went to a joint session of Congress and announced a voting rights bill. He spoke in the language of the movement.

:::note Evidence: President Johnson to Congress on Selma
"Their cause must be our cause too … it is all of us, who must overcome the crippling legacy of bigotry and injustice."

*Adapted from President Lyndon B. Johnson, address to a joint session of Congress on the right to vote, 15 March 1965, broadcast on television. Shortened; the cut is marked. Johnson spoke eight days after Bloody Sunday, to a nation that had seen the film, and he used the movement's slogan "We shall overcome" later in the same speech.*
:::

A federal judge, Frank Johnson, ruled that the march was lawful. On **21 March** about 3,200 people set out from Selma under federal protection, walking about eleven miles a day and sleeping in fields, and on **25 March** some 25,000 entered Montgomery and listened to King outside the capitol. That night **Viola Liuzzo**, a white mother of five from Detroit who was helping to drive marchers home, was shot dead by Klansmen. The Voting Rights Act followed on 6 August (see below). Selma also showed again how the movement used a clear, winnable demand (the vote), a violent local opponent and the cameras, and how voting rights, unlike integrated lunch counters, challenged the political control of the white South.

:::key Judging the methods and achievements of the movement
**Achievements:** the Freedom Rides ended segregation in bus terminals (1961); Birmingham won a local settlement and, more important, a federal bill (1963); the March on Washington built the political case (1963); Freedom Summer built a network and drew national attention to Mississippi (1964); Selma won the Voting Rights Act (1965).
**Limits:** each campaign needed violence in public to succeed; the local gains (Birmingham's agreement, Freedom Summer's registrations) were small; the Albany Movement of 1961–62 failed; and the leaders of the March did not speak for all Black Americans, who were divided over aims and methods.
**Judgement to defend:** the movement **created the crisis** that made federal action possible. Its achievements were greatest where it had a clear target, a local base and a federal law within reach.
:::

## Opposition to civil rights

### Attempts by state officials to stop Black Southerners registering

The vote was the key to political power, and for that reason Southern officials took care to deny it. Their weapons were legal, economic and violent.
- **Tests and taxes.** Registrars, who were local white officials with wide discretion, applied literacy tests unfairly. In Mississippi an applicant could be required to interpret any section of the state constitution to the satisfaction of the registrar, which meant that a Black schoolteacher could fail and a white farmer pass. Several Southern states also charged a **poll tax**, which fell hardest on poor Black sharecroppers.
- **Delay and obstruction.** Registration offices opened for a few hours on a few days a month, applications were lost and questions were hard to answer. The Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960 let the Justice Department sue for registrants, but suing one county at a time took years.
- **Reprisals.** People who tried to register lost their jobs, were evicted from farms and had credit cut off. Violence, from beatings to murder, was used to scare others.

The effect was that in Mississippi only about 6.7 per cent of Black adults were registered in March 1965, in Alabama about 19.3 per cent and in Georgia about 27.4 per cent. The Twenty-fourth Amendment (ratified 23 January 1964) banned the poll tax in federal elections, and in *Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections* (24 March 1966) the Supreme Court banned it in state elections too. But literacy tests and obstruction remained until the Voting Rights Act.

### Bull Connor and Governor Wallace

Two men became the faces of Southern resistance. **Bull Connor**, Birmingham's Commissioner of Public Safety, used police, dogs and hoses against peaceful marchers. His actions damaged the segregationists' case in the North, which is why King wanted him as an opponent. His power ended in May 1963, when Birmingham voters replaced the commission with a mayor and council.

**George Wallace** was elected Governor of Alabama in 1962 and was the segregationists' most effective politician. At his inauguration in Montgomery on **14 January 1963** he declared "segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever".

:::note Evidence: Wallace's inaugural address
"I say segregation now, segregation tomorrow, segregation forever."

*From the inaugural address of George C. Wallace, Governor of Alabama, Montgomery, 14 January 1963. The line is quoted exactly. The speech was written by an aide, Asa Carter, and was meant for Wallace's white Southern supporters; it set out the position that he then tried to defend in practice.*
:::

On **11 June 1963** Wallace stood at the door of Foster Auditorium at the University of Alabama to block the enrolment of two Black students, Vivian Malone and James Hood. It was a gesture agreed in advance, since he had been told that he would be removed by force. President Kennedy federalised the Alabama National Guard (took the state's troops out of the governor's hands and placed them under the President's command), and Wallace stepped aside. In September he used state troopers to try to stop school desegregation in several Alabama cities until Kennedy again federalised the Guard. Wallace's popularity was not confined to the South: in the 1964 Democratic primaries he won between about 30 and 43 per cent of the vote in the Wisconsin, Indiana and Maryland primaries, against the stand-ins who ran in Johnson's place in those primaries, and in 1968 he won 13.5 per cent of the national vote and 46 electoral votes as the American Independent Party's candidate. The attitudes he spoke for were national.

### Southern Congressmen

Opposition in Washington was strong because of the seniority system and the "Solid South" (the Southern states that had voted Democrat since Reconstruction). Southern Democrats, elected by an electorate from which Black people were largely excluded, held many of the chairmanships of committees, which they gained by length of service. In March 1956, 19 Senators and 82 Representatives, almost all from the South, signed the **Southern Manifesto**, condemning *Brown v. Board of Education* as an abuse of judicial power and promising to resist it by all lawful means. They used procedure to block the bills.

:::def Filibuster
A tactic in the US Senate in which senators keep speaking, or threaten to, so as to delay a vote on a bill. At this time it took a vote of two-thirds of the senators present to end debate (cloture), so a determined minority of Southern Democrats could talk a civil rights bill to death.
:::

In 1964 Howard W. Smith of Virginia, who chaired the House Rules Committee, delayed the bill in committee and proposed adding "sex" to Title VII, perhaps to make it unacceptable (it was kept in the Act). The bill then passed the House on 10 February by 290 votes to 130 and faced a filibuster in the Senate led by Richard Russell of Georgia, which lasted for about sixty days of debate (the count varies with the source). It ended on **10 June 1964**, when the Senate invoked cloture by 71 votes to 29 for the first time on a civil rights bill, after the Republican leader Everett Dirksen of Illinois brought enough Republicans over. The Senate passed the bill on 19 June by 73 to 27. The opposition therefore delayed and weakened the laws, and had kept the 1957 and 1960 Acts weak, but by 1964 it could not stop them.

### Local citizens' groups and the reasons for popular opposition

Ordinary white Southerners also resisted. The **White Citizens' Councils** were founded in Mississippi in July 1954, just after *Brown*, and spread across the Deep South. Their members, often bankers, lawyers and businessmen, called them the respectable alternative to the Klan, and they fought integration with economic pressure: sacking Black workers, calling in loans, publishing the names of those who signed petitions. At their peak they had hundreds of thousands of members, by some estimates. Mobs formed at crisis points: when James Meredith enrolled at the University of Mississippi on 30 September 1962, a crowd of students and outsiders rioted, two men were killed, and President Kennedy sent federal marshals and then thousands of troops to restore order.

Why did so many white people oppose change? Several reasons overlapped.
- **Belief in white supremacy and fear of "social equality".** Many white Southerners had been brought up to think of segregation as the natural order and feared that integration would lead to mixed schools and marriages.
- **Economic interests.** Cheap Black labour, in agriculture and in low-paid jobs, benefited landowners and employers, and poor whites feared competition for jobs.
- **States' rights.** The argument that the federal government had no right to impose change on the states, rooted in the memory of the Civil War and of Reconstruction (see [Civil War and Reconstruction (4)](#hist.us.civilwar.4)), gave resistance a respectable constitutional language.
- **Anti-communism.** Segregationists, and FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover, claimed that the movement was run by Communists, and Hoover had King's phones tapped from late 1963.
- **Distrust of "outside agitators".** Northern volunteers, and the federal government itself, were seen as invaders.
- **A justice system under local control.** White police, judges and all-white juries often refused to act. The man accused of murdering Medgar Evers, Byron De La Beckwith, was tried twice in 1964 by all-white juries, which failed to agree; he was not convicted until 1994.

### The Ku Klux Klan in the 1960s

The Ku Klux Klan, which had once had millions of members in the 1920s, was smaller in the 1960s but violent. Several rival Klans operated; two of the most dangerous were the United Klans of America, based in Alabama, and the White Knights of the Ku Klux Klan of Mississippi, founded in 1964. Their members bombed Black homes and churches in Birmingham, burned churches in Mississippi and killed. Among their crimes were the bombing of the 16th Street Baptist Church (15 September 1963), the Neshoba murders (21 June 1964), the killing of Viola Liuzzo (25 March 1965) and of Vernon Dahmer, a Mississippi NAACP leader whose home was firebombed on 10 January 1966 after he offered to pay the poll tax for others. Klansmen included police officers and deputies, and local juries seldom convicted them. After the Neshoba case the FBI, whose agents had often watched without acting, opened a secret campaign against Klan groups in September 1964, which helped to weaken the most violent ones.

The Klan's violence was a double-edged weapon. It terrified Black communities and white moderates in the places where it operated, and it killed, but it also shocked the nation and produced the federal response that the movement wanted, as at Birmingham, Neshoba and Selma.

:::key Judging the opposition
**Forms:** state and local officials (registration barriers, police, courts); Wallace and Connor (public defiance); Southern Congressmen (filibuster, committees); White Citizens' Councils (economic pressure); the Klan (bombings and murders).
**Reasons:** white supremacy, economic interest, states' rights, anti-communism, hostility to "outsiders".
**Effect:** it delayed change for years and made it dangerous, but its violence **helped the movement** by exposing the system, and after 1964–65 federal power overcame it.
**Judgement to defend:** the opposition was **strong enough to delay and to kill but not to win**, because it depended on local control that federal law and the cameras broke.
:::

## The role of the federal government

:::def Executive order
A directive issued by the President to federal officials, with the force of law inside the government and over its contractors. Presidents used executive orders on civil rights because they did not need the agreement of a Congress in which Southern Democrats held the key committees.
:::

### The executive before 1964

The Presidents who came before had taken some steps: Truman ordered the armed forces desegregated in 1948 and Eisenhower sent troops to Little Rock in 1957 (see [Post-war USA (1)](#hist.d.usa45.1) and [(2)](#hist.d.usa45.2)). John Kennedy's record is mixed, and a good answer weighs both sides.
- **For.** On 6 March 1961 Executive Order 10925 required federal contractors to take "affirmative action" to ensure equal treatment, the first official use of the phrase in this sense, and set up a committee on equal employment opportunity. Executive Order 11063 (20 November 1962) banned discrimination in housing financed by the federal government, though only in new housing. Kennedy appointed Black judges, including Thurgood Marshall to the Court of Appeals. His brother **Robert Kennedy**, the Attorney General, and the Civil Rights Division of the Justice Department sent marshals to Montgomery in May 1961, took the case of the Freedom Riders to the ICC, and encouraged the **Voter Education Project** (1962), which paid for registration drives in the South. At the University of Mississippi on 30 September 1962 the administration used marshals and then troops to enforce a federal court order for James Meredith, and in June 1963 it federalised the Alabama National Guard to enrol Vivian Malone and James Hood. On 11 June 1963 Kennedy went on television to call civil rights a moral issue, and on 19 June he sent the bill to Congress.
- **Against.** Kennedy had campaigned on civil rights but, with narrow majorities and the need for Southern votes for his other bills, he did not send a major bill until June 1963. He also appointed several segregationist judges in the South to keep Southern senators on his side. The FBI under J. Edgar Hoover seldom protected civil rights workers; its agents watched, and often did nothing, while volunteers were beaten, and Hoover suspected King of Communist links. In October 1963 Robert Kennedy authorised the FBI to tap King's phones. The pattern before 1964 was therefore **reaction, not leadership**: the executive acted when a crisis (the Riders, Ole Miss, Birmingham) left it no choice.

### Political change: the Civil Rights Acts of 1964 and 1968 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965

**The Civil Rights Act, 2 July 1964.** After Kennedy's assassination on 22 November 1963, Johnson told Congress that nothing would honour Kennedy's memory more than the earliest passage of the bill. He used his skill as a former Senate leader, and the bill passed the filibuster (see above). The Act's main parts were:
- **Title II:** no discrimination in hotels, restaurants, theatres and other places of public accommodation. The Supreme Court upheld it in *Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States* (14 December 1964).
- **Title VI:** federal money could be cut off from any programme or school district that discriminated. This was the Act's most powerful tool in practice: in the autumn of 1964 only about 2.25 per cent of Black pupils in the South attended school with whites, and fear of losing federal funds pushed districts to desegregate.
- **Title VII:** no discrimination in employment on grounds of race, colour, religion, sex or national origin, with an Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) to enforce it. At first the EEOC could only investigate and try to settle complaints.
- **Title I:** a weak voting provision, which is why the next law was needed.

**The Voting Rights Act, 6 August 1965.** The Act took the power over registration away from the Southern states.
- It banned literacy tests and similar devices in states and counties where they were used and where fewer than half of eligible people were registered or had voted in 1964 (mainly Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina, Virginia and many counties of North Carolina).
- It allowed federal examiners to register voters in those areas, and federal observers to watch the elections.
- Section 5 required those areas to get federal approval ("preclearance") before changing any election law, so that officials could not invent new barriers.
- The Supreme Court upheld the Act in *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* (7 March 1966).
The effect was swift. By the end of 1965 about a quarter of a million new Black voters had registered, about a third of them through federal examiners. The chart shows the change in three states.

In Mississippi the share rose almost ninefold (from about 6.7 to about 59.8 per cent) in two and a half years. The chart shows only three states and two dates, so it cannot show how registration varied between counties or how officials tried to limit the effect. This is called vote dilution: arranging the rules so that Black votes count for less, for example by redrawing district boundaries to split Black neighbourhoods among white-majority districts, or by switching to at-large elections, in which all voters in a town choose every seat, so that a white majority wins them all. But it shows clearly why the Act is regarded as the movement's greatest achievement. The number of Black elected officials in the United States rose from about 1,500 in 1970 to about 3,500 in 1975, according to the Joint Center for Political Studies.

**The Civil Rights Act of 1968 and the Fair Housing Act.** Johnson had asked for a fair housing law in 1966 and 1967, and opponents in the Senate had stopped it. After King's assassination on 4 April 1968 and the riots that followed, Congress passed it, and Johnson signed the **Civil Rights Act of 1968** on **11 April**. Its Title VIII, the **Fair Housing Act**, banned discrimination in the sale, rental and financing of most housing on grounds of race, colour, religion or national origin. The Act also made it a crime to cross state lines to incite a riot, a measure supporters of law and order demanded as the price for it, and gave protection to civil rights workers. Its weakness was enforcement: the Department of Housing and Urban Development could only try to settle complaints, so individuals had to sue, and stronger powers came only with an amendment in 1988. In June 1968, *Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.* separately held that a Reconstruction-era law banned private racial discrimination in selling property.

### Social policies: employment and affirmative action

- **Executive Order 11246 (24 September 1965)** required federal contractors to take affirmative action, and Nixon's revised **Philadelphia Plan** (1969) set targets for hiring Black workers in the building trades on federal projects.
- The **Equal Employment Opportunity Act (24 March 1972)** gave the EEOC power to bring cases in federal court, and extended Title VII to state and local governments, schools and universities.
- The Supreme Court's decision in *Griggs v. Duke Power Co.* (8 March 1971) said that employment tests that were not related to the job, but kept Black workers out, were illegal even when no one intended to discriminate.
These measures widened the job market for Black workers in government and large firms, and helped to create a Black middle class. But they did not reach the unemployed, and poverty in the cities stayed high, as the section on assessment below shows.

### Rulings of the Supreme Court

The Supreme Court under Chief Justice Earl Warren was a central actor, and Nixon's appointees changed it only slowly after 1969.
- ***Brown v. Board of Education*** (1954) and the follow-up ruling that desegregation must proceed "with all deliberate speed" (1955) had little effect at first. In *Green v. New Kent County* (27 May 1968) the Court demanded that schools actually be integrated, and in *Alexander v. Holmes County* (29 October 1969) it ordered an end to dual school systems "at once". *Swann v. Charlotte-Mecklenburg Board of Education* (20 April 1971) approved busing as a remedy, though *Milliken v. Bradley* (25 July 1974) limited cross-district busing and left many city schools mainly Black.
- ***Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States*** (1964) upheld the Civil Rights Act, and *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966) the Voting Rights Act.
- ***Loving v. Virginia*** (**12 June 1967**): Richard Loving, a white man, and Mildred Jeter, who was of Black and Native American descent, had married in Washington, DC, in 1958 and were convicted in Virginia of breaking its Racial Integrity Act. The judge suspended their prison sentences if they left the state for 25 years. A unanimous Supreme Court, with Chief Justice Warren writing, held that Virginia's ban violated the Equal Protection and Due Process clauses of the Fourteenth Amendment. Sixteen states, mostly in the South, had laws against interracial marriage and were forced to end them.

**Important:** only federal power could override the Southern states; the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act, with Title VI and the federal examiners, **changed the daily behaviour of officials**; the Supreme Court upheld each law and went further in *Loving* and in schools.

## How much progress was made?

The four tests give a clear picture.

| Area | Progress | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Law and public places | Segregation in public places, interstate transport and employment was illegal by 1964; Loving (1967) struck down marriage bans | Slow, patchy enforcement in some places |
| Vote and power | Mississippi registration from about 6.7 to about 59.8 per cent (1965–67); Black elected officials from about 1,500 (1970) to about 3,500 (1975) | Obstruction by other means; few Black officials in high office |
| Schools | Title VI and the courts ended the dual school system in the South by the early 1970s | In 1964 only 2.25 per cent of Black Southern pupils were in mixed schools; in the North, schools stayed segregated by housing, and Milliken (1974) limited remedies |
| Jobs and income | Title VII, the EEOC and affirmative action opened jobs; Black family income as a share of white family income rose from about 51 per cent (1959) to about 61 per cent (1970), on Census Bureau figures | Black unemployment stayed about twice the white rate; Black poverty was still about 42 per cent in 1966 (against about 55 per cent in 1959) |
| Housing | The Fair Housing Act and *Jones* ended legal discrimination | Weak enforcement; segregated neighbourhoods and "redlining" continued, especially in the North |
| Safety and justice | Federal prosecutions of the Klan began; Black Southerners could use the courts | Local juries and police still protected white offenders; the murderers of Evers and of the four girls were convicted years or decades later |

The conclusion is a mixed one. **The movement won the legal revolution it set out to win**: it ended Jim Crow and made the South a place where Black Americans could vote. It did not win economic equality, and it had little effect on the North, where discrimination was in housing and jobs and not in law, and where the riots of 1965–68 (see [A time of challenges (4)](#hist.d.usa61.4)) showed how many Black Americans felt left behind. King himself recognised this. After 1965 he took the campaign north to Chicago (1966), where open-housing marches in white neighbourhoods met violent crowds, and in 1967–68 turned to poverty, the Vietnam War and the **Poor People's Campaign** (his 1968 campaign for jobs and income for poor Americans of all races), and he was in Memphis supporting striking Black sanitation workers when he was killed.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **Taylor Branch** (1988–2006) tells the story as a drama with King and the people around him at the centre, in which nonviolent protest and violent reaction again and again pushed the nation to act.
- **Adam Fairclough** (1987) treats King and SCLC as shrewd political strategists who chose their battles to win federal action.
- **Clayborne Carson** (1981) puts SNCC's radical, democratic organising at the centre and shows how it differed from the style of the national leaders.
- **Charles Payne** (1995) shows that in Mississippi, change came through years of patient organising, often by women, before any national campaign.

:::

Support: Civil Rights Act and Voting Rights Act, Title VI, federal examiners, marshals and troops, the Supreme Court. **A Level 3 paragraph.** "The federal government was very important in civil rights. *Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with a rough balance, so Table B Level 3. But the paragraph is a **list of two sides** with "so both were important" as its conclusion: there are no criteria, no weighing and no explanation of how the two factors were connected. Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and show the link.** "If the importance of a factor is judged by whether the change could have happened without it, the federal government was essential to the legal change. Black Southerners had tried for decades to register and had been turned away by registrars, and the Southern states would not enforce *Brown*; in the autumn of 1964, ten years later, only about 2.25 per cent of Black Southern pupils were in mixed schools. *Commentary.* Two criteria are applied and tested against knowledge, and the link between the factors begins to be argued. This is Level 4. It still needs a third criterion, the limits of federal action and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view that the federal government was the most important factor is partly valid, but it mixes up two questions: which factor made the change possible, and which made it happen. I shall judge the importance of each factor by three criteria: whether the change could have happened without it, which of them set events in motion, and how far it changed daily life. By the first and third criteria the federal government was decisive; by the second it was not, and on balance I shall argue that the movement was the more important cause of change, and that the federal government was the indispensable means of its success.

On necessity and effect, federal power was essential. For a decade after *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) the Southern states obstructed the Supreme Court, and in the autumn of 1964 only about 2.25 per cent of Black pupils in the South attended school with any whites. Registrars in Mississippi, where only about 6.7 per cent of Black adults were registered in March 1965, could ignore the Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960, which relied on slow suits county by county. Only the federal government could override the states: the marshals at Montgomery in 1961, the troops at the University of Mississippi in 1962, Title VI of the Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964), which put federal money at risk, and above all the Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965), whose federal examiners and suspension of literacy tests lifted Black registration in Mississippi to about 59.8 per cent by September 1967. The Supreme Court added *Heart of Atlanta Motel* (1964), which upheld the Act, and *Loving v. Virginia* (12 June 1967), which ended state bans on interracial marriage. No local campaign could have done this.

But on initiative the movement comes first. The federal government acted when it was forced to. The Kennedy administration, anxious to keep the Southern Democrats' votes, sent no civil rights bill in 1961 or 1962; it acted at Montgomery, Alabama, and at Oxford, Mississippi (the home of the University of Mississippi), only to restore order; and after the failure of the Albany Movement in 1962 federal officials stood back. The change came with Birmingham in May 1963, when Bull Connor's dogs and hoses were filmed, and Kennedy, who had called for patience, went on television on 11 June to call civil rights a moral issue and sent his bill on 19 June. The same pattern repeats in 1965: the Voting Rights Act was announced by Johnson on 15 March, eight days after state troopers attacked the marchers at Selma, and signed within five months. SNCC's slow work in Mississippi and the Black churches' networks provided the local base, as Charles Payne argues, and the courage of ordinary people, such as Fannie Lou Hamer, created the story that the cameras told. Even the opposition's violence, at Birmingham, Neshoba and Selma, worked for the movement by turning Northern opinion. The federal government, in other words, responded rather than led, and Taylor Branch's narrative shows how closely each law followed a crisis.

The third criterion, the effect on life, also limits the case for the federal government. The laws did most for what the law could reach: public places, the vote, school desegregation in the South. They did much less for jobs, housing and income. Fair housing, passed on 11 April 1968 after King's death, was weakly enforced; Black poverty was still about 42 per cent in 1966; and in the Northern cities, where no law enforced segregation, the federal government had no easy tool, and riots broke out in Watts, Newark and Detroit. These limits show that federal action was strong where Congress and the courts could act on the South, and weak where the problem was economic.

In conclusion, the federal government was the most important factor in the sense that the legal revolution of 1964–65 could not have happened without it, and in that sense the view is correct. But it was not the most important *cause*: the movement created the crisis that made Congress act, and the laws were passed only as a response. The two factors worked as a mechanism, with the movement as the engine and federal power as the means of turning protest into law. If one must be chosen, I would choose the movement, because without it the federal government would have gone on, as before 1963, acting only in emergencies."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): three explicit criteria; a balanced argument in which both factors are weighed against each; a historian and a named case are used to support rather than decorate; a clear judgement that distinguishes "decisive" from "most important". Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise figures and dates (2.25 per cent; 6.7 to 59.8 per cent; 2 July 1964; 6 August 1965; 12 June 1967; 11 and 19 June 1963; 15 March 1965; about 42 per cent), selected for the argument and not recited. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 800 words, 35 to 40 minutes.

He did not create the movement, which began with Montgomery's boycott organisers, the Greensboro students and local people such as Fannie Lou Hamer, and the NAACP's lawyers had won *Brown* before he was known. He did not pass the laws, which depended on Johnson's skill and on federal power. His distinctive contribution was to connect the two: to choose the places (Birmingham, Selma) where local protest could be made visible, to frame the cause in the language of the Constitution and the Bible so that white Northerners could accept it, and to hold the movement to nonviolence long enough for the nation to see who was using violence. Without him, the legal changes of 1964–65 might have come later and in a different form; without the local organisers, there would have been no campaign for him to lead. After 1965 his importance declined, as the movement divided and he was unable to solve the problem of the Northern cities. I therefore judge his leadership to have been most important for the *timing* and *moral force* of the legal victories, and less important for their content or for what came after."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (13–14): a clear criterion (what would have been different), a balanced comparison with other factors, a sustained judgement that qualifies "importance" by type. Table B, Level 4–5 (12–13): precise evidence in the paragraph above and in the plan. With a paragraph on the Letter and the March on Washington, this is a Level 5 answer.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the effects of the Birmingham campaign of 1963." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Birmingham campaign of 1963 was very successful. Martin Luther King was arrested and wrote a letter from his cell. Then children marched and Bull Connor used dogs and hoses. Everyone saw it on television. A deal was made and the shops were desegregated. Then the Civil Rights Act was passed in 1964 and Black people got equal rights. So Birmingham was a total success."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the paragraph tells the story in order and then asserts "very successful" and "total success" without defining success. It does not separate the local result from the national one, treats the Civil Rights Act as a direct effect when it passed after Kennedy's assassination, a filibuster and Johnson's pressure, and ignores the violence that followed (the bombing of 15 September 1963). Table B Level 2–3 (5–7): relevant, but no dates for the Act or the Letter, no numbers and nothing on what stayed the same.

**The fix.** Use the same facts to prove an argument. "Birmingham's success was greater nationally than locally. The settlement of 10 May 1963 desegregated lunch counters and fitting rooms in downtown stores, promised some jobs and freed the prisoners, but it was slow to take effect, and was followed the next day by bombings and, on 15 September, by the murder of four girls in the 16th Street Baptist Church. Nationally, the pictures of 3 May changed the debate: Kennedy called civil rights a moral issue on 11 June and sent his bill on 19 June, and it became law, after a filibuster, on 2 July 1964. So the campaign's major effect was to make federal action possible, not to end segregation in Birmingham itself." Now every fact is evidence for a judgement, and the claim is limited and dated.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What did the Freedom Riders of 1961 set out to do, and what did they achieve?
A: CORE riders, Black and white, travelled by bus from Washington on 4 May 1961 to test a Supreme Court ruling that segregation in interstate bus terminals was illegal. They were attacked at Anniston (14 May, bus burned) and Montgomery (20 May). The result was that Robert Kennedy petitioned the Interstate Commerce Commission, which ordered the end of segregation in interstate bus terminals on 22 September 1961, effective 1 November.
Q: Why did Martin Luther King choose Birmingham in 1963, and what was "Project C"?
A: Birmingham was thoroughly segregated and had a violent record, and its Commissioner of Public Safety, Bull Connor, could be expected to answer protest with force. The campaign was called Project C (for "confrontation") and began on 3 April 1963 with sit-ins, marches and a boycott. King learned from the failure at Albany that a campaign needed a clear target and an opponent who would lose his temper.
Q: What were the consequences of the Birmingham campaign?
A: The pictures of children attacked by dogs and hoses (3 May) shocked the nation; a settlement was agreed on 10 May; Kennedy went on television on 11 June and sent his bill on 19 June; Medgar Evers was murdered on 12 June; the 16th Street Baptist Church was bombed on 15 September, killing four girls.
Q: What happened at the March on Washington on 28 August 1963?
A: About 250,000 people, perhaps a quarter of them white, gathered at the Lincoln Memorial for "jobs and freedom", organised by A. Philip Randolph and Bayard Rustin. King gave the "I have a dream" speech. It was peaceful and televised and built support for the civil rights bill, though women were missing among the main speakers.
Q: What was Freedom Summer, and why did it matter?
A: A project of COFO in Mississippi in 1964: over 700 volunteers, mostly white students, taught in Freedom Schools and helped Black residents to register. Three workers, Chaney, Goodman and Schwerner, were murdered on 21 June (found 4 August). Only about 1,600 of about 17,000 applicants were registered, but it drew national attention and set up the MFDP, whose rejection at Atlantic City in August 1964 disillusioned many activists.
Q: What happened at Selma, and what did it lead to?
A: A voting rights campaign by SNCC and SCLC in Alabama. On Bloody Sunday, 7 March 1965, state troopers attacked about 600 marchers on the Edmund Pettus Bridge. Johnson announced a voting rights bill on 15 March, the march to Montgomery took place on 21–25 March (about 25,000 arrived), and the Voting Rights Act was signed on 6 August.
Q: Give four ways in which Southern officials tried to stop Black people from registering to vote.
A: Literacy tests applied unfairly by white registrars; the poll tax; delay and obstruction (limited office hours, lost applications); and reprisals (loss of jobs, eviction, credit cut off) and violence. In March 1965 only about 6.7 per cent of Black adults in Mississippi were registered.
Q: Who were Bull Connor and George Wallace, and what did each do?
A: Connor was Birmingham's Commissioner of Public Safety, who used dogs and hoses on marchers in May 1963. Wallace was Governor of Alabama, who declared "segregation forever" on 14 January 1963 and stood in the door at the University of Alabama on 11 June 1963 before stepping aside.
Q: Name the main forms of opposition to civil rights and one reason why many white Southerners supported that opposition.
A: State officials; Southern Congressmen (the filibuster, the Southern Manifesto of 1956); the White Citizens' Councils; the Ku Klux Klan. Popular reasons include belief in white supremacy, economic interest, states' rights and anti-communism.
Q: Give the main provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
A: 1964: Title II bans segregation in public accommodations; Title VI allows federal funds to be cut off; Title VII bans employment discrimination and creates the EEOC. 1965: suspends literacy tests in areas with low registration, allows federal examiners, and requires preclearance of election-law changes.
Q: What was *Loving v. Virginia*?
A: A Supreme Court case decided on 12 June 1967, unanimously striking down Virginia's ban on interracial marriage. It also ended the laws against interracial marriage in the 16 states that still had them.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons for the opposition of white Southerners to civil rights in the 1960s.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: how widespread each reason was and how far it explains the behaviour of different groups. Reasons: belief in white supremacy and fear of "social equality"; economic interest (cheap labour, jobs); states' rights and memory of Reconstruction; anti-communism (Hoover's suspicion of King); hostility to "outsiders" (Freedom Summer); local control of the justice system. Evidence: registration barriers (6.7 per cent of Black adults registered in Mississippi in 1965); White Citizens' Councils (from 1954); the Southern Manifesto (1956); Wallace's inaugural address (14 January 1963); Klan crimes (15 September 1963, 21 June 1964, 25 March 1965). Level 5 judges which reasons mattered most (for example that white supremacy and economic interest underlay the others) and notes that opposition was not uniform: some white Southerners (businessmen in Birmingham in May 1963) accepted change.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was more important than the Voting Rights Act of 1965.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: breadth of effect, difficulty of what it overcame, permanence. Support for the 1964 Act: ended legal segregation in public places; Title VI (2.25 per cent of Black pupils in mixed schools in 1964); Title VII and the EEOC; a national symbol. Support for the 1965 Act: the vote gave Black Southerners political power (Mississippi registration 6.7 to 59.8 per cent, 1965–67; Black elected officials about 1,500 in 1970 to 3,500 in 1975) and a means of defending their other rights; the 1964 Act's voting provisions were weak. Level 5 argues, for example, that the 1964 Act changed daily life more widely, but the 1965 Act changed power, and that the second depended on the first.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the March on Washington of 28 August 1963 in the progress of civil rights.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: effect on opinion, effect on Congress, effect on the movement, and what it did not change. Importance: about 250,000 people, peaceful and televised; King's speech; the demand for jobs as well as freedom; support from unions and churches; it gave Kennedy's bill, sent on 19 June, a public base. Limits: the bill was still in committee; the Birmingham church bombing came on 15 September; Kennedy's death on 22 November and Johnson's work mattered more for passage; the men chosen as speakers, the toning down of John Lewis's speech and Malcolm X's criticism show divisions. Level 5 weighs the march as a demonstration of strength against the real causes of the Act (Birmingham, Johnson, the filibuster vote) and judges its importance as one of timing and symbol.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Violent opposition did more to help than to harm the civil rights movement.' Evaluate this view.
H: Think of three occasions when violence led to federal action.
H: What did violence cost the movement in lives, and how did it affect SNCC's belief in nonviolence?
A: Indicative content. Help: the Anniston and Montgomery attacks led to the ICC order; Birmingham (dogs and hoses) to the civil rights bill; the Neshoba murders to FBI action and the Civil Rights Act's passage in the same summer; Bloody Sunday to the Voting Rights Act in five months. Harm: the murders of Evers, the four girls, Chaney, Goodman, Schwerner, Liuzzo and Jimmie Lee Jackson; fear kept people from registering (about 1,600 of about 17,000 applicants accepted in 1964); violence helped to turn SNCC away from nonviolence. Level 5 argues that violence helped only in public and in front of cameras, and distinguishes the movement's strategy from the cost paid by individuals.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'By 1968 little had changed in the daily lives of most African Americans.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate the South from the North, and law from income.
H: Use three figures.
A: Indicative content. Support: Black poverty about 42 per cent in 1966; Black unemployment about twice the white rate; segregated housing and schools in the North; riots in 1965–68; King's turn to poverty. Challenge: legal segregation ended (1964); the vote (6.7 to 59.8 per cent in Mississippi); mixed schools in the South after 1964; EEOC and affirmative action; Black family income from about 51 per cent of white family income (1959) to about 61 per cent (1970). Level 5 judges that the claim is true of the North and of the Black poor, but false for the Black South's civil and political life.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the Supreme Court did more than Congress to advance civil rights in the 1960s.
H: Remember that the Court acted in the 1950s too.
H: Which of the Court's rulings depended on a law passed by Congress?
A: Indicative content. For the Court: *Brown* (1954); *Boynton* (1960); *Loving* (1967); *Green* (1968) and *Alexander v. Holmes* (1969) on schools; *Griggs* (1971). For Congress: the Civil Rights Act (1964), the Voting Rights Act (1965) and the Fair Housing Act (1968) were the laws that changed the South; the Court's decisions in *Heart of Atlanta* and *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* upheld them. The Court's rulings of the 1950s had little effect without law and enforcement. Level 5 argues that the Court prepared the ground and approved the changes but that Congress and the executive had the power to enforce them.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which local people, rather than national leaders, made the civil rights movement succeed.
H: Choose two campaigns and ask who started and who finished each.
H: Compare the views of Payne and Branch.
A: Indicative content. Local people: Montgomery's boycotters; the Greensboro students; SNCC's work in Mississippi; Hamer, Moses, Baker and Septima Clark's citizenship schools; Selma's Black residents before King came; the MFDP. National leaders: King's strategy and speeches; Randolph and Rustin's March; Johnson's laws. Level 5 judges that the two were interdependent: local organisers built the base and national leaders gave it national weight, and says which mattered more for which outcome (the vote or the law).
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The progress made in civil rights in the 1960s was greater in law than in life.' Evaluate this view.
H: Divide the evidence into what the law said and what changed in people's lives.
H: Consider the South and the North separately, and the vote against jobs.
A: Support: laws on public places, voting, housing and jobs (1964–68, 1972) against weak enforcement, Black poverty about 42 per cent in 1966, school and housing segregation in the North, the riots of 1965–68, the Kerner Commission's warning. Challenge: the vote rose from 6.7 to 59.8 per cent in Mississippi; Black elected officials more than doubled between 1970 and 1975; the end of the dual school system; the Klan's decline; the new Black middle class. A* judgement distinguishes the places where law changed life (the Southern vote, public places) from those where it could not (the Northern ghetto, jobs), and explains why: laws can change official behaviour but not the distribution of wealth.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'In the years 1961–65 the federal government acted on civil rights only when nonviolent protest and violent reaction left it no choice.' Evaluate this view.
H: Take each major law and ask what crisis preceded it.
H: Where did the federal government act without a crisis, or a crisis produce no action?
A: Support: the Freedom Rides and the ICC order; Birmingham and the bill of 19 June 1963; Neshoba and the FBI's campaign against the Klan; Selma and the Voting Rights Act. Challenge: Albany (crisis, no action); the Kennedy brothers' calculation of votes; Johnson's own commitment and skill; the Cold War image of the United States; long-term causes (Black migration north and the Black vote in the 1960 election); local organising that created the crises. A* judgement says that the crisis-and-response pattern explains the timing of the laws better than their content, and that the movement's strategy was to build the crisis deliberately.
:::
