It gives you one unseen question, no notes and 105 minutes (Paper 2) or 75 minutes (Paper 1), and it expects you to pull the right facts out of your memory and aim them at the question. :::exam How to use this chapter
**You need:** the chapters for your own option, a timer and paper. You do not need the other two options. Work through your option's section; if you are curious, try another one, because the skills are identical.

**The method, for every question:**
1. Read the question, plan for the time the paper would give it (below), then write the answer closed-book.
2. Only then open the answer. Mark yourself against the levels: the indicative content is a guide, not a checklist. A different valid argument earns the same marks ("accept any other valid responses" is on every real mark scheme).
3. Write one line in an error log: the level you reached, the one thing that would have raised it, and the fact you did not know. Revise that fact, then redo the weakest part.

**Times (from the paper guides):** Paper 1 part (a) 20 minutes for 15 marks; part (b) 42 minutes for 25 marks, including a 4-minute plan. Paper 2: 14 minutes for each (a) [10] and 32 minutes for each (b) [20], including a 4-minute plan.

## How the practice is set

- **Paper 1.** Each option has one full question: four sources A to D, part (a) comparing two of them, part (b) on all four. In the real exam each AS topic is the Paper 1 sources topic in one year; these sets use Russia (European), Civil War and Reconstruction (American) and the 1929–39 topic (International). They are not the topics of [Mock Paper 1](#hist.mock.p1), so you can do both. The sources are real documents, shortened, with the provenance a real paper prints. A shortened source keeps the document's own order and marks every cut with dots.
- **Paper 2.** Each topic of each option has one part (a), one part (b) and one extra part (b) to plan. Paper 2 sets two questions in your option, one on each of the two topics that are not the year's Paper 1 topic, so answer these in pairs: one (a) and one (b) on the same topic, in 46 minutes.
- **Answers.** Every answer gives the indicative content (what a good answer could use, not a model to copy) and what a top-level answer does. Marks are never printed as tokens: levels are written in words, as on the real mark schemes.

## Option A: European option (France, Germany, Russia)

### Paper 1 practice: Russia, 1917–24

Russia is the European Paper 1 topic in 2029. Read the four sources, then do both parts without opening the answers. As in the course, dates before February 1918 are Old Style (Julian) unless marked New Style; Russia changed to the New Style calendar in February 1918, so the 1921 dates are New Style.

:::note Sources A to D: Russia, 1917–21
**Source A**

The Commission is to be named the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission and is to be attached to the Soviet of People's Commissars. … The duties of the Commission will be: 1. To persecute and break up all acts of counter-revolution and sabotage all over Russia, no matter what their origin. 2. To bring before the Revolutionary Tribunal all counter-revolutionaries and saboteurs …

*Adapted (shortened) from the decree of the Council of People's Commissars that set up the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission (the Cheka), 7 December 1917 (20 December New Style), in an English translation.*

**Source B**

A republic of Soviets is a higher form of democracy than the usual bourgeois republic with a Constituent Assembly. The Socialist-Revolutionaries came out with united election lists for the Constituent Assembly in the middle of October 1917, but they split in November 1917, after the elections and before the Assembly met. Therefore the lists no longer express the real will of the people.

*Adapted from Lenin, Theses on the Constituent Assembly, written 11 or 12 December 1917 and published in Pravda on 26 December 1917.*

**Source C**

At present the soviets do not express the will of the workers and peasants. We therefore demand that new elections to the soviets be held immediately by secret ballot, with freedom of campaigning beforehand for all workers and peasants. Freedom of speech and press must be given to workers and peasants, and to anarchist and left socialist parties.

*Adapted from the Petropavlovsk resolution, adopted by the crews of the battleships at Kronstadt, 28 February 1921, in the translation in Paul Avrich, The Anarchists in the Russian Revolution (1973).*

**Source D**

The surplus appropriation system was a compulsory state monopoly, driven by the needs of war, not by economic principle. The peasant needs an incentive: it is your job as a proprietor to produce, and the state will take a minimum tax. We are correcting the relations between the proletariat and the peasantry.

*Adapted from Lenin, report on the substitution of a tax in kind for the surplus appropriation system, Tenth Congress of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks), 15 March 1921.*
:::

:::try Paper 1 question: Russia
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and C. Compare these two sources as evidence about the relationship between the Bolshevik government and its opponents.
A: **Indicative content.** *Similarities:* both are about who has the right to rule in the name of the people. Source A names "counter-revolution and sabotage" as enemies of the revolution; Source C names the Bolshevik-run soviets as having lost the confidence of "the workers and peasants". Both therefore justify themselves by claiming to speak for the working people and both see the other side as an enemy of them. *Differences:* Source A is an instrument of control, giving a new body the task to "persecute and break up" opposition "no matter what their origin", with the Revolutionary Tribunal behind it; Source C is a demand for the freedoms that such a body takes away: a secret ballot, "freedom of speech and press", and a hearing for "anarchist and left socialist parties". A looks forward, at the start of Bolshevik rule; C looks back at what it has become after three years. A treats opposition as a crime; C treats it as a right.

*Why they differ (the step to Level 4).* Source A is a government decree, issued six weeks after the October seizure of power, when the Bolsheviks had no majority in the country (they won about a quarter of the votes in the Constituent Assembly elections), civil servants were on strike and the Don Cossacks were rising. It shows what the government feared and the weapon it chose, not what the Cheka did later: "preliminary investigation only" in the decree's next clause suggests limits that were soon passed (the Red Terror was proclaimed in September 1918). Source C was adopted by the crews of the battleships *Petropavlovsk* and *Sevastopol* after strikes in Petrograd over food and fuel, at the end of the Civil War and War Communism. The sailors had been among the Bolsheviks' strongest supporters in 1917, which makes their complaint more telling; but this is a collective resolution by one side in a dispute, and it was soon followed by the Kronstadt rising (28 February–18 March 1921), which the Red Army crushed. Level 4 explains the contrast through these two contexts and weighs the sources: A is reliable on intention but not on practice, C reliable on the grievances of 1921 but partisan about the solution. **Level 2** (4–7) says A is a decree and C is a resolution and one is for the Bolsheviks and one against them. **Level 3** (8–11) adds supported similarities and differences. **Level 4** (12–15) explains why, as above.

Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources. 'The Bolsheviks consolidated their power mainly through force.' How far do these sources support this view?
H: Sort the sources first: which show the use of force or control, and which show concession, argument or the need for consent?
H: Evaluate before you judge: who is speaking in each source, to whom, and why at that moment?
H: Use your own knowledge to say what the sources do not show: the Civil War, the Red Terror, the dispersal of the Constituent Assembly, Kronstadt, the NEP.
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* Source A, the Cheka's founding, shows a state building a political police within six weeks of taking power. Source B, a leader's justification for ignoring an elected Assembly, shows that the Bolsheviks put their own idea of the "will of the people" above an election they had lost; own knowledge: the Assembly met on 5 January 1918 and was dispersed by guards. Source C is evidence from inside the revolution that by 1921 the soviets no longer expressed the people's will and that free speech and a secret ballot had gone. Own knowledge adds the Red Terror (from 5 September 1918), War Communism's grain requisitioning, and Tukhachevsky's attack on Kronstadt. *Challenge:* Source D shows a regime that has to change policy because force had reached its limit: Lenin admits that requisitioning was "driven by the needs of war, not by economic principle" and that "the peasant needs an incentive". It shows that consent from the peasants, the great majority of the people, had to be bought. Source B is also an argument, not only a threat: it claims a rival form of democracy, the soviets, and the Decrees on Peace and Land of 26 October 1917 had given the Bolsheviks real support. Own knowledge: the Reds won the Civil War partly because peasants feared a White victory and the return of the landlords, and the ban on factions (March 1921) shows control also worked inside the party.

*Evaluation (the step to Level 5).* **A** is an official decree, recorded for the government's own use and not published until 1927: sound evidence of the government's aims, silent on practice, and its date (December 1917) is early; it is not evidence that force was the main means by 1921. **B** is a leader's argument published to defend a decision already taken. It is propaganda and says nothing of the three-quarters of voters who chose other parties, but it is strong evidence of how Lenin justified power. **C** is partisan, and written during a revolt, but the revolt itself, and the Petrograd strikes before it, are good evidence that the regime was in trouble; the Bolsheviks' own reaction (the tax in kind within three weeks) confirms it. **D** is the most valuable: a report to the Party's own Congress, addressed to communists rather than to the public, by the man most responsible for the policy; yet it was delivered while the Kronstadt rising was in progress, and the delegates were sent to put it down. A leader could admit the error in the same week as the soldiers moved in.

*Judgement.* A Level 5 answer weighs the two sides and decides. For example: force was the means that kept the Bolsheviks in power (A, B and C, and Kronstadt), but they did not rely on it alone. They conceded when they had to (D, the NEP), used ideas and slogans that appealed to peasants and workers, and benefited from the disunity and unpopularity of their enemies. The sources support the view to a large extent, but D shows the limit of force: Lenin could not feed the cities by terror alone. The weaker answer treats all sources as "bias" and never says which side is stronger.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) ladder: A and C from level to level
**Level 2 (about 6 marks).** "Source A is a decree that sets up the Cheka and says it will persecute counter-revolutionaries. Source C is a resolution from sailors who want free elections. They are different because one is for the government and one is against. Source A is from 1917 and Source C is from 1921."

*Why Level 2:* it identifies differences and asserts them, but only on the surface. There is no similarity, no support from the words of the sources, and "for" and "against" are not explained.

**Level 3 (about 10 marks).** "Both sources are about political control. Source A sets up the Cheka to 'persecute and break up all acts of counter-revolution', while Source C says the soviets 'do not express the will of the workers and peasants'. Both claim to act for the workers and peasants. They differ on freedom: Source A creates a Tribunal to try opponents, whereas Source C demands 'freedom of speech and press'. The first is from the government and the second from the sailors."

*Why Level 3:* similarities and differences are both identified and supported with phrases from the sources. But the reasons are not given: nothing says why a government in 1917 and sailors in 1921 would write like this.

**Level 4 (about 14 marks).** "Both sources claim to speak for the revolution and its workers: Source A justifies a political police as the defence of the revolution against 'counter-revolution and sabotage', Source C claims that the soviets have lost touch with 'the workers and peasants'. They differ over whether opposition is a crime or a right. Source A orders the Cheka 'to persecute and break up all acts of counter-revolution', whatever their origin; Source C says that even 'anarchist and left socialist parties' must be given 'freedom of speech and press'. The context explains the contrast. Source A comes six weeks after the seizure of power, when the Bolsheviks, who had won only about a quarter of the votes in the Constituent Assembly elections, were facing a strike of officials and the first armed resistance; the decree was written to meet a crisis, and its limits ('preliminary investigation only') show it was meant as an emergency tool. Source C comes after three years of civil war, requisitioning and the Red Terror, from sailors who had been the Bolsheviks' own shock troops in 1917, so their break is telling. As evidence, A shows the government's fears and aims but not how the Cheka was used; C is reliable on the grievances of early 1921 but is one side's demand, and the sailors' own call for 'left socialist parties' to be heard shows they wanted a different socialism, not a return of the Tsar."

*Why Level 4:* the comparison is developed and the differences are explained through the context and the nature of each source, with a judgement of what each is good evidence for.

### Paper 2 practice: France

:::try Paper 2 practice: France, 1774–1814
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) divided France.
A: **Indicative content.** The Civil Constitution of the Clergy (12 July 1790) made the Church a department of the state. Priests and bishops would be elected by the voters of the department, including non-Catholics; the dioceses were redrawn to match the 83 departments; and the Pope was not consulted. The Assembly had nationalised Church lands in November 1789 and now had to pay the clergy, so it wanted to control them. The oath of loyalty (27 November 1790) forced every priest to choose: about half refused, and the "refractory" priests were backed by many of their parishioners, particularly in the west, while "jurors" were backed by revolutionary towns. The Pope condemned the settlement in 1791, so loyalty to the Church and loyalty to the Revolution now pulled against each other. Louis XVI, a devout Catholic, signed it under pressure, and when a crowd stopped him leaving Paris on 18 April 1791 to take Easter communion from a priest who had refused the oath, the Revolution began to doubt his sincerity. **Level 4** (9–10) connects these: the Assembly's financial need (Church lands) led to state pay and then state control; control led to the oath; the oath divided priests, parishes and the king, and created the first mass counter-revolution. A Level 2 answer describes the law and says "people were angry".

Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Napoleon Bonaparte fell from power mainly because of his own mistakes.' How far do you agree with this view?
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* the Continental System (1806) hurt France's own trade and made enemies of neutrals; the invasion of Spain and the Peninsular War (1808–14) tied down hundreds of thousands of men in a war of ambush that he could not win; the invasion of Russia in June 1812 was a gamble that destroyed the Grande Armée; conscription and heavy taxes after 1810 wore out French support. *Challenge:* British sea power (Trafalgar, 1805) made the Continental System necessary and invasion impossible; the allies' unity after 1813, helped by British money, brought larger armies together at Leipzig (October 1813); nationalism in Spain, Germany and Russia turned his conquests into oppressed nations; the allies entered Paris on 31 March 1814 and he abdicated on 6 April. Own mistakes themselves came from the nature of his system: a regime based on victory could not afford to stop. **Level 5** (17–20) sets criteria (what caused the loss of resources? the loss of will?) and compares: the Russian disaster was his decision, but the allies' unity and the nationalist reaction turned a defeat into a collapse. A strong answer judges, for example, that his mistakes created the conditions and the allies' unity delivered the fall; a Level 3 answer lists mistakes only.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent did the Revolution of 1789–99 change the lives of the French people?
H: Which groups? Choose three (peasants, the bourgeoisie, women, the clergy) and test each.
H: Distinguish what changed in law from what changed in daily life.
A: **Indicative content.** *Change:* the end of the three Estates and of privilege in law (August Decrees, 4–11 August 1789); equal taxation; peasants freed from personal servitude, with other dues redeemable until 1793; Church lands sold; the 83 departments and elected local government; a new legal system (elected judges, juries); assignats and then inflation that hit workers and savers; the Terror, the Vendée and the wars, which cost lives and rights. *Continuity:* peasants still paid for dues until 1793; most people stayed poor; women gained inheritance and divorce rights but not the vote; the Directory's property franchise restored an elite; Napoleon kept many of the changes (the Civil Code) and ended political liberty. *Level 5* answers choose a test (law, politics, daily life) and weigh groups against each other: for example, the biggest permanent change was legal equality and the end of feudal dues, but the experience of the decade was dominated by war and instability.
:::

### Paper 2 practice: Germany

:::try Paper 2 practice: Germany, 1815–71
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Frederick William IV of Prussia refused the imperial crown offered by the Frankfurt Parliament in April 1849.
A: **Indicative content.** He believed that kings ruled by divine right: a crown offered by an assembly of the people, not by the German princes, was in his view a gift from revolutionaries and was beneath him. The offer was made on the basis of a liberal constitution which Prussia had not agreed and which the king felt limited his power. Austria had rejected the constitution, and he feared war with Austria and Russia if he accepted. His own position had changed since March 1848: his army had regained control in Berlin (November–December 1848), he had imposed a Prussian constitution (5 December 1848), and he no longer needed the Parliament. The vote that made him emperor was narrow in the Parliament itself (267 to 263 for a hereditary emperor), and the Parliament had no army or money to compel him. He refused on 3 April 1849. **Level 4** (9–10) links the king's beliefs, the Parliament's weakness and the change in the balance of power since March 1848, ending with the effect: with Prussia's refusal, the Frankfurt Parliament lost its last chance.

Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Economic developments, not political ideas, did most to bring about the unification of Germany.' How far do you agree with this view?
A: **Indicative content.** *Economic:* the Prussian customs law of 1818 and the Zollverein (1 January 1834: 18 states, about 23 million people, Austria outside) created a common market under Prussian leadership; railways (first line 7 December 1835, about 6,000 km by 1850) tied the states together and made fast troop movements possible, as in 1866 and 1870; Prussian coal and iron gave Krupp guns. *Political:* nationalism and liberalism (Hambach 1832, the Song of the Germans 1841, the Rhine crisis of 1840) gave unity a cause; the Frankfurt Parliament in 1848–49 made the idea national; but those movements failed; unity came through Bismarck's diplomacy and war (Denmark 1864, Königgrätz 3 July 1866, the Ems telegram and war with France 1870, the proclamation at Versailles 18 January 1871). The Zollverein excluded Austria and gave Prussia economic leadership, but it did not unite Germany politically; the southern states stayed out of the North German Confederation in 1866 until the war with France. **Level 5** (17–20) decides: economics created the possibility (Prussian leadership, Austria's exclusion) but the decisive causes were political and military: Bismarck used the situation, and the French war created the emotion. A Level 3 answer describes the Zollverein and then Bismarck without comparing them.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was Austria's decline responsible for the failure of the Frankfurt Parliament? (Plan this one: do not write it in full.)
H: Think about what Austria did in 1848–49: Radetzky, Windischgrätz, Schwarzenberg, and the Kremsier constitution.
A: **Plan.** *For:* Austria's recovery in 1848–49 (Radetzky in Italy, Windischgrätz in Prague and Vienna, Russian help in Hungary, 13 August 1849) ended the revolution in the Habsburg lands and left the Parliament with no way to include Austria in a united Germany (grossdeutsch against kleindeutsch); Austria rejected the constitution. *Against:* Austria's recovery was a response to the Parliament's weakness; it had no army or money and depended on the princes; Frederick William IV's refusal on 3 April 1849 was the decisive blow; and the revolutionaries divided between liberals and radicals, and the middle class and artisans. **Judgement:** Austria's revival removed a possible option, but the Parliament's own weakness and Prussia's refusal were more important.
:::

### Paper 2 practice: Russia

:::try Paper 2 practice: Russia, 1881–1924
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Tsarist regime survived the 1905 Revolution.
A: **Indicative content.** The army stayed loyal in most of the country, even if there were mutinies (the *Potemkin*, June 1905); the end of the war with Japan (Treaty of Portsmouth, 23 August 1905) freed troops. The opposition was divided: liberals, socialists, workers and peasants wanted different things, and the October Manifesto (17 October) split the liberals, who now divided into Octobrists (satisfied) and Kadets (not). Repression followed: the Moscow uprising was put down in December and field courts-martial were set up in August 1906, and roughly 3,000 people were executed over 1906–09. A French loan of about 2.25 billion francs (1906) made the government financially safe. Stolypin's land reform later added support from richer peasants. **Level 4** (9–10) connects these: the concessions of October divided the opposition, which let the army be used against the rest; the foreign loan removed the financial pressure.

Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Trotsky's leadership was the most important reason for the Bolshevik victory in the Civil War.' How far do you agree with this view?
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* Trotsky, as Commissar for War, built the Red Army from scratch with conscription and the use of former Tsarist officers under political commissars; his armoured train kept morale; the Reds were disciplined. *Challenge:* the Reds held the centre (Moscow, Petrograd, railways, industry) while the Whites were scattered round the edges (Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin in the south, Yudenich in the north-west, Wrangel in Crimea); the Whites had no single leader or policy, were associated with the landlords, and received only half-hearted foreign help; the Bolsheviks used War Communism and the Terror to feed the army and control the population; peasants feared a White victory more than Red requisitioning. *Level 5* (17–20) compares the factors against a criterion such as "what made the difference at the points of crisis (1919)": the Reds' geography and the Whites' disunity gave the Reds their opportunity, but Trotsky's army made use of it.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far did the 1905 Revolution change the government of Russia?
H: Compare what changed in law (Fundamental Laws, Duma) with what stayed in practice (Article 87, the Tsar's powers, the electoral law of 3 June 1907).
A: **Plan.** *Change:* the October Manifesto promised civil liberties and an elected Duma with a share in law-making; Fundamental Laws of 23 April 1906; four Dumas; political parties and a press; Stolypin's reforms. *Continuity:* the Tsar kept "supreme autocratic power" in the Fundamental Laws (Article 4), control of ministers, the army and foreign policy, and the right to rule by decree in recess (Article 87); the First and Second Dumas were dissolved and the electoral law was changed on 3 June 1907 to produce a conservative Third Duma. **Judgement:** the form changed, the substance changed little; the regime conceded to survive, not to share power.

## Option B: American option (Civil War and Reconstruction, Gilded Age and Progressive Era, Crash, Depression and New Deal)

### Paper 1 practice: Reconstruction, 1865–66

Civil War and Reconstruction is the American Paper 1 topic in 2029. The sources are from the first year after the war.

:::note Sources A to D: Reconstruction, 1865–66
**Source A**

They ought never to be recognized as capable of acting in the Union, or of being counted as valid States, until the Constitution shall have been so amended as to make it what its framers intended; and so as to secure perpetual ascendancy to the party of the Union; and so as to render our republican Government firm and stable forever. … If we do not furnish them with homesteads, and hedge them around with protective laws; if we leave them to the legislation of their late masters, we had better have left them in bondage.

*From the speech of Thaddeus Stevens, Radical Republican Representative from Pennsylvania, to the House of Representatives, 18 December 1865 (Congressional Globe). Shortened. In the first sentence "They" means the former Confederate states; "them" in the last sentences means the freed people.*

**Source B**

We in our present condition are without protection, so far as the laws of the State are concerned, and but for the strong arm of the military, we feel that we have no where to look for that protection which is essential for the safety of our persons or our property, our wives or our children. … We claim, then, as citizens of this State, the laws of the Commonwealth shall give to all men equal protection; that each and every man may appeal to the law for his equal rights without regard to the color of his skin; and we believe this can only be done by extending to us the elective franchise …

*From "The Appeal", an address by the delegates of the Convention of the Colored People of Virginia, Alexandria, Virginia, 2–5 August 1865, as printed in the Convention's Proceedings (1865). Shortened.*

**Source C**

In all our history, in all our experience as a people living under Federal and State law, no such system as that contemplated by the details of this bill has ever before been proposed or adopted. They establish for the security of the colored race safeguards which go infinitely beyond any that the General Government has ever provided for the white race. In fact, the distinction of race and color is by the bill made to operate in favor of the colored and against the white race.

*From the message of President Andrew Johnson returning the Civil Rights bill to the Senate with his objections (the veto message), 27 March 1866. Shortened.*

**Source D**

Be it further enacted, That all freedmen, free Negroes, and mulattoes in this state over the age of eighteen years found … with no lawful employment or business, or found unlawfully assembling themselves together … shall be deemed vagrants; and, on conviction thereof, shall be fined … fifty dollars … and imprisoned at the discretion of the court …

*From Section 2 of "An Act to Amend the Vagrant Laws of the State", passed by the Mississippi legislature in 1865 (one of the Black Codes), in the document's own terms. Shortened.*
:::

:::try Paper 1 question: Reconstruction
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and C. Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to the role of the federal government in Reconstruction.
A: **Indicative content.** *Similarities:* both treat the future of the freed people as a question of how far the federal government should go: Stevens says they must be given "homesteads" and "protective laws"; Johnson accepts that the bill sets up "safeguards" for "the colored race", even if he objects to their extent. Both appeal to the principles of the Constitution and American history ("what its framers intended"; "in all our history"). *Differences:* Stevens wants Congress to take the lead and to use its power over the conquered South to remake it; Johnson says the bill's system is new and goes "infinitely beyond" anything the government has done for the white race, and he calls it favouritism. Stevens would say that without protection the freed people would be better "left … in bondage"; Johnson treats protection as inequality. Stevens openly says the aim includes "perpetual ascendancy to the party of the Union"; Johnson talks only of principle and avoids party interest.

*Why they differ (Level 4).* Stevens spoke on 18 December 1865, when Congress met for the first time since the war to find that Johnson had already restored Southern governments, which were electing former Confederates and passing Black Codes. He was a leader of the Radical Republicans, who wanted far more than Johnson, and he spoke to persuade other Republicans to take control of Reconstruction; his speech is evidence of the Radical position, not of Congress as a whole. Johnson, a Southern Democrat from Tennessee who had become President when Lincoln was shot, wrote a formal veto message to defend states' rights and his own plan; the words "in favor of the colored and against the white race" tell us about his own attitudes as well as his argument, and his message did not hold: Congress passed the bill over his veto on 9 April 1866. So both sources are strong evidence of how each side wanted to present its case, and weak as evidence of what was generally believed. **Level 2** (4–7) gives similarities or differences (Stevens wants protection, Johnson does not). **Level 3** (8–11) supports both from the words. **Level 4** (12–15) explains why through context, as above.

Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources. 'The main concern of Reconstruction policy in 1865–66 was to protect the freed people.' How far do these sources support this view?
H: Whose policy? Separate what Congress, the President and the Southern legislatures were trying to do.
H: Look for a second purpose in Source A and a different priority in Source C.
H: Do not only call the sources "biased": say what each is good evidence for, given who wrote it and when.
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* Source A names the freed people's needs, "homesteads" and "protective laws", and warns of the alternative. Source B, from the freed people themselves, shows that protection was the central problem as they saw it: "without protection", dependent on "the strong arm of the military", and claiming equal protection through the vote. Source C accepts that the Civil Rights bill is about "safeguards" for Black Americans; its dispute is about how far they should go, which shows that protection was the live question. Own knowledge: the Freedmen's Bureau (extended by Congress in 1866), the Civil Rights Act (9 April 1866) and the Fourteenth Amendment (Congress passed it in June 1866, ratified 9 July 1868). *Challenge:* Source A openly aims at "perpetual ascendancy to the party of the Union", a second purpose for a Radical who needed Black votes and Southern states kept from the old balance of power. Source C shows that the President's priority was the balance between state and federal power and the interests of white citizens; Johnson's own plan (the amnesty of 29 May 1865) had restored Southern governments with almost no guarantees for freed people. Source D shows that the Southern legislatures were mainly concerned to control Black labour: "vagrancy" with "no lawful employment" a crime, and a fine and prison as the penalty, so that policy in the restored states was the opposite of protection. So three groups, three aims.

*Evaluation (Level 5).* **A** is a persuasive speech by a leader of one faction, in a debate to win his colleagues; it is reliable on the Radical position and frank about party motives, which makes it more credible on motive than a speech that spoke only of principle; it does not tell us what most Republicans thought. **B** is a collective address by a convention of Black Virginians, a public appeal to the American people and to the federal government: partisan by purpose but unique in showing the freed people's own priorities, and its words match what the Black conventions of 1865 asked everywhere. It is also a plea in the middle of an unfinished policy: the "strong arm of the military" is still there in August 1865. **C** is a formal presidential message, written to defend a veto; it is reliable on how Johnson argued and what he feared, but it is his case, made after a political quarrel with Congress, and Congress overrode it. **D** is a law, the most precise kind of evidence for what a state government decided; but it is one state, it shows the words and not how magistrates used them, and it is evidence of Southern policy, not of the policy "of Reconstruction" as Congress later defined it.

*Judgement.* A Level 5 answer decides how far: the protection of the freed people was the central question of 1865–66 (all four sources show it), and for Congress and the freed people it was the main concern; but it was not the main concern of the President or the South, and even the Radicals mixed it with party and Union security. The sources support the view only partly: it is true of one side of the argument and false of the policy that was actually in force in 1865. A Level 3 answer sorts the sources into "for" and "against" and calls Source D "biased".

### Paper 2 practice: Civil War and Reconstruction

:::try Paper 2 practice: Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854 increased tension between North and South.
A: **Indicative content.** The Act (30 May 1854), proposed by Senator Stephen Douglas, organised the Kansas and Nebraska territories and repealed the Missouri Compromise's ban on slavery north of 36°30′. It put the decision on slavery in the hands of settlers (popular sovereignty), so slavery could in theory spread into land that had been closed to it for 34 years. Northerners felt that the South had broken a settlement they had relied on and saw the Act as proof of the "Slave Power". Southerners, in turn, saw any Northern attempt to stop slavery in Kansas as aggression. Violence followed in "Bleeding Kansas" (1854–59), and the Brooks–Sumner attack in the Senate (22 May 1856) showed that argument was giving way to force. The Whig Party collapsed and the Republican Party was founded in 1854 on opposition to the extension of slavery, which was a party that existed only in the North, so politics became sectional. **Level 4** (9–10) connects the repeal to the violence in Kansas and then to the new parties; **Level 3** explains one effect well.

Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Lincoln's election in 1860 was the main reason why the Civil War began in April 1861.' How far do you agree with this view?
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* Lincoln won on 6 November 1860 with 39.8 per cent of the vote and 180 of 303 electoral votes, without carrying a single Southern state, so the South could be outvoted for the first time by a president elected by the North alone. South Carolina seceded on 20 December 1860, and six more states by 1 February; they formed the Confederacy at Montgomery. Southerners feared that a Republican president would end the spread of slavery and so, in the long run, slavery. *Challenge:* the election was the occasion, not the cause: the deeper cause was slavery, as the secession declarations themselves say; the Kansas–Nebraska Act, Dred Scott (6 March 1857) and John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry (October 1859) had already shown the failure of compromise; the compromise attempts of winter 1860–61 (the Crittenden proposals) failed because Lincoln refused any extension of slavery. The election led to secession, but not yet to war: war came on 12–13 April 1861 with the bombardment of Fort Sumter, and Lincoln's call for 75,000 volunteers (15 April) brought four more states (Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina) into the Confederacy. **Level 5** (17–20) separates causes of secession from causes of war, and judges that the election was the main reason for secession, but Sumter and the volunteers' call were the more direct reasons for the war; both rested on slavery.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Union generals' failures, not Confederate strengths, explain why the Civil War lasted four years.' How far do you agree? (Plan this one.)
H: Use the idea of "criteria": what made the war last (leaders, geography, resources, politics)?
A: **Plan.** *For:* McClellan's caution, Burnside's defeat at Fredericksburg and Hooker's at Chancellorsville left Lee free in Virginia until Grant became general-in-chief (March 1864). *Against:* the Confederacy needed only to survive, and its defensive strategy and geography made conquest slow; Lee and Jackson won victories (though Lee's attacks cost men the South could not replace); Davis created a working government; the Border States and foreign powers (none recognised the Confederacy) mattered; the Union's advantages (about 22 million people to 9 million, nine-tenths of manufacturing) took time to turn into victory. **Judgement:** the Union's resources made victory likely, but defence, geography and early Union leadership failures set the pace: both explain the four years.

### Paper 2 practice: Gilded Age and Progressive Era

:::try Paper 2 practice: Gilded Age and Progressive Era, 1870–1920
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the People's (Populist) Party emerged in the early 1890s.
A: **Indicative content.** Farmers in the South and West faced falling crop prices and rising debts, high railroad freight charges and costs of storage, and a money supply tied to gold that made debts harder to repay. Earlier organisations had not solved the problem: the Grange (1867) had won the Granger laws, but *Munn v. Illinois* (1877) was followed by court rulings that weakened them; the Farmers' Alliances developed plans such as the sub-treasury scheme, but neither main party adopted them. The two parties seemed to side with railroads, bankers and manufacturers. The Panic of 1893 later made things worse. In July 1892 farmers' leaders and labour groups founded the People's Party at Omaha, and its candidate James Weaver won 22 electoral votes. **Level 4** (9–10) links the economic grievances, the failure of the older organisations and of the main parties, and the turn to a new party.

Q: [20 marks] ★★ How successful was Theodore Roosevelt in controlling the power of big business between 1901 and 1909?
A: **Indicative content.** *Success:* he mediated the 1902 coal strike instead of siding automatically with the owners; the Supreme Court's ruling in the Northern Securities case (1904) broke up a great railroad holding company; the Hepburn Act (1906) strengthened the Interstate Commerce Commission's control of rates; the Meat Inspection and Pure Food and Drug Acts (1906) put federal inspection on business; the Bureau of Corporations gathered information. *Limits:* he distinguished "good" and "bad" trusts and prosecuted fewer than Taft (about 90 suits) did later; the 1906 laws were compromises that business shaped; in the Panic of 1907 he allowed U.S. Steel to take over the Tennessee Coal and Iron Company, which weakened competition; courts weakened much regulation. **Level 5** (17–20) sets criteria (changing the law, changing business behaviour, protecting consumers and workers) and concludes, for example, that he changed the principle of government regulation more than the reality of corporate power.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Immigration was the main reason for rapid industrialisation in the USA after 1870.' How far do you agree? (Plan this one.)
H: Name at least four factors and decide a criterion (labour, markets, capital, technology).
A: **Plan.** *For:* about 26 million immigrants came between 1871 and 1920, supplying cheap labour for steel, mining and railroads and a growing home market. *Against:* natural resources (Mesabi iron ore, Pittsburgh coal, oil); railroads, which created a national market and demand for steel and coal; capital and the modern corporation; technology (Bessemer and open-hearth steel); government support through tariffs, land grants and low regulation; attitudes that celebrated wealth. **Judgement:** immigration was a necessary supply of labour, but railroads and the national market drove the growth, with resources and capital as the base.

### Paper 2 practice: Crash, Depression and New Deal

:::try Paper 2 practice: Great Crash, Great Depression and New Deal, 1920–41
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Franklin D. Roosevelt won the presidential election of 1932.
A: **Indicative content.** Unemployment had risen from about 3 per cent in 1929 to over 20 per cent in 1932 (estimates range from 21 to 25 per cent), and many held Hoover responsible. His response was seen as too little and too late: voluntarism, the Smoot–Hawley tariff (1930), and a Reconstruction Finance Corporation (January 1932) that lent to banks and railroads, not to people; the Hoovervilles; the Bonus Army, cleared by troops under General MacArthur on 28 July 1932, ruined his image. Roosevelt, the governor of New York, offered hope and a "New Deal" and campaigned energetically, and the Democratic coalition of Southern whites, urban workers and ethnic groups, and farmers voted for change. He won 57.4 per cent of the popular vote and 472 electoral votes to Hoover's 59. **Level 4** (9–10) links the Depression, Hoover's record and the appeal of Roosevelt's campaign, and says which mattered most (the Depression: any Democrat would have won).

Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent did the New Deal fail to help the groups hit hardest by the Depression?
A: **Indicative content.** *Help:* relief for the unemployed (the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Civil Works Administration and, from 1935, the Works Progress Administration with about 8.5 million employed); the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation protected small savers; the Wagner Act (1935) backed unions; Social Security (14 August 1935) began pensions and unemployment insurance; farm owners gained through the Agricultural Adjustment Act, with income up about half between 1932 and 1935. *Failure:* sharecroppers and tenant farmers often lost out when landlords cut acreage and kept the payments; Social Security excluded agricultural and domestic workers, which meant most African American and many women workers; African Americans and Mexican Americans met discrimination in relief and in the CCC and NRA; unemployment was still about 14.6 per cent in 1940. **Level 5** (17–20) weighs "hardest hit" by group: the unemployed and the elderly were helped, but the poorest and least powerful, particularly in the South and in farm labour, were helped least. The best answers note that Roosevelt needed Southern Democrats and so avoided challenging segregation.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The Supreme Court was the main obstacle to the success of the New Deal.' How far do you agree? (Plan this one.)
H: Think of the Court (Schechter, Butler), but also of Congress, business and Roosevelt's own choices (the 1937 cuts).
A: **Plan.** *For:* *Schechter* (27 May 1935, 9–0) ended the NRA and *Butler* (January 1936, 6–3) struck down the AAA tax; the Court plan of 5 February 1937 shows how serious Roosevelt thought the problem was. *Against:* the Court changed course in 1937 (*West Coast Hotel v. Parrish*, 29 March; *Jones & Laughlin*, 12 April, upholding the Wagner Act) and the Social Security Act was upheld; the court plan itself failed and cost Roosevelt political support; Southern Democrats and the conservative coalition, business hostility (the Liberty League) and Roosevelt's own decision to cut spending in 1937, which led to the recession of 1937–38, mattered as much. **Judgement:** the Court blocked specific laws, but the limits of success came from the size of the problem and from political and policy choices.

:::try Paper 1 question: the League of Nations
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B. Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to collective security in the League of Nations.
A: **Indicative content.** *Similarities:* both appeal to the principle of collective action against an aggressor: Hoare speaks of "collective resistance to all acts of unprovoked aggression" and Haile Selassie reminds the League of "the assistance promised" by "fifty nations". Both are addressed to the League's Assembly, in Geneva, by a spokesman of a member state. Both treat the Covenant as a promise made by all members to each other. *Differences:* Hoare looks forward and speaks in general terms (he does not name Italy), offering a pledge; Haile Selassie looks back, speaks about a specific case and says the pledge is not being kept ("what are they willing to do?"). A is a statement of policy by a strong power, in a calm diplomatic style; B is a plea by the victim of aggression, in a personal and emotional style.

*Why they differ (Level 4).* Hoare spoke on 11 September 1935, three weeks before Italy invaded Abyssinia (3 October) and in a year when British public opinion, shown by the Peace Ballot, strongly favoured the League; his words were taken as a firm promise by many, but he gave no details of what Britain would do, and Britain and France went on to negotiate with Mussolini (the Hoare–Laval plan, December 1935), while the League's sanctions left out oil, coal and steel. Haile Selassie spoke on 30 June 1936, after the Italians had entered Addis Ababa (5 May 1936) and while the powers were preparing to end sanctions (lifted 15 July). Both speeches had a purpose: Hoare to reassure the British public and the League and perhaps to warn Mussolini; the Emperor to shame the members and to make the case for help. As evidence, A shows what Britain was willing to say, not what it was willing to do, and B shows the victim's view of that gap. **Level 2** (4–7): "both mention the League; one is about Britain, the other Ethiopia". **Level 3** (8–11): supported similarities and differences. **Level 4** (12–15): explains the contrast through the events between the two dates and the purpose of each speaker.

Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources. 'The League of Nations failed to keep the peace in the 1930s because its members were unwilling to act.' How far do these sources support this view?
H: Which sources show members not acting, and which suggest other causes of failure?
H: Source C is by the Soviet foreign minister in 1938. What was the Soviet Union's own position, and does that change how much weight you give it?
H: Own knowledge: Manchuria, Abyssinia and the details of what sanctions were and were not applied.
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* Source B accuses the members directly: "what are they willing to do?" and notes the "promise" they made. Source C says "the League has done nothing" and lists Ethiopia and Austria as victims. Source D shows the League's own commission condemning Japan's actions as not "legitimate self-defence" and Manchukuo as not "genuine", yet the League took no action against Japan (the Assembly accepted the report on 24 February 1933, 42 to 1; Japan then gave notice of leaving on 27 March 1933; no sanctions were applied). Source A, a promise of "steady and collective resistance", contrasts with what followed in Abyssinia: sanctions began on 18 November 1935 but did not cover oil, coal or steel, and the Suez Canal stayed open. *Challenge:* Source C also says that the League "was not any more universal" in its first twelve years than in 1938, so its weakness was built into its membership: the USA never joined, Germany and Japan left in 1933–35, and the USSR joined only in 1934. The League could make its members promises but had no army, and decisions needed unanimity. Source D also shows that the League did act in one sense, by sending a commission and reaching a clear finding; the failure was in the next step. Own knowledge: Britain and France feared Germany and were reluctant to alienate Italy (the Stresa Front, April 1935); the Depression had cut their will to spend on defence.

*Evaluation (Level 5).* **A** is a speech to the League: a statement of intent, not an action, and delivered before the test; its worth is in the contrast with what followed. **B** is reliable on the promise and the fact of aggression, but it is a plea from the victim with an obvious purpose, and it blames the members without explaining their reasons. **C** is the weakest on motive: Litvinov was pushing collective security because Soviet security needed it, the USSR was itself distrusted by Britain and France (and a week later was excluded from Munich), and his list leaves out Soviet actions; yet its point about universality is accurate. **D** is a strong source, the work of an independent commission with a British chairman, though it is a report, not a decision; its significance lies in what happened next, which the source cannot show.

*Judgement.* A Level 5 answer decides: the sources strongly support the view that members would not act (B, C, D), but they also point to why they would not: the League was built without the strength to enforce its decisions and was missing its strongest potential members. So the view is right on the immediate cause and incomplete on the underlying causes; "unwillingness" was a result of the League's structure and of the Depression as much as a free choice. A Level 3 answer would list B, C and D as "agreeing" and A as "disagreeing" with no evaluation.

### Paper 2 practice: Imperialism and world powers

:::try Paper 2 practice: Imperialism and the emergence of world powers, c.1870–1918
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the European powers were able to take control of most of Africa between 1870 and 1914.
A: **Indicative content.** Technology: quinine let Europeans survive malaria; steamers and railways took them inland; the telegraph linked colonies to capitals; repeating rifles and then the Maxim gun gave a decisive advantage, as at Omdurman (2 September 1898). Diplomacy and local power: powers made treaties with African rulers, used chartered companies and used African soldiers and allies; African states were often divided or rivals of one another, which allowed divide-and-rule. Motives and rivalries: economic hopes (raw materials, markets), the "civilising mission", national prestige and strategic fears (Suez and the occupation of Egypt, 1882) made each power move before another did, so the process speeded up. Limits: Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa (1 March 1896) and Liberia was independent. **Level 4** (9–10) links the factors: motives explain why they wanted to, technology and African divisions explain why they could.

Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far did the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 cause the "Scramble for Africa"?
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* the conference, called by Bismarck and the French prime minister Ferry, set rules: free trade in the Congo basin, free navigation of the Congo and Niger, a ban on the slave trade, and "effective occupation" of coasts (Articles 34–35), which meant that a power had to occupy land to claim it, so that claims were rushed; it recognised Leopold's Association (the Congo Free State). *Challenge:* the conference did not divide Africa, and no African ruler attended; the Scramble had begun earlier (Britain's occupation of Egypt in 1882, the Anglo-Portuguese treaty that led to the conference, Stanley's work for Leopold from 1879), and it was driven by economic hopes, national rivalry and strategic fears; most of the partition came after 1885 through treaties made between powers, for example the Anglo-German agreements of 1890. **Level 5** (17–20) sets criteria: did it start the Scramble, speed it up or set the rules? It concludes, for example, that it did not cause the Scramble but regulated and probably accelerated it.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Victory over Russia in 1905 was the main reason why Japan became a world power.' How far do you agree? (Plan this one.)
H: Think about what "world power" means: strength, recognition or alliances?
A: **Plan.** *For:* the victories at Port Arthur (surrendered 2 January 1905), Mukden and Tsushima (27–28 May 1905), the Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905) and the protectorate over Korea (annexed in 1910) showed that a non-European state could defeat a European great power. *Against:* modernisation after 1868 made the victory possible (conscription 1873, the constitution of 1889, industry and the zaibatsu, schools); victory over China in 1894–95 (Shimonoseki) came first; the Anglo-Japanese Alliance (30 January 1902) gave security; and the First World War (Tsingtao, the Twenty-One Demands of 1915, the Pacific islands, a place at the Paris conference as one of the Big Five) confirmed it. **Judgement:** modernisation was necessary, 1905 was the moment of recognition, and the alliance and the war gave the opportunity.

### Paper 2 practice: International relations, 1919–29

:::try Paper 2 practice: International relations, 1919–29
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why France and Belgium occupied the Ruhr in January 1923.
A: **Indicative content.** The London Schedule of 5 May 1921 set Germany's reparations at 132 billion gold marks, a sum Germany said it could not pay. At the end of 1922 Germany fell behind on deliveries of timber and coal, and the Reparation Commission declared it in default (Britain disagreed). France, led by Raymond Poincaré, needed reparations to rebuild its devastated north and to repay its own war debts, and it wanted to weaken Germany; the USA had refused the guarantee treaty of 1919, so France had to act for itself. French and Belgian troops moved in from 11 January 1923 to take coal and goods. **Level 4** (9–10) connects the default, the French need for money and security, and the absence of any other guarantee, and may add the result: passive resistance, hyperinflation (4.2 trillion marks to the dollar in November 1923) and the change of policy that led to the Dawes Plan.

Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far was Gustav Stresemann responsible for the improvement in international relations in the later 1920s?
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* Stresemann ended passive resistance (26 September 1923) and introduced the Rentenmark; as Foreign Minister he pursued "fulfilment": the Dawes Plan (1924), Locarno (October–December 1925), entry to the League (8 September 1926) and the Young Plan (1929); he met Briand at Thoiry. *Challenge:* the Dawes Plan was drawn up by an American-led committee, and American loans made it work; Briand and Austen Chamberlain, with Mussolini's backing for Locarno, had their own reasons to settle; Kellogg–Briand (1928) was a joint French and American initiative. Stresemann's aims included revising Germany's eastern frontiers (his letter of 7 September 1925), so the improvement was real in the west and an illusion in the east; and it depended on American money, which ended with the Crash (October 1929; Stresemann died on 3 October). **Level 5** (17–20) judges that he was the key German actor but one of several: without the Dawes loans and the French change of policy, no settlement would have held, and the improvement was less secure than it looked.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was the League of Nations a success in the 1920s? (Plan this one.)
H: Use the idea of "success in what?": disputes between small states, humanitarian work, disarmament, great-power disputes.
A: **Plan.** *Success:* Aaland Islands 1921 and Upper Silesia 1921 settled; Greco-Bulgarian dispute 1925 (the League ordered a ceasefire and fined Greece); Nansen's work for refugees, the ILO, the Slavery Convention of 1926. *Failure:* Vilna 1920–23; Corfu 1923, when Mussolini defied the League and the Conference of Ambassadors decided; no disarmament; the USA never joined and Germany was not a member until 1926; no army and sanctions only. **Judgement:** effective in small disputes and humanitarian work, ineffective against strong states, and its record in the 1920s hid its structural weakness.

### Paper 2 practice: International history, 1929–39

:::try Paper 2 practice: International history, 1929–39
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Germany and the Soviet Union signed the Nazi–Soviet Pact in August 1939.
A: **Indicative content.** Hitler planned to attack Poland (Case White was ordered in April, the invasion was set for the end of August) and wanted to avoid fighting Britain and France and the Soviet Union together, as Germany had in 1914. The Pact (23 August 1939) isolated Poland. Stalin distrusted Britain and France, who had excluded the USSR from Munich and whose talks in Moscow in the summer of 1939 were slow; Poland refused to let Soviet troops enter its territory; the USSR needed time to rebuild its army after the purges. A secret protocol divided eastern Europe into spheres along the Narew, Vistula and San rivers, so Stalin gained territory. **Level 4** (9–10) links Hitler's timetable for Poland to Stalin's distrust of the West and his wish for security and land.

Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Hitler's aims were the main reason why war broke out in 1939.' How far do you agree with this view?
A: **Indicative content.** *Support:* Hitler's long-term aims (overturning Versailles, uniting Germans, living space in the east) are in *Mein Kampf* (1925–26) and in the Hossbach memorandum of 5 November 1937; he took each step (the Rhineland in March 1936, the Anschluss, the Sudetenland in October 1938, Prague in March 1939) and decided to attack Poland (Case White, April 1939); he saw the Pact as removing the last obstacle. *Challenge:* the policies of Britain and France (appeasement, the guarantee to Poland of 31 March 1939) made war between the powers more likely, as did the League's failure; Mussolini's ambitions and the Anti-Comintern Pact; Stalin's decision to sign the Pact; and the debate about Hitler's intentions (A. J. P. Taylor, 1961: an opportunist; Trevor-Roper, 1961: a plan). **Level 5** (17–20) judges, for example, that Hitler's aims and decisions were the main cause, while appeasement, the Pact and the decision of Britain and France to fight explain the timing and why it became a world war.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Appeasement was a mistake.' How far do you agree? (Plan this one.)
H: Judge by criteria: did it keep the peace? Did it prepare for war? Did it fit public opinion at the time?
A: **Plan.** *Mistake:* Germany gained Austria, the Sudetenland and Czech arms; the dictators' confidence grew; the USSR was alienated (Munich); opposition (Churchill, Eden, Duff Cooper) said so. *Not a mistake:* war was postponed and Britain used the time (Hurricanes, Spitfires, radar, civil defence); public opinion and the Dominions wanted peace; the economy and the military could not support a war in 1935–37; and it made the war a united one. **Judgement:** reasonable in 1935–37 but a mistake in 1938, and it failed in its aim of keeping the peace.
:::

## Mixed recall round: all nine topics

This round mixes the topics. Do not look at the chapters. Give yourself one point for each answer that has the date or name and the reason. A score of 9 or more out of 12 means the facts are secure; below 7, go back to the chapters of the questions you missed (each links to it).

:::try Twelve questions across the options
Q: (#hist.eu.france.2) What did the Brunswick Manifesto of 25 July 1792 threaten, and what happened 16 days later?
A: It threatened Paris with destruction if the royal family was harmed, which made the revolutionaries more angry, not less. On 10 August 1792 the Tuileries was stormed and the king was suspended.
Q: (#hist.eu.germany.4) Why did Bismarck edit the Ems telegram, and what was the result?
A: He shortened it on 13 July 1870 so that it appeared that the King had snubbed the French ambassador and the French had been insulted; France declared war on 19 July, which brought the southern states into war on Prussia's side.
Q: (#hist.eu.russia.4) What did the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (3 March 1918) cost Russia?
A: Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland, Ukraine and Caucasus districts: about a third of its population and most of its coal and iron.
Q: (#hist.us.civilwar.3) Why did Lincoln issue the Emancipation Proclamation, and whom did it not free?
A: As a war measure: the preliminary proclamation was on 22 September 1862 and it took effect on 1 January 1863, to weaken the Confederacy, allow Black soldiers to join the army and win foreign opinion; it did not free enslaved people in the Border States.
Q: (#hist.us.gilded.2) Why did the Pullman strike of 1894 end in defeat for the workers?
A: The federal government sent troops and obtained a court injunction against the strike (*In re Debs*), and Debs was jailed; the American Railway Union was broken.
Q: (#hist.us.depression.4) What happened to the economy in 1937–38 and why?
A: A recession: unemployment rose from 14.3 to 19.0 per cent. Roosevelt had cut spending, payroll taxes began, and the Fed and Treasury tightened money; new spending and easier money ended it.
Q: (#hist.int.empire.2) Why was Fashoda (1898) settled peacefully?
A: Britain was stronger in the area (Kitchener's force had defeated the Mahdists at Omdurman) and France, which saw Germany as the main threat, withdrew; the convention of 21 March 1899 settled the frontiers.
Q: (#hist.int.1919.4) What did the Locarno treaties (1925) guarantee, and what did they leave out?
A: They guaranteed the frontiers between Germany and France and Belgium, with Britain and Italy as guarantors; they did not guarantee Germany's eastern frontiers, only arbitration treaties with Poland and Czechoslovakia.
Q: (#hist.int.1929.3) What was the Munich Agreement (29–30 September 1938) and who was not at the table?
A: Germany, Britain, France and Italy agreed that Germany would occupy the Sudetenland in 1–10 October; Czechoslovakia and the USSR were not at the table.
Q: (#hist.eu.france.4) What was the Concordat of 1801?
A: An agreement (15 July 1801) between Napoleon and the Pope: Catholicism was recognised as the religion of the majority of the French, the Church kept no lands, and the state paid the clergy; the Organic Articles added state controls.
Q: (#hist.us.civilwar.4) Why did the Fifteenth Amendment not secure Black voting rights in the long run?
A: Ratified on 3 February 1870, it banned denial of the vote on grounds of race, but white Southerners used violence, and later poll taxes and literacy tests, and the Supreme Court weakened enforcement (*Cruikshank*); disfranchisement became complete in the 1890s.
Q: (#hist.int.1929.2) Name two reasons why the League could not stop Japan in Manchuria.
A: It had no army and relied on its members; Britain and France were absorbed in the Depression and unwilling to risk war; the USA was not a member; and the Lytton Report was not published until 2 October 1932, over a year after the Mukden incident. Japan left the League in 1933.
:::

## Repair a weak paragraph

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
**Question.** 'The main reason why Reconstruction failed was the resistance of white Southerners.' How far do you agree with this view? [20]

**A student's paragraph.** "White Southerners resisted Reconstruction in many ways. The Ku Klux Klan was founded in 1866 and attacked Black people and Republicans. There were also groups like the White League and the Red Shirts. In 1873 there was a massacre at Colfax in which many African Americans were killed. In Mississippi in 1875 white Democrats used violence to win the election. So white resistance was very important in the failure of Reconstruction and I agree with the view."

**The level.** About Level 2 (5–8, perhaps 7). The facts are accurate and relevant, but they describe resistance without explaining why it worked. There is no other perspective (the Northern part in the failure), no weighing, and the "so" in the last sentence repeats the question instead of reaching a conclusion from the evidence.

**The fix.** "White resistance was the means by which Reconstruction was defeated, but it succeeded because the North was no longer willing to stop it. The Klan (from 1866), the White League and the Red Shirts used violence and intimidation to stop Black men voting, and at Colfax in April 1873 white paramilitaries killed many Black men, with the Supreme Court later (in *Cruikshank*, 1876) making it harder to prosecute such crimes in federal courts. In Mississippi in 1875 the Democrats' campaign of violence won the election and Grant, facing a Northern public tired of intervention, refused to send troops. The Panic of 1873 moved Northern voters' attention to the economy, and by 1877, after the disputed election, the last troops left. The weight of these Northern causes means that violence alone does not explain the outcome: it explains how the Redeemers took the Southern states, and the withdrawal of Northern will explains why they were allowed to keep them."

**Why the fix reaches Level 4 or 5:** it keeps the evidence, but explains why it mattered, introduces an alternative cause (Northern will) and starts a comparison between the two. To reach the top of Level 5, a full essay would then rank them across its paragraphs and say which was the stronger.
:::

## Plan your practice

:::steps
1. **Week 1: one option, closed-book.** Do your option's Paper 1 set in 75 minutes. Mark it with the level tables. Write the error log.
2. **Week 2: Paper 2 pairs.** Do each topic's (a) and (b) as a pair, in 46 minutes. Do the "plan this one" questions as 5-minute plans only: a good plan scores as much practice as an essay when you are short of time.
3. **Week 3: mix.** Do the recall round and the quiz without notes. Then redo one answer you marked at Level 3 and lift it by one level.
4. **Week 4: the real thing.** Take [Mock Paper 1](#hist.mock.p1) and [Mock Paper 2](#hist.mock.p2) under exam conditions, then use the real past papers on the Cambridge site (a link is below).
:::

:::tip Marking yourself honestly
Students almost always mark themselves one level too high on Paper 1 (a), because they believe they have "explained why" when they have only said "it is biased". Ask: did I say what was happening when the source was written, and how that explains the words? If not, stay at Level 3.
:::

## Quiz: mixed facts across the options

:::quiz Check the facts
? When did Nicholas II abdicate (Old Style)?
- 23 February 1917
+ 2 March 1917
- 25 October 1917
- 17 October 1905
> He abdicated at Pskov on 2 March 1917 (Old Style); the strike that began the revolution was on 23 February; 25 October is the Bolshevik seizure of power; 17 October 1905 is the October Manifesto.

? Which law repealed the Missouri Compromise line of 36°30′?
- the Compromise of 1850
- the Dred Scott decision
+ the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854
- the Fugitive Slave Act
> The Kansas–Nebraska Act (30 May 1854) repealed it. The Compromise of 1850 created the Fugitive Slave Act; Dred Scott (1857) said Congress could not ban slavery in the territories.

? Which state or states were outside the Zollverein?
- Prussia
- Bavaria
+ Austria
- Saxony
> Prussia led the Zollverein (1 January 1834) and Austria was outside it; the south German states joined.

? What did the Berlin Conference of 1884–85 do?
- divided Africa among the powers
+ set rules such as free navigation of the Congo and effective occupation of coasts
- ended the Scramble for Africa
- recognised Ethiopia as a European colony
> It did not divide Africa. It laid down rules (free trade in the Congo basin, free navigation of the Congo and Niger, effective occupation) and recognised Leopold's Association.

? Which plan of 1924 reduced German reparations payments and brought American loans to Germany?
- the Young Plan
+ the Dawes Plan
- the London Schedule
- the Hoare–Laval plan
> The Dawes Plan (1924) set payments of 1 billion marks a year rising to 2.5 billion and arranged a loan; the Young Plan (1929) was a later reduction; the London Schedule (1921) set the 132 billion gold marks.

? What happened at Fort Sumter on 12–13 April 1861?
- Lincoln was elected President
+ Confederate forces bombarded the fort, which surrendered
- South Carolina seceded from the Union
- the Emancipation Proclamation was issued
> The bombardment of Fort Sumter began the war; South Carolina had seceded on 20 December 1860, Lincoln was elected on 6 November 1860, and the Emancipation Proclamation took effect on 1 January 1863.

? Which statement about the Directory is correct?
- it was the government of France from 1792 to 1795
+ it governed from November 1795 to Napoleon's coup of 9–10 November 1799
- it was set up by Robespierre
- it ended after Waterloo
> The Directory ran from the Constitution of the Year III to the coup of Brumaire; the Convention governed in 1792–95, the Committee of Public Safety was Robespierre's, and Waterloo was in 1815.

? How many electoral votes did Roosevelt win in 1932?
- 59
+ 472
- 523
- 303
> Roosevelt won 472 to Hoover's 59 in 1932; 523 was his total in 1936; 303 was the total in 1860.

? Why was the Munich Agreement of 1938 criticised?
- it gave Poland to Germany
+ it gave the Sudetenland to Germany without Czechoslovakia or the USSR present
- it was signed by Britain without France
- it was signed after the invasion of Poland
> Munich was signed by Germany, Britain, France and Italy on 29–30 September 1938; Czechoslovakia was not present. Poland was invaded on 1 September 1939.

? Which was a result of the 1905 Revolution in Russia?
- the end of the Tsar's power
+ the October Manifesto and an elected Duma, with the Tsar keeping wide powers
- the abdication of Nicholas II
- the creation of Sovnarkom
> The October Manifesto (17 October 1905) promised a Duma; the Fundamental Laws of 1906 kept the Tsar's wide powers. Abdication and Sovnarkom were 1917.

? Which was a key way in which the Progressive Movement changed politics?
- it abolished the income tax
+ it brought direct primaries, initiative, referendum and the direct election of senators (17th Amendment)
- it ended segregation
- it created the Federal Reserve in 1893
> The 17th Amendment was ratified on 8 April 1913; the 16th Amendment (1913) introduced the income tax and the Federal Reserve was created in December 1913; segregation continued.

:::recap
- Mixed practice is closed-book: unseen question, no notes, real time limits, marked against the real levels.
- Paper 1 (a): similarities and differences, then **why** (context and provenance). Paper 1 (b): sort the sources, evaluate each one, then judge "how far" with your own knowledge.
- Paper 2 (a): connect the factors. Paper 2 (b): weigh alternative perspectives and give a comparative judgement; set criteria.
- Marks are not deducted; the best fit of the level descriptors decides, and a different valid argument earns the same marks.
- Keep an error log: level reached, what would raise it, which fact was missing.
- The facts are the foundation: use the recall round and the quiz to find gaps.
