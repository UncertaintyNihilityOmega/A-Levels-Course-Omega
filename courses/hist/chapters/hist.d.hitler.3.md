In January 1933 about six million Germans were registered as unemployed. By 1939 the registered figure was close to zero, and many Germans never forgot it. That fact explains much of the regime's popularity, yet it hides a harder question: was this a success in its own right, or the by-product of a drive to war that left Germany short of raw materials, short of foreign currency and living with butter rationing by 1939? ## What the Nazis inherited

### Germany in January 1933

Germany was hit harder by the Great Depression than almost any other country in Europe. The American banks that had lent to Germany in the later 1920s called back their loans after the Wall Street Crash of October 1929. German industry sold less, and unemployment rose from about 1.9 million (the annual average for 1929, from the Reich statistical yearbook) to about 5.6 million in 1932, and passed 6 million in the winter of 1932–33. Behind the numbers lay lost savings, short-time working, families living on the dole (benefits were cut in 1931–32 by the governments of Brüning and Papen), and the memory of the hyperinflation of 1923 that made people fear both unemployment and any government that printed money.

Two points matter for the judgement of Nazi success. First, **the worst was already over by the time Hitler took office.** Industrial production had started to rise in the second half of 1932, and the governments of von Papen and von Schleicher had begun to plan public works and credit for businesses. A recovery was under way, and the question for an examiner is how much of what followed was the regime's doing and how much was the normal upswing of a world economy. Second, **Hitler had no economic programme of his own.** The Nazi Party's slogans promised "work and bread", but its leaders disagreed about how, and Hitler cared about the economy mainly as the means of making Germany strong enough to fight. He said as much in a memorandum of 1936 (see below). In 1933 he was willing to leave the day-to-day management to experts such as Schacht, as long as they delivered jobs and rearmament.

## Responses to the Great Depression

### Schacht, deficit financing and the Mefo bills

When Schacht returned to the Reichsbank on 17 March 1933 he did what the British and the Americans were also trying: he put the state's credit behind a recovery. The method was **deficit financing**, in which the government spends more than it receives in taxes and borrows the difference, to give work to the unemployed and to raise demand. The economist John Maynard Keynes was arguing for this approach in Britain; the Nazis used it without his theory and for a different end, since the money went increasingly to arms.

:::def Deficit financing
Government spending that is greater than government income in the same year, paid for by borrowing (or creating credit) rather than by taxation. Used in Germany from 1933 to start work-creation schemes and, from 1934, to pay for rearmament without showing the cost in the budget.
:::

The device Schacht invented for the secret part of the borrowing was the **Mefo bill**. In 1933 the Reichsbank and the big armaments firms (Krupp, Siemens, Rheinmetall and Gutehoffnungshütte) set up a shell company called the Metallurgische Forschungsgesellschaft ("Metallurgical Research Company", Mefo). An arms contractor delivering to the army drew a bill of exchange on Mefo (a written promise to pay later), which accepted it. The Reichsbank would discount the bill (pay cash now for slightly less than its face value), so the contractor was paid at once in cash, and the Reich guaranteed repayment after five years. No cash passed through the budget, and the bills did not appear in the published figures.

:::def Mefo bills
Bills of exchange issued from 1934 by a front company, the Metallurgische Forschungsgesellschaft, to pay armaments firms. They were discounted by the Reichsbank and guaranteed by the Reich after five years. About 12 billion marks were issued between 1934 and March 1938, and the arrangement hid the scale of rearmament from foreign governments and from the German public.
:::

The point of the scheme was twofold. It gave Germany cheap rearmament credit without a public loan, which would have frightened bankers and foreign governments, and it avoided the inflation that open money-printing would have caused. The cost was a debt that would fall due: the bills matured from 1939, and in January 1939 Schacht and the Reichsbank directors warned Hitler that unrestrained spending would bring inflation. Hitler dismissed Schacht on 20 January 1939. The last Mefo bills were issued in March 1938, because the regime no longer needed to hide the extent of rearmament, and it borrowed through Reich treasury bills instead.

### Public works

The first work-creation programmes were launched in the summer of 1933. The **Law for the Reduction of Unemployment** of 1 June 1933 provided money for repairs to buildings, roads, canals, drainage and house-building, along with tax concessions for businesses that took on workers. A "first Reinhardt programme", named after Fritz Reinhardt, a state secretary in the Finance Ministry, put about a billion marks into such projects in 1933, with a further programme later that year. These were useful and visible, but they built on schemes of the Papen and Schleicher governments, and their total was modest next to what the state later spent on arms.

The showpiece was the **autobahn network**. Fritz Todt, a Nazi engineer, was appointed Inspector-General for German Roads on 30 June 1933, and the company that built the roads was founded on 25 August 1933. On 23 September 1933 Hitler cut the first sod of the first section, near Frankfurt. The plan was for about 7,000 km of motorway (later raised after the annexations of 1938), of which about 3,000 km were finished by the end of 1938. At the peak, in 1936, about 125,000 men worked directly on the construction.

Why did the regime build them? The official reason was work for the unemployed, and Todt forecast hundreds of thousands of jobs. But the roads served propaganda (the Führer's achievement, with workers marching to the site with spades), the promotion of the motor car (the Volkswagen scheme below) and national prestige. Historians now doubt the older claim that they were built mainly for the army, since most troops and supplies still moved by rail, and a motorway that employed 125,000 men could not make a big hole in an unemployment problem of millions. The autobahn is the best example of a policy whose *image* exceeded its economic effect.

### The Reich Labour Service

The **Reich Labour Service** (*Reichsarbeitsdienst*, RAD) grew out of the voluntary service of the Weimar years. Under Konstantin Hierl, it took young men out of the unemployment figures and put them on projects such as land drainage, forestry and road building. A law of **26 June 1935** made six months' service compulsory for young men aged eighteen to twenty-five, before their military service, and women were included in 1939. Members lived in camps, wore uniforms, drilled with spades and received only pocket money. The RAD had a double purpose, for it removed hundreds of thousands of young people from the labour market and taught them discipline and "the dignity of labour". It was never numerous (about 200,000 members in 1935, about 350,000 by October 1939), but it is a clear case where a policy lowered the statistics without creating ordinary jobs.

### The Law for the Reduction of Unemployment and marriage loans

The law of 1 June 1933 also introduced **marriage loans**: a newly married couple could borrow up to 1,000 marks, interest free, in the form of vouchers for furniture and household goods. The loan had a condition: the wife had to give up paid work, and the husband's job passed to the unemployed man who replaced her, which removed a woman from the labour market and a family from the dole. For each child, a quarter of the loan was cancelled, so that a couple with four children owed nothing, and the Germans nicknamed this "to child away" (*abkindern*). The scheme was also a population policy (see [Hitler's Germany (4)](#hist.d.hitler.4)). More than a million loans were granted by the end of 1938. When labour grew scarce, from about 1937, the condition that the wife must not work was dropped.

### Did unemployment really fall? Counting and not counting

:::

The registered figure fell from an annual average of about 5.6 million in 1932 to about 4.8 million in 1933, 2.7 million in 1934, 1.6 million in 1936 and 0.9 million in 1937, and in 1939 it was about 0.1 million. This is the strongest evidence for success, and it is true. Real people got jobs. But the figures need care, and an examiner expects you to say why.

- **Who was not counted.** Women who left work on marriage and Jews dismissed from their jobs or driven out of business (see [Hitler's Germany (4)](#hist.d.hitler.4)) did not appear as unemployed. Men in the Reich Labour Service were not counted either, nor were people in prison or in the camps.
- **Conscription.** From 16 March 1935 men were called up, so the army absorbed hundreds of thousands of young men who would otherwise have been unemployed.
- **The world economy.** Germany was not the only country to recover. The upturn had started in 1932, and other countries (even Britain, without a rearmament drive until later) saw unemployment fall in the mid-1930s.
- **The cause.** Most historians put the biggest part of the fall down to rearmament (see below), not to the autobahn or the Labour Service. Public works mattered in 1933–34, but rearmament mattered from 1935.

:::key Responses to the Depression: how successful?
- **Success.** Registered unemployment fell from about 5.6 million (1932 average) to under 1 million by 1937. Schacht's credit techniques restored confidence and avoided inflation (before 1939). The visible projects (the autobahn) gave jobs and enormous propaganda value.
- **Qualifications.** Recovery was already under way in 1932. The figures excluded women, Jews and men in the Labour Service, and conscription removed others. The jobs were increasingly rearmament jobs, financed by hidden debt (Mefo bills, about 12 billion marks). The unemployment problem was solved by Germany's preparing for war.
- **Judgement.** The Nazis ended mass unemployment faster than most other countries, and the credit is real. But the policy that did it was rearmament, and the success was bought with a debt and a dependence on war that the regime could not escape.
:::

## Agricultural policies

### The problem

Agriculture mattered to the Nazis for two reasons. Politically, the farmers of north and east Germany had been the Nazis' most loyal voters in 1932, after years of falling prices and debt. Ideologically, the party idealised the peasant as the "blood source" of the nation (the term of its agriculture expert Darré), the opposite of the "rootless" city. And economically, a country that wanted to fight a war without a repeat of the British blockade of 1914–18 needed to feed itself.

### Tariffs and debt relief

The Weimar governments had already protected German farmers by tariffs on grain, meat and dairy products, and the Nazis kept and extended them. **Tariffs** (taxes on imports) raised the price of imported food, so that German farmers could sell at higher prices, and they cut the food bill in foreign currency, which Germany lacked after 1934. The government also cut the farmers' debts: a law of 1 June 1933 reduced and delayed repayments and lowered interest rates on farm debts, and the debt burden, which had been a major cause of anger, eased as prices rose.

### The Reich Food Estate

:::def Reich Food Estate (*Reichsnährstand*)
The compulsory organisation of everyone in food production, processing and marketing, set up by a law of 13 September 1933 and led by Darré. It fixed prices, set quotas and controlled the marketing of farm produce, as part of the Nazi aim of food self-sufficiency and of looking after the "peasantry".
:::

The Reich Food Estate was a giant cartel (an organisation of producers that fixes prices). Every farmer, every dairy and every grain merchant belonged. It fixed prices, so that farmers received a guaranteed income and consumers paid higher prices than the world market would have asked for. It ran the "Battle for Production" of 1934, a campaign to increase output of grain, fats and fodder, and regulated what was grown. Farmers gained a secure market and in the first years higher incomes. The cost was control: they were told what to produce and what to charge, and they had to deliver to fixed marketing bodies.

### The Reich Entailed Farm Law

:::def Reich Entailed Farm Law (*Reichserbhofgesetz*)
A law of 29 September 1933 that made farms of between about 7.5 and 125 hectares into "hereditary farms": they could not be sold, divided or mortgaged, and passed to a single heir. Only farmers of "German or related blood" could hold them, and only they could be called *Bauern* (peasants).
:::

The law was an ideological measure: it aimed to protect the "peasant family" from debt, to keep farms in one piece and to tie the farmer's "blood" to the soil. Roughly a third of Germany's farmland was affected (estimates vary). It had unwanted effects. The farmer could not use his land as security for a loan, so that he found it hard to borrow for improvements, and the single heir meant that younger brothers and sisters got no share and moved to the towns. Many farmers disliked it.

### What did the policies achieve?

- **Output and self-sufficiency.** German food production rose, and the share of the country's food that came from German farms rose from about 68 per cent (1928) to about 83 per cent (1938), according to the German Historical Museum. That was a real gain, but it still left Germany dependent on imported fodder, fats and oils.
- **Farm incomes.** Farmers' incomes rose from the low point of 1932, but in many cases only relative to the Depression, and they stayed below those of workers in the towns. Rural complaints about debts, controls and the entailed farm law are recorded in reports of the SS security service (the SD) and in the reports of exiled Social Democrats.
- **The flight from the land.** Higher wages in the towns and the labour demands of rearmament pulled workers off the farms. The number of agricultural workers fell by about 440,000 between 1933 and 1939 (to about 1.4 million), so that farmers who were meant to be the backbone of the regime were short of labour. Darré's "blood and soil" ideal did not stop the drift to the cities.

:::key Agriculture: how successful?
- **Success.** Tariffs and price controls gave farmers security. Debt eased. Self-sufficiency in food rose from about 68 to about 83 per cent (1928–38). The Reich Food Estate gave the regime control over supplies.
- **Failure and limits.** Farm workers kept leaving (about 440,000 fewer by 1939). The Entailed Farm Law reduced credit and annoyed many farmers. Germany still needed imported fats and fodder. Price controls made food dearer for consumers.
- **Judgement.** The policy secured the political support of the farmers in the short term and moved Germany closer to food self-sufficiency, but it did not solve the basic problems of a small-scale, labour-short, under-capitalised agriculture, and by 1939 the regime was still short of fats.
:::

## Rearmament and autarky

### Why rearmament mattered

Rearmament was the real centre of Nazi economic policy, because it served Hitler's aims: to destroy the Treaty of Versailles, to make Germany strong and to win living space in the east (see [Hitler's Germany (2)](#hist.d.hitler.2)). It was also a major cause of the fall in unemployment, since arms factories, barracks, airfields and uniforms gave work to hundreds of thousands. The scale is shown by the share of Germany's national output spent on the armed forces.

Even allowing for the uncertainty, the direction is clear: from a few per cent of national output when Hitler took power to a fifth or more by 1939, a share no other peacetime economy of the time had reached. Britain, which began serious rearmament only after 1936, spent far less.

### Schacht's New Plan, 1934

By the summer of 1934 rearmament and the recovery of demand had pulled in imports, and Germany's gold and foreign currency reserves were almost used up. Schacht, who had become Minister of Economics in August 1934, responded in September 1934 with the **New Plan**. It put all imports under licence, so that foreign exchange was spent first on the raw materials that the armed forces needed (oil, rubber, metals) and not on consumer goods. It made trade agreements, by barter or "clearing" arrangements, with countries of south-east Europe and Latin America, which paid for German goods by supplying food and raw materials, so that Germany did not have to find gold or foreign currency. By the end of 1938 Germany had such agreements with more than forty countries. Trade with the poorer countries of south-east Europe grew, which also gave Germany political influence there.

The New Plan worked for a time. It kept the foreign exchange problem manageable, and it was consistent with Schacht's aim: rearmament, but at a pace that the economy could bear.

### The Four Year Plan, 1936

By 1936 Schacht's caution was in conflict with Hitler's impatience. There was a shortage of foreign exchange and food, and in the winter of 1935–36 of fats. Hitler wrote a **memorandum in August 1936** in which, in summary, he said that the economy and the army must be ready for war within four years, and that the struggle against Bolshevism required it. In September 1936 the **Four Year Plan** was announced at the Nuremberg rally, and on 18 October 1936 Hermann Göring was appointed to run it, with power over prices, wages, raw materials and imports.

| | New Plan (1934) | Four Year Plan (1936) |
|---|---|---|
| Leader | Schacht, Reichsbank and Economics Ministry | Göring, plenipotentiary |
| Method | Import licences; barter and clearing agreements | Make it in Germany: synthetic substitutes, state-run industry, controls on prices, wages and labour |
| Aim | Pay for rearmament without exhausting the foreign exchange | Make Germany as self-sufficient as possible and ready for war in four years |
| Attitude to business and the world economy | Worked with the banks and exporters; wanted to keep trade | Overrode them; subordinated trade and profit to the military |
| Result | A short-term fix, losing ground as arms spending rose | More arms, more controls, shortages of labour and materials |

:::def Autarky
Economic self-sufficiency: a country produces for itself what it would otherwise import. For the Nazis it meant reducing imports of food, oil, rubber and metals, so that Germany could not be starved by a blockade as it had been in the First World War.
:::

The Four Year Plan aimed at autarky and at a war economy. Its targets included:
- **Synthetic fuel and rubber.** The chemical firm IG Farben, a leading firm in the plan, expanded the production of petrol made from coal, and of *Buna* (synthetic rubber). These were expensive and slow to grow.
- **Iron and steel.** In July 1937 Göring set up the state-owned **Reichswerke Hermann Göring** to mine and smelt low-grade German iron ore, which the private steel firms (the Ruhr industrialists) thought too expensive. It was a state firm built on the ground of a political decision, and it later took over industries in Austria and Czechoslovakia.
- **Controls.** Prices were frozen (from November 1936) and labour was directed through the labour books (introduced in 1935).
- **Armaments.** The expansion of aircraft, tanks, ships and ammunition factories, and of the Luftwaffe (the German air force), announced openly in March 1935.

### The war economy by 1939: how ready was Germany?

By 1939 Germany had the strongest armed forces in Europe on land and in the air, and an industry that could supply them. But there were serious weaknesses.

- **Raw materials.** Germany still imported most of its oil, nearly all its rubber, and many metals, such as copper and tungsten, and much good-quality iron ore (from Sweden). Synthetic fuel and rubber were growing but far from sufficient. The Four Year Plan fell short of many of its own targets.
- **Foreign exchange and finance.** By 1938–39 the foreign exchange problem had returned, and the Reichsbank directors' warning of January 1939 spoke of inflation and an unbalanced budget. The Mefo bills (about 12 billion marks) were falling due, and the Reich had borrowed heavily and out of sight.
- **Labour.** From 1936–37 there was a shortage of skilled workers in industry and of labour in agriculture. Wages could not be raised under the freeze, so firms competed by bribing workers with extra pay and perks, and the regime reacted with labour books, direction of labour and the *Pflichtjahr* (a "duty year" in farming or domestic work for young women, from 1938).
- **Coordination.** The Four Year Plan, the army, navy and air force, the party and private firms competed for raw materials. There was no single war plan. In 1939 the navy's big ship programme (the "Z Plan") could not be finished.
- **The war Germany planned.** The economy was not ready for a long war against Britain and France, with an American supply line behind them, and Hitler knew it. His solution was short wars of conquest, whose spoils (the iron ore, food and workers of the conquered lands) were to pay for the next stage.

:::key How prepared was the German economy for war by 1939?
- **For.** Military spending reached about a quarter of national output (about 23 per cent in 1939). The armed forces and the arms industry were the strongest in Europe in 1939. Unemployment was ended and labour could be moved to arms work. Controls on prices, wages and imports were in place. The Four Year Plan, the Reichswerke and synthetic fuel pointed at a long war.
- **Against.** Raw materials and foreign exchange were short. The Four Year Plan missed many targets. Labour and skilled workers were scarce. Several authorities competed with each other. The economy could fight a short war on credit but was not ready for a long one, which is why Hitler hoped for quick victories.
- **Judgement.** The economy was prepared for the kind of war that Hitler meant to fight (a series of short wars with the spoils of each financing the next) and unprepared for the war he got in 1939: a long war against Britain and France that was begun before the economy could be rebuilt.
:::

### Guns or butter?

:::def Guns or butter
The choice between spending on armaments and spending on consumer goods, in which more of one means less of the other. In Nazi Germany it was both a real economic choice and a propaganda slogan: Göring in a speech at Hamburg in December 1935 said (in reported wording; translations vary) that iron ore had always made an empire strong, and butter and lard at most made a people fat.
:::

Did the regime really choose guns over butter? The evidence is mixed.
- **Consumption was squeezed.** Butter and other fats were short in the winter of 1935–36, so that the exiled opposition and foreign newspapers made much of the "butter shortage". Wages were frozen at depression levels while production for the armed forces grew. By late August 1939 food and clothing were rationed.
- **But the regime avoided hardship where it could.** Hitler remembered 1918, when hunger and strikes at home had helped to bring defeat, and he feared an outbreak of unrest. The regime tried to supply cheap consumer goods, such as the "people's receiver" radio and the "people's car" (below). It wanted both guns and butter, but could not afford both.
- **The historians' dispute.** Alan Milward, in *The German Economy at War* (1965), argued that Germany pursued a "blitzkrieg economy", fighting short wars so as to avoid a total mobilisation and keep up living standards. Richard Overy, in *War and Economy in the Third Reich* (1994) and earlier articles, argued that from 1936 the economy was being prepared for a large and long war, and that consumers were not protected as much as Milward claimed. Adam Tooze, in *The Wages of Destruction* (2006), stressed that the regime's ambitions were out of proportion to the size of the German economy and that shortages of foreign exchange and raw materials drove policy.

## Living standards

### Employment, hours and pay

For most workers, a job was the main gain. A family with a wage-earner was better off than a family on the dole, and the sense that "things are moving" helped the regime. But the other parts of the picture were more mixed.

- **Employment.** From 1933 to 1939 registered unemployment fell by about 5 million, and by 1937–38 there was a labour shortage. The fear of unemployment faded, and workers could change jobs, which gave them some bargaining power despite the controls.
- **Working hours.** Hours rose. The average industrial working week was about 43 hours in 1933 and between about 47 and 49 hours by 1939, according to different estimates, and in armaments and construction 60 hours was not unusual.
- **Rates of pay.** The state froze hourly wage rates at around depression levels, and it used the Trustees of Labour to set them. Weekly earnings rose, in real terms by about a fifth between 1933 and 1939 on one standard estimate, but mostly because people worked longer hours and earned overtime. Hourly rates did not rise much. Prices of food and of consumer goods were controlled, but rents and the costs of some goods rose, and the deductions from pay for the DAF, the Winter Relief and other party collections increased. On most estimates real wages did not clearly regain the level of 1929 until the late 1930s.
- **Who gained?** Skilled workers in arms industries gained most. Small shopkeepers and craftsmen (the *Mittelstand*), who had expected the Nazis to destroy the department stores and the big firms, were often disappointed, while big firms, especially in heavy industry and chemicals, made large profits.

### The Labour Front and the loss of the unions

On 1 May 1933 the regime declared a paid holiday, the "Day of National Labour", and workers marched in the Nazi festival. On **2 May 1933**, the next day, the SA and police occupied trade union offices all over Germany, arrested officials and took over the unions' funds and banks. On **10 May 1933** the **German Labour Front** (*Deutsche Arbeitsfront*, DAF) was founded under Robert Ley. It was the single organisation for workers and employers, not a union. It could not bargain over pay or strike, and its main purpose was to control the workers.

The **Law on the Organisation of National Labour** of 20 January 1934 made the employer the "leader" of the factory and the workers his "followers". Wages were no longer negotiated by unions: they were fixed by **Trustees of Labour**, state officials appointed from June 1933. Strikes were illegal, and the **labour books** introduced in 1935 recorded each worker's skills and job history, so that the state could direct labour where it was needed. The unions of the Weimar years had won the eight-hour day and collective agreements; all that was gone.

:::def German Labour Front (DAF)
The Nazi organisation that replaced the free trade unions on 10 May 1933 and took over their funds. It was led by Robert Ley, enrolled workers and employers together, could not bargain over pay or call strikes, and was used to control, indoctrinate and entertain the workforce.
:::

### Strength through Joy and Beauty of Labour

The DAF tried to win the workers' goodwill by offering things the unions had never provided. **Strength through Joy** (*Kraft durch Freude*, KdF), set up in November 1933, organised cheap holidays, cruises, theatre and concert tickets, sports and excursions. Ordinary workers who had never left their home town were taken to the Baltic coast, to the Alps or on a cruise to Norway or Madeira, and the regime used the cruises in its propaganda. KdF's own figures claimed millions of participants a year (about 9.6 million in 1937 is a figure often quoted), but most of these were day trips, evenings and sporting activities, and cruises reached only a small minority, because even cheap ones cost more than most workers could save.

**Beauty of Labour** (*Schönheit der Arbeit*), a section of KdF, campaigned for better conditions in the factory: canteens, washrooms, lighting, playing fields and flowers. It was cheap, because the employers were asked to pay, and it made the factory a less unpleasant place.

The most famous scheme was the **Volkswagen** ("people's car"). Ferdinand Porsche designed a small, cheap car, and a factory was begun in 1938 (at the town later called Wolfsburg). Workers were invited to save five marks a week towards a car. About 330,000 people joined the scheme, but none of them received a car, because the factory was turned over to war production, and the savings went into the DAF's funds. The Volkswagen shows the way KdF worked: a real promotion of leisure and consumption, but one that served the regime's purposes in the end.

:::key Living standards: who gained and who lost?
- **Gained.** The unemployed who found jobs. Skilled workers in the arms industries, who earned overtime and could change jobs. Workers who took KdF holidays or enjoyed canteens and sports fields. Big business, which made large profits.
- **Lost.** Free trade unionists, whose organisations were abolished and whose officials were arrested. Workers whose hourly pay was frozen and who worked longer hours. Small shopkeepers and craftsmen, who did not get the promised protection. Women who gave up work. Jews and political opponents, who were driven out of their jobs altogether.
- **Judgement.** Living standards in the sense of *having a job and enough to eat* improved for most Germans in the mid-1930s, and this was the main reason for the regime's economic popularity. Living standards in the sense of *real wages, choice and freedom to organise* did not.
:::

:::note Evidence: what workers thought
The exiled Social Democratic Party (Sopade) collected secret reports from inside Germany from 1934, and the regime's own security service, the SD, reported on public opinion. Both show grumbling about long hours, high prices, the fall in quality of goods and shortages of fats and meat, together with relief at having work. These are valuable but limited sources: the Sopade reporters were hostile to the regime and the SD reports were written for the regime. A good answer compares them with the statistics instead of relying on one.
:::

### The historians' debate

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **Richard Overy**, *War and Economy in the Third Reich* (1994), argues that economic policy was driven by preparation for war from the mid-1930s, and that the Nazi "recovery" was inseparable from rearmament. He challenges the idea that the regime protected consumers.
- **Adam Tooze**, *The Wages of Destruction: The Making and Breaking of the Nazi Economy* (2006), places the economy in an international setting: Germany was a much smaller economy than the United States, and Hitler's plans for a war of conquest and for a consumer society like America's were beyond its means, which is why the regime had to look to conquest.
- **Tim Mason**, in essays collected in *Nazism, Fascism and the Working Class* (1995) and in *Social Policy in the Third Reich* (1993), argued that by 1938–39 the economy was overheating (shortages of labour, rising working-class discontent) and that this pressure pushed the regime to a "flight into war". Many historians, including Overy, think that Mason overstated this and that Hitler's decisions were driven by his own aims, but the thesis is a useful challenge to the idea that the Nazi economy was a success.
- **Alan Milward**, *The German Economy at War* (1965), argued for the "blitzkrieg economy" (see above).
Use these to show that you know the debate, but your marks come from your own argument backed by evidence, not from a list of names.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. A [30] essay gets one mark out of 15 for analysis and judgement (Table A) and one out of 15 for knowledge (Table B). Read the commentary as an examiner would.

:::eg 'Nazi economic policy was a success.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 2 (4–6): some understanding, descriptive, criteria implicit. Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, but the argument lacks balance; weak judgement. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained balanced argument, a clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): a good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Success" must be defined, and it depends on the regime's aims: jobs and prosperity, or rearmament and war. Criteria: (1) jobs; (2) living standards; (3) the regime's own aims (autarky, rearmament); (4) long-term stability. For: unemployment from about 5.6 million to under 1 million; Mefo bills and Schacht; autobahn; the Four Year Plan; rearmament. Against: hidden unemployment; recovery already under way; frozen wages and longer hours; the unions abolished; autarky not reached; debt and shortages by 1939. Judgement: a success in the narrow sense of jobs and in the regime's own aim of rearmament, but not in the sense of lasting prosperity or readiness for a long war.

**A Level 2/3 paragraph (about Table A Level 2, 5 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "Nazi economic policy was a success because unemployment fell from six million to almost nothing. Hitler built the autobahns and Schacht used Mefo bills. People also went on KdF holidays. Rearmament gave people jobs. So Nazi economic policy was a success."

*Commentary.* Accurate and clear, and it names the key policies. But it is a **list of policies** followed by an assertion, and there is no definition of "success", no dates or amounts, no explanation of why unemployment fell, and nothing on the other side. Table A Level 2; Table B Level 3 for the correct but thin detail.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and give the other side.** "If the test is employment, the policy was a success. Registered unemployment fell from an annual average of about 5.6 million in 1932 to about 0.9 million in 1937, and by 1938 there was a labour shortage. Schacht's deficit financing, with the Mefo bills that paid for arms off the books, gave work to the arms industries, and the Reich Labour Service and the autobahn (about 125,000 men at the peak in 1936) took others off the register. But even on this test there are qualifications. Women who left work for marriage loans, Jews who were dismissed and men in the Labour Service were not counted, and the recovery had begun in 1932. The fall in unemployment was mostly the result of rearmament, so it is better to ask what the jobs were for."

*Commentary.* Now the knowledge is selected to prove a point, with a criterion and a qualification, and a link to the main cause. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. It still needs other criteria (living standards, the regime's own aims) and a clear overall judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view that Nazi economic policy was a success is partly right. It is right if success means ending mass unemployment and restoring the state's power to act, and it is right by the regime's own most important test, which was rearmament. It is wrong if success means rising living standards, lasting prosperity or readiness for the war that Hitler began. The best judgement is that the policy succeeded in what it mattered most to Hitler to achieve, and that this success was bought with strains that the regime could not solve except by war.

The case for success starts with employment. Registered unemployment fell from an annual average of about 5.6 million in 1932 to about 2.7 million in 1934, about 0.9 million in 1937 and under 0.5 million in 1938, and by the later 1930s there was a labour shortage. Schacht, who returned to the Reichsbank on 17 March 1933, restored the credit of the state and found a way to finance recovery without inflation: the Mefo bills, about 12 billion marks of which were issued between 1934 and 1938, paid armaments firms without appearing in the budget. Public works, from the Law for the Reduction of Unemployment of 1 June 1933 to the autobahn, begun on 23 September 1933, gave visible jobs, and the regime's agricultural policies (tariffs, the Reich Food Estate of 13 September 1933) raised the share of Germany's food that was home-grown from about 68 per cent in 1928 to about 83 per cent in 1938. By the regime's own aim of making Germany ready for war, military spending rose from about 3 per cent of national output in 1933 to about 23 per cent in 1939. For many Germans, a job after the Depression was enough to make the regime popular.

However, the claim needs qualification at each point. The unemployment figures flatter the regime. The recovery began in 1932, before Hitler took office. The count did not include the women who gave up work to qualify for a marriage loan, the Jews driven out of business, or the men serving in the Reich Labour Service, which became compulsory on 26 June 1935, and conscription from 16 March 1935 took more men out of the market. Most historians believe that rearmament, not public works, accounted for the greater part of the fall, so that 'success' on jobs and 'success' on rearmament are the same success counted twice. On living standards the evidence is mixed at best. Workers' hourly wage rates stayed near depression levels, partly because the Trustees of Labour fixed them after the unions were occupied on 2 May 1933 and replaced by the German Labour Front on 10 May. Weekly earnings rose, but chiefly because the working week lengthened from about 43 hours to 47 or more, and fats and other foods were short in the winter of 1935–36. Strength through Joy gave some workers holidays, but cruises reached only a minority. Autarky was never achieved: Germany still imported most of its oil and rubber in 1939, and the Four Year Plan, announced in 1936, missed many targets.

The decisive question is therefore what the regime wanted. If the aim was a prosperous Germany, the policy failed to deliver the full benefit, since the choice of guns over butter, which Göring defended in 1935, meant that consumption was held down. If the aim was to prepare for war, then the policy succeeded in creating the strongest armed forces in Europe and the controls to direct the economy. Yet even by that test the success was incomplete. By 1938 Germany faced a foreign exchange shortage, a shortage of skilled labour and inflationary pressure, and Schacht and the Reichsbank directors warned on 7 January 1939 that spending was out of control. He was dismissed on 20 January. Tim Mason argued that these pressures pushed Hitler towards war; whether or not that is true, they show that the economic 'success' was not a stable foundation.

In conclusion, Nazi economic policy was a success in the sense of ending unemployment quickly and creating a large military economy, but it was not a success in the sense of lasting prosperity, self-sufficiency or readiness for a long war, and the two kinds of success pulled in opposite directions. The more important test is the regime's own, since the policy was designed for rearmament, and on that test the policy succeeded in the short term and created the conditions for its own crisis."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): the answer defines the claim, uses wide-ranging criteria (jobs, the regime's aims, living standards, long-term stability), argues both sides in detail with links between them (for example, that the two successes are the same success), tests the assumption behind the question, uses a historian for argument, and ends with a supported judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates (17 March 1933, 1 June 1933, 23 September 1933, 26 June 1935, 7 and 20 January 1939), figures (5.6 million to 0.9 million; 12 billion marks; 68 to 83 per cent; 3 to 23 per cent), and named policies used for the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 800–900 words in 40 minutes is the target.
:::

**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess" asks you to weigh how far something was true, so you need criteria for "prepared" (armed forces and industry, raw materials, finance, labour, planning and the kind of war), evidence on both sides and a judgement of the extent.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) military capacity; (2) raw materials and self-sufficiency; (3) finance; (4) labour and organisation; (5) the kind of war. **A Level 3 paragraph (about Table A Level 3, 7 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "The German economy was well prepared for war by 1939. Hitler had introduced conscription in 1935 and built up the army and the air force. The Four Year Plan of 1936 made Germany ready for war in four years, and Göring put the economy on a war footing. Schacht used Mefo bills to pay for arms. So the economy was ready."

*Commentary.* A good deal of relevant knowledge and a link to the question, but all on one side, and there is no criterion for "prepared". The statement "ready for war in four years" repeats the plan's aim as if it were the result. Table A Level 3; Table B Level 3.

**Moving to Level 4: criteria and the other side.** "On the test of military capacity, the economy was well prepared. Military spending rose from about 3 per cent of national output in 1933 to about 23 per cent in 1939, and Germany had the largest air force and the most modern army in Europe. On the test of raw materials, however, it was not. Despite the Four Year Plan, announced at Nuremberg in September 1936 and run by Göring from 18 October, Germany still imported most of its oil and rubber, and the synthetic substitutes were too few and too dear. On finance there was a similar weakness: the Mefo bills, about 12 billion marks, were falling due, and in January 1939 the Reichsbank directors warned that spending would cause inflation. *Commentary.* A good Level 4 paragraph: criteria, precise knowledge, a contrast and a mini-judgement. To reach Level 5, the essay needs a clear idea of *what war* the economy was prepared for, and links between the criteria.

**A Level 5 conclusion (and the paragraph that sets it up).**

"The key to the question is the kind of war. Historians disagree about what the regime intended. Milward argued that Hitler planned a 'blitzkrieg economy' to fight short wars without a general mobilisation, and that this was why consumer goods were protected for so long. Overy replied that the regime was preparing for a large war and that the pressure on consumers was heavier than Milward allowed, and Tooze showed how far Hitler's ambitions went beyond German resources. The evidence supports a middle view. The planners expected to fight a series of short campaigns, each paid for by the conquest before it: the iron ore of Austria and Czechoslovakia, the labour and gold of the lands taken in 1938–39. That was not an economy prepared for a long war against Britain and France, which would need oil, rubber and food from overseas, and the navy's building programme, the Z Plan, could not be completed.

Overall, the German economy was prepared to a high degree for the first stage of Hitler's plan, which was short wars of conquest in central and eastern Europe, and was prepared far less for the war that began in September 1939, when Britain and France declared war after the invasion of Poland. The armed forces, the controls and the credit mechanisms were all in place. The raw materials, the foreign exchange and the organisation were not. The judgement depends on the test: by capacity and direction, the preparation was very large; by staying power, it was partial. The second test matters more, because the purpose of preparation is to be able to win the war that actually comes."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies **criteria**, uses the historians' dispute to argue instead of listing it, and reaches a supported judgement that links back to the question. With the earlier paragraph on capacity, this is Level 5: Table A 14, Table B 13, about 27 out of 30.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the success of Nazi policies towards agriculture." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Nazis helped farmers a lot. They set up the Reich Food Estate and the Entailed Farm Law so that farmers could not lose their farms. Farmers were very happy because they got good prices. So the Nazi policies on agriculture were a success."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): relevant and clear, but it is a **description with an assertion**. There are no criteria for "success", no dates or figures, and one side only. "Farmers were very happy" is untested, and the Entailed Farm Law actually reduced farmers' freedom to borrow. Table B Level 2–3: some relevant knowledge, but vague and without dates.

**The fix.** Define the test and weigh the evidence: "The policy succeeded on its own terms of security and self-sufficiency, but not on its terms of keeping farmers on the land. The Reich Food Estate (13 September 1933) fixed prices and gave farmers a secure market, and tariffs and debt relief ended the crisis of the early 1930s, so that the share of German food from German farms rose from about 68 per cent in 1928 to about 83 per cent in 1938. But the Reich Entailed Farm Law of 29 September 1933 made farms indivisible and unmortgageable, which hurt farmers' credit and the prospects of younger children, and the number of agricultural workers fell by about 440,000 between 1933 and 1939 as wages in the towns drew them away. The policy therefore secured the farmers' political support but did not stop the drain from the land." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What were Mefo bills, and why were they used?
A: Bills of exchange issued from 1934 by a front company, the Metallurgische Forschungsgesellschaft, to pay armaments firms. The Reichsbank discounted them and the Reich guaranteed them after five years. About 12 billion marks were issued (1934 to March 1938). They paid for rearmament without showing it in the budget, and without printing money.
Q: What did the Law for the Reduction of Unemployment of 1 June 1933 do?
A: It provided money for public works and tax relief to firms, and it brought in marriage loans of up to 1,000 marks (in vouchers), conditional on the wife giving up work, with a quarter of the loan cancelled for each child.
Q: Give two reasons why the registered unemployment figures overstate the Nazi success.
A: They left out women who gave up work, Jews driven from their jobs, and men in the Reich Labour Service, and conscription from 1935 removed others; and the recovery had begun in 1932, with most of the fall coming from rearmament.
Q: What was the Reich Food Estate, and what was the Reich Entailed Farm Law?
A: The Reich Food Estate (13 September 1933, Darré) was a compulsory body for all food producers that fixed prices and controlled marketing. The Entailed Farm Law (29 September 1933) made farms of about 7.5 to 125 hectares indivisible, unsellable and unmortgageable, to pass to one heir of "German blood".
Q: What was the New Plan of 1934, and how did it differ from the Four Year Plan of 1936?
A: Schacht's New Plan (September 1934) licensed imports and made barter and clearing agreements, to save foreign exchange for raw materials. Göring's Four Year Plan (announced September 1936, Göring in charge from 18 October) aimed at self-sufficiency and readiness for war within four years, with state-run industry (the Reichswerke Hermann Göring, July 1937), synthetic fuel and rubber and controls on prices and labour.
Q: What happened to the trade unions in 1933?
A: Their offices were occupied on 2 May 1933 and the unions were dissolved. On 10 May 1933 the German Labour Front (DAF), under Robert Ley, replaced them. It could not bargain or strike. Trustees of Labour set wages (law of 20 January 1934).
Q: What were Strength through Joy and Beauty of Labour?
A: KdF (November 1933) organised cheap holidays, cruises and entertainment for workers; Beauty of Labour improved factory conditions (canteens, washrooms, lighting). Both were parts of the DAF and aimed to win workers' goodwill. KdF cruises reached only a minority.
Q: What happened to working hours and pay between 1933 and 1939?
A: Average hours rose (from about 43 in 1933 to between about 47 and 49 in 1939); hourly wage rates stayed near depression levels; weekly earnings rose (by about a fifth in real terms on one estimate) because of longer hours and overtime.
Q: Why was Schacht dismissed?
A: He resigned as Minister of Economics on 26 November 1937 in the disagreement with Göring over the pace of rearmament and the Four Year Plan, and he was dismissed as President of the Reichsbank on 20 January 1939 after he and the directors warned on 7 January that unrestrained spending would cause inflation.
Q: What did Göring mean by "guns or butter"?
A: That the country should put arms before consumer goods. In December 1935 he said at Hamburg (reported wording) that iron ore had made empires strong and butter and lard had only made people fat. In practice the regime tried to avoid hardship but could not afford both.

Success: the fall from about 5.6 million (1932) to under 1 million (1937); the Mefo bills and Schacht's deficit financing; public works (the Reinhardt programme, the autobahn); the Reich Labour Service (compulsory 26 June 1935); the Law for the Reduction of Unemployment (1 June 1933). Qualifications: recovery under way in 1932; women, Jews and Labour Service men not counted; conscription (16 March 1935); rearmament the main cause. Level 5 uses criteria (scale, speed, cause, durability), weighs the policies against each other, and judges that the biggest cause was rearmament, which means unemployment was solved by preparing for war.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Nazi agricultural policy served ideology more than it helped farmers.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. For: the Entailed Farm Law (29 September 1933) and the "blood and soil" ideal; restrictions on credit; control by the Reich Food Estate; the labour drain (about 440,000 fewer workers by 1939). Against: tariffs, price controls and debt relief raised and secured farm incomes; self-sufficiency from about 68 to about 83 per cent (1928–38); the political support of the farmers. Level 5 separates ideology (the peasant ideal) from economics (food security and war), and judges that the two aims often clashed.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that Schacht's economic policies were more successful than Göring's.
H: Define what each man was trying to do before you compare their success.
H: Use at least three criteria: jobs, finance, foreign trade, preparation for war.
A: Indicative content. Schacht: restored credit, the Mefo bills, the New Plan (September 1934), kept inflation down, avoided a currency crisis, but depended on hidden debt and could not solve the raw material shortage. Göring: the Four Year Plan (October 1936) raised arms output and synthetic fuel, set up the Reichswerke (July 1937), but created shortages, waste and competing authorities. Level 5 uses the two men's different aims (stable rearmament against rapid war preparation), and judges according to a stated test.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which Nazi economic policy raised the living standards of German workers.
H: Separate jobs from pay and from freedom.
A: Indicative content. Gains: jobs, overtime, KdF holidays and cruises, Beauty of Labour, labour shortage. Losses: unions abolished (2 May 1933), the DAF (10 May 1933), Trustees of Labour, hourly rates frozen, hours longer (about 43 to 47 or more), labour books (1935), shortages of fats. Level 5 uses criteria and distinguishes groups of workers, and concludes that the standard of life recovered from 1932 but not necessarily beyond 1929.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Four Year Plan failed.' Evaluate this view.
H: What did the plan set out to do, in Hitler's words and in Göring's?
H: Distinguish self-sufficiency from readiness for war.
A: Indicative content. For: Hitler's memorandum of August 1936 asked for an economy fit for war in four years, and by 1939 oil, rubber and many metals were still imported; targets were missed; competing authorities; labour shortage; inflation warnings (January 1939). Against: the Reichswerke Hermann Göring (July 1937); expanded synthetic fuel and rubber; controls on labour, prices and imports; Germany had the strongest armed forces in 1939 and much greater control of the economy than in 1936. Level 5 judges "failure" against both aims, and concludes that it succeeded in subordinating the economy to rearmament but failed in autarky.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the Nazi economy in 1939 was prepared for a short war but not for a long one.
H: What would a "short war" economy need, and what would a long war need?
H: Use the historians' dispute about the "blitzkrieg economy".
A: Indicative content. Support: raw materials, foreign exchange and finance (Mefo bills falling due, January 1939 warning) were short; the navy's Z Plan could not be built; the economy relied on the spoils of Austria and Czechoslovakia; Milward's "blitzkrieg economy". Challenge: Overy argues that the economy was planned for a large war from 1936; military spending reached nearly a quarter of output in 1939; the Four Year Plan and the Reichswerke aimed at a long war. Level 5 reaches a judgement using criteria (planning, resources, finance, what actually happened).
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The main reason for the fall in German unemployment between 1933 and 1939 was rearmament.' Evaluate this view.
H: List the causes of the fall: the world economy, public works, the Labour Service, rearmament, the way figures were counted.
H: What would count as evidence that one cause mattered most?
A: Indicative content. Rearmament: Mefo bills, military spending rising to about 16 per cent of output in 1938 and about 23 per cent in 1939, conscription (1935), the armed forces and arms industry absorbing workers. Other causes: the upswing that began in 1932; public works (autobahn about 125,000 workers at the peak); the Labour Service; the exclusion of women and Jews; changes in the count. Level 5 ranks the causes and links them (rearmament created demand, which public works began).
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Nazi economic policy was designed to prepare for war, and it should be judged only by that aim.' How far does the evidence for the years 1933–39 support this view?
H: Choose a test of "designed to prepare for war": what policy-makers said, what was spent, what was sacrificed.
H: Use at least three of: the Mefo bills, the New Plan, the Four Year Plan, agriculture, living standards.
A: Support: Hitler's memorandum of August 1936; Mefo bills; military spending from about 3 to about 23 per cent of output; the Four Year Plan and Reichswerke; autarky in agriculture (68 to 83 per cent) and synthetic fuel; the squeeze on consumers; the labour controls. Challenge: some policies had other aims (propaganda: the autobahn, KdF; ideology: the Entailed Farm Law; population: marriage loans), and the regime avoided hardship for political reasons (1918 memory); Tooze stresses the mismatch of ambition and resources; Overy argues the opposite. A* judgement says that preparing for war was the dominant aim from 1934 and clearly after 1936, but that other aims shaped specific policies, and that the policy should be judged on its own aim *and* on its effects on people, because a regime that preferred arms to prosperity chose that cost.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Tim Mason argued that economic pressure drove Hitler into war in 1939. How far does the evidence on the German economy in 1938–39 support him?
H: What would you expect to see if the economy was pushing Hitler to war: shortages, unrest, finance?
H: What else drove Hitler?
A: Support: the labour shortage, the foreign exchange crisis, the Reichsbank directors' warning (7 January 1939), the dismissal of Schacht (20 January 1939), rationing from late August 1939, rising armaments spending, hidden debt due. Challenge: Hitler's aims of living space and revenge had been stated since the 1920s and the 1936 memorandum; conquest of Austria and Czechoslovakia financed the economy; working-class unrest was limited by the regime's terror; Overy and others argue that the economic strains were results of the war drive, not its cause. A* judgement treats the economy as a constraint and a pressure, but not as the main cause, and argues from the evidence.
:::

:::recap
- **Starting point**: about 5.6 million registered unemployed (1932 average), over 6 million in early 1933; recovery already beginning in 1932.
- **Depression policy**: Schacht at the Reichsbank (17 March 1933); deficit financing; Mefo bills (about 12 billion marks, 1934 to March 1938); Law for the Reduction of Unemployment (1 June 1933, marriage loans of 1,000 marks); autobahn (first sod 23 September 1933, about 125,000 workers at the peak in 1936, about 3,000 km by 1938); Reich Labour Service (compulsory 26 June 1935).
- **Unemployment**: about 5.6 million (1932), 4.8 (1933), 2.7 (1934), 1.6 (1936), 0.9 (1937), about 0.1 (1939 average). Not counted: women, Jews, Labour Service men; conscription from 16 March 1935.
- **Agriculture**: tariffs and debt relief; Reich Food Estate (13 September 1933, Darré); Reich Entailed Farm Law (29 September 1933); self-sufficiency about 68 per cent (1928) to about 83 per cent (1938); about 440,000 fewer farm workers by 1939.
- **Rearmament and autarky**: New Plan (September 1934); Hitler's memorandum (August 1936); Four Year Plan (September 1936, Göring from 18 October); Reichswerke Hermann Göring (July 1937); synthetic fuel and rubber; military spending from about 3 to about 23 per cent of output (1933 to 1939); Schacht resigned 26 November 1937, dismissed 20 January 1939; rationing from late August 1939.
- **Living standards**: unions occupied 2 May 1933; DAF 10 May 1933; Law on the Organisation of National Labour 20 January 1934; labour books 1935; hours about 43 to 47 or more; hourly pay frozen; KdF (November 1933); Beauty of Labour; Volkswagen scheme (5 marks a week, about 330,000 savers).
- **Judgement**: success in jobs and in the regime's own aim of rearmament, bought with debt, shortages and a dependence on war; not success in lasting prosperity, self-sufficiency or readiness for a long war.
- **Historians**: Overy (1994), Tooze (2006), Mason (flight into war), Milward (1965, blitzkrieg economy).
