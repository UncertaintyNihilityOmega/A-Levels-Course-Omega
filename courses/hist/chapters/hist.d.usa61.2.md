:::why
Two crises defined the foreign policy of the 1960s. In October 1962 the world came closer to nuclear war than at any other moment, and a President's judgement under pressure decided the outcome. In Vietnam the United States, the strongest power on earth, spent more than a decade, more than 58,000 American lives and a huge fortune, and still saw its ally collapse in 1975. Historians ask of both whether the government acted wisely: did Kennedy handle the missile crisis well, and why did the USA go into Vietnam and then find it so hard to get out? These are among the most argued questions in modern American history, and they are Paper 4 favourites.

## How to judge "how well": four criteria

The key question asks how *well* the governments dealt with Cuba and Vietnam. "Well" needs a measure, and a Level 5 answer says which one it is using. Four are useful here.
1. **Aims and results:** did the government get what it wanted, at an acceptable price?
2. **Judgement and alternatives:** were the decisions sensible at the time, given what the leaders knew, and what other choices did they have? (Judge the decision-makers by their information, not by what we know now.)
3. **Costs and risks:** in lives, money, trust and the danger of a wider war.
4. **Consequences:** what did the policy do to the USA's position in the world and to its own society?

On these criteria Cuba and Vietnam look very different. The missile crisis is usually seen as a risky but successful piece of crisis management. Vietnam is usually seen as a failure of policy. But both questions are argued, and the best answers test the popular verdicts.

## Kennedy and Cuba

### Kennedy's view of the Castro regime

When Fidel Castro took power on 1 January 1959 many Americans welcomed the end of the dictator Fulgencio Batista. By 1960 the mood had turned. Castro nationalised American-owned land, sugar mills and oil refineries, signed a trade deal with the USSR in February 1960 and declared that Cuba would be a socialist state. Eisenhower cut the sugar quota, banned most exports and broke off relations on 3 January 1961 (see [Post-war USA (4)](#hist.d.usa45.4)).

Kennedy saw Castro as a threat on several levels.
- **Strategic.** A Soviet ally ninety miles from Florida was a breach of the **Monroe Doctrine**, the American principle that Europe's powers should keep out of the Western Hemisphere, extended to the Cold War.
- **Ideological and regional.** Castro's example, Kennedy feared, would inspire revolution across Latin America. His own answer was the **Alliance for Progress** of March 1961, a ten-year programme of aid and reform for Latin America, for which the USA promised $20 billion, which was meant to prove that democratic reform could work better than revolution.
- **Political.** Kennedy had campaigned in 1960 by accusing the Republicans of being soft on Cuba, and could not now be seen to do nothing.

### The importance of the Bay of Pigs

Kennedy inherited the CIA's plan to land about 1,400 Cuban exiles, trained in Guatemala as **Brigade 2506**, on Cuba's southern coast, in the hope that this would spark an uprising against Castro. The CIA director, Allen Dulles, and the military told Kennedy it would succeed. He agreed on condition that the USA's hand would not show, and on the day he cancelled a second round of air strikes on Castro's remaining aircraft.
- **The landing.** The exiles landed at the **Bay of Pigs** (Bahía de Cochinos) on 17 April 1961. The site was in a swamp, far from the mountains where they could have turned to guerrilla warfare. Castro's air force sank two ships carrying supplies and ammunition, and 20,000 Cuban troops, led by Castro himself, surrounded the exiles. They surrendered on 19 April. About 114 were killed and more than 1,100 captured; in December 1962 Cuba released the prisoners in return for about $53 million of food and medicine.
- **Why it failed.** The plan was based on false hopes: that the Cuban people would rise (they did not), that the invasion could be kept secret (the American press had already reported the plan), and that the loss of surprise did not matter. Kennedy had cut the air strikes and refused to send US forces, and the CIA had assumed he would send them if it went badly.

:::key The importance of the Bay of Pigs
**For Cuba:** it made Castro a national hero, stiffened his regime, and drove him closer to the USSR; in December 1961 he declared himself a Marxist-Leninist.
**For the USA:** a public humiliation in Kennedy's first three months; he accepted responsibility, but within a year the CIA's director, Allen Dulles, and his senior deputies had left, and Kennedy learned to distrust military and CIA advice, which affected his way of running the missile crisis.
**For the USSR:** it made Khrushchev think Kennedy weak and inexperienced. At the Vienna summit in June 1961 he bullied Kennedy, and in August the Berlin Wall went up. Many historians see the Bay of Pigs as one step towards the decision to place missiles in Cuba.
**Judgement to defend:** a **serious failure that did lasting damage**, but one that also taught Kennedy lessons he used well in October 1962.
:::

After the Bay of Pigs the Kennedy administration did not give up. **Operation Mongoose**, approved in November 1961 and supervised by Robert Kennedy, was a programme of sabotage, propaganda and CIA plots against Castro, including attempts on his life. It never came near to succeeding, and persuaded Castro and Khrushchev that another invasion was likely.

### Why the missiles came to Cuba

In the summer of 1962 Khrushchev decided to put medium-range nuclear missiles in Cuba (Operation Anadyr). His reasons, as historians now see them, were these.
- **To defend Cuba** against an American attack, which he thought likely.
- **To correct the nuclear imbalance.** The USA had a large lead in long-range missiles and had put 15 Jupiter missiles in Turkey, close to the USSR. Missiles in Cuba would cover most of the USA's cities at a cost that was far less than building long-range missiles.
- **To strengthen his hand over Berlin**, and to show Communist China that he was a firm defender of the revolution.
The Soviet Union sent about 40,000 troops and many hidden weapons, but did not tell the Americans, who thought there were about 10,000.

### The discovery of the missiles

In September 1962 Kennedy warned that the USA would not accept offensive weapons in Cuba, and Soviet officials, among them the ambassador Anatoly Dobrynin, gave private assurances that none would be sent. On **14 October 1962** a U-2 spy plane, flown by Major Richard Heyser, took pictures of a medium-range missile site at San Cristóbal in western Cuba. They were analysed on 15 October, and Kennedy was told on the morning of 16 October. The USA now knew that Soviet missiles, able to reach Washington and most of the eastern United States, were nearly ready.

### Kennedy's options

Kennedy set up a group of advisers, called the **Executive Committee of the National Security Council (ExComm)**, and met them for a week in secret, while carrying on his public engagements. They considered five options.

| Option | For | Against |
|---|---|---|
| Do nothing or protest through diplomacy | No risk of war | The missiles would stay; Kennedy would look weak; elections in November |
| A secret approach to Khrushchev | Might offer a way out | Khrushchev had already lied; no deadline; delay |
| An air strike on the sites | Would destroy the missiles | It could not be sure of getting them all; Soviet troops would be killed; likely to lead to an invasion and war with the USSR |
| An invasion | Would remove Castro and the missiles | Heavy casualties; likely to bring a Soviet response in Berlin or elsewhere |
| A naval blockade ("quarantine") | A limited step that could be increased; time for Khrushchev to think | Did not remove the missiles; could be defeated by a Soviet ship; 'quarantine' was a legal name for an act of war |

The Joint Chiefs of Staff, with General Curtis LeMay the most forceful of them, urged an air strike followed by an invasion. Robert Kennedy and the Secretary of Defense, Robert McNamara, argued for the blockade. The group moved towards the blockade, which Kennedy chose, calling it a "quarantine" because a blockade is an act of war under international law and a quarantine could be presented as a defensive measure approved by the Organization of American States.

### The exchanges between Kennedy and Khrushchev

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| 22 October | Kennedy tells the world on television; he says the USA will treat any missile launched from Cuba as an attack by the Soviet Union, requiring "a full retaliatory response upon the Soviet Union"; US forces are put on alert (DEFCON 3, later 2 for the Strategic Air Command) |
| 24 October | The quarantine begins at 10 a.m.; Soviet ships approach the line and then stop or turn back; Dean Rusk is said to have remarked that "we're eyeball to eyeball and I think the other fellow just blinked" |
| 26 October | Khrushchev writes privately: the missiles will go if the USA promises not to invade Cuba |
| 27 October ("Black Saturday") | A second letter, read on Moscow radio, demands the removal of the Jupiter missiles in Turkey; a U-2 is shot down over Cuba and its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson, killed; Kennedy decides to accept the first letter and ignore the second |
| 27 October, evening | Robert Kennedy meets Dobrynin and promises that the Jupiters will be removed within a short time, as long as this is never made public |
| 28 October | Khrushchev announces on Moscow radio that the missiles will be dismantled and sent home |

The danger was real. American forces were preparing an air strike and invasion for the first days of the following week; Soviet commanders in Cuba had tactical nuclear weapons, and on the night of 27 October the officers of a Soviet submarine, B-59, argued whether to fire a nuclear torpedo while it was under attack by American ships dropping practice depth charges (according to later accounts, one officer, Vasili Arkhipov, refused). Neither Kennedy nor Khrushchev knew these things at the time, and both wanted to avoid war.

### The outcomes for the USA and relations with the USSR

- **The settlement.** The USSR removed the missiles and the Il-28 bombers, which American aircraft and ships checked. The USA gave a pledge not to invade Cuba, which Kennedy made conditional on UN inspection (never accepted by Castro), and secretly agreed to withdraw the Jupiters from Turkey, which was done by April 1963. The quarantine was lifted on 20 November.
- **Kennedy's reputation.** His handling was widely praised, and he was seen as firm but restrained. Critics, in the military, said he had missed the chance to remove Castro.
- **Castro.** He survived and Cuba stayed Communist. He had been left out of the bargain and was angry, but his regime was now secure from invasion.
- **The USSR.** Khrushchev was seen at home and in Beijing as having backed down. He was removed from power in October 1964, for many reasons, of which Cuba was one. The Soviet military began a build-up of long-range missiles that brought parity with the USA by the early 1970s.
- **Better relations.** The two leaders had stared into the abyss. A "hot line" between Washington and Moscow was agreed on 20 June 1963. In a speech at American University on 10 June 1963 Kennedy called for a new look at the Cold War, and on 5 August 1963 the USA, USSR and Britain signed the **Partial Test Ban Treaty**, which banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water. This began the thaw that led to the détente of the 1970s.

### How well did Kennedy handle the crisis?

:::key The case for and against: was Kennedy's handling a success?
**For:** the missiles were removed without war; he chose the quarantine, a graduated step that kept options open and gave Khrushchev time; he ignored the demand for an air strike from his generals; he combined firmness (the speech of 22 October) with a way out (the secret promise on the Jupiters and the pledge not to invade); the crisis produced the hot line and the Test Ban Treaty.
**Against:** the crisis was partly the result of his own policies (the Bay of Pigs, Mongoose, the Jupiters in Turkey and the build-up of missiles, which made Khrushchev feel threatened); he risked nuclear war over missiles that changed the strategic balance little; he kept the Turkish deal secret from the public and from most of his own government, so that the "eyeball to eyeball" version of events was a half-truth; and luck played a part (the submarine, the U-2 incidents).
**Judgement to defend:** a **success in its outcome and in the way Kennedy managed the final decisions**, but one that cannot be separated from the policies that made the crisis, and from luck.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **Graham Allison**, in *Essence of Decision* (1971), used the crisis to show three ways of explaining government decisions: as the acts of a single rational actor, as the outputs of large organisations following their routines, and as the result of bargaining between officials. His book made the crisis a model for the study of decision-making.
- **Sheldon Stern**, who was a historian at the Kennedy Library and worked on the secretly recorded ExComm tapes, argued in books such as *The Week the World Stood Still* (2005) that earlier accounts, including Robert Kennedy's *Thirteen Days* (1969), gave a misleading picture of the discussions.
- **Robert Dallek**, in *An Unfinished Life* (2003), set the crisis within a biography of Kennedy, crediting his caution under pressure while showing how his earlier policies towards Cuba had helped to provoke it.
Use these to show that you know there is a debate, but your marks come from your own argument backed by evidence.
:::

## US involvement in Vietnam to 1963

### The Geneva Accords and the situation in Vietnam

Vietnam had been a French colony, and the Viet Minh under **Ho Chi Minh**, a nationalist and Communist, had been fighting the French since 1946. After the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu (7 May 1954), the Geneva conference agreed on 21 July 1954 to divide the country **temporarily** at the 17th parallel: Ho's Democratic Republic of Vietnam in the north, a non-Communist state in the south, with elections in 1956 to unify the country. The USA did not sign the Accords, though it said it would not upset them. Most observers thought Ho would win the elections.

The new government in the south, set up with American help, was led from 1954 by **Ngo Dinh Diem**, a Catholic nationalist and anti-Communist in a country that was mostly Buddhist. With American advice he refused to hold the elections of 1956, saying that the North was not free, and in 1955 won a referendum by a rigged vote of about 98 per cent and became President of the Republic of Vietnam. Opposition grew. In 1959 Hanoi began to help the southern Communists, the "Viet Cong" (Vietnamese Communists), and in December 1960 they formed the **National Liberation Front (NLF)**.

### Fears of communism: Eisenhower and the domino theory

American policy rested on **containment**, the idea set out under Truman that Communism must be stopped from spreading (see [Post-war USA (4)](#hist.d.usa45.4)). Vietnam seemed an obvious test, for three reasons: the victory of Mao Zedong in China in 1949 and the Korean War of 1950–53 had made Americans see Communism as a single, centrally directed force; the French defeat seemed to leave a vacuum; and politicians dared not be blamed for "losing" another country, as Truman had been blamed for "losing China".

:::def The domino theory
The belief that if one country in a region fell to Communism its neighbours would fall one after another, like a row of dominoes. President Eisenhower used the image at a press conference on 7 April 1954, saying of Indochina "You have a row of dominoes set up, you knock over the first one…", and it became the basis of American policy in Southeast Asia. Critics said that it ignored the differences between countries and the strength of local nationalism.
:::

Eisenhower therefore backed Diem. The USA created SEATO (the Southeast Asia Treaty Organization, 8 September 1954), sent money, and trained the army of South Vietnam. By the end of 1960 there were about 900 American military advisers in the country.

### Reasons for increased US involvement under Kennedy

Kennedy was anxious not to look weak after the Bay of Pigs and the Vienna summit, and believed that Vietnam was a test of the "flexible response" strategy of fighting local wars. He sent more help.
- **More advisers.** After the report of General Maxwell Taylor and Walt Rostow (October 1961) he rejected a call to send combat troops, but approved large increases of advisers, helicopters and equipment. A new command, the Military Assistance Command, Vietnam (MACV), was set up in February 1962. By the end of 1963 there were about 16,300 American personnel in South Vietnam, and some of them were fighting.
- **New methods.** Special Forces trained and led local troops, and aircraft sprayed defoliants such as Agent Orange on forests and crops (Operation Ranch Hand, from January 1962) to deny cover and food to the Viet Cong.
- **The Strategic Hamlet Program (1962).** Peasants were moved into fortified villages to cut them off from the guerrillas. It failed, because it uprooted people from their land and the Viet Cong infiltrated the hamlets.
- **Why?** To stop the domino effect; to keep his word to an ally; to avoid blame; to avoid a larger commitment by giving a smaller one; and, in the view of some historians, because he saw the war as a way of proving the USA's will after the setbacks of 1961.

### The fall of Diem

By 1963 Diem was losing the war and the support of his people. The army of South Vietnam was beaten at Ap Bac on 2 January 1963, though it had many more men and US helicopters. The Buddhist majority resented the privileges of the Catholic minority and Diem's brother **Ngo Dinh Nhu**, who ran the secret police. When troops fired on Buddhists in Hue on 8 May 1963, protests grew, and on 11 June a monk, Thich Quang Duc, burned himself to death in Saigon, in a photograph that shocked the world. Diem's family made things worse, and in August special forces loyal to Nhu raided the pagodas.

The Kennedy administration, divided, let it be known on 24 August that the USA would not oppose a change of government. On 1 November 1963 a group of generals seized power, and Diem and Nhu were murdered the next day, which Kennedy had not intended. The USA was now more responsible for what followed in Saigon, and the generals, who were unstable, fought each other through 1964, leaving a weaker South Vietnam. Three weeks later Kennedy himself was dead.

:::key Why the USA became involved in Vietnam to 1963
**Reasons:** (1) *containment and the domino theory* (the underlying belief); (2) *the Cold War context* (China, Korea, the defeat of France and fear of blame for "losing" Vietnam); (3) *commitment to Diem* (each step of aid made withdrawal harder); (4) *Kennedy's wish to appear firm* after the Bay of Pigs; (5) *the failure of Diem's regime*, which brought more American help, and then involvement in his overthrow.
**Judgement to defend:** the USA was drawn in by **Cold War beliefs that made Vietnam look like a test of American resolve**, and each limited step (money, advisers, a coup) made the next more likely; nobody decided to fight a big war, but by 1963 there was no easy way out.
:::

## Escalation under Johnson and US strategy, 1964–68

### The Gulf of Tonkin incident

Johnson took over a war that was going badly, and in 1964 an election in which he did not wish to appear either weak or warlike. On **2 August 1964** the destroyer USS *Maddox*, on an intelligence patrol in the Gulf of Tonkin, was attacked by North Vietnamese torpedo boats. The *Maddox* had been near South Vietnamese raids on the North's coast, which the Americans had backed. On 4 August the *Maddox* and another destroyer, the *Turner Joy*, reported a second attack. Later studies, including a National Security Agency history written in 2001 and released in 2005, concluded that the second attack almost certainly did not happen. But Johnson, who ordered air strikes on North Vietnamese bases on 4–5 August, asked Congress for authority to act.

The **Gulf of Tonkin Resolution** of 7 August 1964 passed the House by 416 to 0 and the Senate by 88 to 2 (Senators Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening voted against). It let the President "take all necessary measures to repel any armed attack" and to prevent further aggression. It was in effect a blank cheque. Johnson used it as his legal authority to wage war without a declaration, and he cited it for years.

### Escalation in 1965

Johnson won the election of November 1964 as the candidate of peace, but the South was collapsing, and in 1965 he decided on war. His advisers, notably Robert McNamara and McGeorge Bundy, wanted to bomb the North to force Hanoi to stop supporting the Viet Cong, and to send combat troops to protect American bases.
- **7 February 1965.** The Viet Cong attacked the American base at Pleiku and killed eight Americans. Johnson ordered reprisal raids.
- **2 March 1965.** **Operation Rolling Thunder** began: sustained air attacks on the North, planned to last a few weeks, but lasting until November 1968.
- **8 March 1965.** Two battalions of Marines, about 3,500 men, landed at Da Nang to protect the airbase. They were the first American combat units.
- **April–July 1965.** The Marines were allowed to patrol and fight, and in the summer Westmoreland asked for more men. On 28 July Johnson announced that he would send another 50,000 men at once (a total of 125,000) and double the draft. He did not ask Congress for a declaration of war, did not call up the reserves and did not ask for a tax rise, because he wished to protect the Great Society and did not want to alarm China and the USSR or the public.
- **November 1965.** In the battle of the Ia Drang valley (14–18 November) US troops fought North Vietnamese regulars for the first time in a major battle. By the end of the year about 184,000 Americans were in Vietnam.

### US strategy and tactics, 1965–68

The commander of the American forces, General **William Westmoreland**, chose a strategy of **attrition**: to kill communists faster than they could be replaced, until the enemy gave up ("the crossover point"). The measure was the **body count**.
- **Search and destroy.** Large units, supported by helicopters and artillery, went into the countryside to find the enemy and fight him. The enemy, however, chose when to fight and could slip away into the jungle, into villages or across the border into Cambodia and Laos. The Viet Cong and North Vietnamese used the **Ho Chi Minh Trail**, a network of tracks through Laos and Cambodia, to bring men and supplies south.
- **Firepower.** The Americans used overwhelming force: B-52 bombers, artillery, **napalm** (burning petrol jelly) and defoliants. Operation Ranch Hand sprayed nearly 19 million gallons of herbicides between 1962 and 1971, at least 11 million of them Agent Orange, to strip the forests and destroy crops. The effects included civilian deaths, a wrecked countryside and long-term illness among Vietnamese and some American veterans. It also alienated the villagers whom the USA wanted to win.
- **"Hearts and minds."** From 1967 a pacification programme (CORDS) tried to win the villagers by building schools, clinics and security, but it was small beside the military effort.
- **The soldiers.** Most American troops were conscripts, and many were young and poor: the draft let students and others avoid service by deferment, a source of resentment. They faced booby traps, snipers and an enemy who was hard to tell from the villagers.

### Bombing and Operation Rolling Thunder

Rolling Thunder was meant to stop Hanoi sending men and supplies to the South, to damage its industry and to force it to the negotiating table, by a gradual increase in pressure. It did not work.
- The North's economy was mainly agricultural, with few targets of value. The Chinese and Soviet governments supplied weapons, anti-aircraft guns and missiles, and the Ho Chi Minh Trail was too hard to cut.
- Hanoi mobilised millions of civilians to repair roads and bridges, and bombing made the people more determined.
- Johnson himself picked many of the targets in weekly meetings, and often stopped the bombing for periods in the hope of talks, which let the North prepare.
- The US lost a large number of aircraft, and many airmen were captured.
Over the three and a half years of Rolling Thunder the Americans dropped a very large tonnage of bombs on the North; the figure is variously given as in the hundreds of thousands of tons, and there is no agreed total.

### The effectiveness of US tactics, 1965–68

By the end of 1967 American forces had won every big battle they chose to fight, and the body counts were large. General Westmoreland told the National Press Club in November 1967 that the end was in sight. But the evidence against the tactics was strong:
- the enemy could replace its losses (by some estimates about 200,000 young men reached military age in the North each year) and could choose the scale of the fighting;
- the South Vietnamese government remained corrupt and unpopular, so American victories in the field did not become political ones;
- the cost was high: American deaths rose every year to a peak in 1968 (the war killed about 58,000 Americans in all), and the draft was extended;
- bombing and defoliation killed and displaced civilians and turned many villagers against the USA.

:::key How effective were US tactics?
**Strengths:** overwhelming firepower and mobility; won the big battles; the Viet Cong lost heavily, especially in 1968.
**Weaknesses:** attrition could not win against an enemy that chose when to fight; bombing failed to break Hanoi's will; the Ho Chi Minh Trail stayed open; tactics alienated civilians; the army of South Vietnam was weak; and the aims were unclear.
**Judgement to defend:** the tactics were **militarily powerful but strategically ineffective**: the USA could win battles but not the war, because the war was about political will in Vietnam, which firepower could not supply.
:::

## US withdrawal and the ending of the war, 1968–75

### The role of the media, My Lai and different attitudes in the USA

The Vietnam War is often called the first "television war", and the pictures shown at home affected opinion. Reporters could travel freely, with little censorship, and the networks showed scenes of fighting and of the dead and wounded. Michael Arlen called it "the living-room war". Yet historians disagree about how much influence the media had. Daniel Hallin, in *The "Uncensored War"* (1986), argued that the coverage became critical only after leading politicians in Washington had begun to disagree about the war, so that the press followed elite opinion more than it led it.
- **Opinion polls.** Gallup first asked in August 1965 whether sending troops had been a mistake: 24 per cent said yes. In August 1968, for the first time, a majority, 53 per cent, said it was a mistake, and it mostly stayed above 50 per cent from then on.
- **The anti-war movement.** It began on campuses (the "teach-ins" of 1965), grew with the draft and the casualties, and by 1967 included civil rights leaders, including Martin Luther King, who spoke against the war on 4 April 1967. The Moratorium of 15 October 1969 and the march on Washington of 15 November 1969 were among the largest demonstrations in US history. Opponents (the "doves") were met by supporters of the war (the "hawks"), and Nixon appealed to the "silent majority" that supported him.
- **My Lai.** On 16 March 1968 soldiers of Charlie Company, among them the platoon of Lieutenant William Calley, killed several hundred unarmed villagers, mostly women, children and old men, at My Lai in Quang Ngai province. The US Army's own inquiry later counted 347 dead, and the Vietnamese memorial lists 504. The Army covered it up until a soldier, Ron Ridenhour, wrote to officials, and the journalist Seymour Hersh published the story on 12 November 1969; photographs by Ronald Haeberle appeared in December. Calley was court-martialled and found guilty of murder in March 1971, but Nixon had him moved to house arrest and he was freed in 1974. My Lai did not cause the withdrawal, but it damaged belief in the justice of the war.

### The importance of the Tet Offensive

On **30–31 January 1968**, during the Tet (lunar new year) holiday, the North Vietnamese and Viet Cong attacked more than a hundred cities and towns of South Vietnam at once, including a raid on the American embassy in Saigon, and held part of the old city of Hue for about three weeks. The Americans and the South Vietnamese army beat them back, and the communists lost tens of thousands of fighters, many of them Viet Cong, who never recovered their strength. In a narrow military sense Tet was a victory for the USA.

Politically it was a disaster. Americans had been told the enemy was nearly beaten, and now saw fighting in Saigon. The siege of the Marine base at Khe Sanh added to the alarm. In late February the American commander asked for another 206,000 men. The news commentator **Walter Cronkite**, after a visit, said on CBS on 27 February 1968, "To say that we are mired in stalemate seems the only realistic, yet unsatisfactory, conclusion." (Some historians, among them W. Joseph Campbell, argue that the effect of this broadcast on Johnson has been exaggerated.) On 12 March 1968 Senator Eugene McCarthy, an anti-war challenger, won 42 per cent of the vote in the New Hampshire primary against Johnson's 49 per cent, and Robert Kennedy entered the race.

Johnson turned down the request for 206,000 men and gave a small increase only. On **31 March 1968** he announced a partial halt in the bombing, called for peace talks and ended with the words:

:::note Evidence: Johnson withdraws
"I shall not seek, and I will not accept, the nomination of my party for another term as your President."

*From Lyndon B. Johnson, address to the nation on Vietnam, 31 March 1968. The sentence is quoted exactly. It followed the Tet Offensive and the primary in New Hampshire and shows the political, not the military, effect of Tet.*
:::

Tet therefore marked a **turning point**: it ended the policy of escalation, made negotiation the aim and set the USA on the long road to withdrawal. Peace talks opened in Paris on 13 May 1968, but they made no progress, and Johnson ended all bombing of the North on 1 November.

### Nixon's policy and the promise of "peace with honour"

Nixon was elected in 1968 saying that he would end the war, and later spoke of "peace with honor". His aim was to get out of the war without a visible defeat, keeping South Vietnam alive, and he and his National Security Adviser, **Henry Kissinger**, thought that this could be done through a mix of pressure and talks. Their policy had four parts.
- **Vietnamisation.** The South Vietnamese army would be built up and gradually take over the fighting, while American troops left. Nixon announced the first withdrawal of 25,000 men on 8 June 1969, and the **Nixon Doctrine** (Guam, 25 July 1969) said that in future the USA would help Asian allies with money and arms but expect them to supply the men. In a speech of 3 November 1969 he said, "to you, the great silent majority of my fellow Americans, I ask for your support."
- **Pressure on Hanoi and Cambodia.** Nixon secretly bombed Cambodia from March 1969 and on **30 April 1970** sent American and South Vietnamese troops into Cambodia to destroy communist bases. The move set off angry protests, and on 4 May at Kent State University in Ohio National Guardsmen shot dead four students; two more were killed at Jackson State on 14 May. Congress repealed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution in January 1971. In 1971 a South Vietnamese attack into Laos failed.
- **Diplomacy.** Nixon and Kissinger improved relations with the USSR and with China (Nixon's visit to Beijing was in February 1972), partly in the hope that both would press Hanoi to settle. See [A time of challenges, 1961–74 (4)](#hist.d.usa61.4) for this.
- **A hard line at the end.** After the North's Easter Offensive of March 1972, Nixon mined Haiphong harbour and renewed bombing (Linebacker), and in an eleven-day campaign from 18 to 29 December 1972, the "Christmas bombing", sent B-52s over Hanoi and Haiphong.

### Vietnamisation and the withdrawal of US troops

American troop numbers peaked at about 543,000 in April 1969 and fell to about 24,000 by the end of 1972 (see the chart). Casualties and the draft fell with them, which cut anti-war feeling; the draft ended in 1973. The South Vietnamese army grew to about a million men, with American weapons, and in the Easter Offensive of 1972, with US air power, it held. But it was never self-sufficient: it relied on American supplies and air power, its officers were often corrupt, and the government of President Nguyen Van Thieu was weak.

### The Paris peace talks and the Accords

Public talks in Paris went nowhere. The real bargaining was done in secret between Kissinger and the North Vietnamese negotiator Le Duc Tho from August 1969. Just before the presidential election of 1972 Kissinger said "peace is at hand", but Thieu refused the draft, because it let North Vietnamese troops stay in the South. After the Christmas bombing the talks resumed, and the **Paris Peace Accords** were signed on **27 January 1973**. They provided for a ceasefire, the withdrawal of all American forces within sixty days, the return of American prisoners (591 came home) and political talks between the Vietnamese; they left North Vietnamese forces in the South. The last US combat troops left on 29 March 1973. Kissinger and Le Duc Tho were awarded the Nobel Peace Prize, which Le Duc Tho declined.

### The fall of Saigon

The ceasefire did not hold. Nixon had privately promised Thieu that the USA would respond with force if the North broke the agreement, but he was weakened by Watergate and resigned in August 1974. Congress had already passed the **War Powers Resolution** (7 November 1973, over Nixon's veto) to limit the President's right to wage war, and it cut aid to South Vietnam. In March 1975 the North began a major offensive; Thieu's decision to pull back from the Central Highlands turned into a rout, and the army collapsed. On **30 April 1975** North Vietnamese tanks entered Saigon and the South Vietnamese government surrendered. The last Americans, and many Vietnamese, were evacuated by helicopter from the roof of the embassy and other sites. The war had killed about 58,000 Americans and, by most estimates, between one million and more than three million Vietnamese, military and civilian.

:::key Why the USA withdrew from Vietnam
**Reasons:** (1) *the failure of the military strategy* (attrition and bombing did not break the enemy; the Tet Offensive exposed it); (2) *loss of public and political support* (casualties, the draft, the anti-war movement, the media, My Lai, polls showing a majority against by 1968); (3) *the economic cost* (inflation, damage to the Great Society, the weak dollar); (4) *the weakness of South Vietnam* (a corrupt government that could not win without the Americans); (5) *Nixon's own strategy* (Vietnamisation, détente with China and the USSR, and the need to end the draft); (6) *Congress* (the War Powers Resolution, the repeal of Tonkin and the aid cuts).
**Judgement to defend:** the USA left because **it could not win at a price that Americans would pay**. The decisive turning point was Tet, which destroyed the political will for more escalation; but the actual withdrawal was **Nixon's choice** and designed to save face.
:::

- **Fredrik Logevall**, in *Choosing War* (1999), argued that Johnson and his advisers in 1964–65 were not trapped, because they had alternatives, and chose to escalate for reasons of politics and credibility.
- **Daniel Hallin**, in *The "Uncensored War"* (1986), argued that television reflected the divisions in Washington more than it caused them.

## How well did US governments deal with Cuba and Vietnam?

| Test | Cuba (Kennedy, 1961–62) | Vietnam (Kennedy to Nixon, 1961–75) |
|---|---|---|
| Aims and results | The missiles went and Cuba was not invaded; but Castro stayed | Aim: keep a non-Communist South Vietnam. Failed in 1975 |
| Judgement | Careful: options weighed, the quarantine chosen | Poor: false assumptions, the escalation of 1965 and the weakness of the ally |
| Costs and risks | The risk of nuclear war was very high for a short time | More than 58,000 American dead; over a million Vietnamese; inflation and division |
| Consequences | The hot line, the Test Ban Treaty, the beginning of détente | The "Vietnam syndrome", distrust of government, the War Powers Resolution |

The most defensible overall verdict is that **the USA handled Cuba well and Vietnam badly**, but the two are linked. The Bay of Pigs and Vienna made Kennedy determined to look firm and encouraged his deeper involvement in Vietnam; the success of October 1962 made American leaders believe that firm pressure and limited force could be controlled, which was the thinking behind Rolling Thunder and gradual escalation. The lesson of Cuba, applied to Vietnam, proved a misleading one.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'Kennedy's handling of the Cuban Missile Crisis was a success.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** Criteria: (1) did he get the missiles removed without war (outcome); (2) quality of his decisions (options, restraint); (3) how far the crisis was his own making; (4) the longer-term results. Support: quarantine, ignoring LeMay, firmness and a way out, hot line and Test Ban Treaty. Challenge: Bay of Pigs and Mongoose, the Jupiters, the risk, the secret deal, luck. Judgement: a success in the management and the outcome, qualified by the causes and the luck.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Kennedy's handling of the Cuban Missile Crisis was a success. The Russians put missiles in Cuba and Kennedy found out from a U-2 plane. He set up a blockade and Khrushchev backed down. This stopped a nuclear war. However, some people say it was dangerous and Kennedy secretly agreed to remove missiles from Turkey. So Kennedy did well but there were some problems."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with a balance of sorts, so Table B Level 3 at best. But it is a **narrative with a "however"**: there are no criteria, no dates, no names and no explanation of *why* the handling was good or bad. The last sentence is the "bit of both" that a Level 3 answer often settles for. Table A Level 3 (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply criteria and weigh.** "If success means getting the missiles removed without war, Kennedy succeeded: on 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed to dismantle them, and the USA had not fired a shot. His decisions also stand up to examination. The Joint Chiefs, with General LeMay the most forceful, wanted an air strike and an invasion, which would probably have killed Soviet troops and risked war. Kennedy chose the quarantine of 24 October, a graduated step that gave Khrushchev time to think, and on 27 October he accepted the first of Khrushchev's two letters and ignored the second, which demanded the Jupiter missiles in Turkey. But the outcome was less clear-cut than that. Kennedy privately promised through Robert Kennedy that the Jupiters would be removed, and they went in April 1963, so Khrushchev did get something."

*Commentary.* Now there are two criteria (outcome and decision-making) and the knowledge is used to test them, with dates and names. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. It still needs a third criterion (the causes of the crisis), the risk, the longer-term results and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is largely valid, but it needs two qualifications: it is a better description of how Kennedy managed the crisis than of how he handled Cuba as a whole, and it depends on luck as well as judgement. I shall judge success by four criteria: the outcome, the quality of the decisions, how far the crisis was Kennedy's own making, and the longer-term results.

On the outcome, the crisis was a success. The Soviet missiles were removed: the quarantine began on 24 October, Khrushchev announced the withdrawal on 28 October, and the USA gave only a pledge not to invade Cuba, conditional on UN inspection that Castro never accepted. There was no war, although American forces were preparing an air strike and invasion and Soviet commanders in Cuba held tactical nuclear weapons.

On the quality of the decisions, Kennedy deserves much credit. Within days of the U-2 photographs of 14 October he had assembled ExComm and, unlike the Joint Chiefs, who recommended an air strike and invasion, he settled on a limited, reversible step, the quarantine, which he called by that name because a blockade was an act of war. His speech of 22 October combined firmness (any missile launched from Cuba would require 'a full retaliatory response upon the Soviet Union') with room for a settlement. On 27 October, with the U-2 shot down over Cuba and a second, harder letter from Khrushchev demanding the Jupiters in Turkey, he accepted the terms of the first letter and used Robert Kennedy to tell Dobrynin that the Jupiters would go. The lessons of the Bay of Pigs, where he had trusted military and CIA advice, show in this care. The historian Graham Allison used the crisis in *Essence of Decision* (1971) to show how a government's choices are shaped by its organisations; yet the ExComm tapes show Kennedy repeatedly pushing back against pressure for force.

The first qualification is the causes. The crisis did not come from nowhere. Kennedy's own policies had made Khrushchev think the missiles were needed: the Bay of Pigs of April 1961 and Operation Mongoose suggested another invasion; the USA had put 15 Jupiter missiles in Turkey; and the USA's lead in long-range missiles was large. A leader who helps to cause a crisis cannot be given all the credit for ending it. The second is the risk and the secrecy. The deal on the Jupiters was kept secret, so that the public saw only an American victory, and luck played a part: neither leader knew of the Soviet submarine B-59 or of the tactical nuclear weapons in Cuba. As for the longer term, the results were good: the hot line (20 June 1963), the Partial Test Ban Treaty (5 August 1963) and the start of détente. But Castro survived, the Soviet Union began a build-up of long-range missiles to reach parity, and Khrushchev's humiliation was one step to his fall in October 1964.

Overall, then, Kennedy's handling of the crisis itself was a success by outcome and by method, and the picture of a cautious leader under pressure, as in Dallek's biography, is justified. But as a judgement on his policy towards Cuba from 1961 to 1962, it is incomplete, for the policy that made the crisis likely was also his. The best conclusion is that he deserves high credit for the thirteen days and less for the twenty-one months of policy before them."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): four explicit criteria; the argument is balanced and the qualifications (the causes, the secrecy and luck) are weighed, not added; a named historian is used to support the argument; the conclusion is qualified and sustained. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates and facts (14, 22, 24, 27 and 28 October; the 15 Jupiters; 20 June and 5 August 1963; October 1964), selected for the argument and not recited. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 650 words, 35 to 40 minutes.
:::

*Commentary.* True but undeveloped: the causes are named, not explained, and there is no weighing, no dates, no figures and no names. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 1–2.

**Moving to Level 4: explain how each cause worked and compare.** "The most important reason was the loss of political support at home, and the turning point was the Tet Offensive of 30–31 January 1968. To reach Level 5 it needs a counter-weight (Nixon's strategy and the South Vietnamese factor), a clear criterion and a judgement tested against the alternatives.

**A Level 5 conclusion.**

"Overall, the reasons for the withdrawal were linked, but they were not equal. I distinguish the *cause of the decision to leave* from the *cause of the manner of leaving*. The cause of the decision was the loss of the political will to continue escalation. This was produced by the stalemate on the battlefield, which Tet exposed, and by a public of whom, by August 1968, a majority (53 per cent in Gallup's poll) thought the war a mistake, helped by rising casualties, the draft and the anti-war movement, whose Moratorium of 15 October 1969 was among the largest protests in American history. The media and My Lai added to this, but Hallin's argument that television followed the divisions in Washington suggests that they were more a channel than a cause. The manner of leaving was Nixon's: Vietnamisation, from the first withdrawal of 25,000 on 8 June 1969, cut American troops from about 543,000 in April 1969 to about 24,000 by the end of 1972, which reduced casualties and the draft and so the opposition at home, while the opening to China and the USSR and the bombing of December 1972 were meant to force Hanoi to the Paris Accords of 27 January 1973 and to give 'peace with honour'. I judge the loss of political support after Tet the most important reason, because without it Nixon would have faced no pressure to withdraw at all; and the weakness of South Vietnam the reason why the withdrawal ended in the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975, for no amount of Vietnamisation could supply the legitimacy that its government lacked."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies a clear scheme (the decision and the manner), makes an argued judgement, and links the answer to the outcome. With paragraphs developing each cause, this is Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the effectiveness of US tactics in South Vietnam in the years 1965–68." [30]

A student wrote:

"The US tactics in Vietnam were not effective. The Americans had lots of bombs and napalm, but the Viet Cong hid in the jungle and the Americans could not find them. Rolling Thunder did not work either. America also used Agent Orange. In the Tet Offensive the Viet Cong attacked everywhere. So the Americans lost the war."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the paragraph asserts failure but does not say what "effective" means; it does not weigh the strengths of the tactics (the USA won the battles it chose) against their weaknesses; and it jumps to "lost the war", which is a different question and a different year (1975). Tet is used as evidence for 1965–68 tactics but its military outcome (a defeat for the communists) is not mentioned. Table B Level 2–3 (5–7): relevant but without dates, figures or names.

**The fix.** Use the same facts to prove an argument. "If effectiveness means winning battles, the tactics succeeded: under General Westmoreland's strategy of attrition, with search-and-destroy operations supported by helicopters and B-52s, the Americans won the great battles, for instance at the Ia Drang valley in November 1965, and in the Tet Offensive of 30–31 January 1968 the communists lost tens of thousands of fighters. But if effectiveness means achieving the aim of an independent, secure South Vietnam, the tactics failed. The enemy could replace its losses and choose when to fight, using the Ho Chi Minh Trail; Operation Rolling Thunder, begun on 2 March 1965, did not break the North's will; and the use of napalm and defoliants (nearly 19 million gallons of herbicides were sprayed) turned villagers against the USA." Now every fact is evidence for a judgement, with the criterion stated.

Q: Why did Kennedy regard Castro's Cuba as a threat?
A: It was a Soviet ally ninety miles from Florida, in breach of the Monroe Doctrine; its example might inspire revolution in Latin America (hence the Alliance for Progress of March 1961); and Kennedy had campaigned against the Republicans for being soft on Cuba.
Q: Give three reasons why the Bay of Pigs invasion failed, and its results.
A: The plan assumed an uprising that did not come; the landing site was a swamp, far from the mountains; the secret had leaked and Kennedy cancelled the second air strike and refused to send US forces. About 114 exiles were killed and more than 1,100 captured (released in December 1962 for about $53 million of food and medicine). Castro was strengthened and moved closer to the USSR.
Q: Why did Khrushchev put missiles in Cuba?
A: To defend Cuba against an expected American attack; to correct the nuclear imbalance and answer the 15 Jupiter missiles in Turkey; to improve his bargaining position over Berlin; and to answer China's criticism that he was weak.
Q: What options did ExComm consider, and which did Kennedy choose?
A: Doing nothing or diplomacy, a secret approach, an air strike, an invasion and a naval blockade. He chose the "quarantine", announced on 22 October 1962 and begun on 24 October.
Q: How did the crisis end (26–28 October)?
A: Khrushchev wrote privately on 26 October offering to remove the missiles if the USA promised not to invade Cuba; on 27 October a second public letter demanded the removal of the Jupiters from Turkey; Kennedy accepted the first and ignored the second, but Robert Kennedy told Dobrynin the Jupiters would be removed in due course; Khrushchev announced the withdrawal on 28 October.
Q: Give three outcomes of the crisis for relations between the USA and the USSR.
A: A hot line (20 June 1963); the Partial Test Ban Treaty (5 August 1963); the beginning of détente. Also Khrushchev's loss of prestige and fall in October 1964, and a Soviet missile build-up.
Q: What were the Geneva Accords and why did the USA support Diem?
A: They divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel on 21 July 1954, with elections planned for 1956. The USA, believing in the domino theory and containment, did not sign but backed Diem, who refused the elections, as a non-Communist leader.
Q: What happened in the Gulf of Tonkin and why did it matter?
A: On 2 August 1964 the USS *Maddox* was attacked by North Vietnamese torpedo boats; a second attack reported on 4 August almost certainly did not happen. Congress passed the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution on 7 August (416 to 0 in the House, 88 to 2 in the Senate), which gave Johnson the authority to wage war without a declaration.
Q: What were Operation Rolling Thunder and "search and destroy"?
A: Rolling Thunder was the sustained bombing of North Vietnam from 2 March 1965 to November 1968. Search and destroy was Westmoreland's tactic of sending large units, with helicopters and artillery, to find and kill the enemy as part of a strategy of attrition. Neither broke Hanoi's will.
Q: Why was the Tet Offensive so important?
A: It was a military defeat for the communists but a political shock in the USA: the public had been told victory was near. Westmoreland asked for 206,000 more men, the New Hampshire primary showed opposition to Johnson, and on 31 March 1968 Johnson limited the bombing and withdrew from the race.
Q: What was Vietnamisation, and what were its results?
A: Nixon's policy of building the South Vietnamese army to take over the fighting while US troops withdrew (from 8 June 1969). US troops fell from about 543,000 (April 1969) to about 24,000 (end of 1972); the South Vietnamese army held in 1972 with US air power but collapsed in 1975.
Q: What did the Paris Peace Accords of 27 January 1973 provide, and what happened in 1975?
A: A ceasefire, the withdrawal of US forces within sixty days, the return of US prisoners and political talks; North Vietnamese forces stayed in the South. The ceasefire broke down, Congress cut aid, and on 30 April 1975 Saigon fell.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Tet Offensive was the main reason for the failure of US policy in Vietnam.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate the military result of Tet from its political effect.
H: Consider the weakness of South Vietnam, the failure of the tactics and Congress.
A: Indicative content. Support: the shock to opinion; Westmoreland's request for 206,000 men; the New Hampshire primary; Johnson's announcement of 31 March 1968; the start of talks; the end of escalation. Challenge: the failure of attrition and bombing before Tet; the weakness of South Vietnam; the policies of Nixon; the cuts in aid and the War Powers Resolution (1973); the collapse of 1975. Level 5 distinguishes the *turning point* (Tet) from the *causes of defeat* and judges that Tet decided that the USA would leave, but not that the South would fall.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Nixon's policy in Vietnam was a success.
H: Take Nixon's aims (peace with honour, a surviving South Vietnam) and judge him against them.
H: Consider the costs: Cambodia, Kent State, the bombing.
A: Indicative content. Support: US troops fell from about 543,000 to about 24,000 and the draft ended; the American prisoners returned; the Accords of 27 January 1973; South Vietnam survived until 1975 and held in 1972; the opening to China and the USSR. Challenge: 1969–73 saw more bombing and the invasion of Cambodia (30 April 1970); Kent State; the Accords left North Vietnamese troops in the South; the fall of Saigon (30 April 1975); the Watergate collapse and the cuts in aid. Level 5 judges that Nixon achieved withdrawal with a "decent interval" but not peace with honour.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The USA's failure in Vietnam was due to the way it fought the war and not to the reasons it entered it.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate causes of entry (the domino theory, Diem, Tonkin) from the methods of fighting (attrition, bombing, the draft, limited aims).
H: Consider the weakness of the ally, which belongs to both.
A: Support: the strategy of attrition and the lack of a measure of progress other than the body count; the gradual escalation; the bombing of the North and the ground war in the South; the decision not to call up the reserves; the damage done to the villagers; a war fought with conscripts. Challenge: the original judgement that South Vietnam was vital, and that a non-Communist state could be built there with force, was flawed (Herring, Logevall); a weak and unpopular ally; Hanoi's determination and Chinese and Soviet aid. A* judgement says that the two cannot be separated: the aims could not be achieved by any method that the American public would support.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Cuban Missile Crisis taught the USA the wrong lessons for Vietnam.' Evaluate this view.
H: Think about what American leaders concluded from October 1962: the use of graduated pressure and of limited force.
H: Compare Khrushchev and Hanoi as opponents.
A: Support: the belief that gradual, controlled pressure could make an enemy back down was used in Rolling Thunder; Johnson and McNamara, veterans of ExComm, applied it to an opponent with different interests and stronger will; Hanoi was fighting for its own country, unlike Khrushchev, who was defending a distant ally and a balance. Challenge: the crisis also taught caution and restraint (the quarantine, the hot line), which showed in Johnson's refusal to invade the North; the decision to enter Vietnam was based on the domino theory, which came before 1962. A* judgement says that the crisis made leaders over-confident in their ability to manage escalation, but that the main causes of the failure lay elsewhere.
:::

:::recap
- **Criteria**: aims and results, judgement and alternatives, costs and risks, consequences.
- **Cuba before the crisis**: Castro (1 January 1959); relations broken 3 January 1961; Alliance for Progress (March 1961); Bay of Pigs (17–19 April 1961; about 1,400 exiles; about 114 killed; more than 1,100 captured; ransomed December 1962 for about $53 million); Operation Mongoose (November 1961).
- **The crisis**: U-2 photographs 14 October 1962; ExComm; five options; quarantine announced 22 October, begun 24 October; letters of 26 and 27 October; Jupiters in Turkey; Khrushchev's announcement 28 October.
- **Outcomes**: hot line (20 June 1963); Partial Test Ban Treaty (5 August 1963); Khrushchev's fall (October 1964); Soviet missile build-up; Castro survives.
- **Vietnam to 1963**: Geneva Accords (21 July 1954); Diem; domino theory (7 April 1954); SEATO; about 900 advisers (1960) to about 16,300 (1963); Strategic Hamlets; Buddhist crisis; coup (1 November 1963).
- **Escalation**: Tonkin (2 and 4 August 1964; Resolution 7 August; 416–0 and 88–2); Pleiku (7 February 1965); Rolling Thunder (2 March 1965); Marines (8 March 1965); 125,000 troops (28 July 1965); about 184,000 by the end of 1965; attrition and body counts; napalm and Agent Orange; Ho Chi Minh Trail.
- **Withdrawal**: Tet (30–31 January 1968); 206,000 request; New Hampshire (12 March 1968); Johnson (31 March 1968); My Lai (16 March 1968; public November 1969); Gallup 53 per cent "mistake" (August 1968); Vietnamisation (8 June 1969); troops about 543,000 (April 1969) to about 24,000 (end of 1972); Cambodia (30 April 1970); Kent State (4 May 1970); Paris Accords (27 January 1973); last troops (29 March 1973); Saigon falls (30 April 1975).
- **Judgement**: Cuba handled well but partly caused by Kennedy's own policies; Vietnam a failure of aims and method, with Tet as the turning point and Nixon's strategy shaping how the USA left.
