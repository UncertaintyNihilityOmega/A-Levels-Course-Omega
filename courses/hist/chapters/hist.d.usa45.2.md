## The candidate and the party

### Who Eisenhower was

Dwight David Eisenhower was born on 14 October 1890 in Texas and brought up in Abilene, Kansas, one of six sons of a poor family. He graduated from the West Point military academy in 1915 and spent the next twenty-five years as a staff officer. In 1942 General George Marshall made him commander of American forces in Europe, and he commanded the Allied invasions of North Africa and Italy and, as Supreme Commander, the landings in Normandy on 6 June 1944. After the war he was Army Chief of Staff, president of Columbia University, and from 1950 the first commander of NATO's forces in Europe. He had never held elected office and had not joined a political party. Both parties had tried to recruit him in 1948.

He decided in 1952 that he would run as a Republican, partly out of a belief that the party's isolationist wing under Senator Robert Taft, who had opposed the NATO treaty, would harm American security. At the Republican convention in Chicago in July 1952 he narrowly beat Taft for the nomination on the first ballot, after his managers won a dispute over contested Southern delegations ("Fair Play"). Richard Nixon, a 39-year-old senator from California known for his part in the Hiss case, was chosen as his running mate to please the party's right.

### What kind of Republican?

Eisenhower's political outlook was conservative in money and moderate in everything else. He believed in balanced budgets, a limited role for the federal government and a free economy, but he did not wish to undo the New Deal, and he disliked the idea that a President should denounce his opponents. He called his approach "Modern Republicanism" or, in other moods, "dynamic conservatism". The syllabus uses the first phrase.

:::def Modern Republicanism
Eisenhower's name for a middle course in domestic policy: to keep and run efficiently the main programmes of the New Deal and Fair Deal (Social Security, a minimum wage, public housing), to extend some of them cautiously, to balance the budget where possible, and to leave the rest to private enterprise and the states. Conservative Republicans in Congress, such as Senator William Knowland, thought it a betrayal of the party; liberal Democrats thought it too timid.
:::

## The reasons for Eisenhower's election victories

### The election of 1952

In 1952 Eisenhower beat the Democrat Adlai Stevenson, the Governor of Illinois, by a landslide. The result was 442 electoral votes to 89, with about 34.1 million popular votes (55.2 per cent) to about 27.4 million (44.3 per cent). He won 39 of the 48 states, including four states from the Democratic "Solid South" (Texas, Florida, Tennessee and Virginia) and the large states of the North and West. The Republicans also won narrow control of both houses of Congress, which they lost again in 1954.

The reasons fall into five groups, which are those the syllabus names.

**Personal appeal.** Eisenhower was a war hero who had never seemed to want power, and with his broad smile and plain manner he appeared above party. His slogan "I like Ike" was simple and friendly, and it worked for people who knew little about policy. Voters trusted him to deal with the world as a soldier who knew the realities of war. Stevenson, by contrast, was a witty and elegant speaker whose speeches were admired by intellectuals, and he was called an "egghead" by opponents. He was also a divorced man at a time when this counted against a candidate. His reluctance to run, which Democrats first took as modesty, left some voters wondering whether he wanted the job.

**Use of television.** The 1952 election was the first in which television mattered. About 9 per cent of American homes had a set in 1950, and by the end of the decade nearly nine in ten did, so millions saw the campaign for the first time. Eisenhower's campaign used it as no earlier campaign had. The advertising agent Rosser Reeves produced short filmed spots, called "Eisenhower Answers America", in which the candidate gave brief answers to questions from "ordinary" citizens, and an animated cartoon with a song by Irving Berlin turned "I like Ike" into a jingle. Stevenson's campaign, which regarded television commercials as undignified, made little use of it. Television also served the other side of the Republican ticket: on 23 September 1952 Nixon, accused by newspapers of having an $18,000 secret fund from rich supporters, made a half-hour speech on live television to an audience of about 60 million, in which he said that the only gift he had kept was a cocker spaniel puppy named Checkers that his daughters loved. Eisenhower was ready to drop him, but the "Checkers speech" brought a flood of messages of support, and Nixon stayed.

**Anti-Communism.** The Republicans said that the Democrats had been soft on Communism, with Truman's China policy and the Hiss case as examples, and they promised a firmer line. The party's slogan was "Korea, Communism and Corruption" ("K1C2"). Senator McCarthy attacked "twenty years of treason" and Nixon, in his campaign speeches, tied Stevenson to Truman's policy and to Hiss, whom Stevenson had once given a character reference. Eisenhower himself kept clear of the harshest language (but he refrained from defending Marshall in Wisconsin, as the previous chapter explained). A voter worried about Communism could choose a general who had stood against the Soviets in Europe.

**The promise to resolve the Korean War.** The war that began in June 1950 had been in stalemate since 1951, with peace talks that dragged on, and the casualty lists grew. On 24 October 1952 in Detroit Eisenhower said, "I shall go to Korea". It offered no plan, but it said what voters wanted to hear, that a soldier would end the war, and many saw it as the decisive moment of the campaign. He visited Korea in early December 1952 and an armistice was signed on 27 July 1953, after Stalin's death on 5 March.

**The limitations of the Democrats.** The Democrats had held the White House for twenty years and voters were tired of them: "time for a change" was an appealing argument. Truman was unpopular because of Korea, inflation and the charges of corruption in his administration (the so-called "mess in Washington", involving gifts to officials and the tax service). Truman did not run for another term, and Stevenson, who had been drafted as a compromise at the convention, could neither defend Truman's record nor attack it. The South, angry at the Democratic civil rights stand, was less loyal than it had been.

:::key Why Eisenhower won in 1952
1. **Personal appeal**: war hero, "I like Ike", above party; Stevenson an "egghead" and divorced.
2. **Television**: the "Eisenhower Answers America" spots and cartoon; the Checkers speech (23 September) kept Nixon on the ticket.
3. **Anti-Communism**: "Korea, Communism and Corruption"; "twenty years of treason"; the Hiss case and the loss of China.
4. **The promise to end the Korean War**: "I shall go to Korea" (24 October).
5. **Democratic weakness**: twenty years in office, an unpopular Truman, scandals and a candidate who could not defend or attack the record.
:::

### The election of 1956

In 1956 Eisenhower beat Stevenson again, by a larger margin. He won 457 electoral votes to 73 (one Alabama elector cast a vote for someone else), 41 states to seven, and about 35.6 million popular votes (57.4 per cent) to about 26.0 million (42.0 per cent). The Democrats, however, kept control of both houses of Congress, so that he was a popular President without a party majority. Louisiana was the only Deep South state he won.

The syllabus lists the reasons.

**Handling of Suez and Hungary.** In the last weeks of the campaign two foreign crises burst out. On 23 October a rising in Hungary against Soviet control began, and on 4 November Soviet tanks crushed it. On 29 October Israel invaded Egypt, and on 31 October Britain and France, which had planned the attack with Israel, began bombing Egyptian airfields to seize the Suez Canal, which President Nasser had nationalised on 26 July. Eisenhower opposed the invasion, working with the United Nations and bringing financial pressure on Britain, and a ceasefire took effect on 6 November, the day of the election. The crises did two things. They made voters rally round the President in a time of danger ("this is no time to change leaders"), and they let him claim credit for keeping America out of war. Stevenson's call to end the draft and to end nuclear testing by treaty seemed less convincing while the world seemed to be on the brink.

**Support from African Americans.** Eisenhower won nearly 40 per cent of the Black vote in 1956, a large share for a Republican since the New Deal. The reasons were the Supreme Court's decision in *Brown* (and the fact that he had appointed Earl Warren), his desegregation of the armed forces and of Washington, DC, the civil rights bill which his Attorney General Herbert Brownell had proposed in 1956, the endorsement of Congressman Adam Clayton Powell of Harlem, and Stevenson's caution on civil rights, which was to avoid alienating the South. The vote had not made a difference to the outcome, since he won in a landslide, but it shows that civil rights, even in the mid-1950s, was a factor in national politics.

**Personal appeal.** The man was the same, and perhaps more so. A heart attack on 24 September 1955 and an operation for ileitis on 9 June 1956 made voters wonder if he was fit to serve, but he recovered, and his decision to stand again was welcomed by his supporters. His handling of the crisis in the Middle East made him look wise in matters of war and peace.

**The prosperity of the 1950s.** Unemployment was low (about 4 per cent), incomes were rising, the stock market was rising, and the Republican campaign claimed "Peace, Progress and Prosperity". The Korean War was over, and no young Americans were fighting. The "affluent society" (a phrase later used by the economist John Kenneth Galbraith) was real for most white families, and voters did not want to risk it.

**Democrat opposition.** The Democrats nominated Stevenson again, with Senator Estes Kefauver of Tennessee as his running mate, after a bruising primary contest in which Kefauver had beaten him in Minnesota. Stevenson proposed more spending on welfare, an end to the draft and a test-ban treaty with the Soviet Union, but he could not show that conditions required a change, and the 1952 argument "time for a change" did not apply to a peaceful and prosperous country. A President who stood above party was hard to attack.

## Eisenhower's domestic policies: Modern Republicanism

### Social Security and welfare

Eisenhower did not try to abolish the New Deal. In a private letter of 8 November 1954 to his brother Edgar (adapted here), he wrote that any party that tried to abolish Social Security, unemployment insurance, labour laws and farm programmes would not be heard of again in American politics. His government did what that letter suggests: it extended Social Security in 1954, bringing millions more workers, including many farm and self-employed workers, into the scheme and raising benefits, and in 1956 it added **disability insurance** for workers aged 50 to 64 and allowed women to retire on a reduced pension at 62. It also continued unemployment insurance.

### The Department of Health, Education and Welfare

A new Cabinet department was created in April 1953 (by Reorganization Plan No. 1 of that year, approved on 1 April), combining the Federal Security Agency's functions in health, education and social insurance. Its first Secretary was **Oveta Culp Hobby**, a Texas newspaper publisher who had led the Women's Army Corps in the war, only the second woman to hold a Cabinet post. She resigned in 1955 after problems with the distribution of the new Salk polio vaccine. HEW gave welfare a place at the Cabinet table and was later the department that carried out civil rights policy in schools. It was an example of Modern Republicanism: the government of the New Deal was kept, but run in an orderly way.

### Low-cost housing

The Housing Act of 1949 had promised 810,000 public housing units. Eisenhower's government did not scrap the programme, but it built much less than Truman wanted, and the Housing Act of 1954 changed the emphasis from slum clearance to **urban renewal**, with more help for private building and repairs and for mortgage insurance through the Federal Housing Administration (FHA). Housing policy in the 1950s mainly helped the middle class to buy suburban homes, because FHA and the GI Bill's loans financed them. For poor and Black city dwellers there was less help, and urban renewal sometimes destroyed their neighbourhoods. Historians therefore tend to say that the Eisenhower years extended middle-class home ownership more than they provided "low-cost housing".

### The minimum wage

In August 1955 Congress, with Eisenhower's support, raised the minimum wage from 75 cents to $1 an hour from March 1956, a rise of one-third. The minimum wage covered the same workers as before, so that agricultural and domestic workers were not covered.

### Infrastructure: the Interstate Highway programme

The **Federal-Aid Highway Act** of 29 June 1956 created a National System of Interstate and Defense Highways of 41,000 miles of fast roads between cities. Congress authorised about $25 billion, with the federal government paying 90 per cent of the cost and the states 10 per cent, paid for from a **Highway Trust Fund** filled by taxes on petrol (raised from 2 to 3 cents a gallon), tyres and lorries. Eisenhower had been interested in roads since he took part in the Army's transcontinental motor convoy of 1919, which took 62 days to cover 3,200 miles (about 52 miles a day), and he had been impressed by the German autobahns in 1945. His arguments for it were economic (trade and travel), safety and defence (the roads would allow quick evacuation of cities and movement of troops in a nuclear war), and the Act's full title included "Defense". It became the largest public works programme in American history, costing far more than the first estimate, and it changed the country. It helped to spread suburbs and to move shops and jobs out of the cities, and it harmed railroads and city centres, since many highways were routed through Black and poor neighbourhoods.

### Balanced budgets and fiscal policy

Eisenhower believed that the government should not spend more than it raised. His Treasury Secretary was George Humphrey, a businessman, and his first Budget Director was Joseph Dodge. He balanced the budget in only three fiscal years (1956, 1957 and 1960, with surpluses of about $3.9 billion, $3.4 billion and $0.3 billion), and the deficits of 1958 (about $2.8 billion) and 1959 (about $12.8 billion) were large, because recessions reduced tax revenue and raised spending. The three recessions of his presidency (July 1953 to May 1954, August 1957 to April 1958, and April 1960 to February 1961) tested his policy. Unemployment peaked at about 7.5 per cent in 1958, the highest of his terms. He did not cut taxes or begin large public works programmes in response, relying instead on the "automatic stabilisers" (unemployment insurance and falling tax receipts), on the Federal Reserve cutting interest rates, and on the highways programme and defence spending, which were already going on. Critics, among them Democrats and some economists, said that this made the recessions longer and that the 1960 recession hurt Nixon's campaign. Supporters said that it kept inflation low (generally under 3 per cent a year) and the dollar safe.

### Concerns about the military-industrial complex

Eisenhower was the most military of Presidents, yet he was uneasy about the cost of defence. His "New Look" defence policy of 1953–54 cut the size of the army and relied on nuclear weapons to deter the Soviet Union, because they were cheaper than keeping large conventional forces. But defence still took more than half of federal spending in 1960. After the Soviet Sputnik satellite in October 1957 there was pressure from Congress and the press for more spending on missiles, and he resisted. In his farewell address on 17 January 1961 he told the American people that "in the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex." He said the combination of a huge military establishment and a large arms industry was new in American experience, and that its total influence was felt in every city and every state house. A President who had been a general was warning that the army and its suppliers could distort democracy.

:::def Military-industrial complex
Eisenhower's phrase, in his farewell address of 17 January 1961, for the close relationship between the armed forces, arms manufacturers and their supporters in Congress, which he feared might gain "unwarranted influence" over government policy and weigh on the nation's finances.
:::

:::key Eisenhower's domestic record: the main points
- **Modern Republicanism**: kept the New Deal, extended Social Security (1954 and 1956, with disability insurance), a minimum wage of $1 (from March 1956), a new Department of Health, Education and Welfare (April 1953).
- **Housing**: the 1954 Act moved from public housing and slum clearance to urban renewal and private mortgages.
- **Highways**: the 1956 Act (41,000 miles, about $25 billion, a Highway Trust Fund, 90 per cent federal) was his biggest achievement at home.
- **Money**: three balanced budgets (1956, 1957, 1960); three recessions; unemployment about 7.5 per cent at the peak (1958); low inflation.
- **Limits**: a cautious approach to the recessions; less help for poor city dwellers; no leadership on civil rights; a Congress controlled by Democrats from 1955 and by large Democratic majorities after 1958.
- **The warning**: the military-industrial complex (17 January 1961).
:::

### How effective was he at home?

The case that he was effective is that he governed a peaceful and prosperous country for eight years without a depression, inflated currency or social upheaval, kept the welfare state while cutting its cost, and finished the Interstate Highways. The case against is that he left unsolved the problems of poverty, the decay of the cities and race, and that he was slow to respond to recessions. Another criticism, which Democrats made, is that he did little to use the government's power while the country was rich, so that by 1960 it was under-investing in schools, cities and public services. The strongest comparison is with Truman, whose Fair Deal promised more and delivered less: Eisenhower promised little and delivered it. That, perhaps, is the sense of the phrase "Modern Republicanism".

:::note The historians' debate on Eisenhower's leadership (stretch: useful for A*)
In the 1950s and 1960s many commentators thought Eisenhower a weak and passive President. Richard Neustadt's *Presidential Power* (1960) contrasted his style with Franklin Roosevelt's, and critics said that he preferred the golf course to the Oval Office. From the 1970s, as his private papers were opened, historians revised this view.
- **Fred Greenstein**, *The Hidden-Hand Presidency: Eisenhower as Leader* (1982), argued that Eisenhower was a skilled and active leader who deliberately hid his political role, working through others and letting them take the blame, and that behind the cheerful public face was a calculating organiser. The title gave the revision its name.
- **Stephen Ambrose**, *Eisenhower* (two volumes, 1983–84), a full and mainly favourable biography, presented him as a capable President with a record of peace and prosperity, though Ambrose thought that he should have done more on race.
- **Taylor Branch**, *Parting the Waters: America in the King Years 1954–63* (1988), concentrates on the civil rights movement and its leaders, and shows Eisenhower as a President who was reluctant to lead and responded only when the movement forced him to.
- **Jim Newton**, *Eisenhower: The White House Years* (2011), argues in the tradition of the revisionists that Eisenhower was a careful and effective President who has been underrated, with a stronger grasp of events than he showed in public.
- **For an essay**: the question is whether "effective" means doing what he set out to do (a peaceful, balanced, prosperous government, in which he succeeded), or doing what the country needed (leadership on civil rights and poverty, in which he did not).
:::

## The development of the civil rights movement and the response of the federal government

### The beginning: the NAACP and the courts

By 1950 the **NAACP** (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People), led in its legal work by Thurgood Marshall of its Legal Defense Fund, had been fighting segregation in the courts for more than a decade. It had decided to attack the "separate but equal" doctrine of *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) step by step. In 1950 the Supreme Court ruled that Black students must be admitted to a state law school in Texas (*Sweatt v. Painter*) and must not be segregated within a university (*McLaurin v. Oklahoma*). It then took five cases about school segregation, from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware and the District of Columbia, to the Court under the name of the first, the Topeka case.

:::def Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka
The Supreme Court decision of 17 May 1954 in which Chief Justice Earl Warren, speaking for a unanimous Court (9–0), held that segregation in public education was unconstitutional: "Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." It overturned the "separate but equal" doctrine of *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) as far as schools were concerned. A second ruling in 1955 (*Brown II*, 31 May) told the states to desegregate "with all deliberate speed".
:::

Eisenhower had appointed Earl Warren, a former Republican Governor of California, as Chief Justice in October 1953, after the death of Fred Vinson. Eisenhower is reported to have said privately that he regretted the choice, in part because of the *Brown* decision. He neither praised nor criticised the ruling in public. He said that he would obey the law, and he is reported to have feared in private that the decision would set back progress in the South by years. His reluctance to say that the decision was right gave Southern resistance room.

### Resistance and violence

The reaction in the South was fierce. White Citizens' Councils, begun in Mississippi in July 1954, used economic pressure against Black people who wanted to register to vote or send their children to white schools. In March 1956 101 Southern members of Congress (19 senators and 82 representatives) signed the **Southern Manifesto**, which called the ruling an abuse of judicial power and encouraged resistance. Several states announced a policy of "massive resistance". Alabama banned the NAACP in 1956. By the mid-1950s very few Black children in the South attended schools with white children, and ten years after *Brown* only about 1 per cent did.

The murder of **Emmett Till** shocked the country. Till, a 14-year-old Black boy from Chicago, was visiting relatives in Money, Mississippi, in August 1955, and was abducted and killed on 28 August after being accused of offending a white woman in a store. His mother, Mamie Till-Bradley, decided to hold an open coffin at his funeral in Chicago, so that people could see what had been done to him, and *Jet* magazine published the photograph. An all-white jury acquitted the two men who were charged on 23 September 1955 after a trial that lasted a few days, and they later admitted in a magazine interview that they had done it. The case made many Black Americans, and Northern white Americans, angry about the South, and it affected a generation of young activists.

### Rosa Parks and the Montgomery Bus Boycott

On 1 December 1955 in Montgomery, Alabama, **Rosa Parks**, a 42-year-old seamstress and secretary of the local NAACP, refused to give up her seat on a bus to a white passenger and was arrested. She was not the first to refuse: a teenager, Claudette Colvin, had been arrested for the same act in March 1955. But Parks was respected, and the Women's Political Council, led by the English professor Jo Ann Robinson, and the NAACP's E.D. Nixon decided that this was the case to use. On 5 December, the day of Parks's trial, Black people stayed off the buses. They formed the **Montgomery Improvement Association (MIA)** under a new young minister at the Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, Martin Luther King Jr, who was 26.

The boycott, which involved about 40,000 Black bus riders who walked or shared cars, lasted **381 days**, until 20 December 1956. The city's leaders arrested its organisers, and on 30 January 1956 King's house was bombed. The boycott's legal case, *Browder v. Gayle*, was brought by four Black women to challenge bus segregation, and a federal court decided on 5 June 1956 that it was unconstitutional. The Supreme Court upheld that decision on 13 November 1956 and the order to integrate the buses reached Montgomery on 20 December, so the boycott ended and the buses were integrated on 21 December.

Montgomery matters because it showed that a mass, peaceful, church-based movement could win, because it brought the economic pressure of Black consumers against segregation, and because it made King a national figure.

### King and the SCLC

King, drawing on the Christian idea of love and on Gandhi's method of non-violence, spoke of nonviolent direct action as a way to appeal to the conscience of the nation. In January 1957, after the Montgomery success, Black ministers from several Southern states, with King as their president, formed the **Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC)** in Atlanta (it took the name in August 1957) to organise protests across the South through the Black churches, with the help of Ella Baker and Bayard Rustin. On 17 May 1957, the third anniversary of *Brown*, King spoke at a Prayer Pilgrimage for Freedom at the Lincoln Memorial and called on the government to "give us the ballot", which was the main demand of the SCLC in the next years.

### Little Rock, 1957

The crisis at Little Rock was the first test of the federal government's will. Under the Court's order, Little Rock, Arkansas, planned to admit nine Black students to Central High School in September 1957. The Governor, **Orval Faubus**, said that there would be violence and on 2 September ordered the Arkansas National Guard to surround the school and stop them. On 4 September the nine, among them 15-year-old Elizabeth Eckford, who arrived alone and was cursed by a crowd, were turned away. A federal judge ordered the Guard removed, and on 23 September, after it had gone, a mob outside forced the students to leave the school. Eisenhower, who had met Faubus on 14 September in the hope of a settlement, acted. On 24 September he signed Executive Order 10730, putting the Arkansas National Guard under federal command, and sent about 1,000 paratroopers of the 101st Airborne Division from Fort Campbell, Kentucky, to protect the students. The nine entered Central High on 25 September under armed escort, and the paratroopers withdrew in November, while federalised Guardsmen stayed until the end of the school year. In a television address he said that mob rule could not be allowed to override the courts, and that the world was watching: Soviet propaganda made much of Little Rock.

Eisenhower acted not as a champion of integration but out of duty to uphold the law and out of concern for the country's reputation. In 1958 the Supreme Court in *Cooper v. Aaron* said that states could not defy its rulings. Faubus, however, closed Little Rock's high schools for the school year 1958–59, and integration was long delayed in the South.

### The Civil Rights Act of 1957

The Civil Rights Act of 9 September 1957 was the first civil rights legislation since Reconstruction (1875). It had been proposed in 1956 by the Eisenhower administration, with Attorney General Herbert Brownell, as a way to protect the right to vote. It created a **Civil Rights Division** in the Department of Justice and a **Commission on Civil Rights**, and it let the Attorney General seek court orders against those who obstructed Black people from voting. But it was weakened to get through the Senate, where the Majority Leader, Lyndon Johnson of Texas, led a compromise. A clause providing for jury trials in criminal contempt cases meant that all-white Southern juries could refuse to convict officials, and a part that would have allowed federal action on school desegregation was dropped. Senator Strom Thurmond spoke against the bill for 24 hours and 18 minutes, a record. The 1957 Act was weak, and few Black Southerners registered because of it, but it made civil rights a federal matter and created the Commission and the Division that would be used in the 1960s.

The Civil Rights Act of 6 May 1960 allowed federal judges to appoint referees to help Black citizens register and made it a crime to obstruct court orders by violence or to bomb buildings. It also was weak: it passed after a long Southern filibuster and gave few results.

### Sit-ins and SNCC

In the later 1950s Black students were becoming more impatient than their elders. On 1 February 1960 four students from North Carolina Agricultural and Technical College, Ezell Blair Jr, Franklin McCain, Joseph McNeil and David Richmond, sat down at the whites-only lunch counter of the Woolworth's store in Greensboro and asked to be served. They returned each day with more people. Within weeks sit-ins were taking place in dozens of cities in the South, with a discipline and courage that impressed millions of people watching on television. Woolworth's in Greensboro began to serve Black customers in July 1960.

In April 1960, at a conference of student activists at Shaw University in Raleigh, North Carolina, called by Ella Baker of the SCLC, the students formed the **Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC)**. Baker urged them to remain independent of the older organisations, and they did. SNCC would lead the voter registration drives and the Freedom Rides of the early 1960s. The sit-ins also showed that the movement was now more than lawyers and ministers: it had a new generation.

### How did the federal government respond?

:::key Why civil rights advanced in the 1950s (and why not faster)
- **The courts and the NAACP**: a long legal campaign led by Thurgood Marshall that produced *Brown* (1954).
- **Mass action**: Montgomery (381 days, 1955–56) and the sit-ins (1960) showed what direct action could do, and the SCLC (1957) and SNCC (1960) organised it.
- **Individuals**: Rosa Parks, King, Jo Ann Robinson, Daisy Bates and the Little Rock Nine, Ella Baker, and the Greensboro students.

### Eisenhower and civil rights: assessing the record

A fair assessment weighs his private views against his public acts. In private he doubted that laws could change attitudes, he feared that rapid change would cause violence, and he had friends in the South. In public he did not praise *Brown* or ask citizens to accept it, and he did not meet King until 1958. But he appointed Warren, Justice William Brennan (1956) and judges to the Southern federal courts who enforced the law, such as Elbert Tuttle and John Minor Wisdom on the Fifth Circuit; he completed the desegregation of the armed forces and of schools on military bases and of the District of Columbia; he sent a civil rights bill to Congress; and at Little Rock he used force against a state governor, the strongest federal action on race since Reconstruction. A balanced verdict is that he did what the law required of him and sometimes more, but gave little moral leadership, and that the advance of civil rights in the 1950s came more from the Court, the NAACP and ordinary Black Americans than from the White House.

## How effective was Eisenhower? Weighing the record

Questions on this key question may ask about one part of the record, or about "effectiveness" as a whole. This table gives a way to compare.

| Area | The case that he was effective | The case that he was not |
|---|---|---|
| Winning elections | Landslides in 1952 and 1956; popular above party | A Republican Congress only for two years; Democrats won a landslide in 1958 |
| Domestic policy | Peace and prosperity; kept the New Deal; Interstate Highways; Social Security extended; HEW | Recessions; unemployment of about 7.5 per cent in 1958; neglect of poverty and the cities; housing and race |
| Money | Three balanced budgets; low inflation | Deficits in five years; cautious response to recession |
| Civil rights | Warren; desegregation of the military and DC; Little Rock; the 1957 Act | No moral leadership; no public support for *Brown*; weak Acts |
| Leadership style | Greenstein's "hidden hand"; skilled organisation | Critics' picture of a passive President |

A strong answer starts by defining "effective": did he achieve what he set out to do (balanced, peaceful, prosperous government), or what the country needed (leadership on civil rights and the poor)? On the first test he was highly effective, on the second less so. A defensible judgement is that Eisenhower was very effective at what he chose to do, which was to run a stable government and keep the peace, and less effective where the country needed a President to lead against the grain of public opinion, as on race.

## Exam technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 4.2

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 4.2
1. **Define the criteria first.** For "Assess the reasons for Eisenhower's election victories": criteria could be *necessity* (would he have won without this?), *contribution* and *timing* (1952 or 1956?). For a question on civil rights: the *source of pressure* (courts, movement, federal government), *effect* and *limits*.
2. **Separate 1952 and 1956.** The reasons were different (Korea in 1952; Suez, Hungary and prosperity in 1956). A question on "his election victories" needs both.
3. **Use precise evidence**: 442–89 and 457–73; 23 September 1952; 24 October 1952; 17 May 1954; 381 days; 29 June 1956; 24 September 1957; 41,000 miles; $25 billion; 90 per cent; 7.5 per cent in 1958; 17 January 1961.
4. **Weigh Eisenhower's role on civil rights carefully.** Do not say that he did nothing or that he led the movement. Use the evidence of Warren, the military, Little Rock and the Acts, against his silence on *Brown*.
5. **Do not narrate.** Use the events as evidence for an argument about causes and effects.
6. **Judge.** End with a view that says which factor mattered most and why, and what would change it.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, and the total is out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'Eisenhower was a successful president in domestic affairs.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement that is partly substantiated; a good range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criteria: (1) did he achieve what he set out to do (Modern Republicanism: peace, prosperity, a balanced budget)? (2) did he meet the needs of the country, including the poor and Black Americans? (3) did it last?
- Success: kept the New Deal; Social Security extended (1954, 1956); HEW (April 1953); minimum wage to $1 (1956); Interstate Highways (29 June 1956, 41,000 miles); prosperity and low inflation; popularity (landslides in 1952 and 1956).
- Failure or limits: three recessions (unemployment about 7.5 per cent in 1958); balanced the budget only three times; less public housing; urban renewal and highways hurt poor city areas; no leadership on civil rights; the Democratic landslide of 1958; the military-industrial complex warning came only at the end.
- Judgement: successful by his own measure, less so by the country's needs.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Eisenhower was a successful President at home because there was prosperity in the 1950s and he was very popular. He built the Interstate Highways, which were a big help, and he did not get rid of the New Deal. People had jobs and cars. However, there were also some problems like the recessions and civil rights. Overall Eisenhower was quite successful."

*Commentary.* Relevant and broadly accurate, but general ("prosperity", "a big help", "some problems") with no dates, figures or names, and it credits Eisenhower with prosperity without showing what he did. The conclusion ("quite successful") restates the question. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 2 to 3 (about 6). To move up: precise evidence, causes, and criteria for "success".

**A Level 4 paragraph (one side explained in depth, no comparison).** "Eisenhower's domestic policies were a success because he kept what was popular in the New Deal and ran it efficiently. In 1954 Social Security was extended to millions more workers, and in 1956 disability insurance was added. In April 1953 the Department of Health, Education and Welfare was created, with Oveta Culp Hobby as its first Secretary, and in 1955 the minimum wage was raised from 75 cents to $1. His greatest achievement was the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 29 June 1956, which created 41,000 miles of Interstate roads, 90 per cent paid for by the federal government from a Highway Trust Fund. These measures show that Modern Republicanism was a real programme, and it helped him win 457 electoral votes in 1956."

*Commentary.* Precise, focused and well explained, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). But it deals with only one side and does not test "success" against anything: it does not mention the recessions, the limits of housing policy or the lack of action on poverty and race, so it does not reach a judgement. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs **balance, criteria and a judgement**.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"Success in domestic affairs can be tested in three ways: whether Eisenhower achieved what he set out to do, whether he met the needs of the country, and whether his achievements lasted. By his own aims, which were a peaceful, prosperous and orderly government that kept the New Deal but cost less, he was successful. By the needs of the country, which included Black Americans, the poor and the cities, he was much less so.

His own aims were met. Modern Republicanism promised to keep the popular parts of the New Deal and run them well, and it did. Social Security was extended in 1954 to millions of farm and self-employed workers, and in 1956 it gained disability insurance for workers over 50. The Department of Health, Education and Welfare, created in April 1953, put welfare in the Cabinet. The minimum wage rose by a third, to $1, in 1956. The Federal-Aid Highway Act of 29 June 1956 launched the biggest public works programme in American history, with 41,000 miles of road paid for from a Highway Trust Fund of petrol taxes, so that the cost fell on road users instead of the general budget. There was no depression, inflation stayed low and unemployment was about 4 per cent in the election year of 1956. This is why he won 457 electoral votes, and why the Republican alternative to the New Deal that Taft's wing wanted, a rollback, was never tried. The programmes also lasted: the highways, Social Security and HEW all survived him.

Against this stands the question of what was left undone. The three recessions of 1953–54, 1957–58 and 1960–61 were met by waiting for the automatic stabilisers to work, and unemployment reached about 7.5 per cent in 1958; the budget, which he wanted to balance, was in surplus only in 1956, 1957 and 1960, and the deficit of about $12.8 billion in 1959 shows that policy did not control the economy. In the cities, the Housing Act of 1954 turned from public housing to urban renewal, and the highways were routed through poor and Black neighbourhoods. A policy that served the suburbs and the car passed over those who were left out of the 'affluent society'. Above all, on civil rights he did little to lead: he did not endorse *Brown* and the Acts of 1957 and 1960 were weak. The Democratic landslide of 1958, which gave the party a huge majority in Congress, suggests that voters were looking for a more active government.

These weaknesses, however, belong mostly to the second test, which asks him to do what he did not choose to do. Judged by his own measure he was highly effective. The best judgement is that Eisenhower was a successful President of a satisfied country, which means that his success was real but partial: he handled well the problems that he saw, but did not see, or did not choose to deal with, the problems of those who were outside the prosperity. His farewell warning about the military-industrial complex, delivered a few days before he left office, was itself an admission that he had not been able to curb the spending that went with it."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with **criteria** (his aims, the country's needs, durability) and uses them throughout. Every paragraph weighs one side with precise evidence (April 1953; $1 in 1956; 29 June 1956; 41,000 miles; about 7.5 per cent in 1958; about $12.8 billion in 1959) and ends with a link to the question (Table B, Level 5: about 14). It gives the case for and against, resolves the conflict by showing that the two sides use different tests, and reaches a judgement ("successful by his own measure, partial by the country's needs") that follows from the criteria (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30.
:::

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the reasons for the progress of civil rights in the 1950s.
**Plan.**
- Criterion: which factor, if absent, would most probably have slowed progress most? Use "progress" to mean change in law, in the courts and in organisation.
- Factors: (1) the NAACP's legal strategy and the Supreme Court (*Sweatt* and *McLaurin* 1950; *Brown* 17 May 1954; *Browder v. **A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The reasons for progress can be compared by asking which, if missing, would have done most to hold it back. On that test the work of the NAACP and the Supreme Court was the most important, since it set the legal base on which everything else depended. But in practice the law changed little until mass action made it matter, and the federal government's own part was smaller than the others.

The first reason is the legal campaign. For two decades the NAACP, with Thurgood Marshall as its chief lawyer, had worked to destroy the 'separate but equal' principle step by step, first in graduate schools in 1950 and then in the school cases that were decided as *Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka* on 17 May 1954. The Court's unanimous judgment, written by Chief Justice Warren, held that 'separate educational facilities are inherently unequal'. It changed the moral position of the whole country, since segregation was now unlawful in public education, and *Browder v. Gayle* (13 November 1956) extended the principle to public transport. Without these decisions the movement would have had no law behind it.

But the decisions were not the cause of change in the South. The Court had no means of enforcing them and had said, in *Brown II* of 31 May 1955, that desegregation should come 'with all deliberate speed', words that Southern states took as permission to delay. In the 1963–64 school year, almost ten years after *Brown*, only about 1 per cent of Black children in the eleven former Confederate states were in desegregated schools. What turned a legal victory into a movement was mass action. The Montgomery bus boycott, which lasted 381 days from 5 December 1955, led by the Montgomery Improvement Association under the 26-year-old Martin Luther King Jr, showed that Black Americans could organise economic pressure that was peaceful and sustained, and it brought down segregation on the buses in a way that the courts alone had not. The Greensboro sit-in of 1 February 1960 spread across the South within weeks and led to the creation of SNCC in April. Organisations gave the actions shape: the SCLC, founded in 1957, spread the Montgomery method through the Black churches.

The federal government was a weaker cause. Eisenhower appointed Warren and sent troops to Little Rock on 24 September 1957, the strongest action on race by any President since Reconstruction. But he acted there to enforce a court order and to stop a defiance of federal authority; he did not publicly endorse *Brown*, and Congress passed only the weak Acts of 1957 and 1960, which did little to raise Black voter registration. Wider causes mattered as well. The killing of Emmett Till in August 1955, Little Rock and the sit-ins were seen on television and in newspapers by Northern audiences, which gave Black protest a sympathy that it had lacked, and the Cold War made segregation an embarrassment, because the Soviet Union used it in its propaganda.

Overall, the legal campaign was the necessary condition and mass action the driving force. The Court's judgments were a charter that people could use, but they had to be used by people prepared to risk their jobs, homes and lives. The federal government did the least, and its main role was to avoid being defeated by the South, as it nearly was at Little Rock. The best judgement is that progress in the 1950s came from the movement and the Court working together, with the White House following behind."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** and distinguishes **necessary conditions** (the law) from **driving forces** (mass action) and from **limits** (the weak federal response). Each paragraph is built on precise evidence (17 May 1954; 31 May 1955; 381 days; 24 September 1957; 1 February 1960) and ends with a comparison (Table B, Level 5: about 14). The judgement is argued from the criterion and is not just asserted (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess how far Eisenhower's actions advanced civil rights." [30]

A student wrote:

"Eisenhower did not do very much for civil rights. He did not like the Brown decision and did not support it, and he only sent troops to Little Rock because he had to. The Civil Rights Act of 1957 was not strong. So Eisenhower did not do anything for civil rights, and it was really Martin Luther King and the Court that made the progress."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The paragraph has a claim and some accurate facts, but it **overstates** ("did not do anything" contradicts "sent troops"), asserts ("did not like", "had to") where it should show, and gives no dates or detail. The contrast with the Court and King is not argued. "Only because he had to" is a motive, not evidence. It leaves out Warren, the desegregation of the armed forces and the District of Columbia, and the bill that Brownell proposed, and it names no date for the Act.

**The fix.** Make a claim, use the detail as proof, and weigh the evidence on both sides. "Eisenhower's record on civil rights was limited by his reluctance to lead, but it was not nothing. He acted to enforce the law and not to lead public opinion, so that his contribution was real but reactive." Now each fact is evidence for a point, the claim is limited, and the judgement ("real but reactive") is supported.

Q: Give three reasons why Eisenhower won in 1952.
A: Personal appeal as a war hero ("I like Ike"); the use of television (the "Eisenhower Answers America" spots, the Checkers speech on 23 September); anti-Communism ("Korea, Communism and Corruption"); his promise on 24 October to go to Korea; the weakness of the Democrats after twenty years in office. Q: What were the main reasons for his victory in 1956?
A: The crises of Suez and Hungary (late October to 6 November) made voters rally to him; nearly 40 per cent of Black voters supported him; he was personally trusted and recovered from illness; the economy was prosperous ("Peace, Progress and Prosperity"); and Stevenson, who proposed ending the draft and a test-ban treaty, could not make a case for change. The result was 457 votes to 73.
Q: What was Modern Republicanism?
A: Eisenhower's middle course: keep and run well the New Deal and Fair Deal programmes, extend some of them cautiously (Social Security in 1954 and 1956, a $1 minimum wage in 1956, HEW in 1953), balance the budget where possible and leave the rest to private enterprise and the states.
Q: What did the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956 do?
A: Signed on 29 June 1956, it created a 41,000-mile system of Interstate and Defense Highways, authorising about $25 billion, with the federal government paying 90 per cent from a Highway Trust Fund financed by petrol and other taxes. It changed the American landscape, helping the suburbs and hurting cities and railroads.
Q: How balanced were Eisenhower's budgets?
A: He balanced them in only three fiscal years (1956, 1957 and 1960), with deficits of about $2.8 billion in 1958 and about $12.8 billion in 1959 during recession. There were three recessions (1953–54, 1957–58 and 1960–61), and unemployment peaked at about 7.5 per cent in 1958.
Q: What did Eisenhower say in his farewell address of 17 January 1961?
A: He warned that "in the councils of government, we must guard against the acquisition of unwarranted influence, whether sought or unsought, by the military-industrial complex": the close tie between the armed forces and the arms industry that he feared could distort the nation's priorities.
Q: What did *Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka* decide, and when?
A: On 17 May 1954 the Supreme Court, unanimously, under Chief Justice Warren, held that segregation in public schools was unconstitutional because "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal". *Brown II* (31 May 1955) told the states to desegregate "with all deliberate speed", which allowed delay.

It showed that mass, peaceful, church-based action could win, and it made King a national leader and led to the SCLC in 1957.

He acted to uphold federal law against a defiant governor, and out of concern for America's image abroad, not to promote integration.

A: Indicative content: personal appeal ("I like Ike"); television (the "Eisenhower Answers America" spots; the Checkers speech); anti-Communism ("Korea, Communism and Corruption", the Hiss case, McCarthy's charges); the promise on 24 October to go to Korea; Democratic weakness (twenty years in office, Truman's unpopularity, a candidate who could not defend or attack the record). Challenge: the party split between Taft and Eisenhower; the shift of the Solid South. A Level 5 answer sets criteria (necessity, contribution) and judges, e.g., that the combination of his appeal and the Democrats' weakness was necessary, and the Korea pledge decided the late undecided voters.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Eisenhower's re-election in 1956 was mainly the result of Suez and Hungary.' Evaluate this view.
A: Support: the crises began just before polling (23, 29 and 31 October; the vote on 6 November); voters rallied round the leader; Eisenhower's firm stand against Britain and France; the stalemate in the Democrats' foreign policy argument. Challenge: personal appeal; prosperity (unemployment about 4 per cent); peace since the Korean armistice; African American support (nearly 40 per cent); Stevenson's weakness; the margin of 457 to 73 was so large. Level 5 tests the view against the counterfactual (would he have won without the crises?) and concludes that the crises widened a victory that was already likely.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which Modern Republicanism was different from the policies of the New Deal and the Fair Deal.
A: Support for continuity: Social Security extended (1954, 1956); the minimum wage raised (1955); HEW (1953); public works (Interstate Highways); unemployment insurance. Differences: balanced budgets as a priority; more reliance on private enterprise (the 1954 Housing Act, the Submerged Lands Act, Dixon–Yates); less public housing; smaller response to recessions; no national health insurance. Level 5 judges that it was a managed version of the New Deal and not a reversal, with a more conservative method but similar acceptance of the welfare state.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the Interstate Highway programme for American society in the 1950s.
A: Indicative content: the Act of 29 June 1956 (41,000 miles; about $25 billion; 90 per cent federal; Highway Trust Fund); Eisenhower's motives (the 1919 convoy, the autobahns, defence, trade); effects on suburbs, shops, jobs and car ownership; harm to railroads, city centres and Black neighbourhoods; most construction after 1960. Challenge: the effects were mostly later; roads had been planned since 1944; the suburbs were growing already from the GI Bill and FHA loans. Level 5 weighs the immediate importance (a symbol and a start) against the long-term one (the huge changes of the 1960s and later).
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The Montgomery Bus Boycott was the most important event in the progress of civil rights in the 1950s.' Evaluate this view.
A: Support: 381 days of peaceful mass action; King's emergence; the model for later campaigns; the SCLC in 1957; the Supreme Court decision in *Browder v. Gayle*. Challenge: *Brown* (17 May 1954) changed the legal ground; Emmett Till and Little Rock; the sit-ins of 1960 and SNCC; the court case, not the boycott, ended bus segregation. Level 5 sets criteria (change in law, in organisation, in national opinion) and judges, e.g., that *Brown* was the most important for law and Montgomery for the movement.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that Eisenhower's reluctance to lead was the main reason why civil rights progressed slowly in the 1950s.
A: Support: no public endorsement of *Brown*; no meeting with King until 1958; the Southern Manifesto and massive resistance went unchallenged by him until Little Rock; the Acts of 1957 and 1960 were weak. Challenge: the Southern bloc and the filibuster in Congress; the delay allowed by "all deliberate speed"; white resistance and violence; the limits of the Court; the movement's early stage. Level 5 compares the President's part with the Southern Congress and the Court's ambiguity and judges that his reluctance was a contributing, not the main, reason.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Eisenhower was an effective President.' Evaluate this view.
H: Define "effective": did he do what he set out to do, or what the country needed?
H: Cover at least three areas (elections, domestic policy, civil rights) and rank them.
A: Support: two landslides; peace and prosperity; Social Security, HEW and the highways; Warren and Little Rock; the hidden-hand argument of Greenstein. Challenge: recessions and 7.5 per cent unemployment in 1958; deficits; no leadership on civil rights; neglect of poverty and the cities; the 1958 Democratic landslide; the farewell warning as an admission. Level 5 sets the two tests, judges each area, and reaches an overall view, e.g. that he was very effective by his own aims and less so by the nation's needs.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The federal government did little to advance civil rights in the years 1954–60.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate the three branches: the Supreme Court, the President and Congress.
H: Ask whether the "federal government" should include the Court, and what difference it makes.
A: Support: the President gave no moral lead and did not endorse *Brown*; Congress passed only weak Acts (1957, 1960); the Justice Department had few resources; voting registration changed little; in 1963–64 only about 1 per cent of Black children in the eleven former Confederate states attended desegregated schools. Challenge: the Court's rulings (*Brown*, *Browder*, *Cooper v. Aaron*) were federal action; Little Rock; the Civil Rights Division and Commission; Warren's appointment; desegregation of the military. Level 5 defines "federal government" and judges that the judicial branch did much and the executive and legislative branches little, so the claim is half true.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Eisenhower's "hidden hand" explains his effectiveness as President.' Evaluate this view.
H: Set out Greenstein's argument and the evidence for it (McCarthy in 1954; his organisation of government; the Little Rock decision).
H: Then the evidence against: his silence on *Brown*, his failure to prevent the recessions' effects, and what the hidden hand achieved or failed to achieve.
A: Support: Greenstein (1982) shows that Eisenhower worked behind the scenes, as in his refusal in May 1954 to let officials testify to McCarthy and his pressure on Republican senators, and that his calm public image helped. Newton (2011) adds his command of detail. Challenge: a hidden hand cannot lead public opinion, which was needed on civil rights; Branch (1988) shows his reluctance; the hidden hand might also be a cover for passivity; the recessions of 1957–58 were not answered. A* judgement: the hidden hand explains his effectiveness in management and in the Cold War, where private influence worked, but not in areas such as race where success depended on public moral leadership, so the thesis is right for the means and wrong for the ends.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the civil rights movement succeeded in the 1950s despite the federal government and not because of it.
H: What did the movement achieve by 1960? Court rulings, Montgomery, the sit-ins; the weak Acts.
H: What did the federal government do at Little Rock and in the courts, and was it a help or a hindrance?
A: Support: the movement's greatest gains came from Black Americans' own action (Montgomery, Greensboro) and from the NAACP's lawyers; the President gave no lead and Congress passed weak Acts; massive resistance went largely unchecked; federal slowness allowed the "all deliberate speed" delay. Challenge: the Supreme Court is part of the federal government and its rulings were essential; Eisenhower used troops at Little Rock; the 1957 Act created institutions that were later used; the Cold War gave the government a reason to act. A* judgement: "despite" overstates the case, because the Court was the greatest federal contribution, but "because of" the executive and Congress would overstate it more: the best view is that the federal government's contribution was real but mostly judicial and reactive.
:::

:::recap
- **1952**: Eisenhower beat Stevenson 442–89 (about 55.2 per cent); personal appeal, television (the Checkers speech, 23 September), anti-Communism, "I shall go to Korea" (24 October), Democratic weakness.
- **1956**: 457–73 (about 57.4 per cent); Suez and Hungary (late October to 6 November), nearly 40 per cent of the Black vote, prosperity, personal appeal, weak Democrat opposition; the Democrats kept Congress.
- **Modern Republicanism**: Social Security extended (1954, with disability insurance in 1956); HEW (April 1953); minimum wage $1 (from March 1956); Housing Act 1954 (urban renewal); Interstate Highway Act (29 June 1956; 41,000 miles; about $25 billion; 90 per cent federal).
- **Money**: three balanced budgets (1956, 1957, 1960); three recessions (1953–54, 1957–58, 1960–61); unemployment about 7.5 per cent in 1958.
- **Warning**: the military-industrial complex, 17 January 1961.
- **Civil rights**: *Brown* (17 May 1954) and *Brown II* (31 May 1955); Emmett Till (28 August 1955); Montgomery (1 December 1955–20 December 1956, 381 days); Southern Manifesto (12 March 1956); SCLC (1957); Civil Rights Act (9 September 1957); Little Rock (24 September 1957); sit-ins (1 February 1960) and SNCC (April 1960); Civil Rights Act (6 May 1960).
- **Judgement**: very effective by his own aims (peace, prosperity, order), less effective by the country's needs (race, poverty, cities); on civil rights the advance came mainly from the Court and the movement, with the White House acting when the law was defied.
