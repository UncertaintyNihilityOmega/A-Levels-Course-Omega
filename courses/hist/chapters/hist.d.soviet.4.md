:::why
In August 1980 workers in a Polish shipyard won the right to form a trade union that was not controlled by the Communist Party. Nine years later, in the summer of 1989, that union's candidates won every seat that they were allowed to contest in an election, and a Solidarity prime minister took office in Warsaw. Within seven months the Berlin Wall had been opened, the communist governments of Hungary, East Germany, Czechoslovakia and Bulgaria had gone, and Romania's dictator had been shot. Two years after that the Soviet Union itself was gone. For forty-five years Soviet control of Eastern Europe had rested on troops, secret police and the threat that Moscow would intervene, as it had in 1956 and 1968. Paper 4 asks you to explain why it ended, and above all why it ended so quickly and with so little Soviet violence. To do that you need the Polish story from 1980, the decisions of Gorbachev, the revolutions of 1989 country by country, the pressure of nationalism inside the Soviet Union, and the events of August 1991.

## Poland and Solidarity

### The economic problems facing Poland

Poland's economic crisis came from the policy of its own leader, Edward Gierek, who became First Secretary on 20 December 1970 after workers' strikes had brought down Władysław Gomułka (see [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2)). Gierek's plan was to buy loyalty by raising living standards fast, and to pay for it with Western loans. The government borrowed money from Western banks, which were eager to lend after the oil shocks of the 1970s, bought Western machinery and technology, and hoped to repay by exporting goods to the West. For some years it seemed to work: wages rose, shops had more goods and Poles took holidays abroad.

The plan failed. The imported machinery was often wasted in a rigid planned economy, and the goods made were hard to sell in the West. The world recession after 1973 reduced Western demand, and bad harvests in the late 1970s forced Poland to import food. By 1980 Poland's hard-currency debt was more than 20 billion US dollars, and much of its export earnings had to be spent on repaying the interest. The government covered the shortage of goods by keeping food prices low with large subsidies, which it could no longer afford. Shortages, queues and rationing spread, and the gap between ordinary shops and the special shops for the party elite was visible and resented. Workers' real wages stopped rising and then fell.

Every attempt to remove subsidies had met resistance. In December 1970 price rises had caused strikes on the Baltic coast and the shooting of workers, and Gomułka's fall. On 24 June 1976 Gierek announced large rises in food prices, and strikes at Radom and at the Ursus tractor works led to riots; the government withdrew the rises within a day and then punished strikers with arrests and dismissals. In 1980 the government tried again. On **1 July 1980** it raised meat prices, mainly by moving cuts of meat to "commercial" shops that charged more. Strikes began at once in factories across Poland, and in most cases management gave a pay rise and the strike ended. But the workers had learned that the government would give way, and the strikes did not stop.

### Why Solidarity developed and grew

Economic hardship explains the strikes of 1980, but not the creation of a union that lasted. The reasons for the growth of Solidarity were several.

- **The memory of 1970 and 1976.** Workers remembered that strikes had been put down by force and that they had been betrayed by the official trade unions. Each earlier outbreak had been isolated, and the lesson that they drew was that workers must support each other: they called the new movement *Solidarność*, "Solidarity".
- **The opposition's organisation.** After 1976 intellectuals had set up the Workers' Defence Committee (KOR, 23 September 1976), led by Jacek Kuroń and Adam Michnik, to help the families of workers who were punished. KOR published news, and it taught the idea that Poles should build their own organisations and not wait for the party. In April 1978 activists in Gdańsk founded the Free Trade Unions of the Coast, among them Anna Walentynowicz, Andrzej Gwiazda and an electrician who had been dismissed after the strike of 1976, **Lech Wałęsa**.
- **The Catholic Church.** About nine in ten Poles were Catholics, and the Church was the one large institution that the party had never controlled. It protected dissidents, and after the election of the Polish pope John Paul II on 16 October 1978 and his visit of 2–10 June 1979 Poles had a national moral leader. Many of the strikers of 1980 prayed and heard Mass at the shipyard gate, and the Pope's message that Poles should not be afraid was well known.
- **Weakness of the regime.** Gierek had lost authority through debt, shortages and the corruption of his circle. The party had many members who were weak in their loyalty, and the Polish government could not afford to give up its policy of low prices but could not afford to keep it.
- **The effect of Helsinki.** The Helsinki Final Act of 1975 (see [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2)) gave the opposition a language of rights, and the government had signed it.
- **A new generation of workers.** Young workers in the shipyards and mines of the 1970s had grown up under the party, yet did not remember the fear of Stalin's time.
- **Organisation by method.** The workers did not march into the streets, which was where the army had fired in 1970. They occupied their workplaces and stayed inside, which made it hard to attack them and easier to keep order, and they linked the workplaces by an inter-factory strike committee.

### The events of August 1980 in Gdańsk

On **7 August 1980** the management of the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk dismissed Anna Walentynowicz, a crane operator and a well-known activist, five months before her retirement. Her dismissal angered her workmates. On **14 August** about 16,000 workers went on strike, with the demands that she be reinstated, that Wałęsa, who had been sacked in 1976, be taken back, that wages be raised by 2,000 złoty a month and that a monument be built to the workers killed in 1970. Wałęsa came to the shipyard, climbing over the fence, and took the lead.

The strike nearly ended on 16 August, when the management agreed to a pay rise and Wałęsa announced that it was over. But delegates from other workplaces came to the shipyard to say that their own strikes could not continue without support, and Walentynowicz and another activist, Alina Pienkowska, told the workers not to leave them alone. The shipyard strike turned into a **solidarity strike**, and on 16 August the delegates of many factories formed the **Inter-Factory Strike Committee** (*MKS*) under Wałęsa. Within days more than 300 workplaces had joined it. The committee drew up a list of demands, the **21 Demands**, which were put on boards at the shipyard gate, and on 17 August a priest said Mass for the strikers at the gate.

The government's first response was to cut the telephone lines from Gdańsk and to try to divide the strikers. It sent a deputy prime minister, Mieczysław Jagielski, to negotiate with the committee from 23 August, and the strikes spread to the shipyards of Szczecin and to the mines of Silesia. Intellectuals, among them historians and economists who advised the strikers, came to Gdańsk. The talks were broadcast inside the shipyard by loudspeaker, so that every worker could hear them. On **31 August 1980** Jagielski and Wałęsa signed the **Gdańsk Agreement**, and Wałęsa signed it with a large souvenir pen carrying a picture of the Pope. Similar agreements had been signed at Szczecin on 30 August and at Jastrzębie in Silesia on 3 September.

### The 21 Demands

The demands of the strikers combined economic and political aims, and the order of the list shows what the workers thought mattered most: the first demand was political.

| Group | Demands (numbers as on the strikers' list) |
|---|---|
| **Free unions and the right to strike** | 1. Free trade unions independent of the party and of employers, as promised in the conventions of the International Labour Organisation that Poland had signed; 2. The right to strike and the safety of strikers |
| **Freedom of expression and of conscience** | 3. Freedom of speech, the press and publication, and access to the media for the Church; 4. The reinstatement of people dismissed after 1970 and 1976 and of students expelled for their opinions, the release of political prisoners and an end to the persecution of people for their beliefs; 5. The media to report on the strike committee and its demands; 6. Public discussion of the state of the economy and of reform |
| **Pay and conditions** | 7. Pay for the strike period; 8. A rise of 2,000 złoty a month; 9. Automatic wage increases as prices rise |
| **Food, prices and privilege** | 10. A full supply of food for the home market, with exports only of the surplus; 11. An end to "commercial" prices and to sales in hard currency in special shops; 12. The two main aims were the first demand, a free union, and the fourth, an end to political persecution. The strikers' leaders insisted that the others could not be won unless the first was, since a union controlled by the party could not protect anyone. The workers accepted that the union should not challenge the party's role in the state, an assurance that was written into the agreement, so that they could win a real concession. This was both a clever move and a limit: the agreement had a contradiction in it, for an independent union within a one-party state could not stay within the limits for long.

### The Gdańsk Agreement and the growth of Solidarity

The Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980 gave the workers most of what they asked for. It recognised the right to form free trade unions and the right to strike, promised limits on censorship and access to the media for the Church (the Sunday Mass was to be broadcast), promised to release those prisoners that were named, and offered pay rises and improved conditions. In return the union had to accept the party's leading role in the state, the socialist system and Poland's alliances (that is, the Warsaw Pact). It was the first time in the Soviet bloc that a government had accepted an independent organisation of workers.

On 6 September Gierek was replaced as First Secretary by **Stanisław Kania**, after a heart attack and the failure of his policy. The national organisation of the new unions, called **Solidarity** (*NSZZ Solidarność*, the Independent Self-governing Trade Union), was founded on 17 September 1980, and a court registered it on **10 November 1980**, after a dispute about the party's leading role that the Supreme Court settled by adding the clause to its statute. It grew faster than anyone had expected. By September 1981 it had **about 10 million members**, about a third of the working-age population of a country of 36 million, and it included not just workers but also many members of the Communist Party itself. In 1981 it was joined by **Rural Solidarity**, a union of private farmers (registered on 12 May 1981), and by a students' union. On 16 December 1980 a monument to the workers killed in 1970 was unveiled outside the shipyard gate, as the strikers had demanded.

Solidarity was not only a trade union. It was in effect a national movement, which ran newspapers, held discussions, called for the truth about Polish history (including the Katyn massacre of 1940, which Soviet propaganda blamed on the Germans) and wanted a share in the control of factories and of the economy. At its first national congress in Gdańsk (5–10 September and 26 September–7 October 1981) it called for free elections to local government and for a "self-governing republic", and it adopted on 8 September a "Message to the Working People of Eastern Europe", which encouraged workers in other communist states to form free unions. The Soviet press called this a provocation, and from that moment Moscow's alarm increased.

:::key Why Solidarity developed and grew
1. **Economic crisis**: Gierek's debt-funded strategy failed, the debt was over 20 billion dollars, and price rises (1 July 1980) brought strikes.
2. **Memory**: the shooting of workers in 1970 and the repression of 1976 made workers cautious about tactics and determined to hold together.
3. **Organisation**: KOR (1976) and the Free Trade Unions of the Coast (1978) had built networks of activists and a theory of self-organisation.
4. **The Church**: the strongest independent institution, with a Polish pope (1978) and a papal visit (1979) that showed the nation's strength.
5. **Method and leadership**: workplace occupations, an inter-factory committee, Wałęsa and the advisers who joined the strikers, and the limit that they accepted (no attack on the party's leading role).
6. **A weak regime**: Gierek had lost authority and the government had no money, and it could not use force without risking the loss of the Western loans on which it depended.
:::

### Reactions to Solidarity: the Polish government

The Polish party leaders were divided between those who wanted to work with Solidarity and those who wanted to destroy it. **Kania**, who led the party from September 1980 to October 1981, tried a policy of "renewal": he accepted the agreement, and tried to keep Solidarity within limits by negotiating and by delay. He relied on the Church and on the moderates in Solidarity, among them Wałęsa, to restrain the radicals, and he avoided force, partly because he feared that the Soviet Union would intervene. The government was slow to carry out the agreement, and local officials resisted it. In 1981 relations worsened. On **19 March 1981** police in Bydgoszcz beat Solidarity activists who had come to a council meeting, and on 27 March, after a warning, millions of workers took part in a four-hour general strike. Wałęsa made a compromise with the government on 30 March, to avoid a clash, and the more militant members of Solidarity accused him of surrendering too much.

In February 1981 the Minister of Defence, **General Wojciech Jaruzelski**, became Prime Minister, and on 18 October 1981 he replaced Kania as First Secretary of the party, so that he held the three most powerful posts: party leader, head of government and Minister of Defence. Jaruzelski was a career soldier and a communist, who had served in the Polish army formed in the USSR in 1943. He thought that the country was heading for chaos: the economy was falling (food shortages and rationing, with output down by a tenth or more in 1981), Solidarity's congress had gone further than the government would accept, and the party was losing control. He decided that the army must act.

### Reactions to Solidarity: the USSR

The Soviet leaders watched in alarm. They feared that an independent union in the largest satellite after the USSR itself would spread to other communist states, break the party's monopoly of power, and weaken the Warsaw Pact: Poland was the Soviet Union's main link to its forces in East Germany. Their reaction took several forms.
- **Pressure on the Polish party.** Brezhnev, Suslov, Andropov, Ustinov and Gromyko demanded firmness. A summit of the Warsaw Pact leaders in Moscow on 5 December 1980 warned Kania, and in the next year the Soviet Central Committee sent letters to the Polish party (on 5 June 1981 a sharp one) accusing it of weakness.
- **Military threats.** In December 1980 Soviet forces were put on alert around Poland, and the Warsaw Pact exercise "Soyuz-81" in March and April 1981 kept Soviet troops in and around Poland.
- **Economic help and economic pressure.** Moscow lent the Polish government money and goods, and in 1981 also delayed supplies of raw materials.
- **Propaganda.** The Soviet press attacked Solidarity as "counter-revolutionary" and its leaders as agents of the West.

But the Soviet leaders did not send in troops. The reasons were practical. The war in Afghanistan, begun in December 1979, was already costly. A Soviet invasion of Poland would have met resistance from a population of 36 million and an army that might have fought, and would have brought severe Western sanctions, which Andropov in particular wanted to avoid at a time when the Soviet economy was weak. Notes of the Soviet Politburo, which were published after 1991, show that on 29 October and again on 10 December 1981 senior leaders, among them Andropov, said that Soviet troops would not be sent into Poland. The Soviet leaders wanted the Poles to solve the problem themselves, and to impose order with Polish forces. In this way the invasion that might have been an application of the Brezhnev Doctrine was not made.

### Martial law, 1981

On the night of **12–13 December 1981** Jaruzelski imposed **martial law**. At 6 a.m. on 13 December he broadcast that the nation was at the edge of an abyss and that a Military Council of National Salvation (*WRON*) had taken power. Telephone lines were cut, the borders closed, and a curfew and censorship imposed. Thousands of Solidarity leaders and activists, including Wałęsa, were arrested and interned without trial in the first hours, and Solidarity's activities were suspended. Strikes at factories and mines were broken by the army and the riot police (the ZOMO). On **16 December** troops opened fire on striking miners at the Wujek colliery in Katowice and nine miners were killed. By the time martial law ended, the number of people who died in the repression is thought to have been about 90, although the figure is still disputed.

The USSR welcomed the move and had pressed for it, but had not sent troops. Why Jaruzelski acted is debated.
- **Jaruzelski's defence** is that he chose the lesser evil. He said that the Soviet Union and its allies were about to intervene, that he had a letter from Moscow that he treated as an ultimatum, and that martial law by Poles saved Poland from a Soviet invasion on the 1968 model.
- **His critics** note that the Soviet records show that Moscow had decided not to invade, that he exaggerated the danger of a Soviet intervention to justify his decision, and that his real aims were to save the party, to save the system from the economic collapse and to keep his own position. Some who criticise him accept that he believed that the risk was real. The matter shows how a belief in the Brezhnev Doctrine could act even when the Soviet Union did not intend to use it.

The effects of martial law:
- **Solidarity was driven underground**, and on 8 October 1982 a law dissolved it and all other unions. Wałęsa was released on 14 November 1982 and was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1983. Martial law was suspended on 31 December 1982 and lifted on 22 July 1983.
- **The West imposed sanctions.** The United States and its allies restricted trade and credit, and Poland's economy did not recover. Polish debt was rescheduled, and living standards fell through the 1980s.
- **The regime kept power but lost legitimacy.** In October 1984 a popular priest who supported Solidarity, Father Jerzy Popiełuszko, was murdered by security officers, and the government's trial of his killers showed how little control it had. John Paul II visited Poland again in 1983 and 1987. An underground press and underground university classes kept the movement alive.
- **The movement revived.** In 1988 strikes in April–May and in August, which began in the mines and the Gdańsk shipyard, showed that Solidarity still had support, and in the end led the government to talks.

Martial law shows that force could still stop Solidarity in 1981, when the regime had Soviet backing and the army was loyal. It also shows what changed later: by 1988 the regime was without money or Soviet support, and no longer able to repeat the decision.

:::key Reactions to Solidarity, 1980–81
1. **The Polish government** tried conciliation (Kania, 1980–81), delay and then force (Jaruzelski, 13 December 1981).
2. **The USSR** pressed the Polish leaders, held military exercises and used propaganda and economic pressure, but did not invade: Afghanistan, the cost, the risk of resistance and Western sanctions held it back.
3. **Martial law (13 December 1981)** broke Solidarity's organisation, but not its support: it survived underground, with the Church and the Pope behind it.
4. **The lesson for 1989**: the regime could win by force only while it was ready to use it and had Soviet backing. Once Gorbachev would not give that backing, the balance shifted.
:::

## The impact of Gorbachev's policies on Eastern Europe

### From the Brezhnev Doctrine to the Sinatra Doctrine

When Gorbachev came to power in 1985 the governments of Eastern Europe depended on the promise, in the Brezhnev Doctrine, that Soviet forces would defend them. By the end of 1989 that promise had gone. The change came in four steps.
1. **Equality and non-interference (1985–88).** From the start Gorbachev told the leaders of the Warsaw Pact states that each was responsible for its own country, and he refused to make them copy his reforms. At the same time he made clear that Soviet policy would no longer be to prop up unpopular rulers. His new thinking (see [part 3](#hist.d.soviet.3)) held that every nation had the right to decide its own future, and the Soviet Union could not afford to keep forces and subsidies in Eastern Europe. In 1987 an East German ideologist, Kurt Hager, said that a man whose neighbour was putting up new wallpaper did not have to do the same, which showed how the hardliners felt about his reforms.
2. **The United Nations speech (7 December 1988).** He announced the withdrawal of 50,000 men and 5,000 tanks from Eastern Europe and the principle of freedom of choice (see [part 3](#hist.d.soviet.3)).
3. **A public renunciation (1989).** Gorbachev told the Council of Europe at Strasbourg on 6 July 1989 that the use or threat of force by one state against another was unacceptable and spoke of a "common European home". The Warsaw Pact summit in Bucharest on 7–8 July 1989 accepted that each member should choose its own political path. On 4 December 1989 the five countries that had taken part in the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 condemned it.
4. **The Sinatra Doctrine (October 1989).** On 23 October Shevardnadze said that the Soviet Union recognised the freedom of choice of all countries, including its Warsaw Pact allies. On **25 October 1989**, in Helsinki, the Foreign Ministry spokesman **Gennadi Gerasimov** said that the Brezhnev Doctrine had been replaced by the "Sinatra Doctrine", after the song "My Way": each country would do it its own way.

:::def Sinatra Doctrine
The joke name, coined by the Soviet Foreign Ministry spokesman Gennadi Gerasimov on 25 October 1989, for the Soviet Union's new policy towards Eastern Europe: that each country was free to choose its own way ("I did it my way"), and that the USSR would not intervene to keep communist governments in power. It replaced the Brezhnev Doctrine of 1968. By the time it was announced, Poland already had a non-communist government.
:::

**Why Gorbachev did not use force.** Many factors explain it.
- **His beliefs.** He held that the use of force had failed in Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, and that it was wrong. He wanted to be a man of peace and he valued Western opinion.
- **The cost.** The empire cost the Soviet Union oil, loans and troops, and perestroika needed resources.
- **The need for the West.** He wanted Western credit, trade and arms agreements, and the use of force would have ended them.
- **A hope that reform would last.** He thought that reformist communists, like the Hungarians, would stay in power and make their countries better allies. He believed that reformed socialism would be more popular than the old kind.
- **The lesson of Afghanistan and of Poland.** The Soviet Union had avoided intervention in 1981, and the war in Afghanistan had shown the cost.
- **Pressure on the conservative leaders.** Gorbachev saw Honecker, Husák, Zhivkov and Ceaușescu as obstacles to reform in their countries, and he did not want to save them.

The speed of what followed was not planned. Gorbachev expected the satellite states to change, but not to leave the Soviet system altogether.

### Poland: Solidarity re-legalised and the elections of 1989

By 1988 the Polish regime had run out of options. The economy was failing, living standards were falling, strikes in April–May and August 1988 showed that Solidarity still had mass support, and the government knew that, without Soviet backing, it could not use martial law again. In the summer of 1988 General Czesław Kiszczak, the Interior Minister, opened talks, and on 31 August he and Wałęsa met. After more delays the **Round Table talks** began in Warsaw on **6 February 1989** and ended on **5 April**. They produced an agreement that:
- **re-legalised Solidarity** (it was registered again on **17 April 1989**) and allowed free speech and an independent press;
- set up a **Senate** to be elected in full by free vote;
- reserved **65 per cent of the seats in the lower house, the Sejm, for the Communist Party and its allies**, with the other 35 per cent (161 seats out of 460) open to free contest;
- created the post of a President, to be chosen by the two houses together.

The communists thought that they could keep power by controlling the Sejm and the presidency. In the elections of **4 June 1989** (and a second round on 18 June) Solidarity won **all 161 of the seats that it was allowed to contest in the Sejm and 99 of the 100 seats in the Senate**. Almost all the party leaders on the unopposed "national list" failed to get the 50 per cent of votes they needed, because voters crossed out their names. General Jaruzelski was elected President on 19 July by a very small majority, and the Communists' attempt to form a government failed. On **24 August 1989** the Sejm chose **Tadeusz Mazowiecki**, a Catholic intellectual and Solidarity adviser, as Prime Minister, the first non-communist head of government in the Soviet bloc. The Soviet response was decisive: on 22 August Gorbachev had telephoned the new Polish party leader, Mieczysław Rakowski, and urged him to let the party join a Solidarity-led coalition, since the alternative would be a crisis.

### Hungary: a nationalist revival and an open border

Hungary was the most reform-minded of the satellites. After Kádár was replaced as party leader in May 1988, reformers in the party, among them Imre Pozsgay and Miklós Németh, moved faster than Moscow. In January 1989 Pozsgay described 1956 as a "popular uprising" and not as a counter-revolution, and in February the party accepted the principle of a multi-party system.

**The nationalist movement.** Hungary's opposition was largely nationalist and national. Its main group, the Hungarian Democratic Forum (founded in 1987), was a party of intellectuals who stressed the Hungarian nation and its history, and it also took up the cause of the Hungarian minority in Romania, where Ceaușescu was destroying villages. The party leaders took up national themes too, and in 1989 a large public celebration of 15 March (the anniversary of the revolution of 1848) was allowed. This pride in national history gave the opposition legitimacy against a regime that had been imposed by Soviet tanks in 1956.

**The reburial of Imre Nagy.** The most powerful event was on **16 June 1989**, when **Imre Nagy**, who had been executed on that date in 1958 for his part in the uprising of 1956 (see [part 1](#hist.d.soviet.1)), was reburied with honour in Budapest. A crowd estimated at more than 200,000 attended. A young speaker, Viktor Orbán, demanded free elections and the withdrawal of Soviet troops. The ceremony was both a national act of remembrance and a political defeat for the regime.

**The open border.** On 2 May 1989 Hungary began to take down the barbed-wire and alarm system on its border with Austria. At a "Pan-European Picnic" at Sopron on 19 August 1989, several hundred East Germans, who had come to Hungary as holidaymakers, crossed into Austria. After the East German government pressed Hungary to stop them, the Hungarian government under Németh decided, on 10 September, to let East Germans leave, and from **11 September 1989** its border was open: about 30,000 East Germans left in the following weeks. Hungary set aside an agreement with East Germany to make this possible, and it was one of the main reasons for the fall of the East German regime. The Round Table talks between the party and the opposition ended in September, and on 23 October 1989 Hungary was declared a republic, no longer a "People's Republic". Free elections were held in March and April 1990, and the nationalist Hungarian Democratic Forum won. Soviet troops left by June 1991.

### East Germany and the fall of the Berlin Wall

East Germany had the most rigid government in the bloc and a leader, **Erich Honecker**, who rejected Gorbachev's reforms. It had the Stasi and a wall to hold its people in, but in 1989 its people found other ways to leave and began to speak out.
- **Flight.** Thousands of East Germans left through Hungary (from 11 September), and thousands more occupied the West German embassies in Prague and Warsaw. On 30 September the West German Foreign Minister, Hans-Dietrich Genscher, told the crowd in the embassy garden in Prague that they could leave, and special trains took them across East Germany to the West.
- **Protest.** Every Monday after a service in the Nikolaikirche (St Nicholas's Church) in Leipzig, crowds marched through the city shouting "We are the people". On **9 October** about 70,000 marched, and the security forces, who had been ready to shoot, did not fire. Gorbachev had visited Berlin on 7 October for the fortieth anniversary of the state, and the Soviet troops in East Germany, several hundred thousand, stayed in their barracks.
- **Honecker's fall.** The party leaders removed Honecker on **18 October** and replaced him with Egon Krenz, who promised reform. On 4 November about half a million people demonstrated in East Berlin.
- **The Wall.** On the evening of **9 November 1989** the party spokesman Günter Schabowski, at a televised press conference, read out a new rule allowing foreign travel, and when asked when it began, said that it was "immediately". Crowds went to the crossing points, and the guards, who had no clear orders, opened the gates, first at Bornholmer Straße late that night. The crowds crossed freely and began to pull down the Wall. The historian Mary Elise Sarotte, in *1989: The Struggle to Create Post-Cold War Europe* (2009), stresses how much was accidental in this moment.

After the Wall, the question became whether Germany would be united. West Germany's Chancellor Helmut Kohl set out a ten-point plan on 28 November 1989. In the free election in East Germany of 18 March 1990 the parties that wanted quick unity won. Gorbachev, who had at first said that unity was not on the agenda, agreed in July 1990 that a united Germany could belong to NATO, in return for financial help and the promise that Soviet troops would leave by 1994. The Two Plus Four Treaty was signed on 12 September 1990, and on **3 October 1990** East Germany joined the Federal Republic. That ended the division of Europe that had existed since 1945.

### Czechoslovakia: the Velvet Revolution

Czechoslovakia had the hardline government of Miloš Jakeš, which had been put in place after 1968 and kept power through the policy of "normalisation" (see [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2)). For years the opposition was small and was led by the signatories of Charter 77, among them Václav Havel. Small protests grew in 1988–89: a demonstration of Catholics with candles in Bratislava on 25 March 1988 and demonstrations in Prague in January 1989.

On **17 November 1989** students marched in Prague to mark International Students' Day, the 50th anniversary of the day in 1939 when the Nazis closed the Czech universities after the funeral of a student, Jan Opletal, and riot police beat them on Národní Street. A rumour that a student had died increased the anger. Over the next days the crowds in Wenceslas Square grew, and on **19 November** Havel and other dissidents formed **Civic Forum** (in Slovakia, Public Against Violence), which negotiated with the government. On 24 November the party leadership resigned, on **27 November** a two-hour general strike was held across the country, and at the end of November the party's leading role was removed from the constitution. On **10 December** President Husák swore in the first government for 41 years that the Communists did not control and then resigned. On 28 December Alexander Dubček, the leader of the Prague Spring, became speaker of the parliament, and on **29 December 1989** the playwright **Václav Havel** was elected President. The revolution was non-violent and took about six weeks.

The Czechoslovak revolution was **national** in its symbols (it used the memory of the founder of the republic, Tomáš Masaryk, and of 1918 and of Opletal) and it released the Czech–Slovak question that the communist state had suppressed: after the free elections of June 1990 the two nations argued about the structure of the state, and Czechoslovakia divided peacefully on 31 December 1992. The Soviet role was passive: the Soviet leaders did not intervene, and Soviet troops, which had been there since 1968, left by June 1991.

### Bulgaria and Romania

In **Bulgaria** the leader Todor Zhivkov, who had ruled since 1954, was pushed out by his own colleagues on **10 November 1989**, the day after the Wall opened, and the party agreed to a multi-party system.

**Romania** was different. It was not a Soviet satellite in the same way: it had no Soviet troops, it had criticised the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 and followed an independent line in foreign policy. Its leader, **Nicolae Ceaușescu**, had ruled since 1965 as a nationalist dictator, with a cult of personality, a powerful secret police (the Securitate) and an economy that he had squeezed in the 1980s, exporting food and fuel to pay off foreign debt, so that Romanians lived with cold, hunger and power cuts. He had also rejected Gorbachev's reforms.
- **Nationalism had a part.** The revolt began on 16 December 1989 in Timișoara, when a crowd gathered to protect a pastor from the Hungarian minority, László Tőkés, who was about to be evicted for criticising the regime. Hungarians and Romanians joined together, and the demonstrations spread. On 17 December the army and Securitate fired on the crowd.
- **Ceaușescu's fall.** On 21 December, at a mass rally in Bucharest, the crowd shouted him down on live television. On 22 December the army sided with the demonstrators, and Ceaușescu and his wife Elena fled by helicopter but were captured at Târgoviște. A military court tried them on 25 December, and they were shot the same day. A National Salvation Front, led by former communists such as Ion Iliescu, took power.
- **Violence.** Romania was the only one of the revolutions of 1989 in which a large number of people were killed: estimates run from about 700 to about 1,300, and most of the deaths came after 22 December in confused fighting between the army and Securitate units.

The speed and order of these changes struck contemporaries. Timothy Garton Ash, who was in Prague in November 1989, remarked that in Poland the change had taken ten years, in Hungary ten months, in East Germany ten weeks, and perhaps in Czechoslovakia would take ten days (published in *The New York Review of Books* in January 1990). Each regime was weakened by what happened in the one before, because the people of each country saw that Soviet tanks did not come. That is the **domino effect**: the example of Poland and Hungary in the summer made it harder for the hardline regimes to stand.

### The end of the Soviet bloc

The institutions of the empire were ended in 1990–91. In **1990–91** Soviet troops left Hungary and Czechoslovakia (by June 1991), and agreements were made for their withdrawal from Poland (1993) and from Germany (1994). The Warsaw Pact's military organisation ended on 31 March 1991, and the Pact was dissolved at Prague on **1 July 1991**. Comecon, which had tied the satellite economies to the Soviet one, was dissolved on 28 June 1991.

:::key Why Soviet control collapsed in Eastern Europe in 1989
1. **Gorbachev's decision not to use force**: the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine, in the United Nations speech (7 December 1988), the Strasbourg speech (6 July 1989) and the Sinatra Doctrine (25 October 1989), made it possible for peoples to act.
2. **Economic failure**: the regimes had debts, shortages and falling living standards, and they could not buy support as they once had.
3. **Opposition organisations**: Solidarity, Charter 77 and the Hungarian reformers had prepared leaders, ideas and networks, and the Church was strong in Poland.
4. **The domino effect**: each success (Poland in June, Hungary in the summer) showed that change was possible.
5. **Loss of faith by the regimes themselves**: many communist leaders, particularly in Poland and Hungary, no longer believed in the system or were ready to negotiate, and in East Germany the security forces did not shoot.
6. **The weakness of the hardline regimes**, which could not reform and had no popular support (Honecker, Husák, Jakeš, Zhivkov and Ceaușescu).
:::

## Dissent in the Soviet Union and Soviet reactions

Gorbachev's reforms also released discontent inside the Soviet Union, where there were more than a hundred nationalities and fifteen republics. The syllabus names two examples, Kazakhstan and the Baltic States.

### Kazakhstan, 1986

On **16 December 1986** the Central Committee dismissed Dinmukhamed Kunaev, an ethnic Kazakh who had led the Kazakh party since 1964 and was accused of corruption, and replaced him with **Gennady Kolbin**, a Russian who had never worked in Kazakhstan. Next day students in Alma-Ata (now Almaty) protested in the main square, and the demonstration, which was called **Jeltoqsan** (the Kazakh word for December), grew over three days. Interior Ministry troops, police, volunteers and cadets were sent in, and on 17–19 December they broke up the protest with batons, sappers' shovels and dogs. The official Soviet account said that two people had died, one a volunteer policeman and one a student, and described the protesters as "hooligans" and "nationalist" elements. Numbers of dead and injured are disputed: Kazakh accounts give much higher numbers, and some say that hundreds were killed, but the evidence is incomplete. Many were arrested (the official count was about a hundred, and other accounts give thousands), and some were sentenced.

The events matter for three reasons. They showed that the Soviet leaders still used force when a protest seemed to threaten Russian control. They showed that Gorbachev's policy of replacing officials, which was meant to fight corruption, could produce nationalist anger when it brought in Russians. And they came as the first of several nationalist protests. Gorbachev's later policy was more cautious: in June 1989 Kolbin was replaced by a Kazakh, **Nursultan Nazarbayev**.

### Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia

Glasnost let longstanding quarrels come out. In February 1988 the Armenians of Nagorno-Karabakh, an area inside Azerbaijan, demanded to join Armenia. Violence against Armenians in the Azerbaijani city of Sumgait followed, and conflict between the two republics continued and grew. The Soviet leaders used troops and sent officials but did not settle it. On **9 April 1989** Soviet troops in Tbilisi attacked a demonstration for Georgian independence, and about twenty people died, most of them women. In Baku on **20 January 1990** troops entered the city to stop violence against Armenians and to break the Azerbaijani popular front, and more than a hundred people were killed. These incidents damaged Gorbachev's reputation and strengthened the national movements in Georgia and elsewhere.

### The Baltic States

Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania had been independent between the wars and were taken over by the Soviet Union in 1940 under the secret protocols of the **Nazi–Soviet Pact** of 23 August 1939. Many of their people did not accept the annexation: the Western powers never recognised it, and there had been a national resistance and deportations in the Stalin years.

Glasnost gave them a means to say so. The Baltic movements began with protests about the environment and history, in 1987, and developed quickly into **popular fronts**: the Estonian Popular Front (April 1988), *Sąjūdis* in Lithuania (June 1988) and the Latvian Popular Front (October 1988). At first they supported Gorbachev's reforms and asked for more autonomy. In November 1988 Estonia's Supreme Soviet declared that its laws had priority over the Union's. On **23 August 1989**, the fiftieth anniversary of the Pact, about **two million** people joined hands in a human chain, the **Baltic Way**, which ran about 675 kilometres through Tallinn, Riga and Vilnius. On 24 December 1989 the Soviet Congress of People's Deputies condemned the secret protocols, which removed the legal basis for the claim that the Baltic States had joined the Union freely.

In **1990** the demand became independence. On **11 March 1990** Lithuania, whose communist party had broken with Moscow, declared the restoration of its independence, and Latvia and Estonia followed in May with declarations of a transition to independence. Gorbachev replied with pressure and not with force: the Soviet government cut off oil and gas supplies to Lithuania from April to July 1990, and it declared the declaration void.

In **January 1991** the Soviet response turned violent. On the night of **12–13 January** Soviet paratroops and KGB special forces took the television tower and broadcasting centre in Vilnius, and **14 civilians were killed**, many of them crushed by tanks or shot by troops, with hundreds injured. One Soviet officer was also killed. Gorbachev said that he had not ordered the attack, and he did not punish those responsible, while the Soviet Defence Minister and the Interior Minister defended it. In Riga barricades were built, and five people were killed on 20 January. The attacks produced an international outcry and protest in Moscow, and Yeltsin, in a visit to Tallinn, signed agreements with the Baltic leaders recognising their sovereignty. Lithuania held a referendum on independence on 9 February 1991, in which about 90 per cent of those who voted said yes; Estonia and Latvia held their own on 3 March, and in all three the majorities were large. The Soviet Union recognised the independence of the Baltic States on 6 September 1991, after the August coup.

:::key How the Soviet Union reacted to national dissent, 1986–91
1. **Force**: Alma-Ata (December 1986), Tbilisi (9 April 1989), Baku (20 January 1990), Vilnius and Riga (January 1991). It did not stop the movements and it damaged Gorbachev's authority.
2. **Concessions**: the replacement of Kolbin by Nazarbayev (June 1989), the condemnation of the secret protocols (24 December 1989), talk of a new Union Treaty.
3. **Economic pressure**: the blockade of Lithuania, April–July 1990.
4. **Hesitation**: Gorbachev oscillated between using force and permitting change, and each swing weakened him with one side or the other.
5. **Result**: the Baltic States showed that the Union could be left and led the way for the others.
:::

## The collapse of the Soviet Union

### Opposition to Gorbachev's policies

By 1990 Gorbachev was attacked from three sides, and each attack made him weaker.
- **Hardliners.** Many in the party apparatus, the army and the KGB believed that glasnost and democratisation were destroying the party, the Union and the Soviet gains of 1945. Their voices included Yegor Ligachev, the Leningrad teacher Nina Andreyeva, whose letter of March 1988 defended Stalin, and the "Soyuz" group of deputies formed in December 1990. They disliked the loss of the empire in 1989, the withdrawal of troops and the reunification of Germany inside NATO. Soviet generals were angry that the army was being cut, and officers' housing and pay were affected. On 20 December 1990 Shevardnadze resigned as Foreign Minister, warning that "a dictatorship is coming". Gorbachev gave in to the hardliners for a time in the winter of 1990–91: he appointed Gennady Yanayev Vice-President, Valentin Pavlov Prime Minister and the hardliner Boris Pugo Interior Minister, and he relied on the KGB chairman, Vladimir Kryuchkov.
- **Radical reformers.** Boris Yeltsin, who had been dismissed in 1987, won a seat in 1989 and was elected chairman of the Russian parliament in May 1990. He declared Russian sovereignty on 12 June 1990, left the Communist Party in July, and called for Gorbachev's resignation in February 1991. In the same period he supported the 500 Days plan, which Gorbachev rejected. Yeltsin said that Russia would be free of the Union centre, and he took Russia's share of taxes and resources. Miners' strikes in March–April 1991 demanded Gorbachev's resignation.
- **Nationalists in the republics.** Lithuania, Latvia and Estonia, then Georgia (9 April 1991) and Ukraine, which declared its sovereignty on 16 July 1990, each pushed for independence or at least for control of their own affairs. The "war of laws" meant that republics refused to obey the Union's laws.

**The economic collapse** underlay all three. Shortages, coupons, falling output and strikes in 1990–91 turned the public against the party and against Gorbachev. His popularity, high in the West, was low at home, and in 1991 only a minority of Soviet citizens approved of him. He was caught between two choices, and he lost the support of those who wanted change faster and of those who wanted none.

### The Union Treaty

Gorbachev's reply was to try to keep the Union together in a looser form. In a referendum on **17 March 1991** voters were asked whether they wanted the USSR preserved "as a renewed federation of equal sovereign republics". Six republics (the three Baltic States, Armenia, Georgia and Moldova) refused to hold the vote, and in the nine that did, with turnout of about 80 per cent, more than three-quarters voted yes (across the whole country about 113 million of the 148 million votes cast were yes). But the vote settled little: in Russia a second question, on a directly elected Russian presidency, was also approved, and Yeltsin won that post on 12 June. On 23 April 1991 Gorbachev and the leaders of nine republics agreed the "**9+1**" statement at Novo-Ogaryovo, outside Moscow, and later in the summer a draft **Union Treaty** was prepared. It would have turned the USSR into a loose union of sovereign states, with a common president, foreign policy and army, and the republics would have had control of their taxes and economies. The signing was fixed for **20 August 1991**. It would have cost the hardliners their posts, and many ministries of the Union would have disappeared.

### The coup of August 1991: causes

The coup had long-term causes and a trigger.
- **Fear of losing power and of the end of the Union.** The plotters were Gorbachev's own appointees: the Vice-President, the Prime Minister, the head of the KGB, the Defence Minister, the Interior Minister and others. They believed that perestroika was destroying the country, and that the Union Treaty would complete the destruction.
- **Economic crisis and loss of authority.** Pavlov's price rises, shortages and the "war of laws" had left the government unable to govern, and a hope that firm action would restore order.
- **No support from the West.** At the G7 summit in London in July 1991 Gorbachev received no large aid, and the hardliners felt that he had been humiliated.
- **Failed earlier attempts.** In June 1991 Pavlov, Kryuchkov, Yazov and Pugo had asked the Supreme Soviet to give the government emergency powers, and the request failed, so the plotters decided to act when Gorbachev was away.
- **The trigger.** The Union Treaty was due to be signed on 20 August, and Gorbachev went on holiday to his villa at Foros in the Crimea on 4 August, leaving the capital in the hands of the men who then acted.

### The coup of August 1991: events

- **18 August.** A group of men from Moscow, among them Gorbachev's chief of staff Valery Boldin, Oleg Baklanov, a deputy head of the Defence Council, and General Valentin Varennikov, arrived at Foros and asked Gorbachev to sign a decree of a state of emergency or to hand power to Yanayev. He refused. The telephone lines were cut and he was held in his villa.
- **19 August.** In the morning radio and television announced that Gorbachev was ill and that power was in the hands of a **State Committee for the State of Emergency** (the GKChP) of eight men: Yanayev (Vice-President), Pavlov (Prime Minister), Kryuchkov (KGB), Yazov (Defence), Pugo (Interior), Baklanov, Vasily Starodubtsev (chairman of the Peasants' Union) and Alexander Tizyakov (head of an association of state enterprises). Tanks entered Moscow. At a press conference that afternoon, Yanayev, whose hands shook, looked unsure. **Yeltsin** went to the Russian parliament building (the "White House"), climbed on a tank outside it, and called on the people to resist an unconstitutional coup. He called for a general strike and ordered the Russian authorities to ignore the Committee.
- **20 August.** Tens of thousands of people gathered round the White House and built barricades. The leaders of the Western states condemned the coup. The plotters planned an assault on the building but their commanders, including officers of the KGB's special units, refused to carry it out. During the night of 20–21 August three men were killed in clashes with armoured vehicles in a tunnel on the Garden Ring road in Moscow.
- **21 August.** The Defence Minister ordered the troops to withdraw, and the plotters flew to Foros to see Gorbachev. A delegation from Yeltsin flew there too. Gorbachev, who had been under guard for three days, returned to Moscow on 22 August. The coup had lasted three days, and the plotters were arrested. Pugo killed himself.

### Why the coup failed

:::key Why the coup of August 1991 failed
1. **It was badly planned.** The plotters did not arrest Yeltsin, who was the leading figure in the resistance, did not cut his communications or those of the Russian parliament, and did not control the independent press and Western broadcasts.
2. **It lacked determination.** The committee was a group of men who did not agree, and some were drinking or unwell. They did not order the assault on the White House or arrests, and the leaders hesitated when they faced a crowd.
3. **The armed forces and the KGB divided.** Senior officers refused to take part, units of the army went over to Yeltsin's side, and the units told to attack the White House would not do so. The soldiers were conscripts who might not shoot at fellow citizens.
4. **There was popular resistance.** People came to the barricades in Moscow and Leningrad, whose mayor Anatoly Sobchak led the resistance there, and the glasnost press and independent radio told them what was happening.
5. **Yeltsin had legitimacy.** He had been elected President of Russia by the voters on 12 June 1991, and Gorbachev had never been elected by the people, and Yeltsin's appeal had the strength of a popular mandate.
6. **Gorbachev did not give in**, and the plotters, who wanted a constitutional appearance, could not claim to have his consent. The West refused to recognise the committee.
:::

### The impact of the coup

The coup failed in three days, but it ended the Soviet system.
- **The Communist Party was discredited.** Crowds pulled down the statue of Felix Dzerzhinsky, the founder of the secret police, outside the KGB headquarters on 22 August. Yeltsin suspended the Russian Communist Party, and on **24 August** Gorbachev resigned as General Secretary and asked the Central Committee to dissolve itself. On 29 August the Supreme Soviet suspended the party's activities.
- **Gorbachev lost his authority.** He returned as a President who had been saved by his rival. In Yeltsin's presence the Russian parliament made him read out the minutes of the cabinet that had backed the coup, and Yeltsin took over the Soviet ministries, the state bank and much of the Union's property.
- **Power passed to the republics.** After the coup nearly all of them declared independence. The Baltic States, whose independence the Soviet Union recognised on 6 September, were joined by others.
- **The Union Treaty was dead.** The hardliners had tried to prevent a new Union, and had made it impossible. The Congress of People's Deputies voted itself out of existence on 5 September.
- **Russia took the lead.** Yeltsin and the Russian government, not Gorbachev, now ran the country.

### Gorbachev's resignation and the end of the Union

After the coup the question was whether any form of Union could survive, and what role Ukraine, the second largest republic, would play. On **1 December 1991** Ukraine's voters, in a referendum, chose independence by about 90 per cent, and elected Leonid Kravchuk as President. Without Ukraine, Yeltsin and Kravchuk concluded, there could be no real union. On **8 December 1991** Yeltsin, Kravchuk and the Belarusian leader Stanislav Shushkevich met in a forest near Viskuli in Belarus (the Belovezha forest), and signed the **Belovezha Accords**, which declared that the Soviet Union "as a subject of international law and a geopolitical reality is ceasing its existence", and created the **Commonwealth of Independent States** (CIS). Gorbachev, who was not consulted, called it illegal, but he had no power to resist. On 21 December eleven republics signed the **Alma-Ata Protocol**, joining the CIS. On the evening of **25 December 1991** Gorbachev spoke on television to announce that he was resigning as President, because he could not keep the Union, and he transferred control of the nuclear weapons to Yeltsin. That evening the Soviet flag was lowered from the Kremlin and replaced by the Russian tricolour. On 26 December the upper house of the Supreme Soviet formally voted the USSR out of existence.

## Why did Soviet control collapse? Weighing the causes

The title of the key question is about "1991", but a strong answer separates two collapses. **Soviet control of Eastern Europe** ended in 1989. **Soviet control of the republics** ended in 1991. The causes overlap, but they are not the same.

| Cause | The evidence | Its limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Solidarity and the opposition** | Showed that a mass movement could face the party; the model for the Round Table; won the June 1989 elections; Charter 77 and the Hungarian reformers | Suppressed in 1981 and weak in 1985–88; did not cause the fall of Honecker, Husák or Ceaușescu directly |
| **Gorbachev's decisions** | Ended the Brezhnev Doctrine (7 December 1988, 6 July 1989, 25 October 1989); refused force in East Germany; urged compromise in Poland | He did not intend the end of communism in Eastern Europe or of the USSR |

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
- **Timothy Garton Ash**, *The Polish Revolution: Solidarity* (1983) and *We the People* (1990), argued that Solidarity was a genuine movement of the working class and the intelligentsia, the first of a series of mainly peaceful mass movements, and in 1990 he described 1989 as a year in which peaceful popular movements, together with a Soviet decision not to use force, brought change.
- **Stephen Kotkin**, *Uncivil Society: 1989 and the Implosion of the Communist Establishment* (2009), argues that the role of the opposition has been overstated, and that the communist regimes collapsed from within, as their own elites, weakened by debts to Western banks, lost confidence and the will to rule.
- **Mary Elise Sarotte**, *1989: The Struggle to Create Post-Cold War Europe* (2009), stresses how much depended on accident and on the decisions of leaders in the autumn of 1989 (for example the opening of the Wall on 9 November), and on how Kohl, Bush and Gorbachev shaped what followed.
- **Serhii Plokhy**, *The Last Empire: The Final Days of the Soviet Union* (2014), argues that the end of the USSR in 1991 was driven by the politics of the republics, above all Ukraine, and by the rivalry of Yeltsin and Gorbachev, and not by Western pressure.
They show the main disagreement: whether the collapse came from below (movements and nations), from within (elites that lost faith), from above (Gorbachev's choices) or from outside (the West). Paper 4 rewards your own judgement, supported by evidence, more than a list of names.
:::

:::key A judgement you can defend
Soviet control of Eastern Europe collapsed in 1989 above all because **Gorbachev ended the Soviet guarantee**: without the threat of tanks the regimes, which were already bankrupt and unpopular, could not hold, and the opposition movements (Solidarity above all) were ready to take over. Solidarity was the origin and the model of the process but not a sufficient cause: it had been crushed in 1981 and it was Gorbachev's decisions that allowed it to win in 1989. The **Soviet Union collapsed in 1991** because the reforms that Gorbachev began (glasnost, demokratizatsiya and half-reform of the economy) released national movements and a Russian rival, Yeltsin, that the centre could not control, and the **coup of August 1991** destroyed what remained of the party and the Union Treaty. In both cases economic failure made the system vulnerable, and in both the decisive moments were political decisions, by Gorbachev, by the plotters and by the peoples who acted.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, with a total out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'Solidarity was the most important cause of the collapse of Soviet control in Eastern Europe.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement partly substantiated; a range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criteria: *necessity* (would the collapse have happened without it?), *scope* (how many countries?) and *timing* (did it come before the collapse?).
- For Solidarity: 10 million members; the Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980; it survived martial law underground; the Round Table (6 February–5 April 1989) and the June 1989 elections (161 and 99 seats); the first non-communist government (24 August 1989); the example for Hungary, East Germany and Czechoslovakia.
- Against: Gorbachev's decision not to use force (7 December 1988, 25 October 1989); economic failure and debt; the Hungarian border (11 September 1989); the East German flight and the Leipzig protests; the hardliners had no will to fight; nationalism; Solidarity was crushed in 1981 and was weak in 1985–88.
- Judgement: Solidarity was the most important cause in Poland and a vital example, but not the most important cause across Eastern Europe, where Gorbachev's decision was the necessary condition.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Solidarity was an important cause of the collapse of Soviet control in Eastern Europe. It was started in 1980 in Gdańsk by Lech Wałęsa and it was a trade union that was not controlled by the communists. It had 10 million members. In 1981 the government banned it with martial law, but it came back in 1989 and won the elections. This showed that people could defeat the communists, and soon other countries had revolutions. So Solidarity was the most important cause."

*Commentary.* The facts are accurate and relevant, but the paragraph is a narrative with a conclusion that it asserts: "soon other countries had revolutions" does not show that Solidarity caused them, and no other cause is considered. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 3 (about 7), accurate but general. To move up: set criteria, consider other causes and use exact evidence.

**A Level 4 paragraph (a good case for Solidarity, but not weighed).** "A strong case can be made that Solidarity was the most important cause. It was the first mass independent organisation in the Soviet bloc, formed after the Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980 and registered on 10 November, and it had about 10 million members by September 1981, a third of the working-age population. Even martial law on 13 December 1981 did not destroy it: it survived underground, with the support of the Church, and strikes in 1988 forced the government to open the Round Table talks on 6 February 1989. In the elections of 4 June 1989 it won all 161 seats it could contest in the Sejm and 99 of 100 in the Senate, and on 24 August Tadeusz Mazowiecki became the first non-communist prime minister in the bloc. This showed the peoples of Hungary, East Germany and Czechoslovakia that communist rule could be ended peacefully."

*Commentary.* Precise, well chosen and focused, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). It builds a clear case with evidence for each step. But it considers only one side, and it does not ask whether Solidarity would have won without Gorbachev, or whether it caused the collapse in the other countries. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs the other causes, criteria and a judgement that links them.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The view can be tested on three criteria: whether the collapse would have happened without Solidarity (necessity), how many countries it affected (scope), and whether it came before the collapse (timing). On these tests Solidarity was the most important cause in Poland, an important example elsewhere, and not the most important cause overall. That title belongs to Gorbachev's decision to end the Soviet guarantee of the regimes.

The case for Solidarity is strong in Poland. It was the first independent mass organisation in the bloc, born from the Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980, with about 10 million members by September 1981. Martial law on 13 December 1981 shows that it could be defeated, but it also shows its strength, for it survived underground with the support of the Church and the Pope, and the regime could not restore its legitimacy. Strikes in 1988 forced General Jaruzelski's government to the Round Table of 6 February to 5 April 1989, and the elections of 4 June gave Solidarity every seat it could contest in the Sejm and 99 of the 100 in the Senate. The first non-communist government in the bloc took office on 24 August. It also set an example: the Round Table was a model that Hungary followed, and the success of a peaceful movement showed that communist rule could end without violence. On the criterion of timing, Poland came first.

But on necessity and scope the case is weaker. Solidarity had been crushed in 1981 with Soviet backing and was weak between 1982 and 1988. What changed was not Solidarity but the response of Moscow. Gorbachev's declaration of freedom of choice at the United Nations on 7 December 1988, his renunciation of force at Strasbourg on 6 July 1989 and the Sinatra Doctrine of 25 October 1989 removed the threat that had held the regimes up, and he urged the Polish party to accept a Solidarity government. The regime in Warsaw could not have held an election without risking a Soviet intervention if the Brezhnev Doctrine had still applied. In Hungary the change came from reformers in the party and the opening of the border on 11 September 1989, and in East Germany from the flight of citizens through Hungary and the Leipzig demonstrations of 9 October, not from Solidarity. In Czechoslovakia the Velvet Revolution began with a student protest on 17 November, and Charter 77, not Solidarity, supplied its leaders. In Romania the revolt was local and violent. Everywhere the regimes were bankrupt and had lost belief in themselves, and economic failure was a condition of all the changes, including Poland's.

The causes are connected, and that is the central point. The economy made the regimes weak; Solidarity gave Poland an organised opposition; Gorbachev's decision let the weakness show and let the opposition act. Remove Gorbachev's decision and Solidarity would have faced martial law again; remove Solidarity and Poland would have lacked an organised opposition, but the Hungarian, East German and Czechoslovak changes would probably have come in some other form.

Overall, Solidarity was the most important cause of change in Poland and a vital inspiration to the other countries, but it was not the most important cause of the collapse of Soviet control as a whole. That was Gorbachev's decision, within an economic failure that gave the regimes no alternative. Historians such as Timothy Garton Ash stress the role of the popular movements, and Stephen Kotkin the failure of the regimes themselves; the evidence for 1989 supports a view in which all three, the movements, the elites and Moscow, were needed, but Moscow's decision was the one without which the others would not have worked."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with **three criteria** and tests Solidarity on each. It gives precise evidence on both sides (31 August 1980, 13 December 1981, 6 February–5 April 1989, 161 and 99 seats, 7 December 1988, 6 July 1989, 25 October 1989, 11 September 1989, 9 October 1989, 17 November 1989). It shows how the causes are linked, and the judgement follows from the argument. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13). Total about 27/30.
:::

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the reasons for the failure of the coup of August 1991.
**What the levels want.** An "Assess the reasons" question needs a range of reasons, a criterion for ranking them, and a judgement about which mattered most and how they connect. At Level 5 each reason is explained with precise evidence and weighed against the others.

**Plan.**
- Criteria: which reason, if it had been absent, would have allowed the coup to succeed? Which were the plotters' own mistakes and which the strength of their opponents?
- The plotters' mistakes: Yeltsin not arrested; communications and media not controlled; Gorbachev not won over; lack of will (Yanayev's press conference, the unordered assault on the White House).
- Division of the forces: Alpha and other units refused; army units went over to Yeltsin; Yazov ordered withdrawal on 21 August.
- Popular resistance: crowds at the White House, Leningrad (Sobchak), strikes, independent radio and the foreign press; three killed on the night of 20–21 August.
- Yeltsin's legitimacy: elected on 12 June 1991; the appeal to the citizens of Russia.
- International reaction: condemnation by Bush, Kohl and others.
- Judgement: the main reason was the divided and unwilling armed forces, which showed how far a state that had lived by force had changed; the plotters' mistakes and Yeltsin's legitimacy made that division decisive.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The reasons for the failure of the coup of 19–21 August 1991 can be ranked by one test: which of them, if removed, would have let the committee succeed? On this test the most important was the refusal of the armed forces to act with force, and the other reasons, the plotters' own mistakes, the resistance in Moscow and the legitimacy of Yeltsin, mattered because they produced or reinforced that refusal.

The plotters made serious mistakes. They did not arrest Yeltsin, who flew to Moscow from his dacha on 19 August and went to the Russian parliament, where he climbed on a tank to call on the people to resist. They left the Russian parliament's telephone and fax lines working and did not close down the independent radio or the foreign broadcasts, and so the news that Gorbachev was not ill reached the public. They did not win Gorbachev over, so they could not claim a constitutional transfer of power. Their leader, Vice-President Yanayev, gave a press conference on 19 August at which he looked nervous, and the committee never issued a clear order. Some of these mistakes show a lack of determination, which suggests that they were men who had spent their careers obeying orders and could not command in a crisis.

But the failure of nerve was more than a matter of character. The coup depended on the forces that had always supported the Soviet state, the army and the KGB, and in 1991 they were divided. Senior officers refused to join; some units went over to the Russian side; and when the plotters planned to storm the White House on the night of 20–21 August the commanders of the KGB's special units said that they would not do it. The soldiers were conscripts who could be told to fire on a crowd only with difficulty, in a country where, since 1985, the army had been criticised in public, withdrawn from Eastern Europe and made to hear that it had been wrong about Afghanistan. When three men were killed in the tunnel on the Garden Ring that night, the commanders drew back. On 21 August the Defence Minister, Marshal Yazov, himself a plotter, ordered the troops out of Moscow.

The popular resistance and Yeltsin's legitimacy explain why the soldiers would not act. Tens of thousands came to the White House on 19 and 20 August, in Moscow and in Leningrad, where Mayor Sobchak led the resistance, and the miners and others threatened to strike. This was possible because six years of glasnost had created a public used to speaking and a press that could tell the story, and because there was an alternative authority: Yeltsin had been elected President of Russia on 12 June 1991 by the voters, whereas the committee had no mandate and Gorbachev had been chosen by the Congress. The soldiers faced not a rabble but a lawful government with a popular mandate. Finally, the West condemned the coup, and the plotters had nothing to offer the economy.

Overall, the coup failed mainly because the forces that the plotters needed would not use violence against their own people, and in 1991 they would not because the regime that had given the orders had lost its authority and its fear. The plotters' mistakes made it easy to resist, and Yeltsin's legitimacy made resistance effective, but a determined and united army might have crushed the resistance. The coup's failure therefore tells us as much about the Soviet Union that Gorbachev had created, one where force was no longer automatic, as about the incompetence of the plotters."

*Commentary.* The essay states its test, ranks four reasons with exact evidence (19 August, the night of 20–21 August, 12 June 1991, Yazov's order on 21 August) and shows how they connect. The judgement follows from the argument. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13). Total about 27/30.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the reasons why martial law was imposed in Poland in December 1981." [30]

A student wrote:

"Martial law was imposed because the Soviet Union ordered it. Brezhnev did not want Solidarity to take over Poland, so he told General Jaruzelski to stop it. If Jaruzelski had not done this, the Soviet army would have invaded Poland as it did in Czechoslovakia in 1968. Solidarity had 10 million members and was too strong, so on 13 December 1981 Jaruzelski arrested all the leaders and banned it. This was the right decision and it saved Poland."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The paragraph gives a single reason and **asserts** it: "the Soviet Union ordered it" and "the Soviet army would have invaded" are stated as facts, though the Soviet Politburo's records, published after 1991, show that on 29 October and 10 December 1981 its leaders said that troops would not be sent. It also treats Solidarity's size as a reason without saying why it mattered to the government, and its last sentence is an opinion ("the right decision") and not a judgement based on a criterion. The date and the figure are the only precise evidence.

**The fix.** Give a range of reasons, test the main claim against the evidence and rank them. "Several reasons combined. Jaruzelski gave as his reason the threat of Soviet intervention, and the Soviet Union had pressed the Polish party for firmness since December 1980, with the exercises of March 1981 and the letter of 5 June 1981. But the Politburo's own records show that Moscow had decided not to send troops, so the threat was one that Jaruzelski believed or used, not one that existed. The domestic reasons were at least as strong: Solidarity's congress of September–October 1981 demanded a self-governing republic, the economy was falling, and the party was losing control. Jaruzelski also had the army and the means: by December 1981 he held the three main posts, and martial law was prepared in secret. The most convincing judgement is that martial law was imposed to save the party's rule from Solidarity and from economic collapse, with the fear of Soviet intervention as a real but not decisive factor." Now each fact is evidence for a ranked argument, and the paragraph ends with a qualified judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give three economic reasons for the growth of Solidarity in 1980.
A: Gierek's borrowing left a debt of more than 20 billion dollars; shortages, queues and rationing; the government's price rises of 1 July 1980, which followed those of 1970 and 1976 and brought strikes; falling real wages; and the resentment at the privileges of the party elite.
Q: Why was the first of the 21 Demands more important than the economic ones?
A: It asked for free trade unions independent of the party and employers, and the strikers said that without it no other demand could be enforced. It was a political demand, and it was the one that the government accepted in the Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980, with the condition that the union accept the party's leading role.
Q: Why did the Soviet Union not invade Poland in 1980–81?
A: The war in Afghanistan was costly; an invasion would have met Polish resistance and brought Western sanctions; the Soviet economy was weak; and Soviet leaders, among them Andropov, preferred that the Polish government use its own forces. The Politburo's records for 29 October and 10 December 1981 show a decision not to send troops.
Q: What was the Round Table agreement of April 1989?
A: The agreement reached in talks from 6 February to 5 April 1989 between the Polish government and Solidarity. It re-legalised Solidarity (registered 17 April), set up a Senate with free elections, reserved 65 per cent of Sejm seats for the communists and their allies and created a presidency.
Q: What was the Sinatra Doctrine, and when was it announced?
A: The name given on 25 October 1989 by the Soviet Foreign Ministry spokesman Gennadi Gerasimov to the policy that each Warsaw Pact state could "do it its own way", which replaced the Brezhnev Doctrine.
Q: What happened in Romania in December 1989?
A: Protests began in Timișoara on 16 December, where a crowd gathered to protect the pastor László Tőkés. On 21 December Ceaușescu was shouted down at a rally in Bucharest, on 22 December the army changed sides and he fled, and on 25 December he and his wife were tried and shot. Estimates of the number killed run from about 700 to about 1,300.
Q: Name the members of the State Committee for the State of Emergency who held government posts.
A: Gennady Yanayev (Vice-President), Valentin Pavlov (Prime Minister), Vladimir Kryuchkov (KGB chairman), Dmitry Yazov (Defence Minister) and Boris Pugo (Interior Minister), with Oleg Baklanov, Vasily Starodubtsev and Alexander Tizyakov.
Q: What was agreed at Belovezha on 8 December 1991?
A: The leaders of Russia (Yeltsin), Ukraine (Kravchuk) and Belarus (Shushkevich) declared that the Soviet Union was ceasing to exist and set up the Commonwealth of Independent States. Eleven republics joined it at Alma-Ata on 21 December, and Gorbachev resigned on 25 December.

A: For: the satellites were gone; the economy was failing; the Baltic movements and the nationalities question; the party's monopoly was ending (Article 6, March 1990). Against: the Soviet Union was not Eastern Europe, as no outside power held it together by force but its own army and KGB; the referendum of 17 March 1991 showed support for a renewed Union; a Union Treaty might have been signed on 20 August 1991; the coup, the role of Yeltsin and the vote in Ukraine were contingent events; the system had survived crises before. A* judgement: the end of the old centralised Union was probably unavoidable, but the form and timing, a total dissolution in December 1991 with no successor union, were the result of the events of 1990–91 and the decisions of Yeltsin, Kravchuk and the plotters.
:::

:::recap
- **Poland**: Gierek's debt (more than 20 billion dollars), price rises on 1 July 1980; strike at Gdańsk from 14 August (Walentynowicz sacked 7 August); MKS and the 21 Demands; Gdańsk Agreement 31 August 1980; Solidarity registered 10 November 1980, about 10 million members by September 1981; Kania, then Jaruzelski; the USSR pressed but did not invade (Politburo, 29 October and 10 December 1981); martial law 13 December 1981 (Wujek, nine killed 16 December); suspended 31 December 1982, lifted 22 July 1983.
- **Gorbachev and the satellites**: UN speech 7 December 1988, Strasbourg 6 July 1989, Sinatra Doctrine 25 October 1989; Round Table 6 February–5 April 1989, re-legalised 17 April, elections 4 June (161 and 99 seats), Mazowiecki 24 August.
- **1989**: Hungary (Nagy 16 June; border 11 September); East Germany (Honecker out 18 October; Wall 9 November; reunification 3 October 1990); Czechoslovakia (17 November; Havel 29 December); Bulgaria (10 November); Romania (16 December; Ceaușescu shot 25 December).
- **Dissent in the USSR**: Alma-Ata (16–19 December 1986); Tbilisi (9 April 1989) and Baku (20 January 1990); the Baltic Way (23 August 1989); Lithuania (11 March 1990); Vilnius (13 January 1991, 14 killed).
- **The end of the USSR**: opposition from hardliners and radicals (Yeltsin); referendum 17 March 1991; Novo-Ogaryovo 23 April; the coup 19–21 August 1991 and why it failed; independence declarations; Ukraine's vote 1 December; Belovezha 8 December; Alma-Ata 21 December; Gorbachev resigned 25 December.
- **Judgement**: Gorbachev's decision not to use force was the necessary cause in 1989; Solidarity the origin and model; economic failure the condition; in 1991 the republics, Yeltsin and the coup (Garton Ash, Kotkin, Sarotte, Plokhy).
