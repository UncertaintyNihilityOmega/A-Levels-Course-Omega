:::why
The League of Nations was the first attempt in history to build a permanent organisation of states whose job was to stop wars before they started. It was the idea that the whole peace of 1919 was supposed to rest on, and it lasted twenty-six years. Judging it fairly is one of the hardest tasks in this option: it settled some disputes that could have led to war, helped hundreds of thousands of refugees and improved working conditions around the world, and yet it was powerless when a strong state broke the peace. Knowing why it succeeded in some cases and failed in others also explains the failures of the 1930s, and the creation of the United Nations in 1945.
:::

## Why the League was created

### From the Fourteen Points to the Covenant

The idea of a world organisation to keep the peace was older than 1919. Philosophers had proposed leagues of peace for centuries, and during the war groups in Britain and the USA campaigned for a "League to Enforce Peace". Politicians were attracted by a simple argument: the war had been caused by rival alliances and secret treaties, so if states agreed to settle disputes openly and to combine against any state that attacked another, war would become too risky to start.

**Woodrow Wilson** made the idea the centre of his peace programme. In Point 14 of the Fourteen Points (8 January 1918) he wrote that "a general association of nations must be formed under specific covenants for the purpose of affording mutual guarantees of political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike." For Wilson the League was not an extra: it was the way to correct mistakes in the other treaties, since any unfair frontier could be reviewed later by a body of nations. British thinkers and officials added detailed plans (Lord Robert Cecil and General Smuts of South Africa wrote influential proposals), and the French pressed for a stronger body with its own armed force.

At the Paris Peace Conference a commission of the powers chaired by Wilson himself drafted the **Covenant** (the League's constitution) in January and February 1919. Wilson presented the draft on 14 February, returned to the USA to answer critics, and came back to add changes to win over American opinion: a clause saying that the Covenant did not affect "regional understandings like the Monroe doctrine" (Article 21), and a clause that matters wholly within a state's domestic jurisdiction were not the League's business (Article 15). The conference adopted the Covenant on **28 April 1919**.

:::def Covenant of the League of Nations
The constitution of the League, drawn up at the Paris Peace Conference in 1919 and printed as Part I of the Treaty of Versailles and of the other peace treaties. It set out the League's aims, its organs and the duties of its members.
:::

:::def Collective security
The idea that peace is best kept if every state promises to act together against any state that attacks another, so that an aggressor faces the combined strength of the rest. It replaces rival alliances with a general system.
:::

### Inclusion in all the treaties

A key decision was to put the Covenant into every peace treaty. Some delegates, including Clemenceau, would have preferred to settle the peace first and the League afterwards, but Wilson insisted that the League be bound up with the settlement. His reasons were practical and personal. He hoped the Allies would not drop the League if it was part of the treaties, and he thought that the US Senate could not reject the League without rejecting the entire peace and being blamed for it.

The decision had two consequences. It linked the League with the hated treaties, so that Germans, Hungarians and others saw it as an Allied victors' club as much as a world organisation. And it meant that when the US Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles, it rejected the League too. Wilson's gamble failed: the Senate did vote down the whole package (see the first chapter of this topic). The League came into being on **10 January 1920**, when the Treaty of Versailles came into force, without the country whose president had created it.

### The aims of the League

The Covenant named four aims that you should be able to explain.

1. **Prevent war by collective security.** Members promised to "respect and preserve as against external aggression the territorial integrity and existing political independence of all Members" (Article 10). Any war or threat of war was "a matter of concern to the whole League" (Article 11).
2. **Settle disputes peacefully.** Members agreed to submit disputes to arbitration, to a court or to the Council, and "in no case to resort to war until three months after" the award, decision or report (Article 12).
3. **Reduce armaments.** Article 8 said that peace required "the reduction of national armaments to the lowest point consistent with national safety".
4. **Promote international cooperation** in health, labour conditions, the care of colonial peoples, the control of drugs and of the arms trade (Articles 22 and 23). Article 22 spoke of "a sacred trust of civilisation" for the peoples of the former German and Ottoman colonies, who became **mandates**.

:::def Mandate
A territory taken from Germany or the Ottoman Empire after the war and entrusted by the League to a victorious power (the "mandatory") to administer on its behalf until the people were able to stand by themselves. Mandatories had to report each year to the League.
:::

The League worked through several bodies.

- **The Assembly** was made up of all member states, each with one vote and up to three delegates. It met each September in Geneva, discussed any matter within the League's sphere, elected the non-permanent members of the Council, admitted new members, and approved the budget. Because each member had one vote, small states had the same say as great powers.
- **The Council** was the smaller, more powerful body. It began with four permanent members (Britain, France, Italy and Japan; a fifth seat was reserved for the USA, which never took it) and four non-permanent members chosen by the Assembly (the first were Belgium, Brazil, Spain and Greece). The non-permanent seats rose to six in 1922 and nine in 1926, when Germany took a permanent seat. The Council met several times a year and handled disputes and crises.
- **The Secretariat** was the League's civil service, based in Geneva and led by a Secretary-General, Sir Eric Drummond of Britain (1920–33). Its staff came from many nations and prepared the work of the other organs.
- **The Permanent Court of International Justice** (the World Court) at The Hague, set up under Article 14 and opened in February 1922, gave judgements on disputes states brought to it and advice to the Council and Assembly.
- **The agencies and commissions** (refugees, labour, health, mandates, slavery, disarmament and others) did most of the League's day-to-day work.

The League's home was **Geneva** in neutral Switzerland. It started with 42 members, and grew to 58 at its peak in 1934–35.

:::key Why the League was created
1. **Wilson's Fourteen Points**: Point 14 made a general association of nations the keystone of his peace.
2. **The lessons of 1914**: rival alliances and secret treaties had led to war; collective security was meant to replace them.
3. **A way to correct the treaties**: Wilson believed mistakes in the frontiers could be put right peacefully later.
4. **Inclusion in all the treaties**: the League was to be part of the settlement, not an afterthought.
5. **Hope and fear**: after about nine or ten million military deaths, public opinion in Europe and America demanded a safeguard against another war.
:::

## The weaknesses of the League

Historians usually group the weaknesses of the League into four: restricted membership, leadership, voting and enforcement. You need to be able to give evidence for each and weigh them.

### Restricted membership

A world organisation was supposed to include the world. This one did not.

- **The USA never joined.** After the Senate's votes of 19 November 1919 and 19 March 1920 and the election of the Republican Warren Harding in November 1920, there was no chance of membership. The USA was the world's strongest economy and its absence meant that trade sanctions against any state would be much weaker, and that one of the five greatest powers was missing from the Council.
- **Germany was excluded until 1926.** The defeated states were not invited at the start. Germany joined only on 8 September 1926, after the Locarno agreements of 1925 (see the chapter on improving relations, 1924–29), and took a permanent Council seat. Its exclusion made the League look like a club of victors.
- **The USSR was excluded until 1934.** The Bolshevik government was outside the system and saw the League as a capitalist alliance against it. It joined in September 1934, when it needed protection against Nazi Germany.
- **Others who left.** Brazil left in 1926 after it failed to win a permanent Council seat. Later Japan (1933), Germany (1933) and Italy (1937) gave notice of withdrawal. Article 1 allowed any member to leave "after two years' notice" if it had fulfilled its obligations, so membership was voluntary.

### Leadership

The League was led by Britain and France, the two greatest powers who were members, but they did not want the same things.
- **France** wanted a League that would protect it from Germany: a strong body with the means to enforce the treaty. In 1919 the French delegate Léon Bourgeois proposed an international general staff and armed force; Britain and the USA rejected it.
- **Britain** saw the League as a forum for discussion and conciliation. It would not promise to use its armed forces automatically, because it wanted to avoid commitments on the continent and relied on its navy and Empire. Britain's attention was often directed to the Empire, trade and domestic problems.

Their disagreements prevented action on many issues. Italy and Japan, the other two permanent members, were often more interested in their own gains than in the League. With the USA absent and Germany and the USSR outside, the League's weight depended on two democracies recovering from the war, each looking over its shoulder at its own electorate. The League also lacked a strong personality at its head: the Secretary-General, Drummond, was a careful civil servant who avoided confrontation, and the League's constitution gave him no powers of initiative.

### Voting processes

Article 5 laid down that, except where the Covenant said otherwise, "decisions at any meeting of the Assembly or of the Council shall require the agreement of all the Members of the League represented at the meeting." In other words, **unanimity**. Only procedural matters could be decided by a majority. Parties to a dispute were excluded from the count when the Council voted on a report (Article 15), but the other members still had to agree.

Unanimity protected the sovereignty of states, which they would not give up, and it meant that any decision had the support of all. But it made quick action almost impossible, and it let any single state, great or small, block the League. The League's members also had to agree not only about what had happened but about what to do: during a crisis, the Council could spend weeks arguing.

### Enforcement difficulties

The League had no army and no police. Article 16 set out what should happen if a member went to war in breach of the Covenant: it was "deemed to have committed an act of war against all other Members", who agreed to cut off all trade and financial relations with it. That was **economic sanctions**. But if military force was needed, the Council could only "recommend" to governments what forces they should contribute. No state had to supply any. In 1921 the Assembly also adopted resolutions that in effect left each member free to decide for itself whether a breach of the Covenant had occurred, which made it easier for states to avoid applying sanctions.

There were other gaps. Articles 12 and 15 allowed war to be resumed after three months' delay or if the Council could not reach a unanimous report, and Article 15 left matters of domestic jurisdiction out of the League's scope. And sanctions were hard to use in practice: without the USA, a trade ban would not bite, any state that imposed it risked losing trade, and nobody wanted to be the first. The League therefore depended on moral pressure, public opinion and the willingness of the great powers to act.

|---|---|---|

:::key Four weaknesses to learn
1. **Membership**: the USA never joined; Germany joined in 1926 and the USSR in 1934.
2. **Leadership**: Britain and France led, with different aims.
3. **Voting**: unanimity (Article 5) meant slow and weak decisions.
4. **Enforcement**: no army; sanctions under Article 16 were hard to apply and military action was only "recommended".
Add a judgement: they were built into the design (voting, enforcement), or the result of events (membership), or both.
:::

:::warn Do not blame the League for one weakness alone
Students often write "the League failed because the USA did not join". That is one cause, and a strong one, but the Covenant itself was weak on enforcement and voting, and Britain and France disagreed. Say which weakness mattered most in a given case, and why.
:::

## Collective security and the League in post-war disputes

### How the League was meant to settle a dispute

:::steps
1. **A member brings the dispute** to the Council or Assembly (Article 11 lets any member raise any threat to peace).
2. **The parties submit it** to arbitration, to the Court, or to the Council (Articles 12–15) and agree not to go to war for three months after the decision.
3. **The Council investigates** and tries to settle; if it fails it publishes a report, and if the report is unanimous (apart from the parties), members agree not to attack a party that complies.
4. **If a member goes to war** against the Covenant, Article 16 calls for economic sanctions against it and the Council may recommend military help.
:::

The disputes of the early 1920s tested this system. Most were disputes about frontiers left unclear by the peace treaties. The six you must know are in the syllabus: the Aaland Islands, Teschen, Vilna, Upper Silesia, Memel and the Greco-Bulgarian border dispute.

### The Aaland Islands, 1920–21

The Åland (Aaland) Islands lie in the Baltic Sea between Sweden and Finland. They had belonged to Finland while it was part of Russia, and when Finland became independent in December 1917 they stayed with it. But the islanders, about 25,000 people, almost all Swedish-speaking, wished to join Sweden, and Sweden supported them. Finland refused, arguing that the islands were part of its territory and offering autonomy instead. Tension rose in 1920 after Finland arrested leaders of the island separatists, and Britain referred the dispute to the League.

The Council set up a commission of jurists, which decided that the League had the right to deal with it, and then a commission of three neutral rapporteurs, which visited the islands. In June 1921 the Council decided that the islands should stay with **Finland**, which must guarantee the Swedish language, culture and local autonomy of the islanders; and a separate convention, signed in October 1921, demilitarised and neutralised the islands. Both Finland and Sweden accepted the decision.

*Outcome:* a success. Two small states in dispute, both willing to accept League authority, no great power with a stake, and a compromise that gave each something (Finland the sovereignty, Sweden the guarantees and security).

### Teschen, 1919–20

Teschen (Cieszyn, Těšín) was a small industrial district of Silesia, with coal mines and the railway that linked the Czech lands with Slovakia. Poles and Czechs both claimed it, and in January 1919 Czechoslovak troops invaded; the fighting ended in a ceasefire under pressure from the Allies. A plebiscite was planned but abandoned in July 1920 because of violence. The two states then asked the Allies to decide, and on **28 July 1920** the **Conference of Ambassadors** (a body of Allied ambassadors in Paris) divided the district along the River Olza. Czechoslovakia received about 58 per cent of the area and about two thirds of the people, along with the larger part of the coalfield and the railway; Poland the rest.

*Outcome:* a settlement, but not by the League. You must be exact: it was the Conference of Ambassadors, not the League of Nations, which decided Teschen. The decision ended the fighting, but Poland felt cheated and the grievance lasted: in October 1938, after Munich, Poland seized the area.

### The Polish seizure of Vilna, 1920–23

Vilna (Wilno, Vilnius) was the historic capital of Lithuania, but its population in 1920 was mostly Polish and Jewish, with Lithuanians a small minority. Lithuania and Poland both claimed it. In 1920 Soviet forces took the city and handed it to Lithuania, and Poland and Lithuania then fought along the line between them. In October 1920, under Allied and League pressure, they signed an agreement at Suwałki that left Vilna in Lithuanian hands. On 9 October General **Lucjan Żeligowski**, officially a mutineer but acting on secret orders from Marshal Piłsudski, seized the city and set up a puppet state, "Central Lithuania".

The League protested. It sent a military commission to observe, urged a plebiscite and negotiated a ceasefire in November 1920. In 1921 the Belgian foreign minister Paul Hymans put forward a plan for a federation of Lithuania and Poland. Poland would not accept a plebiscite or the plan, and Lithuania would not accept the loss of its capital. The Council could not agree on a stronger course. Finally, on **15 March 1923**, the Conference of Ambassadors accepted Poland's possession of Vilna. Lithuania refused to recognise this until 1938.

*Outcome:* a failure. The League could not undo the use of force by a determined state that was a friend of France. France, which saw Poland as an ally against Germany and Russia, refused to put pressure on Poland, and Britain would not act alone. There was no army to enforce the League's will, and in the end the League accepted the situation it had condemned.

### The Upper Silesian coalfields, 1921

Upper Silesia, on the German–Polish border, was a rich industrial and coal-mining region. The peace treaty provided for a plebiscite, held on **20 March 1921**: about 59.6 per cent of voters chose Germany and about 40.4 per cent chose Poland. But the vote showed a pattern: the countryside and the east leaned to Poland, the towns and the west to Germany, and the industrial triangle was mixed. Polish nationalists rose in revolt in May 1921, and Britain and France disagreed about what to do (France favoured Poland, Britain Germany). The Allied powers, unable to agree, referred the problem to the League in August 1921.

The Council asked its four non-permanent members (Belgium, Brazil, China and Spain) to study it. They recommended dividing the region, and the Council adopted a plan in October 1921: Poland received just under a third of the area, about half the population and more than 80 per cent of the heavy industry. A German–Polish convention signed at Geneva in May 1922 (drawn up under the Swiss Felix Calonder) protected the economy and minorities on both sides for fifteen years.

*Outcome:* a success, in the sense that war was avoided and the settlement lasted until 1939. It was a compromise that neither side liked (Germans resented the loss of industry; Poles got less territory than they hoped), but it was accepted because the great powers had themselves handed the problem to the League.

### Memel, 1923–24

Memel (Klaipėda) was a Baltic port with a mixed German and Lithuanian population, taken from Germany under the Treaty of Versailles and placed under French administration. Lithuania wanted it as its only port. While the Allies delayed a decision, Lithuanian forces took control in a "revolt" on **10 January 1923**, partly to make up for the loss of Vilna. The Conference of Ambassadors accepted the fact in February 1923 on condition that Memel should have autonomy, and the matter was passed to the League. A League commission chaired by the American Norman Davis drafted a settlement, the **Memel Convention** of May 1924: Lithuanian sovereignty, wide autonomy for the territory, and free transit for the port.

*Outcome:* a mixed result. The League helped to turn a seizure into an agreed arrangement and protected the autonomy of the territory, but it did not reverse the use of force, and Germans noted that the League accepted what Lithuania had done. In March 1939 Nazi Germany took Memel back by ultimatum.

### The Greco-Bulgarian border dispute, 1925

On 18 or 19 October 1925 shots were fired at the border near Petrich and Greek soldiers were killed (Greek accounts say a captain and a sentry; Bulgarian accounts say a soldier who crossed the border after his dog; the accounts differ). Greece sent an ultimatum, and on **22 October** Greek troops invaded Bulgaria and occupied the area around Petrich. Bulgaria appealed to the League. Aristide Briand, the French foreign minister, was at that time President of the Council, and acted at once: he telegraphed both governments demanding a ceasefire and withdrawal, and called a meeting of the Council in Paris. The Council sent British, French and Italian officers to watch the withdrawal. Greece obeyed. A commission of inquiry found that Greece was at fault, and Greece paid Bulgaria 45,000 pounds in compensation.

*Outcome:* a success, within days. But some Greeks complained, with some justice, that a great power would not have been treated so firmly. The success shows the pattern: the League could act when a small state broke the peace and the great powers agreed.

:::key Why did the League succeed in some disputes and fail in others?
1. **Who was involved.** It succeeded against small states (Greece, Finland, Sweden, Bulgaria); it failed against a state backed by a great power (Poland, supported by France, at Vilna) and could not challenge a great power (Italy in the Corfu crisis of 1923: see the next chapter).
2. **Whether the great powers agreed.** Where Britain and France acted together, as in 1925, it worked; where they disagreed (Upper Silesia before it was referred, Vilna), it did not.
3. **Whether the parties accepted its authority.** Finland and Sweden did; Lithuania and Poland did not.
4. **Whether force had already been used.** The League could not reverse a fait accompli (Vilna, Memel).
:::

## The agencies of the League

The League did a great deal more than settle disputes. Much of its practical work was done by its agencies, and some historians think this was its greatest achievement. For the exam you need the purpose, key figures and examples of impact of each of the five agencies in the syllabus.

### The Commission for Refugees

The war and the Russian civil war left millions of displaced people. In 1920 the League took on the task of repatriating prisoners of war still held in Russia and elsewhere; the work was led by the Norwegian explorer and statesman **Fridtjof Nansen**, who in 1921 also became High Commissioner for Refugees. In about two years his office helped to send home more than 400,000 prisoners of war (the figures differ in different accounts). The League also tried to relieve the Russian famine of 1921–22 (with limited success), and in 1923 it helped settle Greek refugees after the war with Turkey (about a million Greeks came from Asia Minor).

The best-known achievement was the **Nansen passport**, created by a conference at Geneva in July 1922: an identity and travel document for people who had no state to issue a passport, mainly Russians at first and then Armenians (1924). It was recognised by dozens of governments and used by hundreds of thousands of refugees. Nansen received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1922. The limits were that the League had little money, and that states were reluctant to admit refugees; the commission could not solve the problem, only reduce it.

### The International Labour Organisation (ILO)

The ILO was created in 1919 by Part XIII of the Treaty of Versailles, which said that lasting peace could be built only on social justice. Its distinctive feature was that it was **tripartite**: each member state sent delegates of governments, employers and workers, and the governments had two seats against one each for employers and workers. Its first conference met in Washington on 29 October 1919 and adopted conventions on the eight-hour day and 48-hour week in industry, unemployment, maternity protection, night work for women and young persons, and a minimum age for work. Its first Director was the French socialist **Albert Thomas**. By 1939 it had adopted more than sixty conventions. Member states were not forced to follow a convention, but they had to put it before their parliaments and to report on progress.

*Impact:* many states raised standards, and the ILO collected information on conditions of work that was not available before. The USA joined the ILO in 1934 although it was not in the League. Its limit was that it could only persuade; governments could and did ignore conventions.

### The Slavery Commission

Article 23 asked members to secure "just treatment of the native inhabitants of territories under their control", and Article 22 banned the slave trade in mandated territories. In 1924 the League set up a Temporary Slavery Commission. It reported in 1925 that slavery and forced labour still existed in many places, and the **Slavery Convention** of 25 September 1926 bound the signatories to prevent and suppress the slave trade and to bring about the abolition of slavery "in all its forms" progressively. Its first article defined slavery as "the status or condition of a person over whom any or all of the powers attaching to the right of ownership are exercised". Ethiopia was admitted to the League in 1923 partly because it promised to act against slavery.

*Impact:* the convention set an international standard and put pressure on colonial governments. In Sierra Leone the British government abolished the legal status of slavery in 1927, which was linked to the League's pressure. But there was no power of inspection, and forced labour continued, as the scandal over Liberia in 1929–30 showed.

### The Disarmament Commission

Article 8 committed the League to arms reduction. It set up a Permanent Advisory Commission of military experts and a Temporary Mixed Commission (1921–24) to draw up plans. In 1922–25 the Assembly examined schemes that offered states security guarantees in return for disarming, such as the draft Treaty of Mutual Assistance (1923) and the Geneva Protocol (1924), but Britain rejected both (the second is in the chapter on improving relations). A Preparatory Commission began work in 1926 for a general disarmament conference, which did not meet until 1932. The 1920s therefore brought almost no actual reduction in armaments, except what the peace treaties imposed on the defeated, and the failure illustrates the problem at the heart of the League: states would not disarm unless they felt secure, and they would not feel secure unless the League could protect them.

### The Mandates Commission

The League supervised the mandates under Article 22. Mandates were divided into three classes according to how ready the people were thought to be for independence.

| Class | Territories | Mandatory |
|---|---|---|
| A (nearest independence) | Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan, Syria, Lebanon | Britain (Iraq, Palestine, Transjordan); France (Syria, Lebanon) |
| B (central Africa) | Tanganyika, parts of Togoland and Cameroons, Ruanda-Urundi | Britain, France, Belgium |
| C (small, remote or sparsely populated) | South West Africa, New Guinea, Samoa, Nauru, Pacific islands | South Africa, Australia, New Zealand, Japan |

The **Permanent Mandates Commission**, set up in 1921, consisted of about ten experts, most of them from countries that held no mandates. Mandatories sent annual reports, which the Commission examined in public sessions in Geneva, and it could receive petitions from the inhabitants. It could not send its own inspectors or compel a mandatory to act, and it could only report to the Council, where the mandatories themselves had seats.

*Impact:* it made imperial rule subject to some international scrutiny for the first time, forced mandatories to explain their conduct and gave petitioners a hearing. But the mandates were still colonies run by the victors, the Commission lacked power, and in practice class C mandates were governed almost as part of the mandatory's own territory.

:::note The historians' debate: how should we judge the League?
- **F.P. Walters**, *A History of the League of Nations* (1952): written by a senior League official, a detailed and sympathetic account which saw the League as a serious attempt that was defeated by the failure of states to support it.
- **Ruth Henig**, *The League of Nations* (2010): a concise modern study which stresses both the League's practical achievements in the 1920s and the limits set by its design and by the policies of Britain and France.
- **Susan Pedersen**, *The Guardians* (2015): a study of the mandates system and the Mandates Commission, which argues that international supervision made imperial rule a matter of international scrutiny even though it did not end empire.
At AS you do not need to name historians; you do need an argument about whether the League's record is better judged by the disputes it settled, the problems it failed to solve, or its agencies.

## Was the League a success in the 1920s? Weighing it up

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the evidence by type.

| Measure of success | Evidence of success | Evidence of failure |
|---|---|---|
| Settling disputes between small states | Aaland (1921), Upper Silesia (1921), Greco-Bulgarian (1925) | Vilna (1920–23): the League could not undo force |
| Dealing with great powers | Nothing in the 1920s | Teschen and Memel settled by the Conference of Ambassadors; Corfu (1923, see the next chapter) |
| Preventing a major war | There was no general war in Europe in the 1920s | The peace owed as much to exhaustion and to the Locarno process as to the League; no great power was tested |
| Humanitarian and social work | Nansen, ILO, Slavery Convention, health work | Little funding; no power to compel |
| Disarmament | Plans, commissions | Almost none achieved |
| Membership | 58 members at the peak | USA never; Germany and USSR late |

:::key A judgement you can defend
In the 1920s the League was **effective in minor disputes and in humanitarian cooperation, and ineffective against strong states**. It succeeded when the parties were small, the great powers agreed and the matter was not vital to anyone, and it failed when a stronger state was determined to have its way. This pattern was already clear by 1925, and it explains the later failures over Manchuria and Abyssinia. The League's greatest weakness was not a single flaw but the combination of its design (unanimity, no armed force) and the limited commitment of its leading members.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the League of Nations failed to resolve the Vilna dispute.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Vilna was claimed by both Poland and Lithuania. In 1920 Poland seized it with General Żeligowski. The League asked Poland to leave but Poland did not. The League also had no army. In 1923 the Conference of Ambassadors gave Vilna to Poland. So the League failed."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes an event; none explains why the League failed. Causation is asserted by "so", not explained. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was that the League had no means of forcing Poland to withdraw. The Covenant allowed economic sanctions under Article 16, but only against a member that went to war in breach of the Covenant, and Poland's action was disguised as a mutiny by General Żeligowski, who acted officially against orders. The League could send observers and propose a plebiscite, but Poland refused and there was no army to make it comply. A second reason was that the great powers did not agree. France saw Poland as an ally against Germany and the Soviet Union and would not press it, while Britain was unwilling to act alone."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was that the League's procedures needed unanimity and the consent of the parties. Under Article 5 the Council could not impose a decision, and the Hymans plan of 1921 for a Polish-Lithuanian federation failed because both sides rejected it: Poland would not give up the city it held and Lithuania would not accept the loss of its capital. In March 1923 the matter was closed by the Conference of Ambassadors, not by the League, which accepted the situation it had condemned.

These reasons were linked. The League had no force of its own, so it relied on the great powers; Britain and France did not agree to use theirs because France's alliance policy made it protect Poland; and the rule of unanimity meant that the League could not act without them. The decisive reason was therefore the lack of agreement between the great powers, because the League's other weaknesses, no army and the need for consent, mattered only because there was no one willing to supply the force that it lacked."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (no force, so dependent on the great powers, who disagreed), with a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

The Members of the League undertake to respect and preserve as against external aggression the territorial integrity and existing political independence of all Members of the League.

*From Article 10 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, 1919.*

**Source B**

The United States assumes no obligation to preserve the territorial integrity or political independence of any other country … under the provisions of Article X, or to employ the military or naval forces of the United States under any article of the treaty for any purpose, unless in any particular case the Congress … shall by act or joint resolution so provide.

*Adapted from the second of the Lodge Reservations, proposed by Senator Henry Cabot Lodge and voted on in the US Senate, November 1919. Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to collective security in 1919.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that League members must protect each other's territory. Source B says that the USA will not have to protect other countries. So they disagree. Source A is from the Covenant and Source B is from a senator. Source B is biased because he did not like the League."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources have more in common than they first seem to. Both are about the same obligation, the guarantee of territory, and both treat it as a serious legal commitment: Source A uses the word 'undertake', Source B is a long and carefully worded text. Both also use the language of 'territorial integrity' and 'political independence'; Lodge did not deny the principle but refused to be bound by it.

On what they say about collective security, however, the sources are directly opposed. Source A says members will 'respect and preserve' the territory of all other members, which is a promise of mutual defence against 'external aggression'. Source B says the USA 'assumes no obligation' to do so, and adds that US forces would not be used 'for any purpose' unless Congress decides. A also treats the commitment as the League's duty, while B reserves the decision for the American legislature in each case.

The context explains this. Source A is the text drafted at the Paris Peace Conference in early 1919, the heart of Wilson's idea of collective security. It does not say how the promise would be carried out: the Council would only 'advise', so the wording is stronger than the machinery. Source B comes from the Senate in November 1919, written by Lodge, the Republican chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee, who opposed the League. His purpose was to protect the Senate's constitutional power to declare war and to prevent the USA from being committed by an international body, and it was part of a political fight with President Wilson. As evidence of attitudes in 1919 they show a gap between two views: A shows the hope of the League's founders, B shows the reluctance of a major power, and the fact that the Senate adopted reservations like B and still did not ratify the treaty shows that the second attitude was strong enough to keep the USA out of the League."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through the nature, origin and purpose of the sources and the context of 1919; and a final judgement of what the sources show together. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The absence of the USA was the League's greatest weakness in the 1920s.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the USA was the strongest economy and a principal founder; sanctions could not work without it (Article 16); the Anglo-American guarantee to France lapsed so France sought security by harsh policies; the League looked like a European victors' club; one of the five permanent Council seats stayed empty; Britain and France were left to carry the League.
- Against 1, design: unanimity (Article 5) and the lack of a force were built in; Article 16 only recommended military action.
- Against 2, leadership: Britain and France differed over the League's purpose.
- Against 3, other missing members: Germany (until 1926) and the USSR (until 1934); the defeated states' absence.
- Against 4, the great powers' will: Vilna and Corfu show that France and Britain, not the USA, decided what the League could do.
- Judgement: the USA's absence was a major weakness but not the greatest: it deprived the League of resources, but the lack of will and agreement in Britain and France, and the Covenant's design, meant that the League would have been weak even with the USA. Criterion: which weakness did most to stop the League acting in a crisis?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The absence of the USA was the greatest weakness of the League. The USA was the richest country in the world and Wilson had created the League. When the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles in November 1919 and March 1920, the USA did not join. This meant that economic sanctions under Article 16 would not work, because the USA could carry on trading with a state that had been punished. It also meant that the League had only Britain and France to lead it, so that it looked like an organisation of European victors. So the absence of the USA was the greatest weakness."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: nothing is compared with the other weaknesses. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other weaknesses merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"However, the absence of the USA does not explain the League's failures in the cases that were tested. In the Vilna dispute of 1920–23 the USA played no part, but the League failed because France would not press Poland and Britain would not act alone. At the Corfu crisis of 1923, when Mussolini defied the League, it was the Conference of Ambassadors, a body of the great powers, and not the Council that settled the matter. The same Britain and France, who were members, could and did make the League work in October 1925, when they agreed that Greece should withdraw from Bulgaria. What decided whether the League succeeded was whether the great powers who were members agreed to act, and that depended on their own interests and not on the USA.

The design of the Covenant was also a weakness the USA could not have corrected. Article 5 required unanimity, so any one state could block action, and Article 16 left military action to recommendation. With American membership these provisions would still have applied. A League with the USA but without a force, without agreement among its leaders and with unanimity would have been slow in a crisis.

Overall, the absence of the USA was a serious weakness, but it was not the greatest. It limited the League's power and its legitimacy, and its effects were felt especially in the matter of sanctions and in France's loss of its guarantee. But the League's record in the 1920s turned on the will of Britain and France and on the Covenant's design, which made the League dependent on that will. The statement is therefore true to a limited extent: the USA's absence made the League weaker, but the weakness that decided its fate was the unwillingness of its leading members to use it."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (which weakness decided outcomes in actual crises). With an opening paragraph on the case for the USA's absence, this is Level 5 (18–20). Note that the strong answer tests the claim against the cases (Vilna, Corfu, 1925), not just against a list of weaknesses.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

Should any Member of the League resort to war in disregard of its covenants under Articles 12, 13 or 15, it shall ipso facto be deemed to have committed an act of war against all other Members of the League, which hereby undertake immediately to subject it to the severance of all trade or financial relations.

*Adapted from Article 16 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, 1919. Shortened; the words are the Covenant's own.*

**Source B**

It shall be the duty of the Council in such case to recommend to the several Governments concerned what effective military, naval or air force the Members of the League shall severally contribute to the armed forces to be used to protect the covenants of the League.

*From the second paragraph of Article 16 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, 1919.*

**Source C**

Except where otherwise expressly provided in this Covenant or by the terms of the present Treaty, decisions at any meeting of the Assembly or of the Council shall require the agreement of all the Members of the League represented at the meeting.

*From Article 5 of the Covenant of the League of Nations, 1919.*

**Source D**

I have loved but one flag and I can not share that devotion and give affection to the mongrel banner invented for a league.

*From a speech by Senator Henry Cabot Lodge in the US Senate, 12 August 1919.*

**Question.** "The League of Nations had the powers it needed to keep the peace." How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (a strong sanction: every member to cut off trade with an aggressor, and an attack on one is an act of war against all). Challenge: B (military force only "recommended"), C (unanimity: any member can block action), D (a leading American politician refuses to join). Qualify: A is limited to a member who resorts to war "in disregard of its covenants" and only to economic measures.

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A and B**: both are part of the same article, written at the Paris Peace Conference in early 1919 as a compromise between British and American caution and French demands for force. They are the most reliable evidence of what the League was *designed* to do, because they are the actual rules. But they tell us nothing about whether the rules worked: Article 16 was never fully applied in the 1920s, and in 1921 the Assembly weakened it by leaving each member to judge whether a breach had occurred.
- **C**: the text of the voting rule, equally reliable as to design. It shows that no decision could be taken against a member's will, but it must be read with the clause that procedural matters could be decided by majority, and with the Article 15 rule that parties to a dispute did not count in a vote on a report.
- **D**: a political speech by a Republican opponent of Wilson, made for the Senate and the press during the fight over ratification, so it is rhetoric and not analysis. It is useful because Lodge's objection, and the Senate's votes, explain why the League lacked the USA, but it says little about the League's powers as such.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "The sources agree that the League was given real tools but not all the tools it needed. Source A shows its strongest power, the collective boycott of an aggressor, which was a real novelty in international law. But Source B shows that it had no army and no authority over members' armed forces: the Council could only 'recommend' contributions, so a state could refuse. Source C shows that the Council and Assembly could not act without agreement, which meant that any single member could stop a decision. Source D shows that the League would lack the support of the USA, whose economy would have made sanctions much more effective. All four are authoritative evidence of what the League was meant to be; none shows what happened. When tested in the 1920s, the pattern fits the sources: at Vilna (1920–23) the League could not make Poland leave, and in October 1925 it stopped Greece only because Britain and France agreed to act. On balance the sources support the view only to a limited extent: Article 16 gave the League an economic weapon, but the combination of a force that was only recommended, a unanimity rule and the absence of the USA meant that it lacked the powers it needed to keep the peace against a determined state."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin and purpose; the answer separates what the sources show about design from what happened, uses own knowledge (Vilna, 1925, the 1921 resolutions) to test them, and answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "To what extent was the League of Nations successful in the 1920s?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The League of Nations was set up in 1920 and had its headquarters in Geneva. It had an Assembly and a Council and a Secretariat. In 1921 it settled the Aaland Islands dispute between Sweden and Finland and gave the islands to Finland. In 1921 it divided Upper Silesia between Germany and Poland. In 1920 it failed to stop Poland taking Vilna. In 1925 it stopped Greece invading Bulgaria. It also had agencies such as the ILO and the Refugee Commission. So the League was quite successful in the 1920s but it did not work in the 1930s."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **description**: it lists events and ends with an assertion. It never says why the League succeeded in some cases and failed in others, it does not weigh the successes against the failures and the last sentence moves to the 1930s, which the question does not ask about. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description, few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the evidence that proves it, then compare. "The League was successful in the 1920s only in a limited sense: it settled disputes between small states and did valuable humanitarian work, but it could not stop a state backed by a great power. At Aaland in 1921 and in the Greco-Bulgarian dispute of 1925 it succeeded because the parties were small and Britain and France agreed. At Vilna it failed because France would not press Poland, and Corfu was settled outside the League. Its agencies, such as the work of Nansen for refugees, gave it a record of real achievement, but they did not touch the question of peace between strong states. So the League's success depended on the matter being unimportant to the great powers." Now each fact serves a judgement.

Q: Which point of Wilson's Fourteen Points proposed the League?
A: Point 14: a general association of nations to give mutual guarantees of political independence and territorial integrity to great and small states alike.
Q: Why was the Covenant included in all the peace treaties, and what was the consequence?
A: Wilson insisted so that the League could not be separated from the peace and the Senate would have to accept both. The consequence was that the League was linked with the unpopular treaties, and the Senate's rejection of the Treaty of Versailles also kept the USA out of the League.
Q: Name the main organs of the League and say what the Council's membership was.
A: The Assembly, the Council, the Secretariat, the Permanent Court of International Justice and the agencies. The Council started with four permanent members (Britain, France, Italy, Japan) and four non-permanent members; the number of non-permanent seats rose to six in 1922 and nine in 1926, when Germany took a permanent seat.
Q: Give two weaknesses of the League's voting procedure and enforcement.
A: Voting: Article 5 required unanimity, so any member could block a decision. Enforcement: the League had no army; Article 16 provided for economic sanctions and only allowed the Council to "recommend" military contributions.
Q: When did Germany and the USSR join the League?
A: Germany on 8 September 1926 (with a permanent Council seat); the USSR in September 1934.
Q: How did the League settle the Aaland Islands dispute?
A: Britain referred it to the League in 1920; commissions of jurists and neutral rapporteurs investigated; in June 1921 the Council awarded the islands to Finland, with autonomy and guarantees for the Swedish-speaking islanders and, by a convention of October 1921, demilitarisation. Sweden and Finland accepted.
Q: Who decided the Teschen dispute?
A: The Conference of Ambassadors, on 28 July 1920, dividing the district along the Olza; not the League.
Q: Why was the League unable to settle the Vilna dispute?
A: Poland seized Vilna in October 1920 and refused a plebiscite or the Hymans plan; France would not put pressure on its ally and Britain would not act alone; the League had no force. The Conference of Ambassadors accepted Polish possession on 15 March 1923.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the League of Nations was able to stop the Greek invasion of Bulgaria in 1925.
A: Indicative content: Greece was a small state with no great-power backer; Britain and France, the leading members, agreed on what to do; Briand, the French prime minister, was President of the Council and acted at once, demanding a ceasefire and withdrawal and summoning the Council; the League sent officers to watch the withdrawal; Greece obeyed, and a commission of inquiry found it at fault and it paid compensation of 45,000 pounds. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: it was the agreement of the great powers that made the League's authority real, and the weakness of Greece that made obedience likely. It ends with a judgement on the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Mandates Commission had little power to change how mandated territories were governed.
A: Indicative content: it had no right to inspect, only to read the mandatories' own annual reports and receive petitions; it could not compel a mandatory to act and could only report to the Council, where the mandatories had seats and voting required unanimity; its members were experts, not governments; C-class mandates were governed almost as part of the mandatory's own territory; the mandatories were Britain, France and others who were the strongest powers in the League. Level 4: connect the lack of powers of investigation with the position of the mandatories inside the League, and judge which mattered more.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the League of Nations' work on humanitarian issues more successful than its work on territorial disputes in the 1920s?
A: For: Nansen and the repatriation of over 400,000 prisoners of war, the Nansen passport; the ILO conventions and the Slavery Convention; fewer limits on the work, as states accepted it because it did not threaten their vital interests. Against: territorial disputes show real successes (Aaland, Upper Silesia, Greco-Bulgarian) as well as failures (Vilna); the humanitarian work had limits in funds and power (no ILO enforcement; slavery and forced labour continued); and the political successes prevented possible wars. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. the scale and lasting effect of the results, and judges, e.g., that the humanitarian record was steadier but less important to peace, while the dispute record was more mixed but included the most significant tests.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The League of Nations failed because Britain and France did not give it the support it needed.' How far do you agree?
A: For: France wanted force while Britain refused commitments; neither wanted to act alone; Vilna (France protected Poland), Corfu 1923 (Britain and France did not support the League against Italy); the 1921 resolutions weakened sanctions; the great powers decided matters themselves in the Conference of Ambassadors. Against: the USA's absence, the exclusion of Germany and the USSR, the unanimity rule and the lack of a force; Britain and France did make the League work when they agreed (1925); and the structure made the League dependent on their will. Level 5 judges, e.g., that the Covenant gave the two powers a decisive role, so their lack of support was the main cause, but it was a lack of agreement as much as of will.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above, "Sources for exam practice question 5"). Compare these two sources as evidence about the League's concern with human welfare.
A: Similarities: both treat the welfare of people as a matter of international concern, and both speak in general terms of principle; both come from official League texts of 1919 and 1926. Differences: A is about the peoples of the mandates and sets out a principle of "sacred trust" which gives the mandatories power over them; B defines slavery to make a legal prohibition possible; A is a statement of an ideal for the colonial peoples, while B is a definition used in a treaty to abolish a practice. Explanation: A was drafted in 1919 by the victors who were taking over the colonies and wished to justify it; B was drafted by a commission of experts in 1926 after reports of slavery in Africa and Arabia, and it could be enforced only by the states that signed. Provenance: A is part of the Covenant, written for the Peace Conference; B is part of a convention that had to be ratified. Level 4 explains the differences through the circumstances and judges which is better evidence of what the League could do (B shows a specific legal standard, A shows an aim that could cover or excuse empire).
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above, "Sources for exam practice question 6"). How far do these sources support the view that the League of Nations was founded to do more than prevent war?
H: Which sources are about aims other than keeping the peace, and which is about the peace itself?
H: Remember that all four sources are provisions of the Covenant: they show intentions. What do you know about how each aim turned out?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: A (labour conditions), B (treatment of native inhabitants) and C (supervision of traffic in women, children and drugs) show social and humanitarian aims set into the Covenant itself. Challenge: D (disarmament) is directly about keeping the peace, and Articles 10–16 (own knowledge) made collective security the League's central purpose. Evaluation with knowledge: all four are the Covenant's own words, so they are the best evidence of what the founders intended, but intentions are not results: the ILO established over sixty conventions, but states could ignore them; the drug and traffic supervision was real work but small; disarmament achieved almost nothing in the 1920s; "just treatment" in B had no means of enforcement. Level 5 judges how far: the sources show that the founders wrote a wide programme into the Covenant, so they support the statement to a large extent as a view of purposes, but peace remained the central aim and the funds and staff the League spent on the other tasks were small.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent was unanimity the main reason for the League's weakness in the 1920s?
H: Which cases show unanimity blocking action, and which show it being unimportant?
H: Compare it with the lack of an army, the absence of the USA and the great powers' disagreements.
A: For: Article 5 meant any state could stop a decision, and the Council could not act at Vilna without Poland's consent; compromises were made to win agreement (the 1921 resolutions on Article 16); unanimity encouraged delay. Against: parties to a dispute were not counted; in 1925 the Council acted swiftly and with unanimity; the lack of force and the absence of the USA meant that even a unanimous decision could not be enforced; the real obstacle at Vilna was France's protection of Poland. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "what prevented action in the cases that mattered?" and judges, e.g., that unanimity mattered when the great powers disagreed, but the underlying cause was their unwillingness to use force.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far did the League of Nations help to maintain peace in Europe between 1920 and 1925?
H: Divide the period into disputes that were settled and disputes that were not.
H: Ask how much of the peace of these years was because of the League and how much because of other things (exhaustion, the Locarno process, the Conference of Ambassadors).
A: For: Aaland 1921, Upper Silesia 1921, Greco-Bulgarian 1925, Memel convention 1924; its forum let small states bring complaints; no general war in Europe. Against: Vilna and the Polish seizure stood; Teschen and Memel were settled by the Conference of Ambassadors; the Ruhr occupation (1923) and the Corfu crisis (1923) happened outside the League's control; peace in these years rested also on the exhaustion of the powers and on the settlements between them. Level 5 judges how far, e.g. the League contributed substantially to peace in minor disputes, but not in the main tests of great power relations.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ "The League of Nations was not a failure in the 1920s; it was never given a chance to work." Using the evidence of this chapter, explain how far you agree.
H: In what sense was the League "never given a chance"? Which of its weaknesses came from the decisions of the founders, and which from later events?
H: Which of the disputes in the 1920s were serious tests?
A: For: the USA's withdrawal was a political accident of Senate politics; the Covenant was a compromise that denied the League the force it needed; Britain and France would not use it for their own reasons; many disputes were solved. Against: the weaknesses were built in by the founders, who chose unanimity and no force; the great powers had a free choice about how to use the League and used it only when it suited them; the test of Vilna shows that the League's structure could not deal with a determined state. A* judgement: the League worked within the limits that its members set for it, so the claim is right that it was not given a chance to act against a strong state, but this was the result of choices made in 1919 and after, not an accident, and so the description "never given a chance" hides the responsibility of its founders and leading members.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ The Mandates Commission is sometimes praised as the most effective part of the League's work. How convincing is this view?
H: What could the Commission do that no earlier body had done?
H: Who sat on it and what could it not do?
A: Strengths: it was a permanent body of experts, examined the annual reports in public, received petitions from inhabitants, produced criticism of mandatories (for example of the treatment of the Bondelswarts in South West Africa in 1922, and later cases), and set a precedent for international supervision. Weaknesses: no inspection, no power to compel; the mandatories sat on the Council; it could not touch the question of independence; C mandates were governed almost as colonies. Judgement: effective as a source of scrutiny and publicity compared with earlier colonial rule, but not as a means of changing how the territories were run, and so the praise is partly justified if the standard is the 1920s empires and unconvincing if the standard is self-government.

:::recap
- **Creation**: Wilson's Point 14 (8 January 1918); Covenant adopted 28 April 1919 and put in every treaty; League in force 10 January 1920; first Assembly 15 November 1920; Geneva; 42 members at the start, 58 at the peak.
- **Aims**: collective security (Articles 10, 11, 16), peaceful settlement (Articles 12–15), disarmament (Article 8), cooperation and mandates (Articles 22 and 23).
- **Structure**: Assembly (one vote each), Council (permanent Britain, France, Italy, Japan; non-permanent 4, then 6 in 1922, 9 in 1926; Germany permanent 1926), Secretariat (Drummond), Permanent Court (1922), agencies.
- **Weaknesses**: the USA never joined; Germany from 1926, USSR from 1934; Britain and France led with different aims; unanimity (Article 5); no army, sanctions only (Article 16).
- **Disputes**: Aaland 1921 (success); Teschen 1920 (Conference of Ambassadors); Vilna 1920–23 (failure); Upper Silesia 1921 (success); Memel 1923–24 (mixed); Greco-Bulgarian 1925 (success; 45,000 pounds).
- **Agencies**: Nansen and refugees (Nansen passport 1922); ILO (1919; Albert Thomas); Slavery Convention (1926); Disarmament Commission; Mandates Commission (A, B, C).
- **Judgement**: effective in minor disputes and humanitarian work, ineffective against strong states; its fate depended on the great powers' will and on a Covenant that gave no force.
