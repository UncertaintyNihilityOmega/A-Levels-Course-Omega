:::why
By 1921 the treaties had been signed and the League was up and running, yet the peace was not settled. Within four years an Italian poet had seized a city, a French and Belgian army had occupied the richest industrial region of Germany, a paper mark had become worth less than the paper it was printed on, and Italy had bombarded a Greek island. This chapter explains why. It matters because the answer is not one cause but several that fed each other: unfinished business from the treaties, money, nationalism, and powers that wanted different things from the peace. Learning to weigh those against each other is the central skill of the Paper 2 essay.
:::

## Unfinished business: Fiume

### The seizure of a city

The first crisis came out of a gap in the peace treaties themselves. In 1915 Italy had joined the Allies after the secret **Treaty of London**, which promised her territory on the Adriatic coast: the Trentino, South Tyrol, Trieste, Istria and much of Dalmatia. It did not promise her **Fiume** (now Rijeka in Croatia), a port with a mixed population in which Italians were the largest group in the town centre and Croats the majority in the surrounding district. Italy's claim to Fiume was based on nationality, but Wilson's principle of **self-determination** (see chapter 1) cut both ways: at Paris he refused Italy both Dalmatia, which the Treaty of London had promised, and Fiume, which it had not. In April 1919 the Italian Prime Minister, Vittorio Emanuele Orlando, walked out of the conference for a fortnight. He came back, but Italians felt that they had paid more than 600,000 dead for a "**mutilated victory**".

:::def Mutilated victory
The phrase (*vittoria mutilata*) used by Italian nationalists in 1918–19 for the belief that Italy had won the war but was being cheated of the territory it had been promised at Paris. It fed the nationalism that Mussolini later used.
:::

On 12 September 1919 the poet and war hero **Gabriele d'Annunzio** marched into Fiume at the head of a few hundred Italian nationalist volunteers, many of them army deserters, and declared that the city was Italian. Regular Italian soldiers in the city did not stop him. The Italian government, led by Francesco Nitti, did not dare use force against a national hero, but it would not annex the city either, because the Allies would not agree. D'Annunzio ruled Fiume for about fifteen months in a theatrical style that Mussolini studied closely: balcony speeches, black-shirted followers, the Roman salute and mass chanting. In 1920 he proclaimed the "Italian Regency of Carnaro" with a constitution that gave music a special role in the state.

### Solution and aftermath

Italy and the new Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia) settled the Adriatic dispute directly in the **Treaty of Rapallo** of 12 November 1920. Italy got Istria, Trieste, Zara and some islands; most of Dalmatia went to Yugoslavia; **Fiume was to be a free state** whose independence both sides would respect. D'Annunzio refused to accept it. In the "Bloody Christmas" of 24–30 December 1920 regular Italian forces, including warships, attacked his legion and forced him to leave. Dozens of people died.

The free state did not last. Italian nationalists, now led by Mussolini, kept up pressure, and a coup by Italian nationalists in 1922 overthrew the free state's government. On 27 January 1924, in the **Treaty of Rome**, Yugoslavia accepted that Fiume would be Italian, while the port suburb of Sušak went to Yugoslavia.

:::warn Two treaties of Rapallo
There are two. The Treaty of Rapallo of **12 November 1920** is between Italy and Yugoslavia (Fiume, the Adriatic). The Treaty of Rapallo of **16 April 1922** is between Germany and Soviet Russia. The syllabus's "Rapallo Pact, 1922" is the German–Soviet one. Always write the date or the countries; examiners mark a confused answer down.
:::

### What Fiume tells us about 1919–29

Fiume mattered more for what it showed than for the city itself:
1. A **victor** state felt cheated by the settlement: dissatisfaction was not only German.
2. The government of a Great Power could not or would not stop an armed adventurer, and the League played no part in the solution: it was settled by direct talks between two states.
3. The methods of d'Annunzio (the uniforms, the salutes, direct action by volunteers) became the model for **Italian Fascism**, so the crisis helped bring Mussolini to power in 1922 and, with him, a government whose ambitions caused the Corfu crisis.

:::key What Fiume shows
1. The peace left victors as well as losers dissatisfied.
2. Nationalism and national feeling could push governments into acts that threatened the peace.
3. Disputes between neighbours were settled by bargaining and force, not by the League.
:::

## The problems of the successor states

### What a successor state was

The collapse of the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian and Ottoman empires in 1917–18 produced new or enlarged states in Eastern and Central Europe. The peace treaties drew their frontiers. These **successor states** were Poland, Czechoslovakia, the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (renamed Yugoslavia in 1929), and enlarged Romania, together with the much smaller Austria and Hungary, which were left as the remains of the old empire.

:::def Successor states
The states created or enlarged out of the territory of the Austro-Hungarian, German, Russian and Ottoman empires after 1918, and the rump states (Austria, Hungary) that were left from the Austro-Hungarian Empire.
:::

Their weakness was part of the reason why tensions remained high. Three kinds of problem recur, and each state shows one or more:

| Kind of problem | What it meant | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethnic** | Large minorities who did not feel they belonged, because self-determination could not be applied everywhere (chapter 1) | Germans and Hungarians in Czechoslovakia; Ukrainians, Germans and Jews in Poland; Croats and Slovenes in Yugoslavia; Magyars in Romania and Czechoslovakia |
| **Political** | Young, fragile democracies, quarrels between parties and regions, and strong men who ended parliamentary government | Yugoslavia (the murder of Radić, 1928; the king's dictatorship, 1929); Poland (Piłsudski's coup, 1926); Hungary (Admiral Horthy's regency) |
| **Economic** | New frontiers cut across railways, markets and rivers; the new states built tariff walls; there was inflation and little capital | Austria's hyperinflation and the League loan; the Polish–German customs war |

### The states one by one

**Poland** had been partitioned for over a century and was re-created in 1918. Its frontiers were not fixed by the treaties alone: they were fought for. In the **Polish–Soviet War** of 1919–21 the Poles under Marshal **Józef Piłsudski** beat the Red Army outside Warsaw in August 1920 and took land well east of the line proposed by the Allies; the Treaty of Riga (18 March 1921) fixed the frontier. Poland also fought Czechoslovakia over Teschen and Germany over Upper Silesia (both settled with League and Allied help: see chapter 2) and seized Vilna from Lithuania in 1920. About a third of its people were not Poles: Ukrainians, Jews, Germans and Belorussians. The "Polish Corridor", the strip of former German land that gave Poland a route to the sea and split East Prussia from the rest of Germany, was the most resented frontier in Germany. Poland was parliamentary in the early 1920s, but weak governments, a currency collapse in 1923 and a military coup by Piłsudski in May 1926 ended the experiment. From 1925 it fought a damaging tariff war with Germany.

**Czechoslovakia** was the most successful: it inherited the industrial heart of the old Habsburg lands, and it kept a working democracy until 1938. But it was a state of Czechs and Slovaks, and its census of 1921 counted just under a quarter of its people (about three million) as Germans, concentrated in the Sudeten border regions, plus Hungarians, Ruthenians and Poles. German and Slovak politicians complained that the state was run by and for the Czechs. In the 1920s German parties joined the government, so this was a tension that did not yet explode. Czechoslovakia's security rested on its alliances: with France (1924) and with Romania and Yugoslavia in the Little Entente.

**Yugoslavia** was a union of Serbs, Croats, Slovenes and others under the Serbian royal family. The Serbs saw it as a greater Serbia; the Croats, led by **Stjepan Radić**, wanted a federation. The **Vidovdan Constitution** of 28 June 1921 created a centralised state, which the Croats refused to accept. The quarrel ended in violence: on 20 June 1928 a Montenegrin deputy shot Radić and other Croat deputies in the parliament; Radić died of his wounds in August. On 6 January 1929 King Alexander suspended the constitution and ruled as a dictator, and renamed the country Yugoslavia in October.

**Hungary** lost about two thirds of its pre-war territory and population in the Treaty of **Trianon** (4 June 1920), leaving about three million Hungarian speakers outside its frontiers in Czechoslovakia, Romania and Yugoslavia. After the brief Communist republic of Béla Kun (March–August 1919) and a counter-revolution, Admiral **Miklós Horthy** became regent in March 1920 in a kingdom with no king. Hungary's one great aim was **revision** of the treaty, which made it the enemy of its three neighbours, and so the reason for their alliance. When the former Emperor Charles twice tried to regain the Hungarian throne in 1921 (March and October), Czechoslovakia mobilised and threatened war.

**Austria**, the German-speaking rump of the empire, was a country of six and a half million people with an enormous capital, Vienna. Its people overwhelmingly wanted union with Germany (**Anschluss**), which the treaties forbade. It suffered hyperinflation in 1921–22 and was saved only by a League of Nations loan, arranged in the Geneva Protocols of October 1922 on condition that it kept its independence and accepted League supervision of its finances. It became the League's flagship success, and a reminder of how fragile the new states were.

**Romania** doubled in size, gaining Transylvania from Hungary, Bessarabia from Russia and the Bukovina, and with them large Hungarian, Ukrainian, Jewish and German minorities. It had no desire for revision, and it joined the Little Entente.

:::key Why the successor states kept tensions high
1. **Minorities** gave neighbouring states (Germany for the Sudeten Germans and for Poland's Germans, Hungary for the Magyars) a reason to demand frontier changes: **irredentism** (claiming land inhabited by your own nationals).
2. **Revisionist** states (Hungary, Germany, Bulgaria) wanted to overturn the treaties; **satisfied** states (France, Czechoslovakia, Romania, Yugoslavia, Poland) wanted to defend them. This split dominated every later crisis.
3. **Economic fragmentation**: the old empire had been a large free-trade area, and the new states put up tariffs and quarrelled over railways and rivers. Poor, divided states were open to extremism.
4. **Weak democracy**: by 1929 Hungary, Poland and Yugoslavia had authoritarian governments of one kind or another.
5. **Great-power rivalry**: France built a ring of allies (Poland 1921, Czechoslovakia 1924, Romania 1926, Yugoslavia 1927) so that Germany could be contained, while Britain stayed out of Eastern Europe.
:::

:::def Irredentism
The policy of claiming territory outside a state's frontiers because people of the same nationality live there. Hungary's claim to the Magyar minorities of its neighbours and Italy's claim to Fiume are examples.
:::

### The Little Entente, 1920–21

The three states most threatened by Hungary joined together. The **Little Entente** was a set of three bilateral defensive treaties: Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia (14 August 1920), Czechoslovakia and Romania (23 April 1921) and Romania and Yugoslavia (7 June 1921). Each promised help if Hungary attacked, and the Czechoslovak and Romanian treaties also aimed to block a Habsburg restoration. Its foreign policy was run by the Czechoslovak Foreign Minister, **Edvard Beneš**, and it was backed by France, which signed its own treaties with each member between 1924 and 1927.

*Aims:* to keep the frontiers of Trianon and prevent a return of the Habsburgs. *Impact:* it worked as a deterrent against Hungary, and it gave France an eastern ring of allies. It also divided Europe into satisfied and dissatisfied states, and it was weak: its members had different priorities (Czechoslovakia feared Germany, while Yugoslavia and Romania feared Hungary and Bulgaria and cared little about Germany), they never built a joint army, and they depended on French support that faded after 1933.

:::key Weighing it: were the successor states a cause of tension or its victims?
A strong answer on the successor states does not blame them. It shows that the **settlement** put minorities and quarrels into states too weak to manage them, and that great powers then used those quarrels. The weak point was the "inconsistent application of self-determination" you met in chapter 1: Keynes, writing in 1919, said the Treaty included "nothing to stabilize the new States of Europe". But the states' own leaders also chose to centralise (Yugoslavia), to seize land (Poland, Vilna and the Polish–Soviet frontier) and to refuse reconciliation (Hungary). Cause and victim at once is the best judgement.
:::

## Reparations, the Ruhr and the German hyperinflation

### The reparations quarrel, 1921–22

Chapter 1 explained how the Reparation Commission set Germany's bill. In **May 1921** the Allies presented the **London Schedule of Payments**: a total of **132 billion gold marks**, to be paid in annuities, backed by an ultimatum threatening occupation of the Ruhr if Germany did not accept within six days. In fact only part of that sum, 50 billion gold marks of the "A" and "B" bonds, was to be paid in the near future; the rest ("C" bonds) was a paper claim that Allied negotiators privately did not expect to collect. Germany accepted and paid the first instalment in 1921, but from 1922 it asked for a **moratorium** (a delay in paying).

France and Britain disagreed about what to do, and this disagreement was the real centre of the crisis:
- **France** needed the money. The northern departments had been devastated, France owed war debts to the USA and Britain, and it expected German payments to rebuild and to repay them. The prime minister from January 1922, **Raymond Poincaré**, a lawyer from Lorraine, believed that Germany was not paying because it did not want to, and that without coercion the treaty would become a dead letter.
- **Britain** (under Lloyd George until October 1922) held that a ruined Germany could neither pay nor trade. A prosperous Germany would buy British goods, and the more reparations were cut the faster Europe would recover. Keynes's *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919) had made this the favourite British argument.
- **Germany** argued that it could not pay: it had lost 13 per cent of its land and a very large share of its coal and iron ore. The government of Chancellor **Joseph Wirth** pursued a policy of "fulfilment" in 1921–22 (paying to show that the sum was beyond Germany's means), and his Foreign Minister **Walther Rathenau** tried the same approach in diplomacy, until right-wing extremists murdered him on 24 June 1922.

The **Genoa Conference** (April–May 1922), which Lloyd George called to link reparations and the reconstruction of Europe, broke down (see below). In the second half of 1922 the mark fell fast, and Germany asked for a moratorium of two or three years. Poincaré refused. On **26 December 1922** the Reparation Commission, by a majority vote in which Britain's delegate dissented, declared Germany in default over deliveries of **timber**; in early January 1923 it found a default over **coal** too.

:::def Moratorium
An agreed delay in making payments. In 1922 Germany asked the Allies for a moratorium on reparations; France refused.
:::

### The Ruhr occupation, 1923

On **11 January 1923** French and Belgian troops, with engineers to run the mines, moved into the **Ruhr**, the coal and steel region on the right bank of the Rhine which produced most of Germany's coal. There were about 60,000 men at first and perhaps 100,000 later (estimates vary). Poincaré's stated aim was not conquest but a **productive pledge** (*gage productif*): to seize coal and revenue so that Germany would pay. Behind it lay a larger French aim, which Poincaré did not mention: to weaken Germany, and perhaps to keep the Rhineland separate from it. Italy sent only a few engineers, and Britain, which thought the occupation illegal, kept its troops out and did not break with France.

Germany answered with **passive resistance**. The government of Chancellor **Wilhelm Cuno**, backed by a Reichstag vote on 13 January (283 to 12), ordered officials and workers in the Ruhr not to obey the occupiers, and paid their wages. Mines stopped, railwaymen refused to run trains for the French, and the French answered by expelling thousands of officials and by arresting and shooting resisters. On 31 March 1923 French soldiers fired on a crowd at the Krupp works in Essen and killed thirteen workers. Albert Leo Schlageter, a former soldier who had blown up a railway line, was executed by the French in May, and German nationalists made him a martyr. In all, well over a hundred German civilians died during the occupation, and the Belgians lost soldiers to sabotage.

The occupation did not work as France had hoped. Production in the Ruhr fell, so France got little more coal than before and spent heavily on troops. Passive resistance cost Germany more: the government paid wages for work that was not being done, tax revenue collapsed, and it paid for the deficit by printing money. This produced the hyperinflation.

### German hyperinflation

:::def Hyperinflation
A period of extremely rapid price rises, when money loses value so fast that prices change daily or hourly. In Germany it ran through 1923, ending in November 1923.
:::

The German currency had been losing value since the war because the government had paid for it with loans and printed money, and not with taxes. The mark was worth about 4 to the US dollar in 1914. It was about 48 to the dollar by late 1919 and a few hundred in 1922. In January 1923, when the Ruhr was occupied, about 17,000 marks bought a dollar. Then the fall became a collapse. The Reichsbank printed notes by the billion to pay the strikers, and prices could double in days. By **November 1923 a dollar was worth about 4.2 trillion marks**.

The effects went beyond economics:
- **Savers and the middle class** were ruined: salaries, pensions and savings in paper money became worthless in weeks. Stresemann himself, in his Nobel lecture of 1927, described the middle class of officials, officers and professionals as paying with the loss of its wealth for its service to the state. Many of them never forgave the Republic.
- **Debtors, landowners and industrialists** with real assets did well, because their debts melted away.
- **Workers** were paid daily and spent the money at once; unemployment grew as businesses closed.
- **Politics** became violent: separatists tried to break the Rhineland away from Germany, the Bavarian government defied Berlin, the Communists planned risings in Saxony, Hamburg and elsewhere, and in November 1923 Hitler's Nazi Party tried to seize power in Munich in the Beer Hall Putsch.

### The end of the crisis

The deadlock could not last. In August 1923 Cuno resigned and the leader of the German People's Party, **Gustav Stresemann**, became Chancellor at the head of a grand coalition. On **26 September 1923** his government announced the **end of passive resistance**: Germany could not go on paying for a strike that was ruining its own people. It was a moment of humiliation, but Stresemann judged that Germany had to end the struggle, restore the currency and then negotiate. A new currency, the **Rentenmark**, was issued in November 1923, supposedly backed by a mortgage on German land and industry, and one Rentenmark was set equal to a trillion of the old marks. With a balanced budget and strict control of printing, the inflation stopped. At the same time Britain and the USA, alarmed by the chaos, pressed France to accept a new study of Germany's capacity to pay. That study became the **Dawes Plan** of 1924 (chapter 4).

:::key Why France occupied the Ruhr in 1923
1. **Money**: France needed reparations to rebuild its devastated north and to repay its own war debts; Germany had defaulted (timber, then coal).
2. **Security**: a weak Germany was a safe Germany; seizing the Ruhr gave France a hold on German industry and a way to keep control of the Rhineland.
3. **Belief that Germany was not trying**: Poincaré thought Germany was deliberately avoiding payment.
4. **Legality**: France and Belgium argued that the Treaty of Versailles allowed "measures" against a defaulting Germany, though Britain called the occupation illegal.
5. **Isolation did not deter France**: the USA stayed out, and Britain, which disagreed, did not stop it. No international body could.
:::

### What the Ruhr crisis did to international relations

- **It split the wartime allies.** Britain and France could no longer agree about the treaty they had made. The Anglo-French entente that had won the war became a quarrel.
- **It showed what a Great Power could do alone.** France ignored Britain, the League and American protests.
- **It also showed the limits of that power.** The occupation did not make Germany pay, it hurt the French franc, and the pressure from Britain and the USA forced France to accept the Dawes Plan.
- **It changed German politics.** It brought Stresemann to power, whose decision to give up resistance and cooperate opened the way for the improvement of 1924–29, but it also helped the extreme right, which blamed the Republic for defeat and inflation.
- **It made the USA return in a financial role**, as a lender, not as a member of the League.

:::note The historians (stretch for A*)
Opinion differs on who was most at fault. **Sally Marks**, *The Illusion of Peace* (1976), and in an article on "the myths of reparations" (1978), argued that the reparations burden was less crushing than Germans claimed and that German governments did not try hard to pay. **Conan Fischer**, *The Ruhr Crisis 1923–1924* (2003), looked closely at the experience of the Ruhr's own workers and employers, and not only at governments. **Zara Steiner**, *The Lights that Failed* (2005), treats the Ruhr as the end of France's attempt to enforce the treaty alone. You do not need these names, but a point such as "historians disagree about whether Germany could have paid" shows awareness.
:::

## Corfu, 1923

### What happened

In 1923 the Greek–Albanian frontier was being marked out by a commission appointed by the **Conference of Ambassadors**, the body of Allied ambassadors in Paris that had been settling frontier questions since 1919. Its Italian member, **General Enrico Tellini**, was an experienced soldier. On **27 August 1923**, on a road in Greek territory near Janina (Ioannina), he and three Italian colleagues and an Albanian interpreter were ambushed and shot dead. Nobody was ever proved to be guilty. Italy blamed the Greeks; the Greek government said that Albanian bandits were more likely.

Two days later, on **29 August**, the new Italian Prime Minister, **Benito Mussolini**, who had held office for ten months, delivered an **ultimatum** to Greece. It demanded, with a reply required within twenty-four hours:
- a formal apology at the Italian legation in Athens;
- a solemn funeral service in the Catholic cathedral in Athens, attended by the whole Greek government;
- honours to the Italian flag from the Greek fleet at Piraeus;
- a strict inquiry, to be finished within five days with the help of the Italian military attaché;
- the death penalty for the guilty;
- an indemnity of 50 million lire within five days;
- military honours for the victims' bodies.

Greece accepted four demands in a modified form (it would not accept an inquiry run in part by Italians, the death penalty before trial, or the indemnity) and offered to let the Hague Court or the League decide. Mussolini called the reply unacceptable. On **31 August** an Italian naval squadron bombarded the old fortress at **Corfu** and Italian troops landed and occupied the island. Greek sources count about sixteen civilians killed and some thirty wounded, mostly refugees from Asia Minor who were living in the fortress; other reports give slightly higher figures.

### The League and the Conference of Ambassadors

Greece appealed to the League of Nations under the Covenant on 1 September. Mussolini's government told the Council that it had no right to interfere, and the Italian delegate refused to discuss the matter. Italy hinted that it would leave the League. The British Foreign Secretary, **Lord Curzon**, called Mussolini's action violent and inexcusable and wanted the League to deal with it. France, which was in the middle of the Ruhr occupation, did not want the League to set a precedent for judging the use of force by a Great Power. After days of argument the dispute was referred to the **Conference of Ambassadors**, which the Italians accepted because it was run by the great powers, not by the League, and the great powers could do what they chose.

On 8 September the Conference proposed the terms. Greece would hold a funeral service and a salute to the Italian flag, conduct an inquiry under the supervision of an international commission, and deposit 50 million lire in a Swiss bank. On 26 September it awarded the 50 million lire to Italy (as compensation, on the ground that the Greek authorities had been negligent). Italian troops left Corfu on **27 September**, having been told by Britain and France that they must go by that date. Mussolini presented the outcome at home as a victory.

:::key Corfu: what it showed
1. A **Great Power** could use force against a weak state and get away with it: Greece paid, Italy won.
2. The **League** was sidelined. The Council did not condemn Italy, and the Conference of Ambassadors, a great-power body, decided the outcome.
3. **France and Britain disagreed**: Britain wanted the League to act, while France, busy in the Ruhr, did not. The League's weakness was the weakness of the great powers' own unity.
4. **Mussolini's methods** (a violent ultimatum, a rapid occupation, nationalist propaganda) were a preview of the 1930s.
5. Corfu is **not** the whole story of the League's record: in 1925 it stopped a Greek–Bulgarian war within days (chapter 2). The difference was that Greece, a small power, was the target of the League's pressure there and not Italy.
:::

:::warn Treat Corfu as a crisis of the settlement, not only a League story
The syllabus lists Corfu among the **crises and tensions** that kept the peace unstable (KQ 8.3). A strong essay on 8.3 uses Corfu as evidence that international relations remained dangerous because states still used force and the victors disagreed, and does not merely repeat the League's weaknesses from chapter 2. Link it to Italy's sense of a "mutilated victory", to Mussolini's need for a foreign success in his first year, and to the Conference of Ambassadors, which belonged to the old way of settling affairs.
:::

## Treaties and conferences, 1920–23

Between 1920 and 1923 the powers tried to build a settled order by conference. Five meetings are on the syllabus. They show the same pattern: each tried to solve a problem the treaties had left, but each left others behind.

### The Washington Conference, 1921–22

**Why it met.** Three problems were pressing. The first was an **arms race** in the Pacific: the USA, Britain and Japan were all building battleships, and the American Congress did not want to pay for a fleet to match the British one and the Japanese one together. The second was the **Anglo-Japanese Alliance** of 1902, which Britain's dominions (especially Canada) and the USA disliked, because it could in theory draw Britain into a war against the USA. The third was the **Far East**: Japan had pressed China with the Twenty-One Demands in 1915 and had been given the former German rights in Shandong at Paris, and the USA wanted the "Open Door" (equal commercial access to China) respected.

President **Warren Harding** invited eight countries to meet in Washington; it was held outside the League. Nine states attended: the USA, Britain, Japan, France, Italy, China, Belgium, the Netherlands and Portugal. Germany and Soviet Russia were not invited. The US Secretary of State, **Charles Evans Hughes**, opened it on **12 November 1921** with a bold plan: scrap many battleships (about sixty-six ships and nearly 1.9 million tons, according to reports at the time) and stop building new capital ships for ten years.

**Three treaties** came out of it:

| Treaty | Signed | Members | Main terms |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Four-Power Treaty** | 13 December 1921 | USA, Britain, France, Japan | Respect each other's island possessions in the Pacific and consult if they were threatened; replaced the Anglo-Japanese Alliance |
| **Five-Power Naval Treaty** | 6 February 1922 | USA, Britain, Japan, France, Italy | Limits on capital ships (battleships) and aircraft carriers; ten years without new capital ships; no new fortifications on Pacific islands (Article XIX) |
| **Nine-Power Treaty** | 6 February 1922 | The nine states at the conference | Respect China's independence and territory and keep the Open Door; no enforcement |

The **Five-Power Treaty** set a total of capital-ship tonnage for each navy: the USA and Britain 525,000 tons each, Japan 315,000 tons, France and Italy 175,000 tons each. That is a ratio of about **5 : 5 : 3 : 1.67 : 1.67** (the last two are often quoted as 1.75). No capital ship could be larger than 35,000 tons or have guns larger than 16 inches. Aircraft carriers were limited to 135,000 tons each for the USA and Britain, 81,000 for Japan and 60,000 for France and Italy. The treaty was to run until the end of 1936. Alongside it, Japan agreed (in a separate treaty signed on 4 February 1922) to hand Shandong back to China.

**Impact.** For several years the Washington system looked like a success.
- It was the first great **arms-limitation** agreement of the modern age, and the three biggest navies did scrap ships. The USA, Britain and Japan stuck to the capital-ship limits until the mid-1930s.
- It ended the **Anglo-Japanese Alliance**, so that Britain no longer risked being drawn into a war between Japan and the USA, but it hurt Japanese pride, since the alliance had meant equality with a Great Power.
- It **defused** tension in the Pacific for a decade.
- But it only limited **capital ships**: cruisers, destroyers and submarines were largely left alone, and the powers began a cruiser-building race. A conference at Geneva in 1927 failed to fix this, and the London Naval Treaty of 1930 partly did.
- **Japan** accepted the 5:3 ratio, but the navy and nationalists thought it an insult. They said Japan had been given inferior status. The ban on new fortifications in the Pacific (Article XIX) meant that neither side could build up bases near the other, which left the American Philippines hard to defend, a point critics raised.
- The Nine-Power Treaty **respected China** in words but had no means of enforcement, and it did not remove Japan's gains in Manchuria. When Japan decided to break the treaties in 1931 there was nothing to stop it.
- Britain, which could not afford a naval race with the USA, accepted **parity** with it: a sign that the USA had become the leading naval power in everything but name.

:::def Capital ship
In the naval treaties of 1922–36, a warship of more than 10,000 tons with guns over 8 inches, in practice a battleship or battlecruiser. Limits on capital ships were the heart of the Washington treaty.
:::

### The Genoa Conference, 1922

The **Genoa Conference** (10 April – 19 May 1922) was the idea of the British Prime Minister **David Lloyd George**, who proposed it at the Cannes conference in January 1922. Representatives of thirty-four European states met in Genoa, among them Germany and Soviet Russia for the first time at a major conference with the Allies. The USA had been invited but did not take part. Lloyd George's aim was to restore the economy of **Europe**: to bring Germany and Russia back into trade with the West, to settle the problem of reparations and the Russian debts, and to stabilise currencies. In effect it was an attempt to repair the economic gaps in the 1919 treaties, as Keynes had urged.

It failed. The French delegation, led by **Louis Barthou**, was determined to protect the Versailles terms and to refuse any reduction of reparations. The Allies demanded that Soviet Russia should recognise the debts of the tsarist governments and return foreign property that had been nationalised. The Soviet delegation, led by **Georgy Chicherin**, answered with its own claims for damage done by the Allied intervention in the Civil War, and no agreement was possible. Genoa produced no financial aid for Russia and no settlement of reparations. The issues were handed to a further conference at The Hague in June–July 1922 that also failed.

**Impact:** Genoa failed, and its most important result was the Treaty of Rapallo, signed on the sidelines.

### The Treaty of Rapallo, 1922

During Genoa the German and Soviet delegations feared that the Allies would settle with Russia at Germany's expense. On **Easter Sunday, 16 April 1922**, the German Foreign Minister **Walther Rathenau** and the Soviet Foreign Commissar **Georgy Chicherin** slipped away from Genoa to the nearby resort of Rapallo and signed a treaty after an overnight negotiation.

:::def The Treaty of Rapallo (1922)
The agreement of 16 April 1922 between Germany and Soviet Russia by which they restored diplomatic and consular relations, cancelled each other's claims for war costs and war damage, and agreed to trade on the most-favoured-nation principle.
:::

Its published terms were modest:
- both governments **waived claims** for war costs, war damages and the expense of looking after prisoners (Article 1);
- Germany waived claims for property nationalised in Russia, provided Russia did not pay such claims to a third party (Article 2);
- **diplomatic and consular relations** were resumed immediately (Article 3);
- trade was to be on the **most-favoured-nation** principle (Article 4) and the two governments would work together to meet the economic needs of both countries (Article 5).

There was nothing in it about military cooperation. But the Reichswehr (the German army) and the Red Army began secret cooperation soon afterwards, training and developing weapons that Versailles forbade Germany, on Russian soil, and the Allies suspected as much.

**Why each side signed.** Germany was isolated and feared a deal between Russia and the Allies; it saw Russia as a potential trading partner and a counterweight to France and Poland. Russia, as an outcast, wanted recognition and trade, and Rapallo broke the united front of capitalist states against it.

**Impact.** Rapallo made a great impression. In Britain and France it gave birth to the fear of a German–Soviet bloc: Poincaré took it as a sign that Germany was not to be trusted, and it hardened French opinion before the Ruhr. For Germany it was a first sign that it had choices, and of an independent foreign policy: the "Rapallo option" of friendship with the East against the West would return in the Berlin Treaty of 1926. Poland, which lay between the two, feared that it could be partitioned again. On the other hand, it did not harm Germany's relations with Britain. Rathenau, who was Jewish and the symbol of "fulfilment" for the extreme right, was murdered by right-wing extremists on 24 June 1922.

### The Treaty of Lausanne, 1923

The Treaty of Sèvres (1920) had broken up the Ottoman Empire and left Turkey with very little. It was never carried out. A nationalist revolt under **Mustafa Kemal** (later Atatürk) formed a rival government at Ankara, and the Greek army, which had occupied Smyrna with Allied permission in 1919 and advanced into Anatolia, was defeated in the **Greco-Turkish War**. In September 1922 the Turks drove the Greeks out of Smyrna, which was burned, and went on to the Straits, where a small British force stood at **Chanak**. For a few days in September and October 1922 Britain and Turkey were close to war; the French and Italians withdrew their troops rather than support Britain, and most of the dominions were unwilling to back it. An armistice at Mudanya (11 October 1922) averted war. The affair cost Lloyd George his position: his Conservative partners walked out of the coalition on 19 October 1922 and he resigned.

A new conference at **Lausanne**, Switzerland, produced the **Treaty of Lausanne**, signed on **24 July 1923** by Turkey (led by İsmet İnönü) and Britain, France, Italy, Japan, Greece, Romania and Yugoslavia, with Lord Curzon as the chief Allied negotiator.
- Turkey kept **eastern Thrace** and **Anatolia**, including Smyrna, and gave up claims to the Arab lands and the islands of the Aegean that went to Greece or Italy. Turkey's frontiers were fixed.
- The **capitulations**, the old privileges of foreigners in Turkey, were abolished (Article 28), and Turkey paid **no reparations**.
- The **Straits** (the Dardanelles and Bosporus) were demilitarised and open to the ships of all nations, under an international commission.
- A separate **convention** (30 January 1923) provided for the compulsory **exchange of populations** between Greece and Turkey: about 1.2 million Orthodox Christians moved from Turkey to Greece, and some 350,000–400,000 Muslims moved from Greece to Turkey (estimates differ). Greeks in Constantinople and Muslims in western Thrace were excluded.
- Turkey's sovereignty was recognised; the republic was proclaimed on 29 October 1923.

**Impact.** Lausanne is the only one of the post-war treaties to be replaced by a negotiated settlement in the early 1920s. It replaced a treaty that could not be enforced with one that Turkey accepted, and Greece and Turkey were reconciled in the 1930s. It showed that a peace made by negotiation among equals would hold when one imposed on a defeated state would not, and it made some Germans and Hungarians hope that Versailles and Trianon could be revised in the same way. But it also set a precedent: Turkey got its revision because it had won the war on the ground, and force had paid. It also uprooted a minority population on a huge scale.

:::key Why the treaties and conferences did not remove the tensions
1. They tackled **separate problems** (the Adriatic, the Pacific, Russia, Turkey) and never the central one, **Germany and reparations**, which the Ruhr then exploded.
2. **Key powers were absent or opposed**: the USA stayed out of Europe's politics, Germany and Russia were excluded from Washington, and France blocked compromise at Genoa.
3. The agreements **had no enforcement**: Washington and the Nine-Power Treaty relied on goodwill.
4. **Some deals created new suspicion**: Rapallo divided Germany and Russia from the West.
5. The one **clear success**, Lausanne, was a revision of a peace imposed on a defeated state, which suggests that treaties made by negotiation lasted better than those dictated.
:::

## Changing relations between the major powers

The great powers' positions shifted in the early 1920s. This section gives the picture for each; the sections above gave the detail.

### Britain

Britain wanted **peace, trade and economic recovery**, including the recovery of Germany. It was tired of European entanglements and worried about its empire and its finances (it owed large war debts to the USA). **Lloyd George** (to October 1922) sought to settle Europe by conference, as at Genoa, and by trade, as with the Anglo-Soviet agreement of March 1921. His successors, **Bonar Law** (1922–23) and **Stanley Baldwin** (1923–24, 1924–29) were more cautious. Britain disliked French policy in the Ruhr, refused to join it, and drifted away from France. It did not want to guarantee Eastern Europe or to commit troops to the continent. The first Labour government under **Ramsay MacDonald** (January–November 1924) was the first to recognise the Soviet Union (1 February 1924) and to back a settlement with Germany and France.

### France

France felt **insecure**. It had lost 1.4 million men; it had a smaller population and a smaller economy than Germany, and the British and American guarantees promised in 1919 had lapsed when the US Senate rejected the treaty. French policy therefore aimed to **enforce Versailles** to the letter, keep Germany weak and build allies in the east (Poland 1921, the Little Entente). This explains the Ruhr occupation. After its failure, French policy changed under the left-wing government of **Édouard Herriot** (1924), which accepted the Dawes Plan and began to talk to Germany.

### Germany

Germany, a republic from 1919, wanted to **revise** Versailles by peaceful means if possible: to end reparations, recover territory in the east and the right to rearm. The governments of Wirth and Rathenau used "fulfilment" in 1921–22. Rapallo showed its willingness to deal with Russia. After the Ruhr, **Stresemann** moved to cooperation with the West (chapter 4). Behind all German policy lay a real internal weakness: hyperinflation, coups and political murders (of the minister Matthias Erzberger in 1921 and of Rathenau in 1922) showed how fragile the Republic was in 1923.

### Italy

Italy was a **dissatisfied victor**. Mussolini's **fascist** government, in power from 31 October 1922, wanted the Italy that he said had been cheated in 1919 to be given a place in the sun: control of the Adriatic, influence in the Balkans, colonies and prestige. Corfu showed the danger. In practice Mussolini was unwilling in these years to break with Britain and France altogether: he joined the Locarno treaties and signed the Kellogg–Briand Pact (chapter 4), and he gave the Allies reason to treat him as a partner, even as he made speeches that promised more.

### The Soviet Union and the challenge of Communism

The Bolshevik government, which won the Civil War by 1920 and became the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics** in December 1922, was both a state and a revolutionary movement. In March 1919 it founded the **Communist International (Comintern)** in Moscow to encourage revolution abroad. That, and its repudiation of the tsarist debts, made the Western powers see it as a **threat**. They had intervened against it in the Civil War, and in the "Red Scare" of 1919–20 the USA arrested thousands of suspected radicals. At the same time the Soviet state needed trade and recognition, and the **New Economic Policy** (1921) and the Anglo-Soviet trade agreement (March 1921) were steps in that direction. It signed Rapallo with Germany, fought a war with Poland in 1920 (which it lost at Warsaw) and was recognised in 1924 by Britain and Italy (February) and France (October). Until 1934 it stayed outside the League. Fear of Communism made some Western leaders want a strong Germany as a barrier, and made all of them cautious about working with Russia; for Germany, Russia was a partner against the West.

### The USA: isolation or involvement?

After the Senate's vote against the treaty and the League in 1919–20, the USA made a separate peace with Germany (the Treaty of Berlin, August 1921) and followed a policy that is usually called **isolationism**: no membership of the League, no alliances, high tariffs (the Fordney–McCumber Tariff of 1922) and tight limits on immigration (1921, 1924). That is only half the truth. The USA was the world's largest creditor and economy, and it took the lead where its own interests were at stake: the **Washington Conference** (1921–22), the **Dawes Plan** (1924) and loans to Germany and Europe, the Young Plan (1929) and the **Kellogg–Briand Pact** (1928). It insisted that Britain and France repay their war debts, which tied reparations to debt repayments, and it refused to recognise Soviet Russia. The better description is **involvement without commitment**: the USA took part by money, by conferences and by pacts, but never by promises of military support, so Europe could not count on it.

### Japan: internal issues and the effect on international relations

Japan emerged from the war as the one Asian Great Power, with a seat on the League Council, a larger empire (the German Pacific islands under League mandate, and Shandong) and a booming economy. But it faced serious problems at home:
- **Politics**: In the 1920s, the "Taishō democracy" saw party governments, led by men such as **Hara Takashi** (assassinated in November 1921), and in 1925 universal male suffrage was introduced. But the army, navy and the nationalist right stayed powerful and the party governments were fragile.
- **Economy**: the post-war boom collapsed in 1920, and the Great Kantō earthquake of 1 September 1923, which destroyed much of Tokyo and Yokohama and killed over 100,000 people, was a severe blow. Rural poverty and unemployment grew.
- **Foreign policy**: governments led by the Foreign Minister **Shidehara Kijūrō** followed a policy of cooperation with the Western powers (the Washington treaties, the Kellogg–Briand Pact) and of trade with China. Nationalists and army officers wanted a harder line.
- **Race and status**: Japan's proposal for a racial equality clause in the League Covenant was rejected in 1919. The American **Immigration Act of 1924** excluded Japanese immigrants altogether, and caused outrage in Japan. Together with the 5:3 naval ratio, it fed the feeling of being treated as inferior.

Japan's relations with the West were therefore calm on the surface but fragile. The 1920s were a time of accommodation, but the underlying issues (China, markets, naval status, race) were unresolved, and the nationalists who wanted a different course gained ground.

:::key How the powers' relations had changed by 1923
1. **Britain and France**, allies in 1918, were at odds over the Ruhr and reparations.
2. **Germany** had stopped being a defeated pariah to become a state with options (Rapallo) and then a victim (the Ruhr).
3. **Italy**'s government had changed to a dictatorship that used threats.
4. **The Soviet Union** was an outsider whose revolution frightened the West, but a trading partner and diplomatic player.
5. **The USA** was absent from the League but vital for money and naval power.
6. **Japan** was cooperating but was increasingly resentful.
No single state was ready to lead, and that is why the tension persisted.
:::

## Why did tensions remain high? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on key question 8.3. Sort the causes by type, then ask how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| **Unfinished business of the treaties** | Fiume and the Adriatic; the frontiers and minorities of the successor states; reparations and war guilt | The treaties had left victors and losers dissatisfied and borders that could not satisfy everyone |
| **Money** | The London Schedule; German default; the Ruhr occupation; hyperinflation; war debts owed to the USA | Turned a quarrel about payment into a military occupation and a social collapse in the centre of Europe |
| **Nationalism and new leaders** | D'Annunzio; Mussolini and Corfu; Hungarian revision; Polish and Yugoslav nationalism | Made governments willing to use force or ultimatums |
| **Disunity of the victors** | Britain against France over reparations; France against the League at Corfu; the USA absent; Russia and Germany outside | No one could enforce the settlement or agree how to revise it |
| **Revolution and outsiders** | The Comintern; Russia's isolation; Rapallo | Fear of Communism and of a German–Soviet bloc made the West suspicious |
| **Outside Europe** | Japan's resentments, the Pacific naval race, China | Washington eased these but did not remove them |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Tensions remained high mainly because the treaties **left Germany and its neighbours with grievances and the victors without agreement on how to enforce them**. The reparations quarrel was the pivot: it turned the disunity of Britain and France into the Ruhr occupation, and the occupation produced the hyperinflation that nearly destroyed Germany, and with it hopes of stability. Other crises (Fiume, Corfu) came from **nationalism** and show that victors could break the peace too. The conferences (Washington, Genoa, Lausanne) worked where the powers' interests overlapped (naval limits, Turkey) and failed where they did not (Russia, reparations). Tension eased only when the main parties, Germany and France, were each ready to compromise in 1923–24.

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why France occupied the Ruhr in 1923.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "France occupied the Ruhr because Germany had not paid reparations. Germany was late with coal and timber deliveries in 1922, so the French and Belgian army went into the Ruhr on 11 January 1923. The German workers went on strike. The mark lost its value and there was hyperinflation."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it mostly describes the occupation and its aftermath. The one reason, non-payment, is stated and not explained, and the last two sentences are about what happened afterwards, not why France acted. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The immediate reason was Germany's default. The London Schedule of 1921 required 132 billion gold marks, of which 50 billion were to be paid in the near future, and by the end of 1922 Germany was behind with deliveries of timber and coal. On 26 December 1922 the Reparation Commission declared Germany in default over timber, with Britain's delegate dissenting, and in early January 1923 over coal. Poincaré, the French prime minister, argued that the Treaty of Versailles allowed the Allies to take 'measures' against a defaulting Germany, and he proposed to seize a 'productive pledge', the coal and revenue of the Ruhr, so that Germany would pay. A second reason was France's need for money. The north of France had been devastated, and France owed war debts to the USA and Britain which it expected German payments to cover. Without reparations the franc and the French budget were under strain."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with accurate support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add security and the failure of other methods, then connect:

"A third reason was security. France had lost 1.4 million men and had a smaller population and economy than Germany. The British and American guarantee promised in 1919 lapsed when the US Senate refused to ratify the treaty, so French leaders thought that the only protection against a future German attack was to keep Germany weak. The Ruhr produced most of Germany's coal and steel. Holding it gave France a hold on German industry and, some French politicians hoped, on the Rhineland, which France wished to detach from Germany or at least keep demilitarised.

These reasons were linked. Reparations were the legal and financial excuse; security was the deeper motive; and Poincaré's belief that Germany was avoiding payment on purpose made coercion seem the only option, since negotiations at Genoa in 1922 had failed and Britain would not support force. France acted alone because the Allies were divided and the USA was absent, so no one could stop it. The decisive factor was probably the combination of French fear and a German default that gave France a legal basis. Without the default, France had no excuse; without the fear, it would not have risked a quarrel with Britain."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (money gave the excuse, security the deeper motive, the absence of any restraint made it possible), with a supported conclusion about the most important combination. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: How far was the Washington Conference of 1921–22 a success?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Criterion: success in what it set out to do (limit naval arms, remove the cause of Pacific tension, settle the Far East), and for how long.
- For: first great arms limit, 5:5:3; 1921 invitations, scrapping of ships; ended the Anglo-Japanese Alliance; Four-Power Treaty; Shandong returned; calm in the Pacific for a decade.
- Against 1: only capital ships limited, cruiser race; Geneva (1927) failed.
- Against 2: Japan's resentment of 5:3 and the 1924 Immigration Act; no enforcement; Nine-Power Treaty ignored in 1931; Germany, Russia and the League absent; nothing for Europe's problems (reparations).
- Judgement: a success within its limited aims and for about ten years, but a success built on goodwill.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The Washington Conference was a success because it limited naval armaments. The Five-Power Treaty of 6 February 1922 set a ratio of capital ships of 5:5:3 for the USA, Britain and Japan, with France and Italy given 175,000 tons each, and the powers agreed to scrap ships and not to build new capital ships for ten years. The Four-Power Treaty ended the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, so Britain no longer risked being drawn into a war between Japan and the USA, and the Nine-Power Treaty promised to respect China. Japan returned Shandong to China. This shows that the conference was a success."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence only asserts. An answer made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the conference's success was narrower than it looked. The treaty limited only capital ships. Cruisers, destroyers and submarines were left alone, so the powers simply moved their building to the unrestricted classes, and the cruiser race that followed led to a conference at Geneva in 1927, which failed, and the London Naval Treaty of 1930. The treaties also had no way of enforcing themselves: the Nine-Power Treaty promised to respect China's independence, but contained no sanction, and in 1931 Japan took Manchuria without any power invoking it. Most important, Japan's acceptance of the 5:3 ratio was grudging. The Japanese navy and nationalists saw it as a badge of inferiority, and the American Immigration Act of 1924, which banned Japanese immigrants altogether, added to the resentment. Finally, the conference said nothing about the problems that were destabilising Europe: Germany and Soviet Russia were not invited, and reparations were not on the agenda.

On the other hand, these failures should be judged against what was realistic in 1921. The USA, Britain and Japan did keep to the capital-ship limits until the mid-1930s, and this was the first time that the great powers had agreed to reduce, and not simply restrict, their battle fleets. The Four-Power Treaty removed the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which had worried the USA and the dominions, and the replacement was a promise to consult, which for the 1920s kept Pacific relations calm, and Britain and the USA accepted parity. Japan's Foreign Minister Shidehara kept the policy of cooperation with the West through most of the decade.

Overall, the conference was a success in its own terms and for a time: it did what the three navies wanted, which was to stop an expensive race in battleships, and it eased Pacific tensions for about a decade. But it was a success of goodwill without enforcement, and it limited only part of the problem. When Japan's government changed after 1931 the treaty system failed. The judgement, therefore, is that it was a considerable success in 1922 and a limited one in the long run."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (success in its own terms and over time). With an opening paragraph on the case for success, this is Level 5 (18–20).

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

The German Reich and the Russian Socialist Federal Soviet Republic mutually agree to waive their claims for compensation for expenditure incurred on account of the war, and also for war damages. [...] Diplomatic and consular relations between the German Reich and the Russian Socialist Federal Soviet Republic shall be resumed immediately. [...] The two Governments shall co-operate in a spirit of mutual goodwill in meeting the economic needs of both countries.

*Adapted from Articles 1, 3 and 5 of the Treaty of Rapallo, signed by Walther Rathenau for Germany and Georgy Chicherin for Soviet Russia, 16 April 1922.*

**Source B**

The Greek Government must offer a full apology to the Italian Minister in Athens. It must hold a solemn funeral service for the dead in the Catholic cathedral in Athens, with the whole Government present. It must punish the guilty by death, after an inquiry carried out with the help of the Italian military attaché, and pay 50 million lire within five days. Greece has twenty-four hours to reply.

*Adapted (a summary in modern English, not the note's own wording) from the demands of the Italian government to Greece after the murder of General Tellini, Athens, 29 August 1923.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about how states dealt with their disputes in the early 1920s.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is a treaty between Germany and Russia and says they agree to waive claims and to trade. Source B is Italy's ultimatum to Greece and makes demands for an apology and money. The two are different because A is peaceful and B is aggressive. They are both from the 1920s."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. The similarity ("from the 1920s") is trivial and nothing is explained. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources have one feature in common: both are about **bilateral** dealings. In Source A two governments settle their affairs directly, and in Source B Italy deals directly with Greece, so neither mentions the League of Nations or any arrangement between the great powers as a whole. Both therefore show states in the early 1920s protecting their own interests by their own methods, not through the new machinery of collective security. Both also concern national honour and money: in A the two states cancel claims for war costs, and in B Italy demands an apology and '50 million lire'.

The differences are in method and balance. Source A is **mutual**: the powers 'mutually agree to waive' claims, 'co-operate in a spirit of mutual goodwill' and resume relations 'immediately', so each gives up something and gains something. Source B is a **one-sided demand** from a Great Power to a small state: Greece must apologise, punish and pay, and has 'twenty-four hours to reply'. Its demand for a death sentence and for Italian officials to take part in the inquiry interferes in Greece's own justice. One is a settlement of disputes by agreement; the other, a threat.

The context explains why. Rapallo was agreed between two states excluded from the Allied system: Germany, the defeated power, and Soviet Russia, the revolutionary outcast. It was signed on the margins of the Genoa Conference, when each feared that the Allies would settle with the other at its expense, so the cancelling of claims and the resumption of relations broke their isolation and was a deal of equals. The ultimatum, in contrast, was issued by Mussolini, in office for only ten months, to punish Greece for the murder of an Italian general, and the lire and honours demanded were designed to humiliate. The Italian reply to the Greek acceptance of only part of the terms, the bombardment of Corfu on 31 August, shows that Mussolini meant it. The nature of the sources also matters. Source A is a signed treaty, a precise record of what two governments agreed publicly, but it says nothing of what they intended privately (we know that army cooperation followed in secret). Source B is a modern summary of a diplomatic note, so it shows what Italy demanded but not why, and does not show the Greek answer or the outcome, when the Conference of Ambassadors made Greece pay and Italy leave.

Both sources therefore show how weak the system was: Rapallo's peaceful deal increased the suspicion of the western powers, and Corfu's use of force was settled by the great powers, not by the League."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context and through each source's nature and purpose; a closing comparison of what each source can and cannot show. A **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

The Treaty includes no provisions for the economic rehabilitation of Europe, nothing to make the defeated Central Empires into good neighbors, nothing to stabilize the new States of Europe, nothing to reclaim Russia. [...] The Council of Four paid no attention to these issues, being preoccupied with others.

*From The Economic Consequences of the Peace, Chapter 6, by John Maynard Keynes, a British economist who had advised the Treasury at the Paris Peace Conference and resigned in protest in June 1919, published December 1919.*

**Source B**

Acting, in our situation, means clearing the way for international agreements and settling the reparations question for good, so as to win back the freedom of the Ruhr and the Rhineland. [...] The whole decision lies in the question: what do you want? If you want reparations, we can reach an understanding with you; if you want the Rhineland, there can be no understanding.

*Adapted (translated and shortened) from a speech by Gustav Stresemann, leader of the German People's Party, to the Reichstag, 17 April 1923, as printed in Günter Schönbrunn, Geschichte in Quellen, volume 5 (1970).*

**Source C**

The Contracting Powers agree to limit their respective naval armament as provided in the present Treaty. [...] The total capital ship replacement tonnage of each of the Contracting Powers shall not exceed in standard displacement, for the United States 525,000 tons; for the British Empire 525,000 tons; for France 175,000 tons; for Italy 175,000 tons; for Japan 315,000 tons.

*Adapted (the metric equivalents are omitted) from Articles I and IV of the Washington Naval Treaty between the USA, the British Empire, France, Italy and Japan, 6 February 1922.*

**Source D**

From the coming into force of the present Treaty, the state of peace will be definitely re-established between the British Empire, France, Italy, Japan, Greece, Roumania and the Serb-Croat-Slovene State of the one part, and Turkey of the other part, as well as between their respective nationals.

*From Article I of the Treaty of Lausanne between Turkey and the Allied Powers, signed 24 July 1923.*

**Question.** 'The peace settlement of 1919 was the main cause of international tension in the early 1920s.' How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (the treaty left Europe's economy and the new states unstable); B (the dispute about reparations, a Versailles obligation, is at the heart of the Ruhr crisis). Challenge or qualify: C (in 1922 the powers could agree to limits on arms, so the settlement did not prevent cooperation, and the tension that the treaty addresses is naval rivalry, not Versailles); D (a different settlement, Sèvres, was replaced by a negotiated one, and peace was restored); B (Stresemann treats the crisis as a question of French intentions: "what do you want?", so enforcement mattered as much as the treaty).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: written by an insider, an economist who had been at the conference and resigned, in 1919 before the treaty took effect. That gives authority, but it is a polemic written to persuade: Keynes exaggerates when he says that the treaty contained "no provisions" (it did contain economic clauses, and the League), and his purpose was to change British policy towards Germany. But the Ruhr crisis and the hyperinflation of 1923 seemed to prove his prediction right, so it is valuable evidence of how contemporary critics saw the gap in the settlement. Stronger for the view than weaker.
- **B**: a German politician, speaking to the Reichstag during the occupation, with the purpose of uniting his audience behind resistance and of showing the Allies that Germany was reasonable. The sharp contrast ("reparations ... understanding; Rhineland ... no understanding") suggests that France wanted more than money, which the French denied. It shows what Germans believed, not necessarily what France intended. It supports the idea that reparations were central, but it also shows the problem was French policy.
- **C**: a signed treaty, accurate about what the five powers agreed. Its limits: it deals only with capital ships and says nothing about causes of tension. It is, however, good evidence that the great powers were able to cooperate where their interests coincided, even in 1922.
- **D**: another signed treaty. It shows that the Allies accepted revision, but it followed the Chanak crisis of September 1922, when Britain and Turkey nearly went to war: the treaty settled tension that a previous settlement had caused.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A and B support the view that the settlement was the main cause of tension, but for different reasons. For Keynes in Source A the fault is what the treaty left out: it contained 'nothing to stabilize the new States of Europe' and gave no thought to the economy. For Stresemann in Source B the issue is the treaty's financial burden, and the choice he gives Germany's opponents, 'reparations' or 'the Rhineland', suggests that tension continued because the victors disagreed about how to use the treaty. Both sources were written with a purpose (Keynes to change opinion, Stresemann to rally Germans and impress the Allies) but the events of 1923, when the occupation of the Ruhr ended in hyperinflation, bear out their main claims. Sources C and D qualify the view from different directions. Source C shows that in 1922 the powers could cooperate to limit their fleets, so tension was not inevitable, and that it was about naval rivalry that Versailles did not address. Source D shows that a peace treaty, Sèvres, could be replaced by a better one at Lausanne, so the problem was not treaties as such but ones that could not be enforced, though it should be noted that Turkey got its revision because it had won on the battlefield. Taking all four, the sources support the view to a considerable extent: the central tensions of the early 1920s, reparations and the new states, came from the settlement. But C and D show that the powers could settle quarrels by negotiation when they wished, and my own knowledge of Fiume and Corfu shows that nationalism and the ambitions of leaders such as Mussolini were also causes, so the settlement was the main cause, but not the only one."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1919–23; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "'The Corfu crisis showed that the League could not deal with a Great Power.' How far do you agree?" [20]

A student wrote:

"In August 1923 General Tellini was killed near the Greek border with Albania. Mussolini was very angry and sent an ultimatum to Greece demanding an apology and 50 million lire. Greece did not accept all the demands, so Italy bombarded the island of Corfu on 31 August and occupied it. Greece asked the League for help but the League did nothing, and the dispute was decided by the Conference of Ambassadors. Greece had to pay 50 million lire. The League was weak and could not stop Mussolini, so the statement is true."

**The level.** Accurate and relevant detail, but it **narrates** the crisis and ends with an assertion. It does not explain why the League failed, it considers no other view, and "did nothing" is not quite right (the Council met and argued with Italy). Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with a claim, support it with the evidence and then challenge it. "The Corfu crisis does show that the League could not deal with a Great Power, because Italy refused to accept its authority: Mussolini told the Council it had no right to interfere, threatened to leave the League, and the dispute passed to the Conference of Ambassadors, where Greece paid 50 million lire and Italy was not condemned. But the League's failure was partly the failure of the powers that ran it. France, then occupying the Ruhr, did not want the League to set a precedent for judging a Great Power's use of force, and Britain, which wanted the League to act, backed down when Italy threatened to withdraw. The League did exert some pressure: the Council met and debated the case despite Italian protests, and Britain and France forced Mussolini to leave Corfu on 27 September. Moreover, in 1925 the League stopped a Greek–Bulgarian war within days. So Corfu shows that the League could not discipline a Great Power against the wishes of the other powers, which is not quite the same as saying it could not deal with one at all." Now each fact is used as evidence, and there is a view, a counter-view and a judgement.

Q: Why did d'Annunzio seize Fiume in 1919, and how was the Fiume question settled?
A: He and Italian nationalists were angry that the peace conference had not given Italy Fiume (and Dalmatia), and he saw a "mutilated victory". Q: What were the three main kinds of problem in the successor states? Give an example of each.
A: Ethnic (the Germans and Hungarians in Czechoslovakia; Magyars in Romania), political (Radić's murder and King Alexander's dictatorship in Yugoslavia; Piłsudski's coup in Poland) and economic (tariff walls; Austria's hyperinflation and League loan).
Q: What was the Little Entente, and who backed it?
A: Three defensive treaties (1920–21) between Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia and Romania against Hungarian revision and a Habsburg restoration, backed by France, which made its own alliances with each member between 1924 and 1927.
Q: What did Germany do in the Ruhr in 1923 and when did it stop?
A: It organised passive resistance: officials and workers refused to obey the French and Belgians, and the government paid them. Stresemann's government ended it on 26 September 1923.

A: Capital ship tonnage in the ratio 5 (USA) : 5 (Britain) : 3 (Japan) : about 1.67 (France) : about 1.67 (Italy), i.e. 525,000, 525,000, 315,000, 175,000 and 175,000 tons. It covered capital ships (and set limits for aircraft carriers), not cruisers or submarines.
Q: Why did the Genoa Conference fail, and what was its main consequence?
A: France would not compromise on reparations and the Allies and Soviet Russia could not agree on tsarist debts and nationalised property. Its main consequence was the German–Soviet Treaty of Rapallo (16 April 1922), signed on its sidelines.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Germany and Soviet Russia signed the Treaty of Rapallo in 1922.
A: Indicative content: both were outcasts: Germany a defeated power excluded from the Allied councils, Russia a revolutionary state the West would not recognise. At Genoa (April 1922) each feared that the Allies would settle with the other at its expense (Germany that the Allies would make a deal over Russian debts and trade; Russia that Germany would join the Allied front). Rapallo gave them recognition, restored diplomatic relations (Article 3), cancelled war claims (Article 1), opened trade on the most-favoured-nation principle and broke their isolation. Germany also saw Russia as a counterweight to France and Poland, and the Reichswehr wanted a place to build and train in ways that Versailles forbade. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects the fear of the Allies' deal, the wish to end isolation and the economic and military advantages, and judges which mattered most (isolation and fear of an Allied deal, because the signature came overnight in the middle of Genoa).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the German mark collapsed in 1923.
A: Indicative content: the war had been paid for by loans and printing, so the mark was already weak (about 4 to the dollar in 1914, a few hundred by 1922); reparations, which Germany had to pay in gold or goods, deepened the budget problem; the Ruhr occupation (11 January 1923) removed Germany's richest industrial area; the Cuno government paid the strikers' wages and lost tax revenue, and financed the deficit by printing money; as confidence fell people spent money at once, so prices rose faster and the Reichsbank printed more (by November 1923 about 4.2 trillion marks to the dollar). Level 4: connect the long-term weakness (war finance), the trigger (the Ruhr) and the government's choice (printing money to pay for resistance), and judge which turned a currency problem into a collapse (the policy of paying for passive resistance by printing).
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Corfu crisis showed that the League could not deal with a Great Power.' How far do you agree?

Evaluation with knowledge: C and D are treaties, accurate on what was agreed, but D followed Turkey's military victory and the Chanak crisis, so the Allies compromised because they had no choice; C was accepted partly because of America's financial leverage and each power's wish to save money, so these were compromises of interest. A is a retrospective speech by a German with a purpose (to present Germany as a victim who now cooperates); B is a pre-emptive polemic. Level 5 judges how far: e.g. the powers compromised where their interests were not at stake (Pacific, Turkey) but not where they were (reparations, security), so the sources show willingness to compromise to a limited extent.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The refusal of the USA to take a leading part in Europe was the main reason why international tensions remained high in the early 1920s.' How far do you agree?
H: Set a criterion: which factor, if removed, would most have reduced tension?
H: Include the USA's roles at Washington and in the Dawes Plan, and not only its absence from the League.
A: For: no American guarantee of France, so France built its own security by force (the Ruhr); the USA's absence from the League weakened it; US demands for war-debt repayment encouraged France and Britain to demand reparations; no American mediation in 1922–23 until the Dawes Plan. Against: the reparations quarrel was between France, Britain and Germany; nationalism and new leaders (Mussolini, d'Annunzio) caused Fiume and Corfu; the successor states' problems; Britain's refusal to commit to France was as important; the USA did act (Washington Conference, 1921–22; loans). Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "removal of this factor would have reduced the tension most", and judges, e.g., that American absence was a necessary background to the Ruhr crisis, but the immediate causes (default, French fear, German resistance) were European.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Washington Conference was called in 1921.
A: Indicative content: an arms race in battleships between the USA, Britain and Japan which the USA (and Britain) wanted to stop because of cost; the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902, which the USA and the dominions disliked; Japanese expansion in the Far East (Twenty-One Demands, Shandong) and American insistence on the Open Door; the failure of the League to settle naval arms (the Senate had refused to join); the Republican administration's wish to take the lead in arms limitation on its own terms (Harding and Hughes). Level 4: connect cost and rivalry to the Pacific problem and judge which was the main reason (the naval race, because it posed the most immediate cost and risk).
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The peace of the early 1920s was an illusion.' How far do the crises of 1919–23 support Sally Marks's title, *The Illusion of Peace* (1976)?
H: Decide whether "illusion" means that peace did not exist at all, or that it rested on foundations that could not hold.
H: Use Fiume, Corfu and the Ruhr as evidence, but also Washington and Lausanne.
A: Support: the crises show that force was still used by states (Fiume 1919, Ruhr 1923, Corfu 1923) and that the settlement had not removed the causes of conflict (reparations, minorities); the USA was absent and the victors were divided; Germany's resentment and the mark's collapse threatened the Republic. Challenge: there was no major war in Europe; the crises were resolved by compromise (Fiume by treaty, Corfu by the Conference of Ambassadors, the Ruhr by the Dawes Plan); Washington limited naval arms and Lausanne made a lasting peace with Turkey; no great power wanted another war. A* judgement: the peace was real in the sense that no state sought a general war, but illusory in the sense that it rested on Germany's weakness and on the great powers' reluctance to act, so it was fragile; the crises of 1923 show both how close the system came to breaking and how it recovered.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Treaties are usually reliable evidence of what states agreed but not of what they intended. How far does the Treaty of Rapallo (1922) show this?
H: Separate what the published treaty said from what happened later (secret military cooperation).
H: Ask what each side wanted, and what the Allies feared.
A: The text (waiver of claims, resumption of relations, most-favoured-nation, cooperation) is modest and contains nothing on military matters. But the Reichswehr and Red Army began secret cooperation, and the Allies suspected it; Germany used Rapallo as leverage against the West, and the Soviets used it to break isolation. Evaluation: the treaty is precise evidence of public commitments and reliable about the legal changes; it is weak on intentions and on the secret cooperation that was not in it, which is known only from later evidence; it was signed overnight at Genoa as a reaction to the Allies' plans, so it also shows each side's fear. Judgement: reliable on what was agreed, incomplete on why and what followed.
:::

:::recap
- **Fiume**: d'Annunzio seized it on 12 September 1919; Italy and Yugoslavia made it a free state (Rapallo, 12 November 1920); the army expelled d'Annunzio (December 1920); Italy annexed it (27 January 1924).
- **Successor states**: Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Romania, Austria, Hungary: minorities, weak politics and economic fragmentation; revisionist against satisfied states; **Little Entente** (1920–21) against Hungary, backed by France.
- **Reparations and the Ruhr**: London Schedule (5 May 1921, 132 billion gold marks); default (December 1922 – January 1923); French and Belgian occupation from 11 January 1923; passive resistance; hyperinflation (4.2 trillion marks to the dollar in November 1923); Stresemann ends resistance on 26 September; Rentenmark; Dawes Plan next.
- **Corfu**: Tellini murdered 27 August 1923; Mussolini's ultimatum 29 August; bombardment and occupation 31 August; the Conference of Ambassadors, not the League, decides; Greece pays 50 million lire; Italy leaves 27 September.
- **Washington** (1921–22): Four-, Five- and Nine-Power treaties; 5:5:3; Anglo-Japanese Alliance ended; success for capital ships, no limit on cruisers, no enforcement. **Genoa** (April–May 1922) failed; **Rapallo** (16 April 1922) Germany and Russia; **Lausanne** (24 July 1923) replaced Sèvres.
- **Powers**: Britain for recovery and against coercion; France for security and enforcement; Germany for revision (fulfilment, Rapallo); Italy under Mussolini from October 1922; USSR and the Comintern; USA involved without commitment; Japan cooperating but resentful.
- **Judgement**: tension stayed high because the settlement left grievances and the victors could not agree on enforcement; reparations were the pivot; nationalism added crises; the conferences worked only where interests overlapped.
