:::why
The revolution of 1917 promised to change everything: marriage, schooling, art, religion and the place of women and of non-Russian peoples. By 1941 some of those promises had been kept in a harsh form, some had been abandoned and some had been reversed. Judging how far Stalin transformed Soviet society means separating what was new from what was a return to older habits, and what was real change from what was propaganda. It is a perfect question for a Paper 4 essay, because the answer is almost never "completely" or "not at all".
:::

## Women and the family

### The first revolution: 1917–28

The Bolsheviks wanted to free women from the old family, which they saw as an institution of property and male power. The **Family Code of October 1918** made civil marriage the only legal one, gave husband and wife equal rights, and made divorce easy: either partner could ask for it. In November 1920 the Soviet Union became one of the first states to make abortion legal in hospitals, free of charge, because the government accepted that women would otherwise turn to dangerous backstreet methods. The party set up the **Zhenotdel** (women's department) in 1919 to bring women into political life, to run literacy classes and to argue for nurseries and communal kitchens. Alexandra Kollontai, who led it from 1920, argued that the family would "wither away" as the state took over cooking, childcare and laundry.

The **Code on Marriage, the Family and Guardianship of 1926** went further in one way: it recognised unregistered (*de facto*) marriages and gave a woman the right to maintenance. The reason was practical. In the 1920s many men left wives and children, and many women were destitute. Wendy Goldman, in *Women, the State and Revolution* (1993), shows that the debates on the code of 1926 were between those who still hoped that the state would replace the family and those who feared that it could not afford to. The state could not: there were nurseries and canteens for only a small part of the population, and unemployment (NEP's urban unemployment) fell heavily on women.

### The Great Retreat

:::def The Great Retreat
A term made popular by the sociologist Nicholas Timasheff in *The Great Retreat* (1946) for Stalin's turn in the mid-1930s away from the radical aims of the revolution (the withering of the family, equality in pay, free experiment in schools and art) towards discipline, hierarchy, the family, Russian patriotism and tradition.
:::

In the 1930s, family policy changed. The regime now saw the family as the basis of a stable society and a source of children for the industrial and military power of the state. Several measures belong together.

- **The draft law of May 1936.** On 26 May 1936 a draft law "On the Protection of Motherhood and Childhood" was published for public discussion. Many people in letters and meetings argued against it, but the main points stood.
- **The decree of 27 June 1936.** It banned abortion, except where the pregnancy threatened the woman's life or health or a serious inherited disease. Doctors who performed an abortion outside these terms could be imprisoned for one to two years. The decree raised the fee for divorce, which now cost **50 roubles for the first divorce, 150 for the second and 300 for the third and later ones**, and it increased the penalties on fathers who did not pay maintenance (one quarter of earnings for one child, one third for two and half for three or more). A mother with six children received **2,000 roubles a year for five years** for each further child, and there were larger payments for ten children. It promised more maternity beds, nurseries and kindergartens.
- **Public messages.** The regime praised motherhood as "a great and honourable duty", mothers of large families were honoured in the press, and the Stalin Constitution (5 December 1936) in Article 122 proclaimed that women had equal rights with men in all fields of life.
- **A wider conservatism.** In 1934 a law made male homosexuality a crime. In 1935 *Pravda* called for the return of the children's New Year tree (banned as religious in the 1920s). "Culturedness" (*kulturnost*: tidy dress, clean homes and good manners) was promoted as a mark of the new Soviet citizen.

The impact is debated. Recorded abortions fell from about 1.9 million in 1935 to about 570,000 in 1937, and the birth rate rose for a time. But illegal abortions went up, and the official figures hide them. The People's Commissar of Health reported that infant mortality rose from 146 per thousand births in 1935 to 162 in 1938. The ban stayed until 1955.

:::key The "Great Retreat" in family policy: for and against the label
- **For.** Abortion banned (1936), where it had been legal since 1920. Divorce made costly, where it had been easy. The family praised, where the 1920s had expected it to wither. Motherhood rewarded with payments and honours. Equal rights proclaimed in 1936 while state policy stressed women's duty to bear children.
- **Against.** Stalin's state had not been consistently radical: the 1926 code was already a practical response to the problems of abandoned women, not an experiment in free love. Nurseries, maternity homes and women's work expanded in the 1930s. The state never returned to the church marriage or to a legal inequality of men and women. The retreat was from the *hope* that the family would wither, not from the principle of women's equality.
- **The best view.** Most historians accept that there was a shift in the 1930s, but differ on its meaning. Timasheff called it a retreat from socialism. Goldman presents it as a change from a dream of freeing women from the family to a policy of strengthening the family because the state could not replace it. Fitzpatrick (*Everyday Stalinism*, 1999) stresses the mixture of old and new in "normal" Stalinist life.
:::

### Women at work

Women's work was the largest change. In the first Five Year Plan, labour was short, men were in the Red Army or in heavy industry, and families needed a second wage because real wages were falling. **Women made up about 24 per cent of the workforce in 1928 and about 39 per cent in 1940** (figures from the sociologist Gail Lapidus's *Women in Soviet Society*, 1978). In industry, women entered fields that had been closed to them, as machine operators, tractor drivers, crane drivers and builders of the Moscow Metro. The press made heroines of them: Pasha Angelina led a women's tractor brigade from 1933, and in September 1938 the aviators Valentina Grizodubova, Polina Osipenko and Marina Raskova flew non-stop from Moscow to the Far East. In the collective farms, women did most of the work as men left for the towns.

But the opportunities had limits:
- **Pay and jobs.** Women were concentrated in lower-paid work, in textiles, teaching, health care and clerical work. Few became managers, and no woman was a full member of the Politburo in this period.
- **The double burden.** Women who worked a full shift also had to queue for food, cook, wash and look after children in cramped communal flats. Nurseries and canteens existed, but never enough.
- **Pronatalism.** After 1936 the state expected women to be both workers and mothers of large families.
- **Political voice.** The Zhenotdel was abolished in January 1930 on the grounds that the "woman question" had been solved, and women's concerns were left to the party and the trade unions.
- **The Hujum.** In Central Asia, from 8 March 1927, the party campaigned against the veil and for the education of women. It met violent resistance, and women who unveiled were sometimes murdered by relatives. It showed how hard it was to transform a society by decree.

:::def Double burden
The situation of Soviet women who worked full time in a factory, farm or office and also did most of the housework and childcare, because the state and the men did not take over those tasks.
:::

### What the evidence shows

Women's position was transformed in one sense: in 1941 many more were in paid work, trained, literate and in towns. But the transformation was not the one the revolution had promised. The family was strengthened, not abolished, and women now had to be workers and mothers. The best judgement is that **Stalin changed women's lives a great deal, but made the family, not women's liberation, the centre of policy**.

## National groups

The Soviet Union was a multinational state, and about half the population was not Russian. Policy on non-Russian peoples changed in the same way.

- **Korenizatsiya ("rooting", or indigenisation), from 1923.** The party promoted local people into the administration, schools and press of each republic and encouraged teaching in local languages. In Ukraine the policy of Ukrainisation gave a great boost to Ukrainian culture. The reason was to win support for Soviet power among peoples who had been ruled by the Russian empire. Terry Martin, in *The Affirmative Action Empire* (2001), called it a policy of positive discrimination for non-Russian nations.
- **The turn of the 1930s.** The Great Turn and the famine hit Ukraine, Kazakhstan and the North Caucasus hard (see [Stalin's Russia (3)](#hist.d.stalin.3)). From 1932–33 local national elites were accused of "bourgeois nationalism". The Ukrainian Commissar of Education Mykola Skrypnyk, who had defended Ukrainisation, killed himself in July 1933. In the Terror of 1937–38 many leaders of the republics, writers and teachers were arrested and shot.
- **Russification.** From the mid-1930s the Russians were presented as the "elder brother" of the Soviet peoples. The Central Asian languages, which had been given the Latin script in the 1920s, were switched to Cyrillic in 1937–40, and on 13 March 1938 Russian became a compulsory subject in all non-Russian schools. The Stalin Constitution of 1936 organised the USSR as eleven republics, and the slogan of the "friendship of the peoples" was spread by songs, films and festivals of national art.
- **Deportations and "national operations".** Entire communities were treated as suspect. In 1936 several tens of thousands of Poles and Germans were moved from the border zone of Ukraine to Kazakhstan. In the autumn of 1937 about **172,000 Soviet Koreans** were deported from the Far East to Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan under a decree of 21 August 1937, in the first deportation of a whole nationality; a large number died in the first years. In the Terror, the NKVD's "Polish operation" (from August 1937) led to the shooting of more than 100,000 people, mostly Soviet citizens of Polish origin. After the German invasion in June 1941 more peoples, such as the Volga Germans, were deported; this is outside the chapter's period and is a closing note only.
- **Jewish citizens.** Antisemitism was officially condemned, and a Jewish Autonomous Region was set up at Birobidzhan in 1934, but Jewish religious life and Yiddish institutions were restricted.

:::key National groups: transformation or continuity?
In the 1920s the state granted forms of national autonomy (languages, schools, republics). In the 1930s it kept the forms and removed the content: republics existed on paper, but national elites were purged, Russian was promoted and some peoples were deported. **The change was real, but it was not the creation of a new equality; it was a move from encouraging non-Russian cultures within a Communist framework to controlling them and ranking the Russians first.** This is a case where the answer to "how far" is "in the opposite direction from the revolution's first promise".
:::

## Youth and education

### Education in the 1920s and the Cultural Revolution

In the 1920s the Commissar of Education, Anatoly Lunacharsky, encouraged experiment. Schools were co-educational, teachers were encouraged to use "project" methods and the "brigade" method of group work, and exams, homework and fixed marks were reduced. The party also wanted to remove illiteracy: in the census of 1926 only about 51 per cent of people aged nine and over could read and write.

In the Cultural Revolution (1928–31) education became a class struggle. Higher education opened to workers and communists through the workers' faculties (*rabfaks*), and thousands of "promoted" workers (*vydvizhentsy*) were sent to colleges to become the "Red specialists" the first Five Year Plan needed. "Bourgeois" professors were attacked and some were arrested. Lunacharsky was replaced in 1929 by Andrei Bubnov. Sheila Fitzpatrick's work on education and social mobility shows that the Cultural Revolution did lift up a generation of men and a smaller number of women from the factory and the village to power. Many of the leaders of the Soviet state in the 1960s and 1970s came from it.

### The return to discipline, 1931–36

By 1931 the experimental methods were blamed for poor results: engineers needed basic maths and science, not group projects. A series of party decrees from September 1931 restored the traditional school.
- **The curriculum and teachers.** Lessons were timetabled by subject, with set programmes and textbooks, and teachers were given authority. Examinations, marks (a five-point scale from 1935) and homework returned. School rules and "behaviour" were stressed in 1935.
- **History.** A decree of May 1934 ordered a return to the teaching of history as a chronological narrative, with kings, wars and heroes, in place of "sociology". New textbooks were written. The *Short Course of the History of the All-Union Communist Party (Bolsheviks)* of 1938, which taught that Stalin was Lenin's true heir, became the basis of political education.
- **Primary schooling.** Four years of primary school became compulsory for all in 1930, and the school system expanded rapidly in towns and villages. By the census of 1939 about 81 per cent of those aged nine and over were literate.
- **Pedology.** On 4 July 1936 a decree condemned "pedology", a science of child development using psychological tests, which was said to label working-class children as backward. Testing was banned.
- **Fees.** On 2 October 1940 a decree introduced fees for the upper years of secondary school and for higher education, which reversed the policy of free education and made it harder for poor children.

:::def Great Retreat in education
The reversal in the early and mid-1930s of the experiments of the 1920s: a fixed curriculum, subject teaching, exams, marks, homework and teachers' authority returned, and a nationalist, chronological history replaced class-based "sociology".
:::

### Higher education and the new elite

Student numbers in colleges and universities rose several times over in the 1930s, and technical training expanded, so that factory workers and peasants' children could rise to be engineers and officials. This was a real transformation of the social structure. It was also directed: the courses were designed to meet the needs of the plans, and the Terror killed many of the teachers.

### Youth organisations

- **The Octobrists** (children aged seven to nine), **the Young Pioneers** (ten to fourteen, founded in 1922) and **the Komsomol** (Communist Youth League, from the age of fourteen, founded in 1918) were the party's organisations for the young. The Komsomol grew from about two million members in the later 1920s to about ten million by 1941.
- **Aims.** They taught loyalty to the party and to Stalin, ran sport and camps, and recruited young people for the Five Year Plans: Komsomol volunteers built Magnitogorsk and the Metro.
- **The Pavlik Morozov cult.** In the early 1930s the press celebrated Pavlik Morozov, a boy killed in a village in 1932, who in the official story had denounced his father, a village official. Historians doubt the details, but the cult taught that loyalty to the state came before loyalty to the family.

:::key Education and youth: how far was society transformed?
- **Real change.** Literacy among those aged nine and over rose from about 51 per cent (1926 census) to about 81 per cent (1939 census). Primary schooling became universal. A new class of workers' children became engineers, officials and managers.
- **Reversal and limits.** The experimental schools of the 1920s were abolished, schools became disciplined and conservative, history became nationalist, and fees (October 1940) limited access. The Pioneers and Komsomol enrolled millions, but many young people joined to get on, not from belief.
- **Judgement.** Education was the area where the regime's transformation was greatest in numbers, and where its method of teaching ended up closest to the pre-revolutionary school.
:::

## Cultural changes

### The Cultural Revolution, 1928–31

:::def Cultural Revolution (Soviet Union)
The period of 1928–31, parallel to the first Five Year Plan, in which the party encouraged "class war" in culture: attacks on "bourgeois" writers, teachers and scientists, the promotion of workers into education, and the domination of groups of proletarian artists. It was not the Chinese Cultural Revolution of the 1960s.
:::

In literature the leading group was **RAPP**, the Russian Association of Proletarian Writers, led by Leopold Averbakh, which demanded that writers served the Five Year Plan and attacked "fellow-travellers", writers such as Pilnyak and Zamyatin who were not communists. Writers were told to write about factories and collective farms. The poet Vladimir Mayakovsky, a supporter of the revolution who had been attacked by RAPP, committed suicide in April 1930. There was an attack on the scientists of the Academy of Sciences, too, and on "bourgeois specialists", and the churches were attacked (see below).

### Socialist Realism

In 1932 the party changed its approach. RAPP was blamed for its sectarian methods, and **a decree of 23 April 1932** dissolved it and all other independent literary groups, and set up a single organisation for each art. The Union of Soviet Writers was organised under the party's control, and at its **First Congress (17 August to 1 September 1934)** the new method, **Socialist Realism**, was set out. Maxim Gorky, the veteran writer, who had settled in the USSR in the early 1930s and was the most famous Soviet author, gave the main address on literature. Andrei Zhdanov, a secretary of the Central Committee, represented the party. According to the statutes of the Union, the writer must show reality truthfully but "in its revolutionary development", and help to educate workers in the spirit of socialism (a paraphrase of the statutes). Stalin was reported to have called writers "engineers of human souls".

:::def Socialist Realism
The official method of Soviet art and literature from 1932–34. Works had to be realistic in form, accessible to ordinary people, optimistic, loyal to the party and showing life as it would be under socialism: heroic workers and peasants, wise leaders and the triumph of the Five Year Plans. Anything else could be condemned as "formalism" or "bourgeois".
:::

### Literature, music, film and art

- **Literature.** Popular Socialist Realist novels included Nikolai Ostrovsky's *How the Steel Was Tempered* (1932–34), about a young Communist hero, and Mikhail Sholokhov's *Quiet Flows the Don* (1928–40), about the Cossacks in the Civil War, which was praised although it was not simple propaganda. Many writers suffered. Osip Mandelstam was arrested in 1934 after writing a poem about Stalin and died in a transit camp in December 1938. Isaac Babel was arrested in 1939 and shot in January 1940. Anna Akhmatova's son was arrested in 1938, and she wrote the poems of *Requiem* but could not publish them in her lifetime. Mikhail Bulgakov wrote *The Master and Margarita* but it was published only in the 1960s.
- **Music.** Dmitri Shostakovich's opera *Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District* was a success after its first performance in January 1934. On 26 January 1936 Stalin went to see it at the Bolshoi Theatre, and on 28 January *Pravda* published an unsigned article called "Muddle Instead of Music", which called the work "formalist", coarse and vulgar. Shostakovich was in danger. He withdrew his Fourth Symphony and wrote the Fifth (first performed on 21 November 1937), which was received as a work of Soviet music in a clearer style. The warning to every composer was plain: the party decided what was acceptable.
- **Film.** Cinema was the most important art for the regime, because it reached millions, including those who could not read. Films such as *Chapaev* (1934), about a Civil War hero, were seen by huge audiences. The director Sergei Eisenstein, famous for *Battleship Potemkin* (1925), was criticised for formalism, and his film *Bezhin Meadow* was stopped in 1937; his *Alexander Nevsky* (1938), with music by Prokofiev, glorified a medieval Russian hero in a patriotic way. Grigory Aleksandrov's musical comedies such as *Volga-Volga* (1938) were popular and showed a cheerful Soviet life.
- **Art and architecture.** Painters were told to show Stalin, the Five Year Plans and happy workers. Alexander Gerasimov's portraits of Stalin were typical. Vera Mukhina's statue *Worker and Kolkhoz Woman* topped the Soviet pavilion at the Paris Exhibition in 1937. In architecture a competition in the early 1930s chose a design for a giant Palace of Soviets, topped by a statue of Lenin, on the site of the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour; it was never built.

### Aims and impact

:::key Socialist Realism: aims and impact
- **Aims.** To mobilise people for the Five Year Plans, to educate them in socialism, to glorify the party and Stalin (see the cult of personality in [Stalin's Russia (2)](#hist.d.stalin.2)), to create a "new Soviet man", and to control the writers and artists who had been independent in the 1920s.
- **Impact on the audience.** Popular: films and books reached millions, and some works (*Chapaev*, *Quiet Flows the Don*, Shostakovich's Fifth Symphony) had real quality. For many readers and viewers the heroic tales gave a sense of purpose.
- **Impact on artists.** The experiments of the 1920s ended. Writers and composers who were not accepted were silenced or killed, and many practised self-censorship. Some wrote "for the drawer", works they could not publish. Others, such as Bulgakov and Akhmatova, found a kind of "inner emigration".
- **Judgement.** The state transformed the organisation of culture completely and the range of what could be said, but it could not control what people felt, and some great works survived by being hidden.
:::

## Religious policies

### Repression of organised religion

The Bolsheviks were atheists, and Marx had called religion the "opium of the people". Already in January 1918 a decree had separated the Church from the state and the schools from the Church, and in 1922 the state seized church valuables, tried clergy and backed a rival group, the "Living Church". In the 1920s the state attacked religion by propaganda more than by force. The **League of the Militant Godless**, founded in 1925 and led by Emelyan Yaroslavsky, ran anti-religious meetings and a newspaper, *Bezbozhnik* (*The Godless*). It claimed millions of members by the early 1930s, but many of these were members on paper.

In 1927 the head of the Orthodox Church, Metropolitan Sergii, issued a **declaration of loyalty** to the Soviet state (29 July 1927), to try to protect the Church. This did not stop the next attack.

- **8 April 1929: the Law on Religious Associations.** It forbade religious groups to teach children, to give charity or to hold meetings outside the church. Religious groups had to register, and local authorities could close their buildings.
- **1929–32: the Cultural Revolution against the Church.** Churches were closed on a large scale, bells were taken down and melted for metal, priests were arrested or taxed heavily, and the **continuous working week** introduced in 1929 removed Sunday as a regular day of rest. On 5 December 1931 the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour in Moscow was blown up to make room for the Palace of Soviets.
- **1937–38: the Terror.** Tens of thousands of clergy and active believers were arrested, and many were shot. By the end of the 1930s most churches had been closed or turned into stores, clubs or museums, and there were very few bishops left at liberty.
- **Other faiths.** Mosques and Muslim schools were closed, and the Hujum was directed against the veil in Central Asia; synagogues and Buddhist monasteries were closed too.

:::def League of the Militant Godless
An organisation founded in 1925 and led by Emelyan Yaroslavsky to spread atheism and to campaign against religion through lectures, publications, exhibitions and the closing of churches. It claimed millions of members in the early 1930s, but many were only nominal, and it faded in the late 1930s.
:::

### The continued influence of the Church

Yet religion did not die. The census of 6 January 1937 included a question on religious belief, which had not been asked before. Among the adults who answered, about **57 per cent said they were believers**, the great majority of them Orthodox. The result was a shock to the leaders. The census was condemned as defective, its results were suppressed, many of the officials who had organised it were arrested (some were shot), and a new census was taken in 1939 which did not ask about religion.

How was belief kept alive? People prayed at home before the icon in the corner, baptised children in secret, celebrated Easter, and kept up the pilgrimages and holy days; in the countryside, where collective farmers did not welcome the closure of the village church, women often led the defence of the church. Churches that remained open were crowded. Daniel Peris, in *Storming the Heavens: The Soviet League of the Militant Godless* (1998), argues that the League was weaker and more ineffective than its figures suggest, which helps to explain why belief survived.

:::note Evidence: the 1937 census
The census of 6 January 1937 was designed to show a growth in the population and the triumph of socialism. It showed instead a smaller population than Stalin's plans had expected (the famine and the Terror lay behind this), and a majority of adults who said they were believers. The regime's response (suppression, arrests and a repeat in 1939) is evidence that it recognised that its policy towards religion had failed to change belief. It is better evidence of the regime's anxieties than of the exact strength of belief, because the figures were never published in the 1930s.
:::

:::key Religion: how far was society transformed?
- **Real change.** Public religious life was largely destroyed: churches closed, clergy killed or silenced, religious education banned, the Church's structure broken. Atheist propaganda and Soviet rituals were put in the place of religion.
- **Limits.** About 57 per cent of adults said in 1937 that they were believers. Belief moved from the churches to the home.
- **Judgement.** The regime changed the *public* place of religion very far, and the *private* beliefs of people hardly at all. (After the German invasion in June 1941 Stalin relaxed the policy, which shows how little the Church had been destroyed; this is a closing note only.)
:::

- **Nicholas Timasheff**, *The Great Retreat* (1946), argued that Stalin retreated from the revolution's radical aims to family, nation and discipline. The idea is useful, but it has been criticised for over-stating the early radicalism and the later conservatism.
- **Sheila Fitzpatrick**, *Everyday Stalinism* (1999), shows daily life in the 1930s as a mix of shortages, privilege and new opportunity, and stresses social mobility; her earlier *The Cultural Front* (1992) studies how culture was used for class politics.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. A [30] essay gets one mark out of 15 for analysis and judgement (Table A) and one out of 15 for knowledge (Table B). Read the commentary as an examiner would.

:::eg 'Stalin's policies towards women were a Great Retreat.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 2 (4–6): some understanding, descriptive, criteria implicit. Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, but the argument lacks balance; weak judgement. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained balanced argument, a clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): a good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Great Retreat" must be defined: a turn away from the revolution's radical aims. Criteria: (1) law on marriage, divorce and abortion; (2) women's work and pay; (3) women's political voice; (4) the aims of the regime. Retreat: abortion ban (27 June 1936), divorce fees, praise of motherhood, Zhenotdel abolished (1930). Against: women's entry into work grew; equal rights in law stayed; the 1926 code was already practical. Judgement: a retreat in family policy, but not in women's work.

**A Level 2/3 paragraph (about Table A Level 2, 5 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "In the 1920s women had many rights. They could get divorced easily and have abortions. In 1936 Stalin banned abortion and made divorce more expensive. He also gave money to women with many children. This was a retreat from the revolution, so Stalin's policies towards women were a Great Retreat."

*Commentary.* Accurate and clear, and it names the change. But it is a **list of two laws** followed by an assertion. It does not define "retreat", ignores women's work, and gives no dates, amounts or reasons. Table A Level 2; Table B Level 3 for the correct but thin detail.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and give the other side.** "If the test is the law on the family, the term 'retreat' fits. Abortion, which had been legal and free since November 1920, was banned on 27 June 1936 except for health reasons, and the fee for divorce, which had been a formality, rose to 50, 150 and 300 roubles for the first, second and third divorce. The reason was pronatalist: the state wanted more children, and it paid mothers of large families 2,000 roubles a year for each child after the sixth. But if the test is women's place in work, the picture is different. Women's share of the workforce rose from about 24 per cent in 1928 to about 39 per cent in 1940, and women trained as drivers and machine operators. A retreat in family law was combined with an advance in paid work."

*Commentary.* Now the knowledge is selected to prove a point, with two criteria and a mini-judgement. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. It still needs a view on what "Great Retreat" means, the reasons for the policy and a clear overall judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view that Stalin's policies towards women were a Great Retreat is partly right. It describes what happened to family law in the mid-1930s well, but it misdescribes what happened to women's work and to the principle of equality, and it assumes that the 1920s were more radical than they were. The best judgement is that there was a retreat from the revolution's hope of replacing the family, but not from the use of women's labour, and that the real change was a shift in what the state wanted from women.

The case for a retreat rests on the law of 1936. The Code of 1918 had made divorce easy and the decree of November 1920 had legalised abortion, in the belief that women would be freed from the family, which Alexandra Kollontai and others expected to wither away. The decree of 27 June 1936 reversed these policies. It banned abortion except where the woman's life or health was in danger, raised the cost of divorce to 50, 150 and 300 roubles, made the payment of maintenance easier to enforce, and paid mothers of large families 2,000 roubles a year for five years for each child after the sixth. In 1934 male homosexuality was made a crime, and in 1935 the New Year tree returned. The Zhenotdel, the party's women's department, had been abolished in January 1930, and the party's claim was that the 'woman question' was solved. The message of the 1930s, which was that motherhood was a 'great and honourable duty', is close to what Timasheff meant by retreat.

However, the term overstates the contrast in two ways. First, the 1920s were less radical than they are often painted. The Code of 1926 recognised unregistered marriages not because the state wished to abolish marriage, but because so many men abandoned wives and children in the 1920s, and Goldman shows that the leaders were already worried that the state could not afford nurseries or canteens to replace the family. The decree of 1936 was a change of emphasis on a practical problem as much as a turn against an ideal. Second, the 'retreat' did not cover women's work. In the first Five Year Plan, when labour was short, the state pushed women into industry, and their share of the workforce rose from about 24 per cent in 1928 to about 39 per cent in 1940, with women becoming tractor drivers (Pasha Angelina from 1933), builders of the Metro and pilots. The Constitution of 5 December 1936 in Article 122 gave women equal rights 'in all spheres', and no one proposed to send women back to the home. Equality in law, paid work and training, which were the main gains of the revolution for women, stayed.

What the regime wanted had changed, however, and that is why 'retreat' has some force. In the 1920s women were to be liberated from the family and to join the party in political life. In the 1930s women were to be both workers and mothers of many children, with no escape from the double burden. The abortion ban shows the cost: recorded abortions fell from about 1.9 million in 1935 to about 570,000 in 1937, yet the People's Commissar of Health reported that infant mortality rose from 146 to 162 per thousand between 1935 and 1938, which suggests that illegal abortions rose. Women gained jobs but lost control over their bodies and the political voice of the Zhenotdel.

In conclusion, 'Great Retreat' is accurate for family law and for the ideal of liberation, but it is not accurate for women's work or for the principle of legal equality, and the 1920s were not as radical as the label implies. A better description would be a change of direction: from a hope of freeing women from the family to a policy of using women both as workers and as mothers for the needs of the state."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): the answer defines the claim, uses wide-ranging criteria (law, work, political voice, the aims of the regime), argues both sides in detail with links between them, tests the assumption behind the label, and ends with a supported judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates (27 June 1936, January 1930, 5 December 1936), figures (50, 150, 300 roubles; 2,000 roubles; 1.9 million to 570,000; 24 to 39 per cent), and named people and historians used for argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 800–900 words in 40 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Assess the extent to which Stalin transformed Soviet culture [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess" asks you to weigh how far something changed, so you need criteria for "transform" (organisation, content, audience, belief), evidence of change and of continuity, and a judgement.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) who controlled culture; (2) what could be produced; (3) what the audience read, saw and heard; (4) what lasted. Change: Cultural Revolution (1928–31); RAPP dissolved (23 April 1932); Union of Writers and the Congress of 1934; Socialist Realism; the attack on Shostakovich (January 1936). Continuity: popular taste; classics; hidden works; Russian traditions. Judgement: a transformation of the organisation of culture, less of its effects.

**A Level 2/3 paragraph (about Table A Level 2, 5 marks; Table B Level 3, 7 marks).** "Stalin changed Soviet culture a lot. In 1934 Socialist Realism was introduced, which meant that artists had to show workers in a heroic way. Writers who did not agree were arrested, like Mandelstam. Shostakovich was criticised in Pravda. So Stalin transformed Soviet culture completely."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant examples, but it **lists** and then asserts "completely". It does not say what "transform" means, and it does not consider what stayed the same or how audiences responded. Table A Level 2; Table B Level 3.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion.** "If transformation means who controls culture, the change was complete. In the 1920s there were rival groups, such as RAPP and the 'fellow-travellers', and artists could experiment. After the decree of 23 April 1932 the groups were dissolved and each art had a single union under party control, and at the First Congress of Soviet Writers in 1934 Socialist Realism became the only permitted method. This control was enforced by rewards, such as publication and a flat in Moscow, and by punishment: Mandelstam was arrested in 1934 and Babel was shot in 1940. On this test, Stalin transformed the organisation of culture."

*Commentary.* A good Level 4 paragraph: it applies one criterion, with dates and names, to reach a mini-judgement. To reach Level 5, the essay needs other criteria (the effect on audiences and on what lasted) and an overall judgement.

**A Level 5 conclusion (and the paragraph that sets it up).**

"On the second test, the effect on what people actually watched and read, the transformation was less complete. Soviet audiences were not simply passive. *Chapaev* (1934) and the musical comedies of Aleksandrov were seen by millions because they were entertaining as well as loyal, and Sholokhov's *Quiet Flows the Don* was admired for its characters, not just its politics. Shostakovich's Fifth Symphony (1937), which followed the attack of January 1936, survived as music because it said more than the party's critics understood. Some of the greatest works of the period, Bulgakov's *The Master and Margarita* and Akhmatova's *Requiem*, were written for the drawer, and were published decades later. This shows that the state could control what was published and performed but not what artists thought.

Overall, Stalin transformed the organisation of culture almost completely: one union for each art, a single approved method and a censor, backed by rewards and by fear. He transformed the content of public culture to a very large degree, with heroic workers, Stalin's portrait and the Five Year Plans in every art. But he transformed beliefs and tastes much less, and the greatest achievements of Soviet culture of the 1930s were often those which escaped or outlived the rules. The judgement therefore depends on the test: by control, the transformation was almost total; by effect, it was partial. The more important test is the second, because a regime that wanted to make a 'new Soviet man' judged its success by what people felt, and in that it fell short."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies **criteria** (control, content, effect), weighs them, and reaches a supported judgement that links back to the regime's own aim. With the earlier paragraph on control, this is Level 5: Table A 14, Table B 13, about 27 out of 30.
:::

:::eg Evaluate how far Stalin's religious policies destroyed the influence of the Church [30] (plan and Level 4 paragraph)
**Plan.** Criteria: (1) the Church's organisation and buildings; (2) the clergy; (3) the beliefs of the people; (4) the regime's own judgement. Destruction: Law of 8 April 1929, closures, the cathedral blown up on 5 December 1931, the Terror of 1937–38. Survival: the census of 6 January 1937 (about 57 per cent of adults believers), worship at home, suppression of the census as a sign of the regime's alarm. Judgement: the Church as an institution was largely destroyed; its influence on belief was not.

**A Level 4 paragraph.** "The influence of the Church was destroyed as an organisation, but not as a belief. After the Law on Religious Associations of 8 April 1929 religious groups could not teach children or run charities, and local authorities were able to close churches. Thousands were closed in the Cultural Revolution against the Church, bells were taken down, the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour was blown up on 5 December 1931, and in the Terror of 1937–38 tens of thousands of clergy and active believers were arrested. But the census of 6 January 1937 showed that about 57 per cent of adults still said they were believers, and the regime thought the result so damaging that it suppressed the census and punished its organisers. Belief survived in the home, in secret baptisms and in the memories of women. The regime had destroyed the Church's public place but not its influence."

*Commentary.* A good Level 4 paragraph: it applies a criterion (organisation against belief), uses precise knowledge, and reaches a mini-judgement. To reach Level 5, the essay would compare the Church with other faiths, explain why repression was not completed (the weakness of the Militant Godless, Peris), and say how the survival of belief affected the regime's later decision to compromise with the Church after 1941 (a closing note only).
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the extent to which education was transformed under Stalin." [30]

A student wrote:

"Education was transformed under Stalin. Before the revolution many Russians could not read, but under Stalin schools were built everywhere and everyone learned to read. Stalin also introduced rules in schools, so children had to behave. The Komsomol also taught young people to be loyal. Education was therefore completely transformed."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): relevant and clear, but it is a **narrative with an assertion**. There are no criteria for "transformed", no dates or figures, and one side only. "Everyone learned to read" is wrong (the census of 1939 gave about 81 per cent), and "introduced rules" misses the point that the 1930s *reversed* the experiments of the 1920s. Table B Level 2–3: some relevant knowledge, but vague and imprecise.

**The fix.** Define the change and test it: "Education changed in two opposite ways. In numbers it was transformed: primary schooling became compulsory in 1930 and literacy among those aged nine and over rose from about 51 per cent in 1926 to about 81 per cent in 1939. In method it was reversed: the progressive experiments of the 1920s, such as group projects and the absence of marks and homework, were ended by decrees from 1931, marks returned in 1935, pedology was condemned on 4 July 1936, and fees for the upper years and for higher education were introduced on 2 October 1940. Education was therefore transformed in its reach, but it moved closer to the old school in its methods." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.
:::

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What were the main provisions of the decree of 27 June 1936?
A: It banned abortion except where a woman's life or health was threatened or a serious disease could be inherited; raised divorce fees to 50, 150 and 300 roubles for the first, second and third and later divorces; made maintenance payments easier to enforce; and gave a mother with six children 2,000 roubles a year for five years for each further child, with bigger payments for ten.
Q: Why did the state change its family policy in the 1930s?
A: It wanted a higher birth rate for the industrial and military power of the state, and it saw the family as a basis of social stability. It could not afford the nurseries and canteens that would have replaced the family, and the abandonment of wives and children in the 1920s worried leaders.
Q: What was the Zhenotdel, and what happened to it?
A: The party's women's department, set up in 1919 to bring women into political and social life. It was abolished in January 1930 on the grounds that the "woman question" had been solved.
Q: Give two opportunities and two limits for women at work in the 1930s.
A: Opportunities: entry into industry and training (about 24 per cent of the workforce in 1928 and about 39 per cent in 1940), and figures such as Pasha Angelina and the aviators of 1938. Limits: lower pay and few women in leadership; the double burden of housework and childcare; the pressure to have many children after 1936.
Q: What was korenizatsiya and how did it change in the 1930s?
A: Korenizatsiya ("indigenisation", from 1923) promoted local people and languages in the non-Russian republics. In the 1930s national elites were purged, Russian was promoted (compulsory in schools from 13 March 1938) and peoples such as the Koreans (about 172,000, in 1937) were deported.
Q: What did the party do to schools between 1931 and 1936, and why?
A: It reversed the experiments of the 1920s: it restored subject lessons, a fixed curriculum, exams, marks (1935), homework and teachers' authority, and condemned pedology (4 July 1936). Engineers and managers were needed who had basic knowledge, and experiments had given poor results.
Q: What were Socialist Realism, RAPP and the First Congress of Soviet Writers?
A: Socialist Realism was the official method of art and literature: realistic, optimistic and loyal to the party. RAPP was the proletarian writers' group that dominated in the Cultural Revolution and was dissolved on 23 April 1932. The First Congress of Soviet Writers (17 August to 1 September 1934) set out the new method under the Union of Soviet Writers.

A: On 28 January 1936 *Pravda* attacked his opera *Lady Macbeth of the Mtsensk District* as "formalist" (after Stalin attended on 26 January). It showed that the party would decide what was acceptable in music, and he withdrew the Fourth Symphony and wrote the Fifth (1937).
Q: What did the law of 8 April 1929 and the events of 5 December 1931 show about policy towards religion?
A: The law banned religious teaching and charity and let the authorities close churches; the destruction of the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour on 5 December 1931 showed the regime's aim of removing religion from public life (the site was meant for the Palace of Soviets).
Q: Why was the census of 6 January 1937 suppressed?
A: It showed a smaller population than expected and about 57 per cent of adults saying they were believers. The regime condemned it, suppressed the results, arrested many organisers and held a new census in 1939 without a religious question.

Challenge: literacy and universal schooling changed what people could do; women in paid work changed households and the economy; a new elite of workers' children ruled; the ending of independent culture and of public religion was real. A* judgement distinguishes public from private life and groups, and argues that the appearance of change in public life was more complete than the change in beliefs, while the change in opportunities (education, work, mobility) was real.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Timasheff called the changes of the 1930s a "Great Retreat". How far does the evidence on the family, education and culture support his view?
H: What would the "retreat" be from? Find the 1920s position in each area.
H: In which area was the "retreat" strongest, and in which was there a "forward march"?
A: Support: the family (abortion banned, divorce costly, motherhood praised); education (discipline, subjects, marks, nationalist history, fees); culture (the end of experiment, Socialist Realism, Russian classics, the return of the nation's past). Challenge: women's work, mass schooling and literacy and the control of culture were new and advanced the regime's aims, and Fitzpatrick sees mixed old and new in "everyday Stalinism". A* judgement says that the "retreat" was in the means and ideals of the 1920s, but not in the regime's power or in its aim of building a socialist state.
:::

:::recap
- **Family**: Code of 1918, abortion legal (1920), Zhenotdel (1919, abolished January 1930), Code of 1926. The Great Retreat: the decree of 27 June 1936 banned abortion except for health, raised divorce fees (50, 150, 300 roubles) and paid mothers of large families (2,000 roubles a year for five years from the seventh child); Article 122 of the 1936 Constitution on equality.
- **Women at work**: about 24 per cent of the workforce in 1928, about 39 per cent in 1940; the double burden; few in leadership.
- **National groups**: korenizatsiya from 1923; from the 1930s Russification (Russian compulsory 13 March 1938), purges of national elites (Skrypnyk, 1933), deportations (about 172,000 Koreans in 1937).
- **Education**: experiments of the 1920s; Cultural Revolution (1928–31); compulsory primary schooling (1930); decrees from September 1931 and marks (1935); history decree (May 1934); pedology condemned (4 July 1936); fees (2 October 1940). Literacy (aged nine and over) about 51 per cent (1926) to about 81 per cent (1939). Octobrists, Pioneers, Komsomol.
- **Culture**: RAPP dissolved (23 April 1932); First Congress of Soviet Writers (17 August to 1 September 1934); Socialist Realism; *Pravda* attack on Shostakovich (28 January 1936); Mandelstam, Babel, Bulgakov, Akhmatova; *Chapaev*, Eisenstein, Aleksandrov.
- **Religion**: League of the Militant Godless (1925); Sergii's declaration (29 July 1927); Law on Religious Associations (8 April 1929); the Cathedral of Christ the Saviour destroyed (5 December 1931); the Terror of 1937–38; census of 6 January 1937 (about 57 per cent of adults believers), suppressed.
- **Judgement**: transformation was greatest in the organisation of public life (culture, religion, schooling) and in women's work and literacy; it was smaller in beliefs and tastes; and in the family and education it combined a real new reach with a return to older methods.
:::
