:::why
Paper 1 is the paper that surprises students most. It is not a memory test of a topic, and it is not an essay: you are given four short sources and must show what they can and cannot prove. Three quarters of its marks are for handling sources and only one quarter for knowledge, so a student who knows the topic well can still score badly by describing the sources instead of weighing them. The skill is learnable, and the mark scheme tells you exactly what each level rewards. This chapter turns that mark scheme into a method you can practise.

## What the paper looks like

**The front page.** Every 9489 question paper, for the June 2024 papers and for the 2027 specimen alike, has the same front page. It says you answer on the enclosed answer booklet and will need only that booklet. The instructions are "Answer one question from one section only" and list the three sections: Section A, the European option; Section B, the American option; Section C, the International option. The information box says the total mark for the paper is 40 and that the marks for each question or part are shown in square brackets. Nothing else is supplied and nothing else is mentioned as allowed: no map, no list of dates, no extra text. Everything you need is on the four sources and in your head.

**Which question you answer.** Each section holds one question, headed by the title of that year's Paper 1 topic: for example "France, 1774–1814". You answer the question in your own option. You do not choose between questions, and you do not choose between topics. If you studied the European option, you answer Section A, and the topic is fixed by the exam year (see the table below).

**The question.** The question begins "Read the sources and then answer both parts of the question." Then come the four sources, then the instruction "Answer both parts of the question with reference to the sources", then the two parts. Both parts are compulsory.

**The sources.** There are four, A to D. At least three are written and at most one is visual (a cartoon, a photograph or a poster). The whole set is at most 600 words. After each source comes its **provenance**, a short italic line that tells you what the source is, who made it and when, for example "From a letter written by … to …, March 1920." The provenance is part of the evidence. A visual source comes with its title and a short line explaining any words printed on it. Sources can be extracts from official documents, speeches, newspapers, letters, diaries, memoirs, petitions, reports and cartoons.

**The two parts.**
- **(a) [15]** has one fixed form: "Read Sources A and B. Compare these two sources as evidence about …" and then the issue. You use only A and B.
- **(b) [25]** begins "Read all of the sources." and then takes one of these forms: "How far do these sources agree that …?", "'…' How far do these sources support this view?" or "To what extent do the sources support this view?" You use all four sources, and your knowledge of the period to interpret them.

:::warn Old papers look slightly different
The 2024–26 syllabus used the same front page, but its Paper 1 was titled "Document Question" and the topics were different (some options have been replaced). Old papers are still excellent practice for sources that come from a topic you study, but the 2027–2029 papers are the standard to aim at, and the mark scheme tables have been updated. Always mark your practice with the tables in this chapter, not with an old mark scheme.
:::

## Which topic is on your paper?

Every AS option has three topics. In any exam year, **one** of them is the Paper 1 topic and the other two are on Paper 2. The topic changes every year in a fixed three-year rotation.

| Exam year | European option | American option | International option |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2027 | France, 1774–1814 | The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920 | Imperialism and the emergence of world powers, c.1870–1918 |
| 2028 | Liberalism and nationalism in Germany, 1815–71 | The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41 | International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation |
| 2029 | Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924 | The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77 | International history, 1929–39: the rise of extremism and the road to war |

So every topic you study will be a sources topic once in three years, and an essay topic in the other two. That has three consequences for how you prepare.

:::key How to prepare for your year's Paper 1 topic
1. **Learn all three topics of your option.** Paper 2 needs the other two, and your own topic's chapters teach both papers. A topic that is not on Paper 1 this year is on Paper 2.
2. **Treat your Paper 1 topic's four key question chapters as the source of your context.** Paper 1 gives you short extracts and asks you to explain them. Your knowledge is what lets you say why a source was written, who it was written for, and what it leaves out.
3. **Practise the skill on every topic, not only your Paper 1 topic.** Each AS topic chapter in this course has a worked Paper 1 (a) and (b), at every level. Sources are the same kind of document wherever they come from.
4. **Build a context bank for your topic.** For each key question write down about ten dated events and the three or four key documents of the period (the type of document, who wrote it, why). The best way to explain a source is to link it to what was happening that week.
5. **Timed practice in the last weeks.** One (a) in 20 minutes, one (b) in 40 minutes, then mark yourself with the tables below.
:::

## What the examiners are testing

Two assessment objectives (AOs) are tested on every mark of Paper 1.

:::def AO3: Historical sources
Understand, analyse, evaluate and interpret a range of historical sources in context. On Paper 1 this is three quarters of the marks: what a source says, what it shows, how far it can be trusted for a particular question, and how its origin and purpose shape it.
:::

:::def AO1: Historical knowledge
Recall, select and use appropriate historical knowledge. On Paper 1 it is one quarter of the marks, and it is knowledge **used on the sources**: the context that explains why a source says what it says.
:::

Two things follow. First, you do not earn much by writing everything you know about the topic. The lowest level of part (b) is described as "an essay about the topic which may be illustrated by the sources". Second, you do not earn much by writing about sources with no knowledge either: the top level asks for "specific contextual knowledge" used to evaluate each source.

## Reading the four sources: a method

Spend the first eight minutes or so reading. Do not start writing the moment you see Source A.

:::steps
1. **Read both questions first.** (a) names the two sources and the issue; (b) gives the statement you will weigh. Underline the key words: "evidence about the intentions", "how far", "economic problems". Everything you write must be about these words.
2. **Read each source's provenance before its text.** It tells you the kind of document and the author, and your mind should ask: what would this person have wanted to say, and to whom?
3. **Read each source and write one line in the margin**: what does this source say about the issue in the question? Use your own words and a short quotation. Mark whether it supports the statement, challenges it, or does both.
4. **Add the context.** In two or three words beside each source write the event that explains it: "just after the hall was closed", "a year after the harvest failed", "during the election campaign".
5. **Plan (a) and (b) before you write either.** Even a five-line plan raises the level of an answer.
:::

### Nature, origin and purpose

The mark scheme speaks again and again of three things. They are the tools of AO3.

:::def Nature, origin and purpose (NOP)
**Nature**: what kind of source it is (a private letter, a law, a speech, a cartoon, a newspaper article, a memoir written years later). **Origin**: who made it, when and where, and what that person knew or wanted. **Purpose**: why it was made and for whom: to persuade, to inform, to command, to record, to defend the author. Together, in their context, they decide how much weight a source can bear for a particular question.
:::

| Kind of source | Often strong evidence of | Often weak evidence of |
|---|---|---|
| A private letter or diary | what the writer privately thought or saw on a day | what other people thought; the whole picture |
| A law, decree or official order | what the government intended or wanted to happen | whether it was obeyed or worked |
| A speech | what the speaker wanted the audience to believe or do | what the speaker really believed |
| A newspaper article | what was reported or argued, and what the paper's readers were told | the facts of what happened (depends on the paper) |
| A cartoon or poster | the message the maker wanted to spread; popular feeling it counted on | measured opinion; detailed events |
| An official report or statistics | the scale of something, collected by the state | what the people counted felt; what was left out |
| A memoir written years later | how the author wanted to be remembered | the details of the time; the author's role |
| A petition or list of grievances | what a group asked for and how they described their problems | whether the demands were typical or just a model copied from elsewhere |

Read the table as a starting point, not as a rule. A private diary can lie, and a government order can show exactly what happened. The honest question is always: **strong or weak evidence of what, for this question?**

:::key A source is never just "reliable" or "unreliable"
Say what the source is **good evidence for** and what it is **poor evidence for**, for the question in front of you. A cartoon is poor evidence of what most people thought, but excellent evidence of the message a campaign wanted to spread. A single source can be strong for one question and weak for another. Level 5 of part (b) asks you to assess "whether the source is stronger or weaker as evidence in response to the question". Notice the last phrase: the strength depends on the question.
:::

:::warn Avoid the generic comment
"This source is biased because it is from the king" is the comment the mark scheme describes at Level 3: "comments about bias or reliability, which are not based on contextual understanding". Every source has a point of view. The skill is to say **what** the point of view is, **why** the author held it **at that moment**, and **what difference it makes** to the evidence. "Written three days after the deputies had defied him, to restore his authority" is contextual. "He is the king so he is biased" is not.
:::

## Part (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence

### What the question asks

"Compare these two sources as evidence about [an issue]." Three words matter.
- **Compare**: put the sources side by side, in both directions: how they are alike and how they differ.
- **As evidence**: not only what they say, but what they are worth as proof. This is where context, nature, origin and purpose come in.
- **About [the issue]**: stay on the issue named. A source may say many things; use the parts that bear on this issue.

### The levels of Table A

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 12–15 | A **developed comparison**: explains **why** the points of similarity and difference exist, through contextual awareness and/or source evaluation |
| 3 | 8–11 | Compares the views, identifies **both** similarities **and** differences, and supports them with content from the sources |
| 2 | 4–7 | Identifies similarities **or** differences (may be one-sided), supported from the sources; **or** identifies both but only asserts them, without support from the sources |
| 1 | 1–3 | **Describes** the content of each source; may make very simple comparisons ("one is a letter, the other a speech") that are not developed |
| 0 | 0 | No engagement with the sources |

Marks are never deducted. Examiners find the best-fit level, then give the top mark if the answer **convincingly** meets it, a middle mark if it **adequately** meets it, and the lowest if it **just** meets it.

:::key The step from level to level
- **Level 1 to 2**: stop describing each source in turn. Start **comparing**: one point, both sources.
- **Level 2 to 3**: make **both** similarities and differences, and **support each from the sources** with a short quotation or close reference.
- **Level 3 to 4**: add the **why**. Use the context, and the nature, origin and purpose of each source, to explain the similarity or the difference.
:::

### The ladder: one comparison, four levels

Here are two real sources from the French Revolution, which you meet again in [France (1)](#hist.eu.france.1). Read how the same two sources are used at each level.

:::eg Part (a) ladder: Level 1 to Level 4
**Source A**

The National Assembly, considering that it has been called to establish the constitution of the kingdom, to bring about the regeneration of public order and to maintain the true principles of the monarchy, and that nothing can prevent it from continuing its deliberations wherever it is forced to meet, decrees that all members of this Assembly shall immediately take a solemn oath never to separate, and to reassemble wherever circumstances require, until the constitution of the kingdom is established on firm foundations.

*Adapted from the Tennis Court Oath, sworn by the deputies of the National Assembly at Versailles, 20 June 1789.*

**Source B**

The King wishes the ancient distinction of the three orders of the State to be preserved in its entirety, as essentially linked to the constitution of his kingdom. He declares null the decisions taken by the deputies of the Third Estate on 17 June, and those which followed, as illegal and unconstitutional. No new tax shall be established without the consent of the representatives of the nation. I order you, gentlemen, to separate at once, and to go tomorrow morning each to the chamber set aside for your order.

*Adapted from the declarations read on behalf of Louis XVI, and his closing words, at the royal session (séance royale) of the Estates-General, Versailles, 23 June 1789.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about who held authority in France in June 1789.

**Level 1 (1–3 marks): describes each source.**
"Source A is the Tennis Court Oath. The deputies swear never to separate until there is a constitution. Source B is the king's speech. He says the three orders must stay separate, and he says the decisions of 17 June are null. Both sources are from 1789."

*Why Level 1.* Accurate, but it summarises each source in turn and never compares them. The only link ("both are from 1789") is a very simple point and is not developed.

**Level 2 (4–7 marks): one comparison, supported, or two asserted.**
"The sources disagree about the Assembly. Source A says it will not separate until the constitution is made, but in Source B the king orders the deputies 'to separate at once'."

*Why Level 2.* A relevant difference, supported from both sources. But there is no similarity, so the comparison is one-sided. A student who wrote "The sources are similar in some ways and different in others because one is by the deputies and the other by the king" would also be at Level 2, because the points are asserted and not supported from the sources.

**Level 3 (8–11 marks): similarities and differences, each supported.**
"Both sources claim to be defending the constitution. Source A says the Assembly will 'maintain the true principles of the monarchy', and Source B says the orders must be kept because they are 'linked to the constitution of his kingdom'. They differ about who has the authority to decide it. In Source A the Assembly says that it 'has been called to establish the constitution' and that nothing can stop it meeting 'wherever it is forced to meet'. In Source B the king declares the decisions of 17 June 'null' and 'illegal' and orders the deputies to meet by order."

*Why Level 3.* Both similarity and difference, each supported with short quotations. It does not yet say **why** the sources agree and differ, so it cannot reach Level 4.

**Level 4 (12–15 marks): explains the similarities and differences.** Add a paragraph such as:
"The context explains the clash. Source A was sworn in a tennis court because the deputies had found their hall closed on 20 June, and the words 'never to separate' answer the fear that the king would dissolve them. It is a public pledge meant to bind the deputies and to rally Paris, so it presents the Assembly as defending the monarchy, not attacking it. Source B was read three days later at the royal session, where the king wanted to regain control by offering a concession on taxation while restoring voting by order. That is why both claim the constitution: each side wanted to show it was the lawful one. Source B is strong evidence of what Louis wanted. It is weak evidence of where authority lay, because the deputies stayed in the hall after he left, and on 27 June he ordered the clergy and nobility to join the Assembly."

*Why Level 4.* The similarities and differences are explained through the occasion, the purpose and the audience of each source, and the paragraph ends with a comparison of their value as evidence for **this** issue. That is a developed comparison.
:::

### How to write part (a)

Write about 20 minutes, three to four paragraphs. No fixed word count is set, but a developed Level 4 answer is usually around 400 to 500 words.

1. **Similarities** (one short paragraph): one or two points both sources share, each with a short quotation from each. Say what the point is before you quote.
2. **Differences** (one or two paragraphs): the main points on which they differ, again with quotations from both. Compare on the **same point**: "On who holds authority, A says …, but B says …".
3. **Why** (one or two paragraphs): explain the similarities and differences using the context and the provenance. Why was each written, by whom, when, for whom? What had just happened? What does each source choose to say or to leave out?
4. **A final sentence** that says which source is more useful on the issue and why, or how they complement each other.

:::exam Technique for part (a)
- Write "Source A … whereas Source B …" in **one sentence** on each point, so that you are comparing and not describing.
- Use only Sources A and B. Do not use C and D in (a): they are for (b).
- Use the issue named in the question as your guide. Ask in every paragraph: "does this tell me something about the issue?"
- Quote briefly: a phrase of three to eight words. Never copy out a whole sentence.
- Put your own knowledge into the "why" paragraph, as context. It is not a place to write a narrative.
- The question says "as evidence". A closing judgement on how the sources work together as evidence is the mark of the top of Level 4.
:::

## Part (b) [25]: how far do the sources support the statement?

### What the question asks

Part (b) gives you a statement (or a question) and asks how far the four sources support it. The three standard forms are:
- "How far do these sources agree that …?"
- "'…' How far do these sources support this view?"
- "To what extent do the sources support this view?"

All three ask for the same thing: use every source to build a case **for** the statement, a case **against** it (or against part of it), judge the strength of the sources as evidence, and decide on a balance.

### The levels of Table B

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 21–25 | **Evaluates** the sources to reach a judgement: understands all the sources; explains in detail how they support and/or challenge; uses specific contextual knowledge (nature, origin or purpose in context) to evaluate them; assesses whether each source is **stronger or weaker as evidence** for this question; judges which side is stronger, addressing **"how far"** |
| 4 | 16–20 | A **developed response** explaining how the sources support and challenge: detailed use of **all** the sources; represents their arguments accurately (may show nuance); makes explicit, well-explained links to the question; uses context to help interpret the sources |
| 3 | 11–15 | Uses the sources to **support and challenge**: points from the sources on both sides; may misread one or two; links to the question are implicit; comments on bias or reliability that are not based on context |
| 2 | 6–10 | Uses the sources to support **or** challenge: valid points on one side; or an attempt at both sides in which one side is misread; or argues that all the sources support (or all challenge) |
| 1 | 1–5 | **General** use of the sources: describes content without showing support or challenge; or an essay on the topic with the sources as illustration; or assertions with no reference to content |
| 0 | 0 | No engagement with the sources |

:::key The step from level to level in part (b)
- **Level 1 to 2**: use the sources to answer the question, not to illustrate an essay: each source must be used to **support or challenge** the statement.
- **Level 2 to 3**: use the sources on **both sides**.
- **Level 3 to 4**: be **accurate** and **detailed** about every source, link each use **explicitly** to the question, and use context to interpret the sources (not just "it is biased").
- **Level 4 to 5**: **evaluate**. For each source, say whether it is stronger or weaker evidence for this statement and why; then give a **judgement** that answers "how far".
:::

### A method for part (b)

:::steps
1. **Sort the sources.** On a quick grid write the four sources down the side and three columns across: *supports*, *challenges*, *qualifies* (supports one part of the statement, challenges another). A source can appear in more than one column. If every source ends up in the same column, look again: the mark scheme gives Level 2 to an answer in which "all the sources support or challenge" and a good question nearly always has sources on both sides.
2. **Evaluate each source for this statement.** For each, one line: *what it is good evidence of* and *what it is weak evidence of*, using its nature, origin and purpose and the context you know.
3. **Decide your judgement before you write.** Is the statement largely true, partly true or largely false, and what is the key reason? Write it at the top of your plan.
4. **Write in arguments, not in sources.** Two or three paragraphs, each making a point that answers "how far" and using **two or more** sources for or against it (one paragraph for the case for, one for the case against, one for the qualification). Name every source at least once. Do not write "Source A says … Source B says … Source C says …" in four disconnected paragraphs: it can still score if every paragraph is tied to the question, but it tends to turn into description and to leave out the comparison and the judgement.
5. **Finish with the judgement.** Two to four sentences: which side is stronger, why, and how far the statement holds. Say what the sources **cannot** show.
:::

### Evaluating a source as evidence: the ladder

Using the same two sources as in part (a), suppose the statement in part (b) were: "'By June 1789 Louis XVI had lost control of events.' How far do these sources support this view?"

:::eg Part (b) ladder: how one source is handled at Levels 3, 4 and 5
**Level 3: a comment on bias.**
"Source B challenges the view, because the king says 'I order you, gentlemen, to separate at once', so he is still giving orders. But Source B is from the king, so it is biased and cannot be trusted. Source A supports the view because the deputies say they will never separate."

*Commentary.* Both sides, correct readings, but the link is implicit and "biased" is a general comment with no context. Level 3.

**Level 4: detailed, linked, with context.**
"Source B challenges the view because the king declares the decisions of 17 June 'null' and orders the deputies to separate, which shows he still claimed the power to command. Yet he also promises that 'no new tax shall be established without the consent of the representatives of the nation', a concession that shows he was already bargaining with the Third Estate. Source A supports the view: the deputies swear 'never to separate' until the constitution is made. It was sworn three days earlier, after the king's officials had closed the deputies' hall, so it was a defiance of the royal will."

*Commentary.* Both sources are read accurately and linked to the statement, with context to explain them and a nuanced point (the concession). Level 4.

**Level 5: evaluate the sources and judge.**
"Source B is strong evidence of what Louis wanted: it was read at a royal session called to restore control after the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly on 17 June, and its tone is one of command. But it is weak evidence of how much power he still had, because it is a statement of intention, and the events that followed contradicted it: the deputies stayed in the hall after he left, and on 27 June he ordered the clergy and nobility to join the Assembly. Source A is a collective pledge of defiance, which makes it excellent evidence of the Assembly's resolve, and its wording about the 'true principles of the monarchy' shows that the deputies still presented themselves as loyal. Taken together, the two sources suggest that by 23 June Louis could still command in words but could no longer make the deputies obey. On the sources alone, the view is therefore largely supported: control of events had begun to pass from the king to the Assembly, although he kept the army and the ability to act, as the troop movements of early July would show."

*Commentary.* Each source is assessed as stronger or weaker evidence **for this question**, in context, and the paragraph reaches a judgement on "how far". Level 5.
:::

### The judgement

The last paragraph is where many answers lose their top marks. A Level 5 conclusion does three things:
1. **It answers the question as set.** If the question is "how far", say how far: "largely", "only partly", "to a limited extent".
2. **It weighs the two sides.** Say which side of the argument is stronger and why: the more reliable sources, the more important point, the longer-term evidence.
3. **It is honest about the limit of the evidence.** "These sources cannot show what the peasants thought, since all four were written by townsmen" is a real evaluation.

:::exam Technique for part (b)
- Use **every** source, and name each one at least once. An answer that leaves out a source cannot show "detailed use of all the sources".
- Do not paraphrase at length. Quote the key phrase and explain what it shows about the statement.
- Put your own knowledge **inside** your treatment of the sources, to explain and test them. It is not a separate essay. An essay "illustrated by the sources" is Level 1.
- Use the question's own words in every paragraph ("this supports the view that …", "this challenges the claim that …").
- Do not write "reliable" or "unreliable" without saying **for what**.
- Leave about four minutes at the end for the judgement, and write it even if you are short of time. A two-sentence judgement is better than none.
:::

## Timing

The paper gives you 75 minutes for 40 marks: about 1.9 minutes a mark. The plan below is a suggestion, not a Cambridge rule. Practise with it and change it to suit you.

| Minutes | Task | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | Read the questions, the four sources and the provenance; annotate | n/a |
| 20 | Part (a): plan (2 min), write (18 min) | 15 |
| 4 | Part (b): sort the sources and plan | n/a |
| 38 | Part (b): write | 25 |
| 5 | Check, add a missing source or the judgement | n/a |
| **75** | **Total** | **40** |

Part (b) carries 25 of the 40 marks, so it deserves more than half of your writing time. Do not spend 35 minutes on part (a) because it comes first. If you are running short in part (b), write the judgement before you finish the last source.

## Common mistakes

These come straight from the wording of the levels: each is a thing the mark scheme says does **not** earn a higher level.

:::warn The ten commonest ways to lose marks on Paper 1
1. **Describing instead of comparing** (part (a), Level 1). "Source A says … Source B says …" with no word like "whereas" or "both". Compare point by point.
2. **Asserting without support** (Level 2). "They agree on some things" with no quotation. Always quote the phrase.
3. **Ignoring "as evidence".** The question is about value as proof, not about content only.
4. **One-sided comparison.** Similarities only, or differences only, caps part (a) at Level 2.
5. **Generic bias.** "It is biased because he was the king / a worker / a politician." Say what the point of view is and why it was held at that moment.
6. **Reading a source wrongly.** Level 3 of part (b) "may misread one or two of the sources". Re-read the extract and the provenance before you decide what it shows.
7. **Not using all four sources in part (b).** A missing source costs the "detailed use of all the sources" of Level 4.
8. **An essay on the topic.** Writing everything you know about the period, with a source mentioned now and then. This is Level 1.
9. **All sources on one side.** If your grid shows every source supporting the statement, look for the source that qualifies it or the limit of what it proves.
10. **No judgement.** An answer that never says "how far" cannot reach Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak answer
Part (b) question: "'By June 1789 Louis XVI had lost control of events.' How far do these sources support this view?" Here is a student's answer to the part of the question on Sources A and B.

"Source A is the Tennis Court Oath and Source B is the king's speech. Source A is biased because it was written by the deputies and they wanted to get a constitution. Source B is biased because the king did not want to lose his power. Source A says they will never separate. Source B says that they must separate. So the sources disagree. In 1789 the Estates-General met on 5 May and there were arguments about voting. On 17 June the Third Estate called itself the National Assembly. On 14 July the Bastille was stormed. In August the Declaration of the Rights of Man was passed. So the king lost control of events."

**The level.** Level 3, at the bottom of the level (11 marks), and only because it uses two sources on both sides and reads them correctly. Its weaknesses keep it there: (1) the only comments on origin are generic "biased" judgements; (2) it never says **why** either source is weaker or stronger evidence; (3) the second half is a narrative of the Revolution that uses no source and does not explain how the events show a loss of control; (4) it does not mention Sources C or D at all; (5) "so the king lost control" is an assertion, not a judgement answering "how far".

**The fix.**
- Replace "biased" with context: "Source A, sworn three days before the king's speech, is a defiant pledge, so it is strong evidence of the deputies' resolve."
- Use the events to **test** the sources, not to tell the story: "Source B shows the king still gave orders, but the deputies stayed in the hall, so Source B shows what he wanted and not what he could do."
- Use all four sources, each linked to the statement.
- End with a judgement: "largely, but not wholly, supported: the king had lost control of the Assembly by late June but still commanded the army."
:::

## Using past papers and mark schemes

The best practice is a **real paper under real conditions**. Cambridge publishes specimen papers and mark schemes for the 2027 syllabus, and recent past papers, on its website (link below).

- **The 2027 specimen Paper 1** has one question in each section: France (Section A), the Gilded Age and the Progressive Era (Section B) and Imperialism (Section C), the 2027 rotation. Use the section for your option, whichever year you sit; the format is the same every year.
- **Old past papers (2024–26 syllabus)** have the same front page and a similar style. Use them for sources on topics that survive in the new syllabus, and mark them with the tables in this chapter.
- **The mark schemes** list "indicative content" for each part: the similarities and differences the examiners expect for (a), and the arguments for and against for (b). They always add "Accept any other valid responses". So compare your answer with the indicative content, but remember you can earn full marks with a point that is not on the list, if it is valid and well supported.

:::recap
- Paper 1: **1 h 15 min, 40 marks**; one question, two parts, from your option; four sources (A to D), at most 600 words, at least three written, at most one visual; booklet only.
- Marks: **(a) 15** and **(b) 25**; AO1 25% and AO3 75% of the paper; AS weight 40%, A Level 20%.
- **Rotation:** France, Germany, Russia (European); Gilded Age, Crash and Depression, Civil War (American); Imperialism, 1919–29, 1929–39 (International), in 2027, 2028 and 2029 respectively.
- **(a)** compares A and B **as evidence**: Level 1 describes; Level 2 compares one-sidedly or without support; Level 3 gives supported similarities and differences; Level 4 explains **why**, through context and the nature, origin or purpose of the sources.
- **(b)** uses all four sources and the question's words: Level 2 one side; Level 3 both sides; Level 4 detailed, accurate, explicit links, context; Level 5 evaluates each source as stronger or weaker evidence and judges **how far**.
- Within a level: **just** (lowest mark), **adequately** (middle), **convincingly** (highest). Marks are positive and never deducted.
- A source is never simply reliable: say **what** it is good evidence of, **for this question**.
- Common errors: describing; asserting; generic bias; leaving out a source; an essay on the topic; no judgement.
- Suggested timing: 8 min reading, 20 min part (a), 4 min planning (b), 38 min writing (b), 5 min checking.
:::
