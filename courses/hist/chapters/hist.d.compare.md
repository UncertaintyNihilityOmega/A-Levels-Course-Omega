:::why
Between 1922 and 1933 three of Europe's great powers came under one-party dictators who claimed to be saving their nations from chaos, and within twenty years those dictators had changed the world. You will study at least two of them for Paper 4. Looking at them together shows what was special in each case and what was shared, and it trains the most useful habit in A Level history: testing an explanation against a different case. "Mussolini won because the elites feared socialism" means more once you see that Hitler's elites feared the same thing, and that Stalin faced no such elites at all.
:::

:::try Warm-up
Q: Who invited Mussolini to become prime minister in 1922, and on what date did he take office? (from [Mussolini's Italy (1)](#hist.d.italy.1))
A: King Victor Emmanuel III invited him to form a government on 29 October 1922, after the March on Rome had begun (28 October), and he took office on 31 October. The king had refused to sign a decree of martial law that would have used the army against the Fascists.
Q: What was Stalin's party post, and why did it help him against rivals in the 1920s? (from [Stalin's Russia (1)](#hist.d.stalin.1))
A: General Secretary (from April 1922). It gave him control over appointments, so he could fill local party posts and the Congress delegations with supporters, which his rivals Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin did not match.
Q: What did the Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 allow, and what was the vote? (from [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1))
A: It let the government make laws without the Reichstag's consent, including laws that departed from the constitution, for four years. It passed by 444 votes to 94 after the Communist deputies had been arrested or driven out and with the SA and SS in the hall.
:::

## Why compare, and what to be careful about

A comparison asks two questions about each pair of cases: **what do they share**, and **where do they differ**. Both answers teach you something. Italy, the Soviet Union and Germany shared a time (the years after the First World War and the Great Depression), a political form (one party, one leader, a secret police, a controlled press, a cult) and a tendency to see themselves as the answer to the failure of liberal democracy. They differed in their ideas (fascism and Nazism on the right, communism on the left), their scale (the numbers killed), their social basis, and what they wanted for the world.

:::warn Three cautions before you start
1. **They were not the same.** Fascist Italy, Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR had different ideologies, different aims and very different scales of violence. "Dictatorship" is a useful category, but when you say "all three" you must be able to say what they shared in each case. Beware of arguing that everything the three did was equal.
2. **Do not rank suffering.** Comparing the *scale* of terror is a historical question; deciding whose victims "mattered more" is not. State numbers and their sources, and write about victims as people.
3. **The Holocaust is distinct.** The Nazi regime's aim of murdering every Jewish man, woman and child in Europe has no counterpart in the other two regimes. The persecution of Jews is part of the Nazi story here, but the genocide itself is treated in [The Holocaust (1)](#hist.interp.holocaust.1), not summarised in a table.
:::

:::def Dictatorship
A system of government in which one person or one party holds power without being checked by law, elections or a free press, and can silence opponents by force.
:::

:::def Totalitarianism
A term used by political scientists and historians for a dictatorship that tries to control not only politics but the whole of life: the economy, culture, education and even beliefs, through one ideology, one party, mass propaganda and terror. Whether any regime fully achieved this, and which regimes count, are the central questions of the debate in the second half of this chapter.
:::

## Key question 1: how did the dictators gain control?

### Crisis after 1918

None of the three regimes came to power in a stable country.

- **Italy.** About 650,000 Italians died in the First World War, and many of the survivors came home to unemployment and inflation. Italy had joined the Allies in 1915 after the secret Treaty of London promised it land, and at the peace settlement it received less than it had been promised: the "mutilated victory". In September 1919 the poet Gabriele D'Annunzio seized Fiume with a force of nationalist ex-soldiers, and the government could not remove him for more than a year. In 1919–20 strikes and land occupations (the "Red Two Years", *Biennio Rosso*) frightened owners and the middle class. Coalition governments rose and fell. See [Mussolini's Italy (1)](#hist.d.italy.1).
- **Russia.** The Bolsheviks came to power in the middle of a war and a hunger crisis. The Tsar had fallen in February 1917; the Provisional Government failed to end the war, and the Bolsheviks seized power on 25 October 1917 (7 November New Style). Civil War (1918–21) followed, and by 1921 the economy had collapsed, so Lenin introduced the New Economic Policy. When Lenin died on 21 January 1924, the party he left behind was the only legal political force in the country. See [Russia (3)](#hist.eu.russia.3), [Russia (4)](#hist.eu.russia.4) and [Stalin's Russia (1)](#hist.d.stalin.1).
- **Germany.** The country was defeated in November 1918, the Kaiser fled and a republic was proclaimed. The new Weimar Republic had to accept the Treaty of Versailles, and its first years brought uprisings, the Ruhr occupation of 1923 and a hyperinflation. Stability returned between 1924 and 1929, but the Great Depression brought it down: about 5.6 million registered unemployed in 1932. See [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1).

:::key What the crises had in common
1. **A war that left people feeling cheated** (Italy's "mutilated victory"; Germany's "stab in the back" and Versailles; Russia's sacrifices for a war that the Tsar lost).
2. **Economic misery** that the existing system could not cure (inflation and unemployment in Italy; hyperinflation and then mass unemployment in Germany; hunger and collapse in Russia).
3. **Weak, divided governments** and politicians who did not command the respect of the people.
4. **A fear of revolution** on the right and an appeal of revolution on the left.
Where they differed is the starting point: Italy and Germany were liberal or democratic states, and the dictators had to destroy them from within; Russia's liberal government had fallen in 1917, so the Bolsheviks inherited a one-party state that Stalin then captured.
:::

### Routes to power: legal and revolutionary

:::cols
:::col Italy
- A movement of violent squads (*squadristi*) from 1920 and a "dual policy": violence in the provinces and deals in parliament.
- 28 October 1922: the March on Rome. The king, advised that the army might not obey, refused to sign martial law and appointed Mussolini on 29–31 October.
- 1922–26: step by step. The Acerbo Law (1923), the election of April 1924, the murder of Matteotti (10 June 1924) and the laws of 1925–26 ended political opposition.
:::
:::col USSR
- 1917: an armed insurrection led by a small party, then a civil war.
- 1922–29: Stalin used the party machine. As General Secretary from April 1922 he controlled appointments; he allied against Trotsky with Zinoviev and Kamenev, then against them with Bukharin, then against Bukharin (1928–29).
- By 1929 his rivals were defeated, and his fiftieth birthday in December 1929 launched the cult.
:::
:::col Germany
- Failed putsch (November 1923), then a switch to elections. Support grew from 2.6 per cent (1928) to 37.3 per cent (July 1932).
- 30 January 1933: President Hindenburg appointed Hitler Chancellor of a coalition cabinet.
- 1933–34: the Reichstag Fire decree (28 February), the Enabling Act (23 March), the banning of parties and unions, the Night of the Long Knives (30 June–2 July 1934), and on Hindenburg's death (2 August) the offices of president and chancellor combined in Hitler.
:::
:::

Two of the routes look alike, and one does not.

- **Italy and Germany: appointed, with violence behind.** In both countries a leader whose party was not the biggest group, or had no majority, was appointed to office by the head of state: the king in 1922 (when the Fascists held only about 35 of the 535 seats) and Hindenburg in 1933 (when the Nazis had 33.1 per cent in November 1932 and no majority). In both, the appointment followed months of pressure, of private deals with conservative politicians who thought they could control the newcomer, and of violence by party militias (the *squadristi*, the SA). In both, the leader then used the office to dismantle opposition from within. This is why historians speak of Hitler's "legal revolution" and of Mussolini's rise as a "seizure of power" that was also a handover. The two cases are close enough to carry the same warning: the men who thought they were using the dictator found that he was using them.
- **Russia: a seizure and then a capture.** The Bolsheviks took power by force in 1917, but Stalin's own rise was a different story: a contest inside a party that already held all the power, fought through committees, appointments and alliances. He was not appointed by anyone outside the party, and no election was involved. The comparison is useful because it shows that the *same* ruthlessness (using one rival against another, controlling appointments) worked in a system without elites at all.

:::note Hitler studied Mussolini
The Beer Hall Putsch of 8–9 November 1923 in Munich came a year after the March on Rome, and Hitler had admired Mussolini's success: a small party had shown that a march on the capital, backed by a threat of force and elite acquiescence, could win office. The putsch failed (Bavarian police fired on the marchers), and Hitler concluded that he must reach power by legal means with violence in the background. In the end he did what Mussolini had done in practice: he was appointed, not carried in. See [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1).
:::

### The role of elites, the army and violence

:::key Three questions that separate the cases
1. **Who handed over power, and why?** Italy: the king and conservative politicians who feared socialism and thought they could tame Mussolini. Germany: Hindenburg, Papen and some conservative leaders who believed they could "box in" Hitler in a coalition with only three Nazi ministers out of eleven. USSR: no outside elite; the party was the elite, and Stalin was its General Secretary.
2. **What did the army do?** In Italy it did not oppose the March on Rome, and the king would not use it. In Germany the army (the *Reichswehr*) stayed neutral in 1933 and in August 1934, after Hindenburg's death, its officers and men swore an oath to Hitler personally. In the USSR the Red Army was under party control, and Stalin purged its leaders in 1937–38.
3. **How much did violence matter?** Violence by the *squadristi* and the SA intimidated opponents and showed the elites that the Fascists and Nazis were useful. In the USSR the secret police (the Cheka from 1917, the OGPU from 1923) was a weapon of the party from the start, and in the 1920s Stalin's party rivals were removed from office, expelled and sent into exile, not shot: the first of the old leaders to be executed were Zinoviev and Kamenev, in August 1936.
:::

:::warn Do not say "big business put Hitler in power"
Some businessmen supported the Nazis and some gave money, but many industrialists preferred conservative parties, and historians disagree about how much the business elite mattered. The case that is firm is that conservative politicians, Hindenburg and the circle round him decided to appoint Hitler in January 1933. Likewise, in Italy the landowners and some industrialists supported the squads in 1921–22, but it was the king and politicians who invited Mussolini. Write "conservative elites" and name them.
:::

**What it means for the key question.** To explain how a dictator gained control, say what the crisis was, then what route he took and who let him through. The comparison suggests a criterion for an essay on [Mussolini's Italy (1)](#hist.d.italy.1) or [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1): *"how far did he win power by his own strength, and how far was it handed to him?"* In both countries the honest answer is "both", and the proportion changes the judgement.

## Key question 2: how did they govern?

### Terror and the police

All three regimes had a secret police, special courts and camps, and all three used fear to stop opposition. Where they differ most is in **scale** and **targets**.

:::cols
:::col Italy
- **OVRA**, the political police, under the police chief Arturo Bocchini (1926–40): informers and files, not mass arrests.
- The **Special Tribunal** (from November 1926) tried political cases before army and militia officers.
- **Confino**: internal exile to a remote village or island, imposed without trial.
- Squadrist violence (beatings, castor oil, burning of union halls) mattered most in 1921–26.
- The racial laws of 1938 targeted the Jews (about 46,000 people, 0.1 per cent of the population).
:::
:::col USSR
- **OGPU**, then the **NKVD** (from 1934), the party's police and the manager of the Gulag.
- The **show trials** of 1936–38 and the **Yezhovshchina**, the mass terror of 1937–38.
- **Gulag** camps and colonies, deportations of "kulaks" (1930–31) and of national groups.
- Targets: party members and officials, army officers, "former people", peasants, national minorities and ordinary citizens named in quotas.
- Terror touched the ruling party itself.
:::
:::col Germany
- The **Gestapo**, the **SS** and **SD** under Himmler and Heydrich, with **block wardens** and informers.
- **Special Courts** (1933) and the **People's Court** (1934); **concentration camps** from March 1933 (Dachau).
- Early targets: Communists, Social Democrats, trade unionists. Later: Jews, Sinti and Roma, the "asocial", and open opponents.
- A large share of the Gestapo's cases began with denunciations by ordinary people.
- Terror escalated in war, and the regime's racial policies led to genocide (see [The Holocaust (1)](#hist.interp.holocaust.1)).
:::
:::

The records of each regime show how different the scale was. They are **not on the same basis**, so do not set one against another row by row.

| Regime | What its own records show | Measured how |
|---|---|---|
| Italy | The Special Tribunal tried about 5,600 people and convicted about 4,600 between 1927 and 1943; it passed 42 death sentences, of which 31 were carried out. Over ten thousand were sent to confino over the regime's life. | Court and administrative punishments for political offences |
| USSR | 681,692 people shot in 1937–38 (353,074 in 1937 and 328,618 in 1938) on NKVD orders; about 1.6 million arrested for political crimes. About 1.5 million people were held in Gulag camps on 1 January 1941. | Police and camp records, published from the Soviet archives in the 1990s |
| Germany | About 5,200 death sentences passed by the People's Court between 1934 and 1945. About 6,500 Gestapo officers in 1937. Tens of thousands in concentration camps before 1939. | Court records and staffing estimates. These are only some of the channels; most of the regime's killing of "racial enemies" took place outside the courts |

On the **same base** (the share of the population), the Soviet Great Terror of 1937–38 shot about 0.4 per cent of the Soviet population in two years (681,692 out of about 170 million, the 1939 census figure, which later demographers think was inflated by a few million), which is about one person in 250. In Italy, 31 political executions in sixteen years were carried out in a country of about 42 million (the census of 1931). These are different kinds of repression, and Nazi Germany sits between them in peacetime and far beyond them in war. The Holocaust cannot be measured on this scale at all.

:::key What the comparison shows about terror
1. **A difference of scale, then of kind.** Mussolini's regime killed dozens of opponents before the war and imprisoned thousands; Stalin's killed hundreds of thousands in two years and imprisoned millions; Hitler's regime killed far fewer political opponents before 1939 than Stalin's did, but its terror was aimed at groups defined by race, and that led to genocide in wartime.
2. **Different targets.** Italy's terror hit political opponents; the Soviet terror hit the party and the whole society on quotas; the German terror hit political opponents first and then racial and social groups.
3. **Informers did much of the work.** In Italy (OVRA), in the USSR and in Germany, the public's own denunciations supplied much of the police's information, which shows both fear and willingness.
:::

### Propaganda and the leader cult

All three regimes controlled the media and built a cult of a leader. Each had its own image.

- **Mussolini, *Il Duce*.** A man of action, who threshed wheat stripped to the waist, skied and flew planes, and whose speeches from a balcony were broadcast to crowds. The press was controlled from his own office, which became the Ministry of Press and Propaganda in 1935 and the Ministry of Popular Culture in 1937. The cult was strong but met competition from the Church and the monarchy, whose loyalty ran in other directions. See [Mussolini's Italy (2)](#hist.d.italy.2).
- **Hitler, *der Führer*.** The saviour of the nation, "above party", presented by Joseph Goebbels' Ministry of Propaganda and Enlightenment (from 13 March 1933) through radio (cheap "People's Receivers" were sold from 1933), film, the Nuremberg Rallies and the 1936 Olympics. Ian Kershaw's *The 'Hitler Myth'* (1987) argued that this cult made Hitler more popular than his party. See [Hitler's Germany (2)](#hist.d.hitler.2).
- **Stalin, the "Great Leader".** The cult began at his fiftieth birthday in December 1929 and presented him as Lenin's disciple and then as the wise father of the people, with Soviet history rewritten in the *Short Course* of 1938 and the arts bound by Socialist Realism. See [Stalin's Russia (2)](#hist.d.stalin.2).

What they share is that the leader was presented as the solution to the nation's problems, and the media allowed no rival image. What differs is the **setting**: Mussolini's cult lived alongside an independent king and Church; Hitler's was the centre of a party-state that said it embodied the "will of the people"; Stalin's rested on a system that had abolished private property and a free press altogether.

### Party, state and the dictator's real power

:::cols
:::col Italy
- Mussolini was *Duce* of the party and Head of Government, but **the king remained head of state** and the army swore its oath to him.
- The **Fascist Grand Council** was made a constitutional body (1928), and the cabinet lost its collective role.
- The party (PNF) organised and propagandised, while the real administration stayed with **prefects** and civil servants. 
- On 25 July 1943 the Grand Council voted against him and the king dismissed him: **a dictator who could be sacked.**
:::
:::col USSR
- Power sat in the **Communist Party**, which ran the state, the army and the economy through its committees.
- Stalin held the party's top post (General Secretary), and did not hold the formal chairmanship of the government until 1941.
- The **Constitution of 1936** promised rights and elections, and the party stayed above it.
- The Terror broke the party's independence, and by 1938 Stalin's decision was final.
:::
:::col Germany
- Hitler held the offices of Chancellor and president combined as **Führer** from 2 August 1934, with an army oath to him by name.
- The **Führerprinzip** ("leader principle") put his word above law.
- Party agencies, state ministries and the SS overlapped and competed, with Hitler as arbiter, and the cabinet met rarely.
- No institution could remove him, and he stayed in power until his suicide on 30 April 1945.
:::
:::

### How far were they "totalitarian"?

:::note The historians of totalitarianism
- **Hannah Arendt**, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* (1951), argued that Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR (from the 1930s) were a new form of government, not just a harsher tyranny: they ruled through terror and an all-explaining ideology over an "atomised" mass of people. She did not count Mussolini's Italy, which she saw as a one-party dictatorship that stopped short.
- **Carl Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzezinski**, *Totalitarian Dictatorship and Autocracy* (1956), listed features that such regimes shared: an official ideology covering every part of life; a single mass party led by one man; terror by a secret police; a monopoly of mass communications; a monopoly of weapons; and a centrally directed economy. The list is a tool, and it has been criticised as too static and as built on the Cold War (their book appeared when the USSR and the West were rivals).
- **Ian Kershaw and Moshe Lewin** (eds), *Stalinism and Nazism: Dictatorships in Comparison* (1997), brought together historians of both regimes to compare them. The essays are a good example of how comparison shows differences as well as likenesses.
- **Richard Overy**, *The Dictators: Hitler's Germany, Stalin's Russia* (2004), compares the two regimes through their leaders, their use of terror, their economies and war, and finds important similarities in method while insisting that their ideas and results were different.
- **R.J.B. Bosworth**, *Mussolini's Italy* (2005, with the subtitle *Life under the Dictatorship, 1915–1945*), describes how Italians lived under the regime and stresses how much it depended on compromise, and how patchy its control was.
:::

The Friedrich and Brzezinski list gives a tidy way to test each regime. The table says how well each regime fits each feature. It is a judgement, not a score, and a Paper 4 answer would justify each cell with evidence from its own topic.

| Feature | Italy | USSR | Germany |
|---|---|---|---|
| An official ideology covering all life | Yes, but vague and changeable | Yes (Marxism-Leninism as defined by Stalin) | Yes (Nazism, with race at the centre) |
| One mass party under one leader | Yes, from 1926 | Yes | Yes, from July 1933 |
| Terror by a secret police | Limited (OVRA, Special Tribunal, confino) | Yes, on a vast scale | Yes, growing, and aimed at racial groups |
| Monopoly of mass communications | Mostly, but the Church ran its own press and schools | Yes | Yes, through Goebbels' ministry |
| Monopoly of weapons | No: the army was loyal to the king | Yes: the Red Army was under party control | Mostly, after the oath of 1934, though the army kept some independence till 1938 |
| Central direction of the economy | Partly (the corporate state, the IRI), with private firms | Yes (Gosplan, Five Year Plans) | Largely (the Four Year Plan from 1936), but with private ownership |

:::key How far were they totalitarian? Three positions you can defend
1. **A graded view.** The Soviet Union under Stalin and Nazi Germany came closest to the model; Italy was a strong dictatorship that fell short of it, because the king, the Church and the army stood outside Mussolini's control. Mussolini himself used the word *totalitario*, and in 1925 summed up his programme as everything inside the state, nothing outside it, nothing against it; the practice was far behind the slogan.
2. **A "weak dictator" challenge.** Some historians stress the messy reality inside Nazi Germany. Hans Mommsen, in an essay of 1976 ("National Socialism: Continuity and Change"), is associated with the view that Hitler was a "weak dictator" who let rival agencies compete, so that policy came from the pressure of those below. Ian Kershaw's phrase "working towards the Führer" (an article of 1993 and his *Hitler: Hubris*, 1998) is a middle position: officials acted on what they thought Hitler wanted, which made the regime radical without a plan.
3. **A consent challenge.** All three regimes won real support, through jobs, order, national pride or social mobility. A regime that is said to rule only by terror misses this. The more a regime relied on active support, the less "totalitarian" its image looks.
:::

**What it means for the key question.** For an essay on how effectively a dictator governed, the comparison helps you choose a criterion: *"how far did the regime control, and how far did it rely on consent or on compromise?"* Mussolini's compromises with the king and Church, and the way the dictator in Germany and the USSR removed such limits, are the best evidence for a judgement in either direction.

## Key question 3: what did they do to the economy?

All three dictators believed that the economy must serve the state, and all three led their countries into rearmament and a drive for self-sufficiency (autarky). They did it in very different ways.

:::cols
:::col Italy
- **De Stefani's orthodox policies** (1922–25), then the "battles": for grain (launched 20 June 1925), for the lira ("Quota 90", December 1927) and for land (the Pontine Marshes; Littoria founded 18 December 1932).
- **Corporate State**: the Rocco Law (3 April 1926) banned strikes; the Ministry of Corporations followed in July 1926.
- **Depression**: the IMI and the **IRI** (January 1933) rescued banks and industry, and made the state a large owner.
- **Autarky** from 1936, after sanctions.
:::
:::col USSR
- **NEP** (1921–28), then the **Great Turn**: the first Five Year Plan (from 1 October 1928).
- **Collectivisation** from late 1929: about nine tenths of households were in collective farms by 1937. **Dekulakisation**: about 1.8 million deported in 1930–31.
- A **famine** in 1932–33 with excess deaths of about 5 to 8 million.
- Heavy industry first: Magnitogorsk, the Dnieper dam. Three plans by 1941.
:::
:::col Germany
- **Schacht** at the Reichsbank (March 1933), work creation, the **Mefo bills** (1934–38: about 12 billion marks of hidden rearmament credit), the **New Plan** (September 1934).
- **Four Year Plan** (from October 1936, under Göring): rearmament and self-sufficiency to prepare for war.
- Registered unemployment from about 6 million (January 1933) to close to zero by 1939.
- The **Labour Front** (DAF, 10 May 1933) replaced the trade unions.
:::
:::

### What they shared

- **The state took over control.** In every case the market ceased to decide what was made. In Italy it was the corporations and the IRI, in Germany the Four Year Plan, in the USSR Gosplan.
- **Rearmament and autarky.** All three aimed for self-sufficiency in war materials: Italy after the League's sanctions of 1935–36, Germany from the Four Year Plan of 1936, and the USSR from the first Five Year Plan, because Stalin feared isolation and invasion.
- **Workers' independent organisations were abolished.** The Rocco Law (1926) in Italy, the destruction of the unions and the DAF in Germany (1933), and the party's control of Soviet unions meant that none of the three allowed a free labour movement.
- **Living standards were not the first aim.** Consumption was squeezed so that investment and arms could grow.

### What differed

:::key Where the economies differed
1. **Ownership.** Private ownership was largely abolished in the USSR, with industry and farms owned by the state or collectives. In Italy and Germany it survived, under state direction.
2. **Aims.** Italy's corporate state aimed at a "third way" between capitalism and socialism, and delivered mostly a framework in which business and the state worked together. Germany's economy was bent to war and expansion. Stalin's aim was to build socialism and catch up with the West, and he was prepared to pay any human price.
3. **Human cost.** Collectivisation and the famine of 1932–33 cost millions of lives, and the Five Year Plans were built partly with forced labour. Nothing in Italy or in Germany in peacetime compares in scale, although the Nazi economy relied increasingly on forced labour once the war began, and its policies led to the confiscation of Jewish property.
4. **Success.** Germany reached full employment, but at the price of an economy that could be sustained only by conquest; the USSR built heavy industry on a huge scale, but with waste, famine and a long-term weakness in agriculture; Italy's results were mixed (the lira, grain output and the reclaimed land were partly successes), and its economy was not ready for the war of 1940.
:::

**Who paid?** In Italy, industrial workers, through cut wages and the banning of strikes; in Germany, consumers and workers, through limited wages, long hours and shortages of consumer goods, and the people forced out of their jobs and property; in the USSR, peasants first, then workers and consumers, through the sacrifice of living standards to industry. This question of "who paid" is a useful criterion for an essay on [Mussolini's Italy (3)](#hist.d.italy.3), [Stalin's Russia (3)](#hist.d.stalin.3) or [Hitler's Germany (3)](#hist.d.hitler.3).

## Key question 4: what did they do to society?

:::cols
:::col Italy
- **Women**: the Battle for Births (Ascension Day speech, May 1927), a tax on bachelors from 1927, a 10 per cent quota on women's jobs in 1938.
- **Youth**: the ONB (law of 3 April 1926), merged into the GIL in 1937; school textbooks controlled.
- **Religion**: the Lateran Pacts (11 February 1929) settled the quarrel with the Church; tensions followed over Catholic Action and the racial laws.
- **Minorities**: forced Italianisation of Slovenes and Croats in the north-east; racial laws against Jews in 1938.
:::
:::col USSR
- **Women**: equality in law from 1917–18, mass entry into industry, then a pro-family turn with the ban on abortion (27 June 1936).
- **Youth**: the Komsomol (1918) and Pioneers (1922); schools returned to discipline in the 1930s.
- **Religion**: campaigns of closures and arrests, the League of the Militant Godless (1925), and clergy shot in 1937–38.
- **Minorities and "enemies"**: kulaks, "former people", and national groups such as Koreans deported in 1937.
:::
:::col Germany
- **Women**: *Kinder, Küche, Kirche*, marriage loans (1 June 1933), the Mother's Cross (1938); policy changed when rearmament needed workers.
- **Youth**: the Hitler Youth, made the state youth organisation in December 1936 and compulsory in 1939.
- **Religion**: the Concordat with the Vatican (20 July 1933), then a church struggle with Protestants and Catholics.
- **Minorities**: Jews, Sinti and Roma, the disabled, the "asocial": the Nuremberg Laws (1935), the pogrom of 9–10 November 1938, and genocide in war.
:::
:::

**What they shared.** All three wanted more babies (pro-natalism) for the nation or the cause, set up mass youth organisations to shape the next generation, and tried to make loyalty to the regime the first loyalty. All three persecuted groups they labelled enemies.

**What differed.**

- **Religion.** Mussolini and Hitler made deals with the Church (1929 and 1933) and later quarrelled with it, but neither tried to destroy it. Soviet policy aimed at the eventual end of religion, and its violence against clergy and believers was on a different scale.
- **Women.** Nazi policy, which stressed the home, collided with its own labour shortage after 1936; Soviet policy needed women in the factories and fields and also wanted large families; Italian policy tried to reduce women's work but had less power to do it.
- **Minorities.** The Italian regime's racial laws of 1938 followed its alliance with Germany and an earlier colonial racism, and they were far less deadly in Italy than German policy was; the Soviet regime persecuted people mainly as class enemies or as suspect nationalities, and the Nazi regime defined its enemies by race and sought to remove them from Europe.
- **How far they changed society.** Mussolini's regime changed the surface of Italian society more than its structure (the Church, the family and local loyalties were stronger than the movement). Nazi Germany reshaped institutions and persecuted many, but left class and religion largely in place. The Stalinist regime transformed Soviet society more than the other two did: a peasant country became an urban industrial one in a decade, and the old elites and much of the old culture were destroyed.

**What it means for the key question.** An essay on social change can use the Soviet case as a benchmark for how much a regime *can* transform society when it controls everything. That makes Mussolini's limits and the Nazis' mixture of change and continuity easier to judge.

## The links between the regimes, 1922–45

The three regimes did not live in separate worlds. They watched, copied, helped and fought each other.

| Date | Link |
|---|---|
| 28 Oct 1922 and 8–9 Nov 1923 | Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch followed the March on Rome and copied its idea of a march on the capital |
| 14–15 Jun 1934 | Hitler and Mussolini meet at Venice for the first time |
| 25 Jul 1934 | Austrian Nazis murder Chancellor Dollfuss; Mussolini sends troops to the Brenner Pass, and Hitler backs off |
| 11–14 Apr 1935 | Stresa Front: Italy, Britain and France condemn German rearmament |
| 2 May 1935 | The Soviet Union signs a mutual-assistance treaty with France; the Comintern adopts the Popular Front against fascism in July–August |
| 3 Oct 1935 | Italy invades Abyssinia; the League's sanctions push Mussolini towards Hitler |
| Jul 1936 onwards | The Spanish Civil War: Germany and Italy back Franco, the USSR backs the Republic |
| Oct–Nov 1936 | Rome–Berlin Axis (Mussolini's "axis" speech in Milan, 1 November) |
| 25 Nov 1936; 6 Nov 1937 | Anti-Comintern Pact: Germany and Japan (1936); Italy joins (1937) |
| 12 Mar 1938 | Mussolini does not oppose the Anschluss |
| 29–30 Sep 1938 | Munich: Mussolini helps to arrange the conference |
| 22 May 1939 | Pact of Steel: a full military alliance between Germany and Italy |
| 23 Aug 1939 | Nazi–Soviet Pact, with a secret protocol dividing Eastern Europe; Germany invades Poland on 1 September, and the USSR on 17 September |
| 27 Sep 1940 | Tripartite Pact: Germany, Italy and Japan |
| 22 Jun 1941 | Operation Barbarossa: Germany attacks the USSR and the Pact ends |
| 25 Jul 1943 | Mussolini is removed; Italy leaves the war in September, and Germany occupies the north and centre |

:::key Three patterns in these links
1. **Imitation, then dependence.** Hitler copied the March on Rome's method in 1923; by 1938 it was Mussolini who followed Hitler's lead over the Anschluss (and, many historians think, partly over the racial laws, although they disagree about this), and by 1943 Italy was a junior partner that Germany had to rescue.
2. **Ideological rivalry.** Fascism and communism were declared enemies. The Anti-Comintern Pact, the Spanish Civil War and the Popular Front all show this, and Hitler's speeches against "Judeo-Bolshevism" were part of his programme. The Nazi–Soviet Pact of August 1939 was therefore a surprise, a partnership of convenience between two enemies that lasted until Hitler broke it in June 1941.
3. **The three together shaped the road to war.** The failure to stop Italy in Abyssinia, Germany in the Rhineland, and the response to the Spanish war are the story of [International history, 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2), [(3)](#hist.int.1929.3) and [(4)](#hist.int.1929.4); the alliance of Nazi Germany and Stalin's USSR in 1939 made the Second World War possible, and their war from 1941 decided it.
:::

## How to use comparison in a Paper 4 essay

The Paper 4 question is always on **one** topic: "Assess the reasons why Stalin …" or "'…' Evaluate this view." It never asks you to compare two dictators. The mark scheme rewards a balanced analysis that uses valid criteria, a judgement and precise knowledge *of the topic in the question*. So comparison has a small and specific job.

:::key When comparison earns marks, and when it loses them
1. **Use it as a benchmark, in one sentence.** "Unlike Hitler, who had the army swear an oath to him personally in August 1934, Mussolini never controlled the army, which was loyal to the king." The sentence sets a standard against which to judge the regime you are writing about.
2. **Use it to support a judgement or a criterion.** A comparison can show *how far* something went: "Stalin's terror was on a scale that Mussolini's never approached, so 'terror state' means something different in each case."
3. **Keep the focus on the topic of the question.** If a paragraph spends more words on another regime than on the one in the question, it has drifted. The other regime is evidence for your argument, not the subject.
4. **One comparative sentence per paragraph at most, and not in every paragraph.** Two or three in a whole essay is plenty.
5. **Be sure of the fact.** A wrong comparative detail costs more than a right one gains. Use a fact you know well and can state with a date.
6. **Never use comparison in place of knowledge.** The knowledge mark (Table B, 15 marks) is for precise detail on your topic. A comparison adds nothing to Table B unless the detail on your own topic is also there.
:::

:::steps
1. Read the question and underline its topic and its command word ("Assess", "Evaluate").
2. Plan the argument for your own topic first: the reasons or points for and against, and the criterion you will use to judge them.
3. Ask: where would a benchmark from another regime make my judgement clearer? Choose at most two places.
4. Write the comparative sentence so that it ends by returning to your own topic ("so Mussolini's power depended on the king").
5. In the conclusion, you may use a comparison to say how unusual or typical the case was, but the judgement itself must answer the question.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 answers that use comparison well, and badly

**Mark levels.** A Paper 4 essay is marked out of 30 in two tables, each out of 15. Table A (analysis and judgement): Level 5 (13–15) is a balanced, analytical argument with valid wide-ranging criteria and a clear, supported judgement; Level 4 (10–12) is mostly analytical with uneven balance; Level 3 (7–9) has some analysis but lacks balance. Table B (knowledge): Level 5 (13–15) is wide-ranging, precise and fully focused; Level 4 (10–12) is a good range with uneven depth; Level 3 (7–9) is adequate but narrow or imprecise.

:::eg A Level 5 paragraph with one comparative sentence
**The question.** "Mussolini's power depended more on the king and the elites than on his own party." Evaluate this view. [30]

**A paragraph from the answer (the case for the view).**

"The strongest evidence for the view is that Mussolini's power rested on institutions that he did not own. He became prime minister in October 1922 not because the Fascists had won an election, since they held about 35 of 535 seats, but because Victor Emmanuel III refused to sign the martial law decree that would have sent the army against the March on Rome, and invited him to form a government on 29 October. The monarchy never lost its place: the king remained head of state, the armed forces swore their oath to him, and he held the power to dismiss his prime minister. That power was real. On 25 July 1943, after the Fascist Grand Council voted against Mussolini, the king dismissed him and had him arrested. The elites mattered too. Landowners and industrialists had paid for the squads in 1921–22, and the Vatican's support after the Lateran Pacts of 1929 gave the regime a legitimacy that its own party could not provide. Compared with Hitler, who after the oath of August 1934 faced no institution that could remove him, Mussolini's position was always conditional on the king's consent. This suggests that the party was the instrument of his power and the king the source of its legality. But this is only one side of the question: the party's role in creating the climate of violence in which the king and the elites felt they had no better choice, and in building a mass base after 1925, has to be weighed against it."

*Commentary.* The paragraph states a clear argument at the start, supports each claim with a precise fact and date, ends by returning to the question, and sets up the other side. The single comparative sentence ("Compared with Hitler …") sets a benchmark that sharpens the point about Mussolini's dependence; it does not take the paragraph away from Italy. Repeated for the case against and with a comparative judgement, this is the kind of writing that earns **Level 5 (13–15) on Table A**, and the precise evidence supports **Level 5 (13–15) on Table B**.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph: too much comparison
**The same question.** "Mussolini's power depended more on the king and the elites than on his own party." Evaluate this view. [30]

A student wrote:

"Mussolini's power depended on the king, but this was very different from Hitler. Hitler became Chancellor in 1933 and after Hindenburg died in 1934 he combined the posts of president and chancellor, and the army swore an oath to him. He destroyed the unions and the other parties and used the SS and the Gestapo to terrorise opponents, and later he killed millions of Jews. Stalin was also different, because he had no king at all and he killed hundreds of thousands in the Terror of 1937–38 and sent millions to the Gulag. By comparison, Mussolini was a much milder dictator, who used the OVRA and the Special Tribunal. So the king had a role in Italy that nobody had in Germany or Russia."

**The level.** Table A: **Level 2 to 3 (about 5–7)**, because the paragraph never makes an argument about how much Mussolini's power depended on the king: it mostly says that the other regimes were different. Table B: **Level 2 (about 4–6)**, since nearly all of the detail is about Hitler and Stalin, and the Italian knowledge is limited to one claim and two names. The paragraph is accurate but off the question: its subject has become the other regimes.

**The fix.** Keep the single comparison that does work (the oath to Hitler against the oath to the king) and spend the rest of the space on Mussolini: the king's refusal to sign the martial law decree in October 1922, his invitation to Mussolini on 29 October, his formal powers, the Grand Council vote and the dismissal of 25 July 1943, and the support of landowners, industrialists and the Church. Then weigh that against the party's role. Cut everything about the SS, the Gestapo, the Holocaust and the Gulag, since none of it helps to judge Mussolini's dependence on the king.
:::

:::eg A full Level 5 plan: "Assess the reasons why Stalin gained control of the Soviet Union by 1929"
**What the levels want.** The question asks for **reasons** (Assess): a balanced analysis of several, with a criterion for judging which mattered most, a judgement and precise knowledge. Level 5 is 13–15 on each table. Use comparison only to sharpen the criteria or the judgement.

**Criterion.** Which reasons were *necessary* (without them he could not have won), and which only *helped*?

**Plan.**
1. **The party machine** (necessary). General Secretary from April 1922; control of appointments filled the Congress with his supporters; the Testament was read to delegates in May 1924 and not published, and Stalin kept his post. *Comparative sentence:* "Unlike Mussolini and Hitler, Stalin needed no appointment from outside, because control of the party machine was the whole route to power."
2. **The weaknesses and mistakes of his rivals** (important). Trotsky was the strongest rival, but was disliked by many leaders and associated in the minds of colleagues with the fear of a "Bonaparte"; Zinoviev and Kamenev allied with Stalin against Trotsky in 1923–25 and were beaten themselves in December 1925; Bukharin backed Stalin against the Left and then fell in 1928–29. Each rival thought that Stalin was a lesser figure who could be used. *Comparative sentence:* "This underestimation was a pattern: the Italian liberals who hoped to tame Mussolini in 1922 and the conservatives who thought they would box in Hitler in 1933 made the same mistake."
3. **The arguments over policy** (helped). "Socialism in One Country" (December 1924) appealed to a party that wanted to believe the revolution could survive alone, and Stalin's switch to the Left's industrialisation policy in 1928 came after he had defeated the Left.
4. **Changing alliances and timing** (necessary for the pace). He fought one group at a time (Trotsky with the Right, then the Right with the Left's policy).
5. **Repression and propaganda** (secondary before 1929). Rivals were expelled and exiled, not shot; the cult ("Lenin's disciple") began at the funeral of 1924 and grew at his fiftieth birthday on 21 December 1929.

**Judgement.** The party machine and the divisions of his rivals together explain the result: Stalin's control of appointments made it possible and his rivals' mistakes made it certain. Terror and propaganda explain how he *kept* control after 1929 more than how he won it.

**A Level 5 paragraph and conclusion (extract).** "Control of the party machine was the necessary reason. Stalin had been General Secretary since April 1922, and the post, which had seemed administrative, gave him the power to appoint and move officials, so by the time of the Fourteenth Congress in December 1925 a majority of the delegates owed their jobs to him. Unlike Mussolini and Hitler, who needed the king or the president to put them in office, Stalin needed no outside appointment: his rivals could be beaten within the party and by its rules. But the machine would not have been enough without his rivals' mistakes. Zinoviev and Kamenev helped to defeat Trotsky in 1923–25 and found themselves isolated at the Fourteenth Congress; Bukharin helped to defeat the United Opposition in 1926–27 and was next. Each believed Stalin was the lesser danger. Overall, the machine made his victory possible and his rivals' misjudgements made it certain, while repression played only a small part before 1929."

*Commentary.* The comparative sentence appears once, at a point where it sets a criterion (an appointment from outside against a machine from inside) and then returns to Stalin. Every claim has a date or a name. The conclusion makes a judgement against a stated criterion (necessary against helpful). This is Level 5 (13–15 on Table A and 13–15 on Table B), if the rest of the essay keeps this standard.
:::

Q: Which of the three regimes took power first, and about how many years was that before Hitler's appointment?

Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Mussolini's rise to power in 1922 depended on the weakness of the liberal state.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. For: post-war unemployment and inflation; the "mutilated victory" and Fiume (September 1919) showed a government unable to act; the "Red Two Years" frightened the middle class; governments rose and fell; Giolitti and others hoped to tame the Fascists; the king refused to sign martial law on 28 October. Against: Mussolini's own strengths (the dual policy of violence and deals; the squads of Balbo and Farinacci; his skill as a propagandist and politician), the support of landowners and industrialists, the Fascist Party's growth in 1921–22, and the choice of the king, which was not forced. A comparison helps here: Hitler's appointment in January 1933 also followed a crisis of the system and a decision by the head of state, which suggests that the weakness of a democratic state is a common necessary condition, and that the decision of the king or the president was the decisive moment. A Level 5 answer sets a criterion (what was necessary, what was sufficient?) and judges, for example, that weakness made the rise possible while the king's decision made it certain.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess how successful the Corporate State was in controlling workers and employers in the 1930s.
A: Indicative content. Success in control: strikes banned (Rocco Law, 1926), free unions replaced by Fascist syndicates, the Charter of Labour (1927), the Ministry of Corporations (1926) and the National Council of Corporations (1930), and the Vidoni Pact of 1925. Limits: employers kept their power and the corporations did little to plan the economy; workers' real wages fell in the Depression; the corporations were a framework, not a system of economic decision-making; the IRI (1933) did more to shape industry. A comparison helps: in Germany the Labour Front (1933) similarly replaced the unions but was run by the party, while in the USSR the unions were under party control, so Italy's employers kept more independence from the regime than German or Soviet managers did. Level 5 argues that the Corporate State controlled workers far more effectively than employers.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Stalin's use of terror was the main reason why he stayed in power in the 1930s.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. For: the Great Terror of 1936–38 (the show trials; Order 00447; 681,692 shot) removed the old Bolsheviks and the army's leaders, Kirov's murder (1 December 1934) justified it, and the NKVD and Gulag deterred opposition. Against: the cult of personality and propaganda (the *Short Course*, Socialist Realism), the loyalty of the new generation of officials promoted into the places of the purged, real achievements (industrialisation, welfare, education, social mobility), nationalism and fear of invasion, and the Constitution of 1936 as a source of legitimacy. A comparison helps: Hitler also used terror, but his regime depended heavily on consent from the economic recovery and the Führer myth. That suggests that no dictator stayed in power by terror alone, which sets a criterion for judging how far terror was "the main" reason. A Level 5 answer decides that terror was the main means of removing rivals but not the sole reason that the regime lasted.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the impact of collectivisation on Soviet agriculture to 1941.
H: Define "impact" with criteria: output, people, the state's control, and long-term effects.
H: Use numbers with sources, and say where historians' estimates differ.
A: Indicative content. Impact: about nine tenths of households in collective farms by 1937; the state could take grain at fixed prices and feed the towns; about 1.8 million deported in 1930–31 (dekulakisation); the loss of livestock; the famine of 1932–33 (excess deaths estimated at 5 to 8 million, with about 5.7 million as Davies and Wheatcroft's central estimate); harvests that stayed low through the 1930s; the lasting loss of trust in the state; but also the supply of labour and grain to industry, which made the Five Year Plans possible. Comparison: Italy's Battle for Grain (1925) raised wheat output while damaging other crops, and Nazi agricultural policy protected farmers; neither used force on a scale that approached Stalin's, so the comparison shows the extent of Soviet coercion. Level 5 judges the impact by a criterion (for example, did collectivisation achieve the state's own aims?) and concludes that it delivered control and labour at an enormous human cost and without lasting gains in output.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the Enabling Act in the creation of a Nazi dictatorship.
A: Indicative content. For: the Act (23 March 1933, 444 to 94) let the cabinet pass laws without the Reichstag, including laws that broke the constitution, and was the legal basis for banning the parties, unions and other bodies in 1933. Against: the Reichstag Fire Decree of 28 February had already removed civil rights; terror by the SA and the arrests of Communists and Social Democrats made the vote possible; the Night of the Long Knives and the army's oath of August 1934 were as important as the Act in making Hitler's position secure; Hindenburg's death on 2 August 1934 removed the last check. A comparison helps: Mussolini's dictatorship was built by a series of laws of 1925–26 after the Matteotti crisis rather than a single Act, so the Enabling Act's importance lay in its speed and legal form. Level 5 judges that the Act was the key legal instrument but that it depended on the violence and the arrests that came before it, and on the removal of the army and the presidency afterwards.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the success of Nazi economic policies in preparing Germany for war by 1939.
H: Decide what "success" means: reaching particular targets, or being ready for the war that came in 1939.
H: Compare the Four Year Plan's targets with the outcome, and use the debate on "guns and butter".
A: Indicative content. Success: unemployment fell from about 6 million to near zero; rearmament spending grew with the Mefo bills and the Four Year Plan (October 1936); output of key war industries grew; the Labour Front controlled workers. Limits: the plan to reach self-sufficiency fell short (Germany still imported raw materials such as oil, rubber and iron ore); the economy was strained by labour and raw-material shortages by 1938–39; consumer goods were squeezed; the regime's war plans depended on conquest to pay for the economy (Austria and Czechoslovakia in 1938–39 brought gold and resources); Göring and Schacht clashed (Schacht resigned in November 1937). A comparison helps: Stalin's Five Year Plans prepared the USSR for war with a larger economy but at a higher human cost, and Italy's economy was not ready for war in 1940, which shows how far the Nazi economy was prepared in relative terms. Level 5 judges, with a criterion, that the economy was ready for a short war of conquest but not for a long one.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ For understanding, not an exam question type: Compare the use of terror in Fascist Italy and Stalin's USSR. Explain two similarities and two differences.
A: Similarities: both used a secret police (OVRA; NKVD) and special courts or tribunals; both used informers; both punished opponents by exile or camps (confino; the Gulag). Differences: the scale (31 executions by the Special Tribunal in sixteen years against 681,692 shot in two years in the USSR); the targets (Italian terror hit political opponents and, from 1938, Jews, while the Soviet terror reached the ruling party, the army and ordinary people through quotas). This is useful as practice for the benchmark sentences of the essay and is not set in the exam.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ For understanding, not an exam question type: 'Hitler's Germany was more totalitarian than Mussolini's Italy.' How far do you agree?
H: Use the Friedrich and Brzezinski features as your criteria.
H: Decide how much weight to put on terror and how much on control of institutions.
A: For: monopoly over the army after 1934; the party's control of all organisations (DAF, Hitler Youth); the SS and Gestapo; stronger control of schools and media; the Führerprinzip. Against: the polycracy of rival agencies; the German army and churches kept some independence; Italy's regime also had a one-party state, a cult, a secret police and a plan to "fascistise" society; Mussolini himself used the word. A top answer judges that Germany came nearer the model, and that the main difference was the king's, the Church's and the army's independence in Italy, and states this with evidence from both. This is a discussion question for understanding and not an exam format.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: For understanding, not an exam question type: 'The three dictatorships were more alike than different.' How far does the evidence support this view?
H: Choose criteria first (aims, methods, scale, effects), then say what the regimes share and where they differ.
H: Use numbers on the same base, and name the source of each.
A: Alike: one party and one leader; a secret police and camps; control of the press and a leader cult; rearmament and a drive for self-sufficiency; the abolition of free unions; pro-natalism and mass youth organisations; a claim to have saved the nation. Different: their ideas (nation and race against class); their aims (empire in the Mediterranean; living space in the east and the destruction of the Jews; building socialism and catching up with the West); the scale of killing (31 political executions in sixteen years in Italy; 681,692 shot in two years in the USSR; the regime in Germany killed far fewer political opponents before 1939 than Stalin's but went on to genocide); the institutions that limited them (the king, Church and army in Italy; none in the USSR after 1938 or in Germany after 1934); ownership of the economy; and the treatment of religion. A top answer chooses a criterion (for example, whether the regime tried to remake society) and decides that the shared form (the one-party dictatorship) masks deep differences of aim and scale.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Fascist Italy was a totalitarian state.' Evaluate this view. (An exam-style question of the type the board could set on the nature of Fascist government.)
H: Choose criteria for "totalitarian" and test Italy against each, with evidence from [Mussolini's Italy (2)](#hist.d.italy.2).
H: Decide where one comparison (to Hitler's oath or to the Soviet terror) improves the judgement.
A: For: a single party from 1926, the cult of the Duce, control of the press (the Ministry of Popular Culture, 1937), the Special Tribunal and confino, youth organisations (the ONB and GIL), the corporate state, the slogan "everything inside the state". Against: the king remained head of state and commander of the army; the Church kept its schools and Catholic Action; the Fascist Grand Council could depose Mussolini (25 July 1943); the prefects and civil service remained; terror was limited; business remained independent; and Arendt did not count Italy as totalitarian. A comparison helps once: the contrast with the oath to Hitler in August 1934 shows what a totalitarian claim to the army would have meant. A Level 5 answer judges that the regime was ambitious and aimed at totalitarian control but did not achieve it.
:::

:::recap
- **Not an exam question type.** Paper 4 sets one question per topic ("Assess …", "Evaluate this view"), and you answer two. Comparison is a tool for criteria and judgement, used in one sentence.
- **Gaining control.** Italy (1922) and Germany (1933): appointed by the king or the president, with party violence behind and conservatives who thought they could control the leader. USSR: a one-party state that Stalin captured through the General Secretary's post and the mistakes of his rivals (1922–29). Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch (8–9 November 1923) followed the March on Rome.
- **Governing.** All three: secret police, special courts, camps, controlled media and a leader cult. Scale: Italy's Special Tribunal (42 death sentences, 31 carried out), the USSR's 681,692 shot in 1937–38, the Gestapo's reliance on informers. Totalitarian model: Friedrich and Brzezinski (1956); Arendt (1951); Mommsen and Kershaw on Hitler's power. The king could dismiss Mussolini (25 July 1943); no institution could remove Hitler or Stalin.
- **Economies.** The state took control and all three aimed at autarky; free unions were abolished; only the USSR abolished private ownership; the human cost was by far the greatest in the USSR.
- **Societies.** Pro-natalism, mass youth movements and persecuted "enemies" in all three; religion attacked in the USSR, bargained over in Italy and Germany; the Soviet regime changed society most.
- **Links.** Axis (1936), Anti-Comintern Pact (1936, 1937), Pact of Steel (22 May 1939), Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939), Barbarossa (22 June 1941), the fall of Mussolini (25 July 1943).
- **Cautions.** The regimes were not the same; do not rank suffering; the Holocaust is distinct; compare numbers only on the same base.
