## The Great Powers and their tensions, c.1890–1914

By 1890 Europe had five **Great Powers** (Britain, France, Germany, Russia and Austria-Hungary), with Italy a weaker sixth. They controlled most of the world's trade, colonies and armies, and each worried about the others' growth. The tensions of this period are the "long-term causes" of the war in the sense that historians use that phrase; whether they made war *likely* is one of the central arguments of the next chapter.

:::def Great Power
A state strong enough to influence events across Europe and the world, with a large army or navy, an advanced economy and an empire or wide interests. In 1914 the Great Powers were Britain, France, Germany, Russia, Austria-Hungary and Italy (and, outside Europe, the USA and Japan).
:::

### Germany: the rising power

The new German Empire, proclaimed in 1871 after the defeat of France, became the strongest industrial power in continental Europe within a generation. Its population grew from about 41 million in 1871 to about 65 million in 1911, and by 1913 it produced roughly 17.6 million tonnes of steel against Britain's 7.7 million (figures from standard economic histories; the exact numbers vary slightly by source). Its universities, chemical and electrical industries and railways were the envy of Europe.

Germany's constitution gave great power to the Kaiser, the army and the Prussian elite, with a Reichstag elected by all adult men but with limited power over the government. The Kaiser, **Wilhelm II** (reigned 1888–1918), was impulsive, nationalistic and prone to bluster; after he dismissed **Bismarck** in March 1890, the cautious system by which Bismarck had kept Germany's rivals divided gave way to a more adventurous policy.

:::def Weltpolitik
"World policy": the German policy, from the late 1890s, of seeking world power status through colonies, naval strength and a larger share of overseas trade, expressed in Bülow's demand of 6 December 1897 for Germany's "place in the sun".
:::

Weltpolitik had domestic roots (it appealed to nationalists and industrialists and was meant to unite Germans behind the Kaiser) and international consequences (it alarmed Britain, France and Russia, who saw their own empires threatened). Germany's fear was equally real: it was surrounded by rivals, France to the west and Russia to the east, and its leaders talked of being "encircled" after 1907. Whether Germany was an aggressor seeking domination or a frightened power lashing out is the first question the historians answer differently.

### Anglo-German rivalry

Britain, the world's greatest naval and imperial power, had stayed out of continental alliances for most of the nineteenth century. This "isolation" came to feel dangerous after the Boer War (1899–1902), when most European opinion was hostile. The Kaiser's telegram of 3 January 1896 congratulating the Boer president Kruger after the Jameson Raid had already shown how German and British interests could collide. From 1898 Germany began to build a battle fleet (see the section on the arms race), which Britain regarded as aimed at itself. By 1907 British opinion, in the Foreign Office especially, regarded Germany as the chief danger to British security. But the rivalry was not total: Britain and Germany were each other's major trading partners, the royal families were related, and in 1912–14 relations improved (an agreement on the Baghdad railway, a German-backed line across the Ottoman Empire, and on dividing Portugal's African colonies between them, was nearly finished in summer 1914).

### Franco-German enmity

The loss of **Alsace-Lorraine** to Germany in 1871 left France with a grievance and a wish for *revanche* (revenge), though by the 1900s few French people wanted a war to recover it. France, with a population of about 39 million in 1911 that barely grew, could not match Germany's numbers, so it needed allies. The two Moroccan Crises (1905–06 and 1911) pitched Germany against France over the control of Morocco: both left Germany diplomatically isolated, which hardened German fears of encirclement and French suspicion of German bullying. In 1913 **Raymond Poincaré**, a Lorraine politician and strong nationalist, became President of France; historians argue over how far he strengthened France's firmness towards Germany and Russia's confidence in French support.

### Austria-Hungary: a multinational empire under strain

The Habsburg Empire (the Dual Monarchy of Austria and Hungary since 1867) was ruled by **Emperor Franz Joseph** (reigned 1848–1916). It had about 51 million people, none of its nationalities a majority: Germans and Magyars together made up less than half. Its Slav peoples (Czechs, Poles, Croats, Slovenes, Serbs and others) were increasingly restless, and the growth of the independent Kingdom of Serbia next door, whose nationalists dreamed of uniting all South Slavs, seemed to Vienna a mortal threat: if Serbia could draw Bosnia's Serbs and Croats away, the Empire might fall apart. This fear explains why Austria-Hungary was ready to risk war in 1914, and why historians such as Samuel Williamson (an American historian of Austria-Hungary; the names in this chapter are explained again in "Tagging the events") put Vienna, not Berlin, at the centre of the story.

### Russia after 1905

Russia, with the largest population in Europe (about 170 million people) and the largest peacetime army, was defeated by Japan in 1904–05 and shaken by the revolution of 1905, which forced the Tsar to grant a parliament (the Duma). After 1905 it recovered fast: the economy grew, the army was reorganised and rearmed. Russian foreign policy had two aims that mattered in 1914. First, to protect its position as protector of the Orthodox Slavs, above all Serbia (the idea of Pan-Slavism); a second humiliation after the Bosnian crisis of 1908–09 would, many ministers believed, be intolerable. Second, to secure its access to the Mediterranean through the Straits (the Bosporus and Dardanelles at Constantinople), through which a large part of its grain exports passed. Historians such as Sean McMeekin (a historian of Russia and the Ottoman Empire) make this second aim central.

:::key Five great-power tensions and what each means for responsibility
1. **Germany's rise and Weltpolitik**: used both to argue that Germany sought world power (Fritz Fischer, a German historian who argued in 1961 that Germany bore the main blame) and that Germany felt encircled and weak (those who stress German fear).
2. **Anglo-German rivalry**: navy and empire; used to argue that Britain was drawn in by a naval race it had helped start, and by those who say the rivalry was not the main cause.
3. **Franco-German enmity**: Alsace-Lorraine and Morocco; mainly a background cause, important for how the alliances hardened.
4. **Austria-Hungary's fear of Serbia**: used to argue that Vienna chose war to save the Empire (Williamson).
5. **Russia's Balkan and Straits ambitions**: used to argue that Russia's policy made a Balkan war into a European one (McMeekin).
:::

By 1914 Europe was divided into two armed groups. How they formed, and how binding they were, matters because both "Germany was dragged in by Austria" and "Britain was dragged in by France" are arguments about alliances.

:::def Alliance system
The network of agreements by which, by 1914, Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy (the Triple Alliance) faced France, Russia and Britain (the Triple Entente). An **alliance** is a treaty promising help; an **entente** is an understanding that falls short of one.
:::

What the system did and did not do:
- It gave each side confidence, and each side *fear* that it could not afford to let its partner be defeated. Germany could not let Austria-Hungary be beaten; France could not let Russia be beaten.
- It was **not** a machine that made war automatic. Italy, a member of the Triple Alliance, declared neutrality on 2–3 August 1914 because the alliance was defensive and Austria had struck first. Britain, in the Entente, had no treaty duty to fight.
- It meant that a quarrel in the Balkans could involve every Great Power in a few days, once the decisions were made. For historians of "shared responsibility" the alliances explain how a local crisis escalated; for historians of German responsibility they are background to the choices that Berlin made on 5–6 July.
- The Moroccan Crises (1905–06, 1911) **tested** the Entente and strengthened it: Britain supported France at Algeciras in 1906 and in 1911 (Lloyd George's Mansion House speech of 21 July 1911 warned Germany), and secret British and French military staff talks began in 1906. In 1912 the two navies agreed a division of tasks (the French fleet concentrated in the Mediterranean, the British in the North Sea), which left Britain with a moral claim to protect the French Channel coast.

:::warn Do not say the alliances "caused" the war
This is a common slip. The alliance system made the war **wider** and **faster**, but the decisions of July 1914 were taken by ministers who chose their policy. Germany chose to support Austria; Russia chose to support Serbia; Britain chose to support France and Belgium. A top-level answer says what the alliances made possible and what the decision-makers actually chose.
:::

## Militarism and the arms race

:::def Militarism
The belief that military strength and military values should have a large place in a state, together with the influence of generals and armies on policy. In pre-1914 Europe it showed in conscription, large standing armies, the prestige of officers, and military planners' influence on diplomacy.
:::

### The naval race

Britain's navy was the world's largest, and British policy held that it should be at least as strong as the next two navies combined (the "two-power standard"). In 1897 Admiral **Alfred von Tirpitz** became Germany's Secretary of State for the Navy, and the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 began a programme to build a battle fleet. Tirpitz's "risk theory" held that a German fleet strong enough to threaten serious damage to the Royal Navy would deter Britain from attacking Germany. In Britain it looked like a challenge. In 1906 the launch of HMS *Dreadnought*, a battleship with ten 12-inch guns and steam turbines, made every earlier battleship obsolete. The race now restarted on equal terms: both nations built dreadnoughts as fast as their budgets would allow. Britain stayed ahead, with a 1909 "naval scare" over whether Germany was building faster than announced, and in 1912 Britain agreed with France to concentrate in different waters.

The **Haldane Mission** of February 1912, sent by the British government to Berlin to find a deal in which Germany would slow its building and Britain would give some assurance about its conduct in a continental war, failed. By 1913 Germany had in practice slowed its naval building: its leaders chose to spend more on the army, because Russia's recovery looked more worrying than Britain's fleet. In the next chapter you will see how historians use this fact on both sides.

### The armies

Continental armies depended on **conscription**: men served for two or three years and then went into reserves, so that the army could grow enormously on mobilisation. After 1900 the general staffs argued that speed was everything, because the first blow would be decisive.

| Power | Key measure | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| Germany | Army laws of 1912 and 1913 | Raised the size of the peacetime army |
| France | Three-Year Law, 7 August 1913 | Lengthened conscript service from two years to three, to offset France's smaller population |
| Russia | The "Great Programme" (1913) | A large expansion of the army, to be completed by about 1917 |
| Britain | Small professional army; Territorial Force from 1908 | A British Expeditionary Force of about six divisions could be sent to France |

The Russian programme matters especially. Many German officers believed that by 1916–17 Russia's army would be too strong to fight, and used this to argue that, if war was coming, it was better sooner than later: the idea of **preventive war**. This was one of the reasons given by Moltke, the German Chief of the General Staff, on 8 December 1912 and again in 1914 (see the documents below).

:::key What the arms race meant for responsibility
- **For Germany's guilt:** Germany built a fleet that challenged Britain and an army ready to strike first, and its leaders spoke of war as unavoidable.
- **For shared responsibility:** every Power armed, and the fear of falling behind was general; the arms races produced mutual fear, not a plan.
- **For caution:** none of the arms races of 1890–1914 *itself* led to a decision for war. Several powers increased spending in 1913–14 and none declared war until July 1914. Whether arms made war likely or only made it deadly is a major argument.
:::

## The Balkans

The Ottoman Empire, "the sick man of Europe", had been losing its European lands for a century, and each retreat created new states and new rivalries. By 1900 Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, Romania and Montenegro had won independence; their nationalists wanted territory that the Ottomans, the Austrians, and one another held. For the Great Powers the Balkans were a place where their own interests collided: Austria-Hungary feared Serbia's nationalism, and Russia, a Slav and Orthodox power with an interest in the Straits, presented itself as the protector of the Slavs.

### The Bosnian Crisis, 1908–09

In October 1908 Austria-Hungary annexed **Bosnia-Herzegovina**, provinces it had occupied and administered since 1878 and that many Serbs claimed as their own. It had first secured Russia's likely acceptance (at a meeting in September 1908 between the two foreign ministers, Aehrenthal and Izvolsky, whose understanding was imprecise), but when Izvolsky found that his bargain about the Straits was rejected by the other Powers, Russia protested. In March 1909 Germany told Russia to accept the annexation, and Russia, still weak after the war with Japan, backed down. Serbia was furious. The crisis had three lasting results: Russian leaders vowed not to be humiliated again; Serbian nationalism sharpened, and secret organisations such as **Narodna Odbrana** (founded 1908) trained young men to work for the union of South Slavs; and Germany's open support for Austria-Hungary made it look like Vienna's backer in any Balkan quarrel.

### The Balkan Wars, 1912–13

In October 1912 Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece and Montenegro (the Balkan League, formed with Russian encouragement) attacked the Ottoman Empire and in a few weeks won most of its European territory. The First Balkan War ended by the Treaty of London of 30 May 1913 (not the Treaty of London of 1839 about Belgium). The victors then quarrelled over the spoils, and in June–August 1913 the Second Balkan War set Bulgaria against its former allies; it ended with the Treaty of Bucharest (10 August 1913). Serbia emerged with nearly double its territory and with higher confidence, and Austria-Hungary with a deeper fear of it. In October 1913 Austria sent an ultimatum to Serbia to withdraw from Albania (a new state created in 1913), and Serbia complied. In December 1913 the "Liman von Sanders affair" angered Russia: a German general was appointed to command Ottoman troops in Constantinople, close to the Straits, and Russia protested until the post was reduced.

:::key Why the Balkans mattered for 1914
1. They created a state (Serbia) whose nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary and which Russia felt it must protect.
2. They set two Great Powers (Austria-Hungary and Russia) against each other in a region where neither could easily retreat after 1909.
3. Austria-Hungary's success in 1913 in forcing Serbia to give way, with only an ultimatum, encouraged some in Vienna to think that a firmer threat in 1914 would also work.
4. They made the war's trigger a Balkan event: the assassin came from the networks the Bosnian Crisis and the Balkan Wars had produced.
:::

## The war plans

Every Great Power had plans for war, and the plans mattered because they set *timetables*. A plan that depended on speed made delay dangerous and made mobilisation itself a kind of aggression.

:::def Mobilisation
The process of calling up reservists, bringing an army to war strength and moving it to its stations by rail. In 1914 the great armies could not do this halfway: the plans meant that a state that mobilised first had a military advantage and a state that waited risked invasion, so mobilisation was close to a declaration of war.
:::

| Power | Plan | Idea |
|---|---|---|
| Germany | **Schlieffen–Moltke plan** (Schlieffen's memorandum of 1905, modified by Moltke) | Face two enemies by defeating France in about six weeks through Belgium, then turn east against Russia, which was expected to mobilise slowly |
| France | **Plan XVII** (adopted 1913) | A rapid offensive towards Alsace-Lorraine and the Ardennes, with its strength concentrated on its German frontier |
| Russia | **Plan 19** (revised 1912) | Mobilise against both Germany (East Prussia) and Austria-Hungary (Galicia) |
| Austria-Hungary | Plans "B" (Balkans) and "R" (Russia), under Conrad von Hötzendorf | A smaller force to strike Serbia (Plan B) and a main army to face Russia (Plan R), with a flexible group in between |
| Britain | Naval blockade; a small Expeditionary Force | Not a commitment: the army talks with France gave a British army a likely place in a French war |

The **Schlieffen–Moltke plan** was the most dangerous plan. Because it required a rapid defeat of France before Russia's army was ready, Germany could not fight Russia without fighting France too. And because the route to France ran through **Belgium** (whose neutrality Britain, France and Prussia had guaranteed in 1839), the plan made a British intervention far more likely. Chief of the General Staff **Helmuth von Moltke** (the Younger) knew this, but the plan was too rigid to change; by 1914 there was no German plan for fighting Russia alone.

:::key What the war plans meant for responsibility
- Historians who think the plans **made war automatic** (A.J.P. Taylor, a British historian, wrote the classic account, *War by Timetable*, 1969) say the decisions of 30 July to 4 August were forced by railway timetables.
- Historians who blame particular **decision-makers** say the plans were tools: it was Germany that chose, on 31 July, to send ultimatums that started the plan. The timetable shows what was *possible*, not what any individual *had* to do.
- The plans make clear that 1 to 4 August 1914 could not have been stopped by a few conversations, but they do not show that the decisions of 28–30 July were fixed.
:::

## Sarajevo, 28 June 1914

Archduke **Franz Ferdinand**, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, went to Bosnia in June 1914 to watch army manoeuvres and to open a museum in Sarajevo. The date, 28 June, was the anniversary of the Battle of Kosovo (1389), sacred to Serb nationalists, a coincidence that offended many Serbs. A group of young men, members of **Young Bosnia** (a movement of Bosnian students, mostly Serbs, who wanted freedom from Austria and union with Serbia), were waiting along the route. They had been armed, trained and smuggled across the border with the help of members of a Serbian secret organisation, **Unification or Death** (the "Black Hand", formed in 1911), led by Colonel Dragutin Dimitrijević, known as "Apis", the head of Serbian military intelligence, with the assistance of Major Vojislav Tankosić.

The first attempt failed: a bomb thrown by Nedeljko Čabrinović bounced off the archduke's car and wounded people behind. Later that morning, after a visit to the town hall and a hospital, the royal car took a wrong turning and stopped almost beside **Gavrilo Princip**, a 19-year-old Bosnian Serb, who shot Franz Ferdinand and his wife, **Sophie**, Duchess of Hohenberg. Both died within minutes.

The assassination was not by itself a reason for a European war: assassinations of royalty had occurred before (Empress Elisabeth in 1898, King Umberto of Italy in 1900, King Alexander of Serbia in 1903). What made 1914 different was the policy that Vienna and Berlin built upon it. Who in Serbia knew about the plot, and whether Prime Minister **Nikola Pašić** and his government were aware, is still debated: Serbian officials gave a vague warning in Vienna that was not understood, and the Austrian investigation found little evidence that the Serbian government was involved.

### Vienna decides: 28 June to 23 July

Vienna's first reaction was to wish to punish Serbia. **Count Berchtold**, the foreign minister, and General **Conrad von Hötzendorf**, the Chief of the General Staff, wanted war with Serbia, and Conrad had wanted it for years. The Hungarian prime minister, **István Tisza**, resisted at first, fearing a Russian reaction and a rise in the number of Slavs in the Empire, but on 14 July he agreed, provided the demands were presented as a diplomatic action. Vienna's first need was German backing: if Russia intervened, Austria-Hungary could not face it without Germany.

### The blank cheque, 5–6 July

On 5 July Count Alexander Hoyos, Berchtold's chief of staff, took letters from Franz Joseph to Berlin. At a lunch with the Kaiser, the Austro-Hungarian ambassador **Szögyény** was told that Austria-Hungary could count on Germany's "full support" (the words are those of Szögyény's report, preserved in the Austrian archives), and that the Kaiser thought it must not delay. On 6 July the German Chancellor, **Theobald von Bethmann Hollweg**, confirmed this. The pledge is known as the **blank cheque** because it was given without Germany knowing what Austria would do. The Kaiser then left on his annual cruise, and several German leaders went on leave, so that nothing would look unusual.

What did Berlin intend? Three views, which you will meet again in the next chapter, are possible from the same evidence:
- Germany wanted a **local war**: Austria would crush Serbia, Russia would not interfere, and the Triple Entente would be humiliated or split.
- Germany took a **calculated risk**: it knew that the action might lead to a continental war, and decided it was acceptable, perhaps even welcome, since Russia was growing stronger.
- Germany wanted a **continental war** and used Vienna's quarrel as the occasion.
The first is the view of those who think of Berlin as hoping for a limited war; the second uses the phrase "calculated risk", which historians link to the diaries of Kurt Riezler, a close aide of Bethmann Hollweg; the third is the strongest form of the Fischer thesis.

### The ultimatum, 23 July

Vienna waited until the French President and Prime Minister left St Petersburg, so that France and Russia could not coordinate a reply. On **23 July**, at 6 pm, Baron Giesl, the Austrian minister in Belgrade, handed the Serbian government an **ultimatum** with ten demands and a deadline of 48 hours. It required Serbia to condemn anti-Austrian propaganda, dissolve Narodna Odbrana, dismiss officers and officials named by Vienna, and (the demands that Serbia could not accept) to allow Austro-Hungarian officials to work in Serbia against the conspiracy and to take part in the judicial inquiry. Berchtold intended it to be rejected: he wanted a war with Serbia, not a diplomatic success.

### Serbia's reply, 25 July

Serbia replied on **25 July**, just before the deadline expired. The reply was drafted with great care: it accepted most of the demands, some with reservations, and refused the point that Austrian officials should take part in the inquiry on Serbian soil, offering to submit the dispute to the Hague tribunal or the Great Powers. Vienna regarded it as inadequate and broke off relations the same day. When the Kaiser, back from his cruise on 27 July, read the reply, he judged in a marginal note that it removed any reason for war (translations of his words vary). Historians disagree on the reply: for some it was a nearly complete acceptance, which showed that Vienna wanted war whatever Serbia said; for others (Clark) it was a clever diplomatic rejection that left Serbia's real control of the conspiracy unanswered.

### From local war to world war: 28 July to 4 August

:::steps
1. **28 July:** Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia (about 11 am). Berlin, having first encouraged speed, now asks Vienna to "halt in Belgrade" and negotiate; Vienna does not agree.
2. **28–29 July:** Russia decides on partial mobilisation against Austria-Hungary alone, announced on 29 July. The Russian military says that partial mobilisation is technically difficult and that it will have to move to general mobilisation.
3. **29 July:** The Kaiser and the Tsar exchange telegrams (the "Willy–Nicky telegrams"). Grey warns the German ambassador that Britain may not stand aside. Bethmann Hollweg, hoping for British neutrality, urges Vienna to negotiate (in the night of 29–30 July).
4. **30 July:** Russia orders **general mobilisation**, after Tsar Nicholas II cancels and then confirms the order under pressure from Sazonov and the generals. It takes effect on 31 July.
5. **31 July:** Germany declares a state of "imminent danger of war" and sends ultimatums: Russia must stop mobilising within twelve hours; France must say whether it will stay neutral. Russia does not reply.
6. **1 August:** Germany declares war on Russia (evening). France orders general mobilisation. Germany mobilises.
7. **2 August:** Germany demands free passage through Belgium. Britain's Cabinet promises naval protection of the French Channel coast.
8. **3 August:** Germany declares war on France. Belgium rejects the ultimatum. Grey tells the House of Commons that Britain has obligations of honour and interest.
9. **4 August:** German troops cross into Belgium. Britain demands that Germany respect Belgian neutrality; the ultimatum expires at 11 pm London time (midnight in Berlin) and Britain is at war with Germany. Bethmann Hollweg complained to the British ambassador that Britain was going to war over "a scrap of paper".
:::

:::key Seven decision points in the July Crisis
Historians often ask not "who started it?" but "when could a different decision have prevented a continental war?". Each of these is where the argument is fought.
1. **Vienna (early July):** to use the assassination to destroy Serbia by war.
2. **Berlin (5–6 July):** to give the blank cheque and to hope the war stayed local.
3. **Vienna (23 July):** to issue an ultimatum designed to be refused.
4. **Vienna (28 July):** to declare war on Serbia despite the reply.
5. **St Petersburg (28–30 July):** to mobilise, first partially and then fully.
6. **Berlin (31 July):** to send ultimatums to Russia and France and begin the Schlieffen plan.
7. **London (2–4 August):** to enter the war because of Belgium and France.
:::

### Why Britain went to war

Britain was not bound by treaty to fight for France or Russia, and the Cabinet was divided. Grey and the Prime Minister, **H.H. Asquith**, regarded the Entente as a moral commitment and believed that a German victory over France would leave Britain isolated; much of the Liberal Cabinet and of the Liberal party wanted to stay out at first. The German ultimatum to Belgium changed the argument: Belgian neutrality, guaranteed by the 1839 Treaty of London, gave Britain a reason that its public understood. Two ministers resigned, but the Cabinet and Parliament held together. Grey's speech in the Commons on 3 August presented the war as one of honour and of interest: the independence of Belgium and the safety of the Channel ports.

:::warn Do not tell the July Crisis as a list of dates
Paper 3 punishes narrative that is not linked to a claim. When you use the July Crisis, say which decision you are discussing and which interpretation it supports or weakens. For example: "Berlin's pledge of 5–6 July is evidence for Fischer's claim that Germany accepted a risk of continental war, but it is equally consistent with a belief that the war would stay local."
:::

## The documents historians argue over

Paper 3 extracts often rest on a small number of famous documents. You do not need to quote them, but you need to know what each is, who made it, and what each school makes of it, because that is the knowledge that explains and tests an extract. Here are seven, each with its provenance in the board's style. None is reproduced at length: where a few words are quoted they are the document's own, from an English edition.

**1. The blank cheque.** *Report by Count Ladislaus Szögyény-Marich, Austro-Hungarian ambassador in Berlin, to Count Berchtold, 5 July 1914; Austrian documents edited by Ludwig Bittner and others (1930); English translation in German History in Documents and Images.* Szögyény reported that Wilhelm II told him Austria-Hungary could count on Germany's "full support" and that the Kaiser did not want delay. It is an Austrian record of a German conversation: Germany left few records of 5–6 July, so everything depends on it. *Fischer school:* Berlin accepted, even welcomed, a continental war. *Others:* it shows that Berlin wanted a quick, local war, and that Vienna had asked for it. *Williamson:* the initiative came from Vienna and Germany removed the restraint.

**2. The Austro-Hungarian ultimatum.** *Note from the Austro-Hungarian government to the Serbian government, delivered at Belgrade by Baron Giesl, 23 July 1914.* Ten demands, with 48 hours to reply; two (the ones that would let Austrian officials work in Serbia) were designed to be unacceptable. *Used to show:* that Vienna intended war (the usual view), or that Austria had a real grievance, since Serbian officials had helped the assassins (Clark's emphasis).

**3. The Willy–Nicky telegrams.** *Telegrams in English between Kaiser Wilhelm II and Tsar Nicholas II, 29 July–1 August 1914, first published by Germany in its White Book at the outbreak of war.* They show two cousins trying to avoid war while their generals prepared for it. The Tsar wrote that he foresaw being overwhelmed by pressure; the Kaiser answered that the weight of decision rested on the Tsar. *Used to show:* that Germany blamed Russia for the first step, and (by those who stress Russian mobilisation) that Germany reacted to Russia. The German publication means the selection of texts, and Germany's purpose in publishing them in August 1914, need to be weighed.

**4. The "War Council" of 8 December 1912.** *Diary of Admiral Georg Alexander von Müller, Chief of the Kaiser's Naval Cabinet, entry for 8 December 1912; published after the Second World War.* After Britain warned that it could not accept the destruction of France, Wilhelm II called Moltke, Tirpitz, Müller and others to the palace. Müller recorded that Moltke thought war unavoidable and "the sooner the better", and that Tirpitz asked for a delay of about eighteen months so that the navy would be ready. Bethmann Hollweg was not present. *Fischer:* proof that Germany's leaders were deciding on a war of aggression, which led to the army law of 1913. *Critics:* a conversation, not a decision; nothing was ordered, the Chancellor was absent, and the meeting shows how the generals talked, not what the government planned.

**5. The September Programme.** *Memorandum, "Provisional notes on the direction of our policy on the conclusion of peace", from Chancellor Bethmann Hollweg, 9 September 1914 (during the Battle of the Marne).* It set out war aims: security for Germany against France and Russia "for all imaginable time", a weakened France, Russia pushed back, a Belgium reduced to dependence, and a central European economic association led by Germany. *Fischer:* the programme shows Germany's goals of world power and continuity with pre-war aims. *Critics:* a wartime paper written when Germany expected victory, summarising the demands of many pressure groups, which shows what Germany wanted when it began winning, not why it began the war.

**6. Grey's speech of 3 August 1914.** *Speech by Sir Edward Grey, British Foreign Secretary, in the House of Commons, 3 August 1914 (Hansard).* He told the Commons that Britain's obligation to France was one of honour and interest, that Belgian neutrality and the safety of the Channel coast were at stake, and that standing aside would cost Britain its good name. *Used to show:* a reluctant government, persuaded by Belgium (the usual view), or (Ferguson) a decision that was not forced on Britain by honour but by strategic fear and that made a European war a world war.

**7. Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles.** *Treaty of Versailles, signed 28 June 1919, Article 231.* "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." It was meant to establish Germany's liability for reparations, but Germans read it as a verdict of guilt, which started the "war-guilt" debate you will study in the next chapter.

:::key How to use a document in an answer
Say **what it is** (nature and origin), **what it shows** (the fact), and **what a historian does with it** (the interpretation). Then say what limits it: whose record, written when, published why. This is the same skill as Paper 1, used here to explain an *argument* rather than to prove a fact.
:::

## Tagging the events: who uses what

Historians do not disagree about most of the facts; they disagree about which facts matter. The labels below are the ones the next chapter develops: the **Fischer thesis** (Germany bore the main responsibility, having planned or knowingly risked a war of aggression); **shared responsibility** (several powers, including Germany, were jointly responsible; Fay, Clark); **Austria-Hungary** (Williamson); **Russia** (McMeekin); **Britain** (Ferguson); and **contingency** (the war came from decisions that might have been different; Afflerbach and Stevenson, Clark).

| Event or evidence | Fischer thesis | Other readings |
|---|---|---|
| Weltpolitik, the Navy Laws (1898, 1900), Anglo-German rivalry | German drive for world power | German fear of encirclement; Britain's own naval and imperial ambitions |
| Moroccan Crises (1905, 1911) | German bullying of France | Germany's defeats increased its fear; France and Britain were also provocative |
| 8 December 1912 "War Council" | A plan for war by 1914 | A conversation, not a decision; Tirpitz wanted delay |
| German army laws, 1912–13 | Preparation for a war | Response to Russia's growth and to the French law |
| Bosnian Crisis, 1908–09; Balkan Wars, 1912–13 | Background only | Austria-Hungary's fear of Serbia (Williamson); Russia's humiliation and ambitions (McMeekin) |
| Sarajevo and the Black Hand | The occasion, used by Germany and Austria | Serbian nationalism as a cause (Clark) |
| The blank cheque, 5–6 July | Germany accepted a continental war | Germany hoped for a local war; Vienna took the initiative (Williamson) |
| The ultimatum, 23 July | Designed to be refused, with German consent | Austria had a real grievance (Clark) |

## Using this knowledge on Paper 3

A good knowledge paragraph is built on a claim in the extract. In practice this means one of four moves for each fact above.

:::key Four moves for knowledge in Section A
1. **Name the event and its date**, exactly: "On 5 July 1914 Germany gave Austria-Hungary its 'full support'."
2. **Say what the extract claims it shows**: "The historian reads this as Germany accepting the risk of a larger war."
3. **Test it with a second fact**: "But on 28 July the Kaiser proposed that Austria halt in Belgrade, and on 29–30 July Bethmann urged restraint, which suggests Berlin hoped for a local war."
4. **Say what the choice reveals about the approach**: "By stressing the pledge and not the later restraint, the extract shows a historian concerned with intention and with German policy rather than with how the crisis got out of control."
:::

:::eg Table B: a claim tested with knowledge, level by level
**The claim (written for practice; not a historian's words).** "In July 1914 German leaders knowingly accepted the risk of a continental war."

**What the levels want (Table B).** Level 3 (9–11) is generally accurate and relevant; Level 4 (12–14) is mostly relevant and accurate; Level 6 (18–20) is detailed, accurate and entirely relevant: every fact is doing a job.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "In July 1914 there was a crisis. On 28 June Archduke Franz Ferdinand was killed in Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary sent an ultimatum to Serbia and Germany supported it. Then Russia mobilised and Germany declared war on Russia. Germany also declared war on France and invaded Belgium, and Britain declared war. So Germany did risk a big war."

*Commentary.* Accurate and about the right events, but a narrative with no dates, no sources and no testing; the last sentence only repeats the claim. Level 3.

**A Level 4 paragraph (adds explanation).** "On 5–6 July Germany gave Austria-Hungary the 'blank cheque': the Kaiser told the Austrian ambassador that Austria could count on Germany's full support, and said it should not delay. Berlin knew that an Austrian attack on Serbia could bring in Russia, because Russia had been humiliated in the Bosnian Crisis of 1908–09 and had promised to protect Serbia. This supports the claim that German leaders accepted the risk of a larger war."

*Commentary.* Detailed and accurate, and each fact explains why the claim might be true. But the paragraph only supports the claim and does not test it.

**A Level 6 paragraph (explains, tests and links to the approach).** "The claim is well supported by the pledge of 5–6 July. The Austro-Hungarian ambassador reported that the Kaiser said Austria could count on Germany's 'full support' and should not delay, and the Chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, confirmed it on 6 July, though both knew that Russia might defend Serbia. Moltke's remark in December 1912 that war was unavoidable, recorded in Admiral Müller's diary, and the fact that German planners expected Russia's army to be stronger by 1917, show a leadership that thought a war was likely and that the sooner it came the better. But the evidence also shows that the risk was a 'calculated' one: Germany pressed Vienna to act quickly so as to present Europe with a fait accompli (a thing done before others can object), and it expected Russia, France and Britain to hold back. When Russia and Britain did not, Berlin's confidence broke: the Kaiser, reading Serbia's reply on 28 July, proposed a halt in Belgrade, and Bethmann sent urgent telegrams to Vienna on 29–30 July. The claim therefore holds if 'accepted the risk' means a choice made with eyes open, but not if it means a plan for a continental war. A historian who writes in this way has chosen the decisions of Berlin as the centre of the story, which tells us that the approach is concerned with intention and with who chose what, not with how the crisis escaped control."

*Commentary.* Every sentence of knowledge does a job: the date and the pledge explain the claim; the 1912 diary and the 1917 expectation extend it; the Kaiser's halt proposal and Bethmann's telegrams test it; and the last sentence says what the choice of facts reveals about the approach. Level 6.
:::

:::eg Explain the significance of Belgium's neutrality for the outbreak of the First World War
**What the levels want.** A short significance answer scores on knowledge and on explaining what the fact changed. A good one says what the event was, what it changed, and for whom.

**Answer (about 170 words).** "Belgium's neutrality had been guaranteed by the Treaty of London in 1839 by Britain and other powers. It mattered for the outbreak of the war in two ways. First, it mattered to Germany. The Schlieffen–Moltke plan required German armies to defeat France in about six weeks by marching through Belgium, so when Germany sent an ultimatum to Brussels on 2 August demanding free passage and invaded on 4 August, it made a British intervention far more likely. Second, it mattered to Britain. The Liberal Cabinet had been divided on 1–2 August, with Britain bound to France only by an understanding (the Entente Cordiale) and not by a treaty. The invasion of Belgium gave Grey and Asquith a cause that united the Cabinet and the Commons, and on 3 August Grey spoke of honour and interest. Britain's ultimatum expired at 11 pm on 4 August. Belgium was therefore the issue on which a European war became a war involving the British Empire, though historians such as Niall Ferguson argue that Britain's real motive was the strategic fear of a German victory over France."

*Commentary.* Names the treaty and the date, connects the plan to Britain's decision, uses specific dates, and links the answer to an interpretation.
:::

:::eg Which events would a historian who blames Germany emphasise, and why?
**Question.** Name four events that a historian who believes Germany bore the main responsibility would emphasise, and four that a historian who thinks responsibility was shared among several powers would stress. Explain each choice in a sentence.

**Answer.**
- *Germany primarily responsible:* (1) the Navy Laws and Weltpolitik, which show German ambition and a challenge to Britain; (2) the "War Council" of 8 December 1912 and the army laws of 1913, which suggest preparation; (3) the blank cheque of 5–6 July, which shows Berlin encouraging Vienna to act without restraint; (4) the ultimatums to Russia and France of 31 July and the invasion of Belgium, which show Germany starting the continental war.
- *Responsibility shared:* (1) the Bosnian Crisis and Balkan Wars, which show Serbian nationalism and Austria-Hungary's fear; (2) the Black Hand's links with the Sarajevo plot; (3) Russian mobilisation on 29–30 July; (4) the French–Russian alliance and the confidence of Poincaré's France, and Britain's naval and imperial rivalry with Germany.
- *Why:* Each side picks the events that fit its central question: those who ask "who started it?" look at the decisions of 5–6 July and 31 July; those who ask "how did it become possible?" look at the long-term tensions and the Balkans.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
An extract argues that Russia's mobilisation made a European war unavoidable. A student wrote:

"On 28 July Austria declared war on Serbia. Russia mobilised on 30 July. Germany then declared war on Russia on 1 August and on France on 3 August. Britain declared war on 4 August. This shows that Russia was to blame for the war because Russia mobilised first."

**The level.** Table B **Level 3**: the dates are accurate and relevant, but the paragraph tells a story and its conclusion ("Russia mobilised first, so Russia was to blame") is the historian's claim restated, not tested. It does not say that Russia first ordered partial mobilisation against Austria alone, nor that Germany had already given its "blank cheque", nor that Germany's war plan made a German response to any Russian mobilisation almost automatic.

**The fix.** "Russia's mobilisation was a key step: the partial mobilisation of 28–29 July became general on 30 July, and Germany's ultimatum of 31 July demanded that it stop. But the claim that Russia 'caused' the war is hard to test with the order of events alone. Russia was responding to Austria's declaration of war on Serbia on 28 July, which followed the ultimatum of 23 July and Germany's pledge of 5–6 July; and the Schlieffen–Moltke plan meant that any Russian mobilisation would lead Germany to attack France as well. So the mobilisation was the step at which a local war became a European one, but whether that step was the decisive cause depends on whether the decisions in Vienna and Berlin are treated as earlier steps. A historian who puts Russia first is giving priority to the mobilisation over those earlier choices, and this shows an approach that reads the crisis from St Petersburg." This version names the claim, tests it with earlier facts and says what the emphasis reveals.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name the members of the Triple Alliance and the Triple Entente, and say which links were alliances.
A: Triple Alliance: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy (the Dual Alliance of 1879, the Triple Alliance of 1882). Triple Entente: France, Russia, Britain. The Franco-Russian link (1892–94) was an alliance; Britain's links with France (1904) and Russia (1907) were ententes (understandings), not promises to fight.
Q: What was Weltpolitik, and why did it alarm Britain?
A: German policy from the late 1890s to gain world-power status through colonies, naval strength and trade. The German Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 began a battle fleet which Britain regarded as a challenge to its naval supremacy.
Q: What was the Bosnian Crisis of 1908–09, and what were its results?
A: Austria-Hungary annexed Bosnia-Herzegovina in October 1908. Russia protested but gave way in March 1909 after Germany backed Vienna. Results: Russian determination not to be humiliated again, sharper Serbian nationalism (Narodna Odbrana was founded in 1908), and German support for Austria-Hungary in the Balkans.
Q: Why did the Schlieffen–Moltke plan matter in 1914?
A: It required Germany to defeat France in about six weeks, through Belgium, before turning against Russia. So Germany could not fight Russia alone, and a march through Belgium made British intervention likely.
Q: What was the "blank cheque" and when was it given?
A: Germany's promise, on 5–6 July 1914, of full support to Austria-Hungary in whatever action it chose against Serbia, without knowing what that would be.
Q: What did the Austro-Hungarian ultimatum of 23 July demand, and how did Serbia respond?
A: Ten demands in 48 hours, including that Austrian officials take part in the investigation inside Serbia. Serbia replied on 25 July, accepting most but not those, and offered to refer the dispute to the Hague tribunal; Austria-Hungary broke off relations.
Q: Put these in order: Russian general mobilisation; Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia; German ultimatum to Russia; Germany invades Belgium.
A: Austria-Hungary declares war on Serbia (28 July); Russian general mobilisation (30 July, effective 31 July); German ultimatum to Russia (31 July); Germany invades Belgium (4 August).
Q: Why did Britain declare war on 4 August 1914?
A: Germany ignored the demand that it respect Belgian neutrality (guaranteed by the 1839 Treaty of London); Britain also had a moral commitment to France and a strategic fear of German domination of the Channel coast.
Q: Why was Italy not at war in August 1914?
A: The Triple Alliance was defensive, and Austria-Hungary had acted first against Serbia, so Italy declared neutrality on 2–3 August. It joined the Allies in 1915.

A top answer says that the same event is used by each side to support a question ("who made the first move?" or "who made it possible?") and chooses a criterion.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Explain how far the Schlieffen–Moltke plan limited the choices of German leaders in 1914.
A: It required a rapid defeat of France through Belgium before Russia was ready; so Germany had no plan for fighting Russia alone, and a Russian mobilisation meant war with France too; it made Belgium's neutrality irrelevant to the General Staff, and so made British entry likely. But Bethmann Hollweg did overrule Moltke's demand for immediate mobilisation on 29 July; the Kaiser could have chosen to accept the halt in Belgrade; and the plan was a tool of choices made earlier. A Level 4 answer judges that the plan narrowed options from 30 July but did not fix the decisions of 5–29 July.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the alliance system did not make war automatic in 1914.
A: Italy, a member of the Triple Alliance, declared neutrality because the alliance was defensive; Britain had no treaty obligation to France or Russia; Germany and Austria chose to act; the alliances were defensive and each Power decided on its own timetable. The alliances did, however, make each Power fear to abandon its partner. Level 4 judges that the alliances widened the war once started, not that they started it.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice; not a historian's words.) "The Austro-Hungarian government chose war in July 1914 and used German support to avoid being restrained." Use your knowledge of the July Crisis to explain and test this claim.
H: Which facts show Vienna choosing war? (Berchtold, Conrad, the 7 July council, the ultimatum, the declaration of war on 28 July.)
H: Which facts show Germany's part? (The blank cheque; the demand that Vienna act quickly.)
H: What could Vienna have done differently? (Tisza's resistance; Serbia's reply; the halt in Belgrade.)
A: Support: Berchtold and Conrad wanted war with Serbia; at the council of 7 July all but Tisza favoured war; Tisza agreed on 14 July; the ultimatum of 23 July (ten demands, 48 hours) was meant to be refused; Vienna broke off relations on 25 July though Serbia accepted most demands; war was declared on 28 July, the day the Kaiser proposed a halt. Germany's pledge of 5–6 July removed the restraint of fear of Russia. Test: Serbia's reply and the Kaiser's halt proposal show that restraint was possible and Vienna refused it; Austria-Hungary was driven by fear for the Empire (Serbian nationalism, the Bosnian Serbs), which can be read as a defensive motive; Tisza's resistance shows the decision was not unanimous. Level 6 knowledge uses dates and names and ends by saying what the claim reveals about an approach centred on Vienna's decisions (Williamson).
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice.) "Russia's mobilisation turned a Balkan war into a European war." Use your knowledge to explain and test this claim.
H: What did Russia decide on 28–30 July, and why?
H: What did Germany do in response?
H: What happened before 28 July that limits the claim?
A: Support: partial mobilisation decided on 28 July (announced 29 July) and general mobilisation on 30 July (effective 31 July); Germany's twelve-hour ultimatum of 31 July; the German plan meant war on Russia led to war on France; the Franco-Russian alliance (mobilisation clause); Sazonov's firmness; Russia's long-term aims (the Straits). Test: Russia reacted to Austria's declaration of war on Serbia on 28 July; Germany's pledge and Vienna's ultimatum came first; the Tsar hesitated and cancelled the general order once; Germany declared war on Russia before Russia declared war on Germany; the claim shows a historian who reads the crisis from St Petersburg.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice.) "The war became a world war because Britain chose to enter it." Use your knowledge to explain and test this claim, and say what it shows about the approach of a historian who makes it.
H: What were Britain's commitments to France and Belgium before August 1914?
H: What did the Cabinet decide on 2, 3 and 4 August, and why?
H: What would have happened to France and the Channel if Britain had stayed out?
A: Explain: Britain was not bound by treaty to France or Russia; the Entente (1904) and staff talks (from 1906), the naval agreement of 1912, and Grey's reluctance to promise anything; German invasion of Belgium on 4 August; the Commons and the Cabinet; Britain's empire joined the war, making it worldwide. Test: Britain's entry came after Germany's ultimatums to Russia and France (31 July) and the Schlieffen plan, so the continental war existed before Britain joined; Germany's invasion of Belgium made British entry almost certain; a German victory over France would have threatened the balance of power. The claim is Ferguson's kind of argument: it asks whether Britain could have stayed out, which shows an interest in the decisions of a power outside the Balkans and in counterfactuals. Level 6 sees that this approach takes the war's *wideness* as its question, rather than its origin.
Q: [10 marks] ★★★ Choose two of the seven decision points of the July Crisis and explain how each could be used by a historian who argues for contingency (that different decisions might have prevented the war).
H: Which decisions were closest to being reversed?
A: Examples: 28 July (Kaiser's halt in Belgrade proposal; Vienna did not agree), 29–30 July (Tsar Nicholas cancelled, then reinstated general mobilisation), 30 July (Bethmann's telegrams urging restraint on Vienna), 2–4 August (Britain's Cabinet was divided until Belgium). A contingency argument emphasises that each choice could have gone the other way, which fits the sort of historians who stress decisions over structures; it does not deny that long-term tensions made war possible.
:::

:::recap
- **Tensions**: Germany's rise and Weltpolitik; Anglo-German naval and imperial rivalry; Franco-German enmity over Alsace-Lorraine and Morocco (1905, 1911); Austria-Hungary's fear of Serbia; Russia's recovery after 1905 and its interest in the Balkans and the Straits.
- **Alliances**: Dual Alliance (7 October 1879); Triple Alliance (20 May 1882); Franco-Russian alliance (1892–94); Entente Cordiale (8 April 1904); Anglo-Russian Convention (31 August 1907); Belgium guaranteed in 1839.
- **Arms**: German Navy Laws (1898, 1900); HMS *Dreadnought* (10 February 1906); Haldane Mission (February 1912); army laws of 1912–13 and the French Three-Year Law (7 August 1913); the Russian Great Programme.
- **Balkans**: Bosnian annexation (October 1908); Balkan Wars (October 1912–August 1913); Serbia's growth; Liman von Sanders (December 1913).
- **War plans**: Schlieffen–Moltke (through Belgium); Plan XVII; Plan 19; Austrian Plans B and R. Mobilisation was close to war.
- **Crisis**: Sarajevo 28 June; blank cheque 5–6 July; ultimatum 23 July; Serbian reply 25 July; Austria declares war 28 July; Russian partial (28–29 July) and general (30 July) mobilisation; German ultimatums 31 July; war on Russia 1 August; war on France 3 August; invasion of Belgium and Britain at war 4 August.
- **Documents**: blank cheque, ultimatum, Willy–Nicky telegrams, War Council (1912), September Programme (1914), Grey's speech (3 August), Article 231.
