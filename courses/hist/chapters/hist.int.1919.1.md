On 11 November 1918 the guns stopped after a war that had killed roughly nine to ten million soldiers and wrecked four empires. The statesmen who met in Paris two months later had to build a lasting peace from the ruins, with millions of voters demanding both justice and revenge. They produced five treaties, and almost nobody was content with them: Germany called its treaty a *Diktat*, Italy said it had been robbed, France thought it too weak, Hungary lost two thirds of its land. ## Europe in 1919: the world the peacemakers inherited

The war had ended in a way that made every later argument harder. On 11 November 1918 German troops were still on foreign soil: the Armistice stopped the fighting, but no Allied army stood in Germany. The Kaiser, Wilhelm II, had fled to the Netherlands and a German republic had been proclaimed on 9 November. Many Germans therefore felt the army had not been beaten on the battlefield. In fact the Allies had broken the German army in the summer and autumn of 1918, and its leaders themselves had asked for an armistice. But the feeling mattered more than the facts, as you will see later.

Four empires had disappeared: the German, the Austro-Hungarian, the Russian and the Ottoman. In their place were revolutions, newly proclaimed states and armed men without orders. In Russia the Bolsheviks, who had taken power in October 1917, were fighting a civil war, and Britain, France, Japan and the USA had sent troops to support their enemies. In January 1919 communists rose in Berlin; in March a communist government (Béla Kun's) took power in Hungary, and in April a "Soviet Republic" briefly ruled Bavaria. For the men in Paris, the threat of Bolshevism spreading west was real, and it shaped many decisions.

The cost of the war weighed on all of them. Roughly nine to ten million soldiers were dead. France had lost about 1.4 million men and the ten northern departments where the Western Front had run were wasted ground: mines flooded, towns shelled flat. Britain had borrowed heavily, mainly from the USA. The USA, by contrast, had joined late (April 1917), lost about 116,000 men and emerged as the world's greatest creditor. And the peoples of Europe wanted two things that did not fit together: a peace that would make war impossible, and a Germany that was made to pay.

:::def Armistice
An agreement to stop fighting while peace terms are negotiated. The Armistice of 11 November 1918 disarmed Germany enough that it could not restart the war, but it was not a peace treaty. The treaties of 1919–20 are the peace.
:::

## The negotiations at Versailles

### The Paris Peace Conference

The conference opened on **18 January 1919** at the French Foreign Ministry on the Quai d'Orsay in Paris. The date was chosen on purpose: on 18 January 1871 the German Empire had been proclaimed in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, after France's defeat in the Franco-Prussian War. France meant to reverse that humiliation in the same place. Delegates from about thirty countries came, with thousands of experts, secretaries and journalists, and the city became the capital of the world for six months.

It was never a single negotiation. The delegates divided into more than fifty commissions and committees that studied frontiers, reparations, colonies, finance and the new League of Nations. Yet the real decisions were taken by very few people. At first a **Council of Ten** (two representatives each from the USA, Britain, France, Italy and Japan) decided matters. By March it was too large and too leaky, and on **24 March 1919** the **Council of Four** began to meet in private: Woodrow Wilson, Georges Clemenceau, David Lloyd George and Vittorio Orlando. Japan's delegates were dropped from the inner circle and, when the Italians walked out in April, the "Big Three" decided alone.

### The aims of the Big Three

The Big Three came with aims shaped by their own countries' experience. You must be able to state each aim and explain what lay behind it.

:::def Self-determination
The principle that peoples with a common nationality should be free to choose their own government and, where possible, to have their own state. Wilson said on 11 February 1918 that "self-determination" was "not a mere phrase" but "an imperative principle of action".
:::

:::def Collective security
The idea that peace is best kept if all states promise to defend any one of them against attack, so that an aggressor faces the combined power of the rest. It replaces rival alliances with a general system, and it was the idea behind the League of Nations.
:::

**Woodrow Wilson of the USA.** Wilson came as a moral leader. In his **Fourteen Points** of 8 January 1918 he had set out his vision: "open covenants of peace, openly arrived at" (no more secret treaties), free trade, reduction of armaments, a fair adjustment of colonial claims, national frontiers "along clearly recognizable lines of nationality", an independent Poland with access to the sea, and in Point 14 "a general association of nations". The Germans had asked for the Armistice on the understanding that these would be the basis of peace. Wilson believed the League of Nations was the key, because it could put right any mistakes in the treaties later. He was also a Democrat facing a hostile Congress: the Republicans had won control of the Senate in the elections of November 1918, and he had not taken a leading Republican with him to Paris.

**Georges Clemenceau of France.** France had been invaded twice in forty-four years (1870 and 1914). It had about 40 million people against Germany's roughly 65 million, its industrial north was in ruins, and about 1.4 million Frenchmen were dead. Clemenceau's first aim was **security**: to make Germany too weak to attack again. Marshal Foch wanted a separate Rhineland state and the Rhine as a military frontier. Clemenceau took less: a demilitarised Rhineland with Allied occupation, French control of the coal of the Saar, strict limits on the German army, and a promise from Britain and the USA to come to France's aid if Germany attacked. His second aim was **reparations**: France should be repaid for the destruction and Germany's economy should not be left stronger than France's. He was sceptical about Wilson's ideals: his own view, according to Keynes (who disliked him), was that the great illusion was France and the great disillusion was mankind.

**David Lloyd George of Britain.** Lloyd George had won a landslide in the "coupon election" of 14 December 1918 in a campaign in which British politicians promised to make Germany pay, and some called for the Kaiser to be tried, and one minister, Sir Eric Geddes, is remembered for promising to squeeze Germany "until the pips squeak". In Paris he was more moderate than his speeches. Britain had already gained what it most wanted: the German navy was out of action and the German colonies were in British hands. Lloyd George wanted Germany weakened but not destroyed, because Germany was a major trading partner, and he feared that a very harsh peace would push Germany into the arms of the Bolsheviks. He also did not want France to dominate the continent. Between the two, he looked for compromise. He would not accept Wilson's "freedom of the seas" (it would have weakened the Royal Navy). In the **Fontainebleau Memorandum** of 25 March 1919, written after a weekend of discussion in the forest outside Paris, he warned his colleagues: "You may strip Germany of her colonies, reduce her armaments to a mere police force and her navy to that of a fifth rate power; all the same in the end if she feels that she has been unjustly treated in the peace of 1919 she will find means of exacting retribution from her conquerors." Yet he also bowed to domestic opinion on the cost of the war, and it was Lloyd George, with support from Smuts, who pressed for soldiers' pensions and allowances to be counted in the reparations bill.

**Vittorio Orlando of Italy.** Italy had joined the Allies in 1915 after the secret **Treaty of London**, which promised it the Trentino, South Tyrol up to the Brenner Pass, Trieste, Istria, much of the Dalmatian coast and a share of colonies. About 600,000 Italians had died. Wilson regarded the secret treaty as the old diplomacy, and because Point 9 of his Fourteen Points had promised Italy frontiers "along clearly recognizable lines of nationality", he would not give Italy lands with large Slav and German populations. The city of Fiume (now Rijeka) was not even in the Treaty of London, but Italians in the town demanded it.

:::key What each of the Big Three wanted
1. **Wilson**: a "just peace" built on self-determination and a League of Nations; no secret deals.
2. **Clemenceau**: security against Germany (Rhine, army limits, occupation, guarantees) and heavy reparations.
3. **Lloyd George**: a settlement that kept Britain's naval and imperial position, did not destroy German trade or feed Bolshevism, and did not hand France dominance.
4. **Orlando**: the lands promised to Italy in 1915 and Fiume.
Because the aims clashed, most clauses were compromises, and each leader could say the treaty was either too harsh or too weak.
:::

### Representation of other powers

The conference was meant to be a peace of all the Allies, but in practice the powers were not equal.

- **Great powers and small powers.** The five great powers (the USA, Britain, France, Italy and Japan) had five delegates each. Smaller allies such as Belgium, Serbia and Brazil had two or three, and others one. Small states resented being summoned only when their own affairs were discussed.
- **The British Dominions.** Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa and India each had delegates and signed the treaty in their own right. Their prime ministers (Billy Hughes of Australia, Jan Smuts of South Africa) argued hard for their own aims: Hughes insisted on keeping the former German New Guinea.
- **Japan.** Japan sat as a great power. It asked for a clause on **racial equality** in the League Covenant (the League's founding charter, which became part of every peace treaty), first put forward by Baron Makino on 13 February 1919 and revised on 11 April. Eleven of the seventeen members of the League commission present voted in favour and none against (the other six did not vote for it), but Wilson, in the chair, ruled that such an important change needed unanimity, and the Dominions, especially Australia, were opposed. Japan was angry. It did, however, keep the German Pacific islands north of the equator (as a mandate: a territory the League entrusted to a victor to govern until it could stand alone) and take over German rights in the Chinese province of **Shandong**.
- **China.** China had joined the Allies in 1917 and expected Germany's rights in Shandong to be returned to it. Instead the conference left them to Japan. News of this brought students into the streets of Beijing on 4 May 1919 (the **May Fourth Movement**), and the Chinese delegation refused to sign the Treaty of Versailles.
- **Colonial peoples.** Peoples hoping for self-determination in the colonies were turned away. The Vietnamese nationalist Nguyen Ai Quoc (later known as Ho Chi Minh) tried to present a petition for rights in French Indochina, and was ignored. The Arab leader Emir Faisal attended, but the Arab lands of the Ottoman Empire were divided between Britain and France as mandates (see the Japan bullet above for the meaning).
- **The defeated powers.** Germany, Austria, Hungary, Bulgaria and the Ottoman Empire were not allowed to take part in the discussions. Each was called in at the end and handed a finished treaty. They were permitted to send written comments, and Germany's led to only small changes.
- **Russia.** Russia had been the Allies' ally until 1917 but the Bolshevik government was not invited, and the Allies could not agree whether to support its enemies, negotiate with it or contain it. Wilson's proposal in January 1919 for a meeting of all the Russian factions on the island of Prinkipo in the Sea of Marmara came to nothing, because the anti-Bolshevik "Whites" refused to attend.

:::key Why representation mattered
The treaties were imposed, not negotiated. The defeated saw them as dictated; small states felt overruled; China, Japan and the colonial peoples felt their claims had been ignored. That made it easy for every excluded group to say the settlement had no legitimacy.
:::

### The Treaty of Versailles with Germany

The treaty had 440 articles. Germany signed it on **28 June 1919** after the Allies threatened to resume the war. Its main terms are in five groups.

**Territory.** Germany lost about **13 per cent of its European territory** (roughly 70,000 square kilometres) and about 10 per cent of its people (roughly six to seven million).
- **Alsace-Lorraine** went back to France (Germany had taken it in 1871).
- **Eupen-Malmedy** went to Belgium.
- **North Schleswig** went to Denmark after a plebiscite in 1920.
- **Posen and West Prussia** went to the new state of Poland. This gave Poland the sea access promised in Wilson's Point 13, but the strip of land (the "Polish Corridor") cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany.
- **Danzig**, a port with an almost wholly German population, became a Free City under the League of Nations; **Memel** was placed under Allied control.
- The **Hultschin** district went to Czechoslovakia.
- A plebiscite in **Upper Silesia** was to decide the future of that industrial region (see below).
- The **Saar**, with its coal, was put under League of Nations administration for fifteen years; its mines went to France (Article 45) as compensation for the destruction of French mines. A plebiscite after fifteen years was to decide its future.

**Colonies.** Germany lost all of its overseas colonies (Article 119). They were not simply handed to the victors but became **mandates** of the League of Nations, run by Britain, France, Belgium, Japan, South Africa, Australia and New Zealand (see the next chapter).

**Armed forces.** Germany's army was limited to **100,000 men** (Article 160) enlisted for twelve years, with no conscription, no tanks, no heavy artillery, no poison gas and no military aircraft. Its navy was cut to a handful of old ships and 15,000 men, with no submarines. A General Staff was forbidden. The **Rhineland** (the German land west of the Rhine and a strip 50 kilometres wide east of it) was to be **demilitarised** (Article 42) and the land west of the Rhine, with bridgeheads, was to be **occupied** by Allied troops for fifteen years (Article 428).

**Anschluss forbidden.** Article 80 made Germany acknowledge "the independence of Austria" as "inalienable" unless the Council of the League of Nations agreed. The ban on union between Germany and Austria is called the *Anschluss* ban.

**War guilt and trials.** Article 231 made Germany accept responsibility for the loss and damage caused by the war (see "Reparations" below). Article 227 charged the former Kaiser with "a supreme offence against international morality and the sanctity of treaties". The Netherlands, where he lived, refused to hand him over, so he was never tried.

**Reparations.** Germany had to pay for the damage done to Allied civilians. The sum was left to be fixed later (see below).

### The other treaties

Each treaty began with the Covenant of the League of Nations (the League is the subject of the next chapter) and then dealt with the defeated state.

**Saint-Germain (Austria), 10 September 1919.** The multinational empire of Austria-Hungary, with some 50 million people, was dissolved before the conference met. The Austrian republic that remained, with about 6.5 million people, was a small state with a capital (Vienna) far too big for it. The treaty recognised Czechoslovakia, Poland, Hungary and the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes (Yugoslavia) as independent, gave Italy the South Tyrol up to the Brenner Pass, the Trentino, Trieste and Istria, limited Austria's army to 30,000 volunteers, and (like Versailles) forbade union with Germany. Many Austrians doubted that so small a state could survive on its own, which is one reason they wanted union with Germany.

**Neuilly (Bulgaria), 27 November 1919.** Bulgaria had fought on the German side. It lost Western Thrace to Greece (and with it its coast on the Aegean Sea), some small border areas to Yugoslavia, and confirmed the loss of Southern Dobruja to Romania. Its army was limited to 20,000 men and it had to pay reparations.

**Trianon (Hungary), 4 June 1920.** Hungary suffered the heaviest losses of any defeated state. Its territory fell from about 283,000 to about 93,000 square kilometres, a loss of about **two thirds**, and its population by more than half. Slovakia and Ruthenia went to Czechoslovakia; Transylvania and part of the Banat to Romania; Croatia and the Vojvodina to Yugoslavia; a western strip, the Burgenland, to Austria. About three million ethnic Hungarians found themselves living in other countries. The army was limited to 35,000 men. Hungarians still call Trianon a national catastrophe.

**Sèvres (Ottoman Empire), 10 August 1920.** The treaty carved up what remained of the Ottoman Empire. The Arab provinces became mandates (Syria and Lebanon to France; Iraq and Palestine to Britain). The Straits were opened to all ships under an international commission. Greece received Eastern Thrace and the right to administer the area around Smyrna (Izmir) for five years, after which a vote would decide its future. Armenia was to be independent and the Kurds might gain autonomy. The Sultan's government signed, but Turkish nationalists led by **Mustafa Kemal**, who had set up a rival assembly at Ankara in April 1920, rejected it. Kemal's armies defeated the Greeks in 1922, and in the **Treaty of Lausanne** (24 July 1923) the Allies accepted a new Turkey with Eastern Thrace and Smyrna, no Armenia or Kurdistan, and no restrictions on its army. Sèvres is therefore the only treaty that was torn up by force.

:::key The five treaties in one table
Versailles (Germany) and Trianon (Hungary) were the most resented; Saint-Germain (Austria) and Neuilly (Bulgaria) reduced the defeated to small, weak states; Sèvres (Ottoman Empire) was overturned within three years. Together they redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East, left about nine new or restored states on the map (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, a small Austria, an independent Hungary, Finland, Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania) and left the defeated determined to revise them.
:::

## Difficulties created by the treaties

### Boundaries and minorities

Wilson had promised frontiers drawn by nationality. In the heart of Europe the nationalities were so mixed that no frontier could do it, and the peacemakers also had other aims: to weaken Germany, to give the new states defensible borders and economies and to reward allies.

| New or enlarged state | Problem |
|---|---|
| Czechoslovakia | About 3 million Germans (nearly a quarter of the population) in the Sudetenland, as well as Hungarians, Poles and Ruthenes; the Slovaks wanted more autonomy than they got |
| Poland | About 30 per cent of its people were Ukrainians, Jews, Germans or Belarusians; it was at war with its neighbours until 1921 |
| Yugoslavia | Serbs, Croats and Slovenes, many of them hostile to Serb dominance; German, Hungarian and Albanian minorities |
| Romania | Transylvania contained well over a million Hungarians |
| Italy | About 250,000 German-speaking South Tyroleans in the Italian Tyrol |
| Austria | Wanted union with Germany; forbidden |

To protect them the Allies made the new states sign **Minority Treaties** (Poland signed on 28 June 1919, the same day as Versailles), putting minority rights under the guarantee of the League. But the treaties applied only to some states, not to Italy or France, which made the new states resentful, and minorities who petitioned the League often got little.

### Inconsistent application of national self-determination

The peacemakers did not apply self-determination consistently. This is the single most important point for a Paper 2 answer on difficulties of the treaties.

- **Applied against the defeated.** The Austrian assembly declared in November 1918 that German-Austria was part of the German republic, but Article 80 of Versailles and a matching article of Saint-Germain stopped it. Sudeten Germans (about 3 million) were placed in Czechoslovakia without a vote, and the 250,000 or so Germans of the South Tyrol were handed to Italy, the Allies' promise of 1915 outweighing the principle. The three million Hungarians left outside Hungary had no say. Danzig's German population was not asked either.
- **Applied for the victors.** Alsace-Lorraine returned to France without a plebiscite; the French claimed that its people were French. Poland got territory with German majorities when strategic or economic reasons weighed more than nationality.
- **Plebiscites held only in some places.** Plebiscites decided North Schleswig (1920, where the north chose Denmark and the centre chose Germany), Allenstein and Marienwerder (July 1920, which voted overwhelmingly for Germany), Carinthia (October 1920, where the vote kept most of the area in Austria) and Upper Silesia (March 1921).
- **Colonies.** The Fourteen Points had promised "a free, open-minded, and absolutely impartial adjustment of all colonial claims". The German colonies became mandates under the victors; no one asked the people living in them.

:::warn Do not say the peacemakers ignored self-determination
Many frontiers did follow nationality: Poland's heartland, Czech lands, Denmark's border, the Baltic states, Yugoslavia's South Slav core. The mistake in the peace was inconsistency, not indifference. The best answers show where the principle was applied, where it was set aside and why (security, strategy, the economy, punishment of the defeated).
:::

:::key Why self-determination caused problems
1. **No clear unit.** Nationalities were mixed; a new state always left minorities (US Secretary of State Robert Lansing asked in December 1918 what unit Wilson meant: a race, a territory or a community).
2. **Other aims overruled it**: security (Rhineland, Sudetenland frontier), economics (Upper Silesia coal) and punishment (Austria, Hungary, Germany).
3. **It was applied unevenly**, so the defeated could claim double standards.
4. **It raised expectations** among peoples who were not given states (Irish, Egyptians, Indians, Arabs, Vietnamese).
:::

## Reactions of the victors and the defeated

### Germany

Germans received the terms on 7 May 1919 with shock. They had expected a peace based on the Fourteen Points, and they were presented with a treaty they had had no part in drafting, with only fifteen days to comment. Count Brockdorff-Rantzau, the head of the German delegation, answered the Allied leaders without rising from his seat, which many present took as a deliberate insult. He rejected the idea of German guilt, and he accused the Allies of continuing the food blockade of Germany after the Armistice.

The head of the government, Philipp Scheidemann, had asked the National Assembly in May what hand would not wither if it signed away Germany in such chains, and his government resigned rather than sign. The Allies threatened to resume the war within five days (later extended), the army leadership said Germany could not resist, and on 22 June the Assembly voted by 237 to 138 (with five abstentions) to accept the treaty. On 21 June, before signing, German officers had ordered the fleet interned at **Scapa Flow** to be scuttled, so that it could not be shared among the Allies; 52 of the 74 ships sank.

The signing on **28 June 1919** in the Hall of Mirrors was a bitter day for Germans. The nationalist newspaper *Deutsche Zeitung* put the single word "Vengeance!" on its front page. Germans called the treaty a ***Diktat***, a dictated peace, and the claim that Germany had been the sole cause of the war the "war-guilt lie". They also resented the loss of territory with German populations, the army limits that left Germany defenceless when its neighbours were armed, the occupation of the Rhineland and above all reparations.

A myth developed alongside the *Diktat*. Because the army had not been defeated on German soil, nationalists claimed the German army had been "stabbed in the back" by socialists, Jews and politicians at home. Field Marshal Hindenburg used a version of it when he gave evidence to a Reichstag committee on 18 November 1919. The politicians who had signed the Armistice (the "November criminals") and later the treaty were hated. The Weimar Republic (the German republic of 1919–33) was thus tied to the hated settlement from birth.

:::def Diktat
The German word for a dictated settlement. Germans used it for the Treaty of Versailles because they were not allowed to negotiate it and had to sign under threat of invasion.
:::

:::def Stab-in-the-back myth (*Dolchstoss*)
The false claim, made by nationalists after 1918, that the German army had not lost the war but had been betrayed by republicans, socialists and Jews at home. It made the Weimar politicians who signed the Armistice and the treaty targets of hatred.
:::

### France

France was the most active critic among the victors. Many French leaders, especially Foch and the president, Raymond Poincaré, said the treaty did not give France enough security. Germany was still much larger than France and its industry was intact. The Rhine was not the frontier; the Rhineland would be demilitarised and occupied only for fifteen years, and French opinion feared that after they left, the Germans would march back. Foch is reported to have said that this was not a peace but "an armistice for twenty years" (the remark is passed down by others, and the exact wording varies). Clemenceau had given way over the Rhineland in return for a promise of immediate help from Britain and the USA if Germany attacked (the Treaty of Guarantee, also signed on 28 June 1919).

That promise soon failed. When the US Senate refused to ratify the Treaty of Versailles, the American guarantee fell, and the British one, which depended on it, lapsed too. France was left with the treaty's terms, a doubtful commitment to enforce them and no allies except the new states of eastern Europe, which it began to court (an alliance with Poland in 1921, one with Czechoslovakia in 1924, and links with the Little Entente of Czechoslovakia, Romania and Yugoslavia). The French Chamber nevertheless approved the treaty by a large majority in October 1919, and in January 1920 Clemenceau lost his bid for the presidency, partly because he was thought to have been too soft.

### Britain

British opinion changed quickly. In 1918 and 1919 many wanted Germany punished. By 1920 many thought the treaty too harsh, and the British government's priority was to get trade and the Empire back to normal and to avoid a continental commitment. Several British leaders signed the treaty with misgivings: Smuts, for South Africa, did so with a statement that it was not a satisfactory peace.

The loudest critic was the economist **John Maynard Keynes**, a Treasury official at the conference, who resigned on 7 June 1919 and published *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* in December 1919. He argued that the reparations demands could not be paid and that the treaty ignored the need to rebuild Europe's economy: "If we aim deliberately at the impoverishment of Central Europe, vengeance, I dare predict, will not limp." The book was a bestseller, and it persuaded many in Britain and the USA that the treaty was a "Carthaginian peace" (a peace meant to destroy the loser, like Rome's treatment of Carthage in 146 BC). Keynes was an insider and a gifted writer, but he was also an opponent of the treaty's leaders and wrote quickly, so historians now treat his picture with caution (see "Weighing it up").

### The USA

Wilson came home to find that the treaty he had brought back was unpopular with a large part of the Senate. The Republican senator **Henry Cabot Lodge**, chairman of the Foreign Relations Committee, led a group who wanted changes. Some, "reservationists", would accept the treaty with amendments; a smaller group of "irreconcilables" opposed it entirely. Lodge told the Senate on 12 August 1919: "I have loved but one flag and I can not share that devotion and give affection to the mongrel banner invented for a league." His objection concerned especially **Article 10** of the League Covenant, which committed members to preserve one another's territorial integrity. He proposed fourteen reservations; the second said that the United States "assumes no obligation to preserve the territorial integrity or political independence of any other country" unless Congress agreed.

Wilson refused to accept the reservations. In September 1919 he began an 8,000-mile speaking tour to win public opinion, but collapsed, and on 2 October he suffered a stroke that left him partly paralysed. On **19 November 1919** the Senate voted against ratifying the treaty with the Lodge reservations (39 for, 55 against). Most Democrats, on Wilson's instructions, voted against that version, which is why it failed. A second attempt on 19 March 1920 was again short of the two-thirds majority needed (49 for, 35 against). The Republican Warren Harding won the presidency in November 1920 promising a "return to normalcy". The USA signed a separate treaty with Germany on 25 August 1921 and did not join the League.

The consequences were serious: the League lost its most powerful potential member, the guarantee to France lapsed, and the peace had no strong American support. Part of the American public felt the Europeans had cheated Wilson and wanted nothing more to do with Europe.

### Italy

Italy was the victor most angry at the outcome. It gained the Trentino, the South Tyrol up to the Brenner, Trieste and Istria, but not most of Dalmatia, not Fiume and no share of the German colonies. The Italian poet and nationalist Gabriele D'Annunzio called it a "mutilated victory" (*vittoria mutilata*). Orlando left the conference in protest on 24 April 1919 after Wilson appealed over his head to the Italian people, and he returned in early May to find the matter closed. His government fell in June. The weakness of the liberal government, and the anger of ex-soldiers, helped the rise of Benito Mussolini and his Fascists, who took power in October 1922. In September 1919 D'Annunzio seized Fiume with a band of volunteers and held it for fifteen months (see the chapter on tensions after Versailles).

### Japan

Japan came out of the conference with real gains: a place among the great powers and later a permanent seat on the League Council, the German islands north of the equator (as mandates) and Germany's economic rights in Shandong. But it felt it had been treated as a lesser power. The refusal of the racial equality clause, and the way the Western powers regarded Japanese claims, left a deep sense of grievance. In China, Japan's gain caused the May Fourth Movement and anti-Japanese boycotts. At the Washington Conference in 1921–22, under US pressure, Japan agreed to return Shandong to China (see the next chapters).

### The position of Russia

Russia was the absent power that haunted the conference. Under the **Treaty of Brest-Litovsk** (3 March 1918) the Bolsheviks had signed away Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland and Ukraine to Germany, and the Allies had condemned that treaty; Article 116 of Versailles cancelled it and left Russia free to claim reparations. But the peacemakers refused to deal with the Bolsheviks. Several of the new states (Poland, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania) were created from former Russian land, and Romania took Bessarabia. The result was that Russia's western frontier was settled without Russia. When Poland and Soviet Russia fought a war in 1920, it ended in the Treaty of Riga (March 1921) with Poland holding lands in the east that were not Polish by population. The Bolsheviks, outcasts, denounced the Versailles system as an imperialist plot and looked for friends among other outcasts (Germany, in the Treaty of Rapallo of 1922). The Allied western powers thus saw the USSR as a threat, and the USSR saw them as enemies. This is explained in the chapter on tensions after Versailles.

:::key Reactions in one line each
- **Germany**: a *Diktat* that was unjust, with a "war-guilt lie" and crushing reparations.
- **France**: too soft; no security after the USA withdrew.
- **Britain**: first satisfied, then increasingly critical (Keynes); wanted trade and calm.
- **USA**: the Senate rejected it; withdrawal from European commitments.
- **Italy**: a "mutilated victory"; Fiume.
- **Japan**: gains, but insulted over racial equality; Shandong.
- **Russia**: excluded; hostile; its borders settled without it.
:::

## Reparations

### The question of war guilt

The Allies needed a legal reason for making Germany pay. **Article 231** of the Treaty of Versailles supplied it. In the English text it says that the Allied and Associated Governments "affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." The next article, **Article 232**, admits that Germany's resources "are not adequate" to make "complete reparation" for the damage, and limits the claim to damage done to civilians and their property (later extended to pensions and allowances for soldiers).

It matters what the article actually says. The word "guilt" is not in it. It speaks of *responsibility* for *loss and damage*, and the Allied lawyers who drafted it (the young American adviser John Foster Dulles is often credited with a leading part) intended it as the legal basis for a bill that Germany could not otherwise be asked to pay. Similar clauses appeared in the treaties with Austria, Hungary and Bulgaria. But to most Germans, and to Brockdorff-Rantzau, it was a confession that Germany alone had caused the war, and the treaty's Article 227 trial of the Kaiser made that reading easy. No German government would admit it, and the "war-guilt lie" became a rallying cry for every nationalist from 1919 to 1939. The German government set up a department to attack it (the *Kriegsschuldreferat*) and encouraged historians to argue Germany's innocence. This is why the clause was so harmful: it was a legal device that Germans felt as a moral accusation, and it united Germans of all parties in rejecting the treaty.

### The size of the bill

The treaty did not fix the sum. It set up a **Reparation Commission** to report by 1 May 1921 and demanded 20 billion gold marks (cash and goods) by then. Opinion about what Germany could pay ranged enormously. The French and many British politicians wanted a very large sum. Keynes thought that the Allies had a right under the pre-Armistice promises to claim between $8 billion and $15 billion for civilian damage, and that adding soldiers' pensions had nearly doubled the bill without any such right. He argued that the wise course was to ask Germany for a fixed sum of **$10 billion** (about 2,000 million pounds) in final settlement.

The settlement came in the **London Schedule of Payments** of **5 May 1921**, announced after the Reparation Commission decided on 27 April. The total was **132 billion gold marks**, which at the pre-war gold value of about 4.2 marks to the dollar was about 31 billion dollars (a gold mark was worth about a quarter of a dollar). It was divided into three classes of bonds:

| Class of bond | Amount (billion gold marks) | What it was for |
|---|---|---|
| A | 12 | Issued at once and paid first |
| B | 38 | Issued at once and paid after A |
| C | 82 | Held back until the Allies judged that Germany could pay; in practice a paper claim |
| Total | 132 | |

Germany was to pay 2 billion gold marks a year, plus 26 per cent of the value of its exports. The Allies gave the German government an ultimatum: accept within six days or the Ruhr would be occupied. The government of Joseph Wirth accepted on **11 May 1921**.

### The issue of payment

How much could Germany actually pay, and how? There were three difficulties.
- **The transfer problem.** To pay in gold or foreign currency Germany had to export more than it imported. But the Allies did not want a flood of German goods that would hurt their own industries, and several of them raised tariffs.
- **The loss of resources.** Germany had lost its colonies, its larger merchant ships, roughly half of its iron ore and roughly a sixth of its coal through the territorial clauses, and it was left with a huge government deficit after four years of paying for the war by borrowing. The mark kept falling (the hyperinflation of 1923 belongs to the next chapters).
- **Willingness.** Germans of all parties believed reparations were unjust and that to pay was to accept guilt. German governments therefore tried every method of delay. The Allies, in turn, read German failure to pay as evidence that Germany could not be trusted, which led to the occupation of the Ruhr by France and Belgium in January 1923 (see the next chapters).

:::exam Reparations: the key facts to learn
Learn five numbers: Article 231 (guilt/responsibility), the 1 May 1921 deadline, the 132 billion gold marks of 5 May 1921, the A, B and C bonds (12, 38 and 82 billion), and the 50 billion of A and B bonds. If you can say that only about 38 per cent of the headline sum was to be paid, you can show that the bill was both a real burden and a smaller one than it looked.
:::

:::note The historians' debate: how harsh was the settlement?
- **John Maynard Keynes**, *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919): the treaty was a Carthaginian peace; the reparations were impossible to pay and would wreck Europe's economy. His view dominated British and American opinion between the wars.
- **Sally Marks**, "The Myths of Reparations" (*Central European History*, 1978): the real burden was the 50 billion of A and B bonds. The C bonds were designed to satisfy Allied publics. Germany could have paid, but chose not to, and German governments used the bill for propaganda.
- **Margaret MacMillan**, *Peacemakers* (2001, published in the USA as *Paris 1919*): the peacemakers worked under impossible pressures and did their best; the treaty was not the cause of the Second World War, which came from decisions taken later in the 1930s.
- **Zara Steiner**, *The Lights that Failed* (2005): a long history of European international relations from 1919 to 1933 that treats the settlement as the start of an unstable order whose fate depended on later choices.
At AS you do not need to cite historians; you do need an argument. Using the idea that "Keynes's picture has been challenged" is enough to show nuance.
:::

## Why was there such extensive dissatisfaction? Weighing it up

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then compare.

| Source of dissatisfaction | Who felt it | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Treaty terms felt too harsh (territory, army, reparations, guilt) | Germany, Hungary, Austria, Bulgaria | Fed revisionism and made the new republics unpopular |
| Treaty terms felt too weak (no Rhine frontier, no firm guarantee) | France | Made France insist on strict enforcement |
| Promises unfulfilled | Italy, Japan, China | Italy's "mutilated victory", Japan's racial equality, China's Shandong |
| Inconsistent self-determination, new minorities | Germans, Hungarians, Slovaks, Ukrainians, Austrians | Made the new states unstable and gave later leaders a pretext for demanding change |
| Process: imposed, not negotiated; excluded Russia | Germany and Russia, small states | Gave the treaties little legitimacy |
| American withdrawal | France, the League | Removed the guarantor of the peace |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The settlement was a compromise between three leaders with different aims, made in six months under great pressure, and a compromise satisfies nobody: it was harsh enough for Germany to resent and not firm enough for France to feel safe. The most serious cause of dissatisfaction was the one that affected the strongest of the defeated powers, Germany: the combination of guilt, reparations and territorial loss, which it could blame for its troubles. But the settlement might still have worked if the USA had stayed involved; its withdrawal turned dissatisfaction into danger.
:::

Four points help you judge "how harsh was it?":
1. **Compared with what the Allies could have done**, it was moderate. Germany remained united (Foch wanted the Rhineland separated) and kept most of its industry; its losses of land were mostly lands with Polish, Danish or French populations.
2. **Compared with what Germany had done**, Brest-Litovsk (1918) took from Russia about a third of its population and most of its coal.
3. **Compared with what Germans expected**, it was a shock: they thought they had agreed to the Fourteen Points.
4. **Compared with what the victors could enforce**, it was hard: the treaty needed a strong coalition to keep it, and the coalition fell apart.

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Germany objected to the Treaty of Versailles.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Germany objected to the Treaty of Versailles because it lost land, including Alsace-Lorraine and the Polish Corridor. Germany had to pay reparations of 132 billion gold marks. The army was cut to 100,000 men. Germany had to accept Article 231, which said it was to blame for the war. Germany also lost its colonies."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it only lists terms. It never says why any of these made Germany object. That is a description of the treaty, not an explanation of a reaction. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main objection was to Article 231. The Germans read it as an admission that Germany alone had caused the war, which they denied: Count Brockdorff-Rantzau told the Allies on 7 May 1919 that it would be a lie. Every German party opposed it because accepting guilt seemed to justify everything else in the treaty, including reparations. A second reason was the way the treaty was made. The Germans had asked for an armistice expecting a peace on the Fourteen Points, but they were not allowed to take part in the negotiations and were given the finished terms with fifteen days to reply. They called it a *Diktat*. The scuttling of the fleet at Scapa Flow on 21 June and the resignation of the Scheidemann government rather than sign show how deep the feeling was."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was the material cost. Germany lost about 13 per cent of its European land, including Posen and West Prussia, which created a Polish Corridor that cut East Prussia from the rest of Germany. It also lost its colonies and its army was cut to 100,000 men while its neighbours kept large armies. The reparations bill, fixed at 132 billion gold marks in 1921, seemed to Germans to be a burden that would last for generations. These terms hurt, but a defeated country expects to pay a price.

These factors were linked. Germans might have borne the territorial and military terms as the price of defeat, but Article 231 turned them into evidence of guilt, and the lack of negotiation made it impossible to argue. The war-guilt clause was also the legal basis for reparations, so the part that hurt most financially was also the part that hurt most in honour. The main reason Germany objected was therefore the way in which the treaty told Germans that they had been wrong and then took the means of putting the matter right: guilt, imposed without discussion, justified everything else."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the manner of the treaty and Article 231 made the material terms unbearable), and a supported conclusion about the main reason. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that Germany accepts responsibility for the loss and damage. Source B says that Germany does not accept that it was the only one guilty. So they disagree. Source A is part of the treaty and Source B is a speech by a German. Source B is biased because he is German."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share some common ground: both are about the same issue, Germany's responsibility for the war, and both treat it as the central question. They also share a vocabulary of causes: Source A speaks of 'aggression' by Germany, and Source B answers directly by claiming that Germans believed they were fighting 'a war of defense'. Neither source discusses the war's actual causes in detail; both are statements of position.

On the question of what Germany should accept, however, they are directly opposed. Source A states that Germany 'accepts' responsibility and describes the war as 'imposed' on the Allies by German 'aggression'. Source B says that to 'confess' to being 'the only ones guilty' would be 'a lie', and it denies that Germany was 'alone' guilty. There is also a difference in what is claimed. Source A is about responsibility for 'loss and damage', a financial and legal claim. Source B answers as if it were a charge of guilt, a moral claim, and so partly talks past it.

Provenance and context explain these differences. Source A is part of a treaty written by the Allies, in the legal language of the commission on reparations, as the basis for a bill; it does not use the word 'guilt'. Its purpose was to justify reparations, and it was drafted in the knowledge that the Germans would sign under threat of invasion. Source B is a speech made on 7 May 1919, the day the Germans first saw the terms. Brockdorff-Rantzau was addressing the Allied leaders and, equally, the German public, and he chose to answer as a proud defender of German honour: he spoke without rising, which the Allied leaders took as an insult. So his choice to read Article 231 as an accusation of guilt is partly an attitude taken for effect. As evidence about war guilt in 1919 the sources are more useful together: A shows what the Allies officially claimed and why, B shows how Germans took it, and the gap between them explains the lasting dispute."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through the nature, origin and purpose of the sources and the context of the signing; and a final judgement of the sources together as evidence. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The peace settlements of 1919–20 were a fair compromise.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: a compromise between Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George (Foch's Rhine frontier refused; no partition of Germany; the League included; the sum of reparations left open until 1921); Germany lost mostly land with non-German populations; Germany had imposed a harsher peace on Russia at Brest-Litovsk.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The peace settlements were a fair compromise. Clemenceau wanted to take the Rhineland and dismember Germany, but Wilson and Lloyd George stopped him, and he settled for a demilitarised Rhineland and occupation for fifteen years. Wilson wanted a League of Nations and got it, and Lloyd George kept the British navy's freedom of the seas. Germany was made to pay reparations but the sum was not fixed until 1921, and Germany lost only 13 per cent of its land. So the treaties were fair compromises between the Allies."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than argues: a compromise between the victors is not the same as a fair settlement for all. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with the arguments against merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet a compromise among the victors is not a fair settlement, and the treaties were unfair in both their making and their effects. In the making, the defeated were excluded: Germany was handed the finished treaty on 7 May 1919 and given fifteen days to comment, and the Germans had understood that the Armistice had been agreed on the basis of the Fourteen Points. In the terms, the principle of self-determination was applied unevenly: the Austrian assembly's declaration of November 1918 that Austria was part of Germany was overridden by Article 80 of Versailles, three million Sudeten Germans were placed in Czechoslovakia without a vote, and about three million Hungarians were left in neighbouring states by the Treaty of Trianon, while Poles, Danes and French were given the benefit of the principle. Article 231 meant that the settlement was founded on a charge that Germans refused to accept, and for them it deprived all the other terms of legitimacy.

Nor was the compromise fair to the victors. France gave up the Rhine frontier and was promised an Anglo-American guarantee, which lapsed when the US Senate voted against ratification on 19 November 1919. Italy received less than it had been promised in 1915 and called the result a 'mutilated victory'. Japan's proposal on racial equality was defeated even though a majority of the commission voted for it. A settlement in which every victor was disappointed and all the defeated were resentful was not a balance between fair claims but a fragile bargain.

Overall, the settlements were a compromise but not a fair one. The weight of the argument lies with the opposing view, because fairness has to be judged by whether the people affected could regard the outcome as just and whether it could last. Compared with Brest-Litovsk, Versailles was not savage, and the Allies were right that Germany could pay the A and B bonds. But the way it was made and the unequal application of its principles meant that a settlement that was moderate in its terms was seen as unjust by the strongest of the defeated powers, and it fell apart when the USA withdrew."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (acceptable to those affected, and lasting) and shows what the argument for the statement gets right. With an opening paragraph on the case for compromise, this is Level 5 (18–20). Note that "compromise" (a process among the victors) and "fair" (an outcome for all) are different ideas: separating them is itself a mark of a strong answer.

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1919; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly and separates harshness as perceived from harshness as fact. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "'The Treaty of Versailles was too harsh on Germany.' How far do you agree?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 in the Hall of Mirrors. Germany lost Alsace-Lorraine to France, Eupen-Malmedy to Belgium and North Schleswig to Denmark, and Posen and West Prussia to Poland. The Saar was put under the League of Nations for fifteen years. Germany's army was limited to 100,000 men and it was not allowed to have tanks, submarines or an air force. Germany lost its colonies. Article 231 said Germany was responsible for the war and had to pay reparations of 132 billion gold marks. So the treaty was very harsh on Germany."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **description**: it lists the terms and ends with an assertion. It never says why any term was harsh, it never considers the other side (that Germany was not partitioned, that the Allies' terms were milder than Brest-Litovsk, that the real reparations bill was 50 billion) and it makes no comparison. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description, few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, choose the terms that prove it, then give the counter-argument. "The treaty was harsh in how it was imposed and in the burden of Article 231, but not in the material terms. Germans objected most to Article 231 because they read it as an admission of guilt and it was the basis for the reparations bill; and they were given the terms to sign without negotiating. In material terms, however, Germany lost 13 per cent of its European territory, mostly lands with non-German populations, and kept its unity and industrial base, while the real reparations burden, the A and B bonds, was 50 billion gold marks, not 132. The treaty was therefore harsh in its method and its moral message, but less so in what it took." Now each fact serves a judgement.

Q: Who were the Big Three and what did each want most?
A: Wilson (USA): a just peace based on the Fourteen Points and a League of Nations. Clemenceau (France): security from Germany and reparations. Lloyd George (Britain): a settlement Germany could live with, keeping Britain's naval and imperial position, and a balance of power in Europe.

A: Germany and the other defeated powers, and Russia. The terms were handed over for signature, so the defeated called them a Diktat, and the absence of Russia left its western frontier settled without it.
Q: Give five terms of the Treaty of Versailles.
A: For example: loss of 13 per cent of territory (Alsace-Lorraine to France, Posen and West Prussia to Poland); loss of colonies as mandates; army limited to 100,000, no tanks or air force; Rhineland demilitarised and occupied; Article 231 and reparations.
Q: What did the Treaty of Trianon do to Hungary?
A: It took about two thirds of Hungary's territory and more than half its people; about three million Hungarians were left in neighbouring states (Slovakia and Ruthenia to Czechoslovakia, Transylvania to Romania, Croatia and the Vojvodina to Yugoslavia).
Q: Why was the Treaty of Sèvres replaced?
A: Turkish nationalists under Mustafa Kemal rejected it and defeated the Greeks in 1922; the Treaty of Lausanne (24 July 1923) gave Turkey Eastern Thrace and Smyrna and dropped the plans for Armenia and Kurdistan.
Q: Give three examples of inconsistent application of national self-determination.
A: Austria forbidden to unite with Germany (Article 80); Sudeten Germans put in Czechoslovakia without a vote; South Tyrol's Germans handed to Italy; Danzig; Hungarians left in Romania and Czechoslovakia. Plebiscites were held only in a few places such as North Schleswig and Upper Silesia.
Q: Why did the US Senate reject the Treaty of Versailles?
A: Lodge and other Republicans objected to Article 10 of the Covenant, which they thought could commit the USA to war without Congress's consent. Wilson refused to accept the reservations and the treaty failed twice (19 November 1919 and 19 March 1920).
Q: What does Article 231 actually say, and why did it cause trouble?
A: That Germany and her allies accepted responsibility for causing all the loss and damage suffered by the Allies as a result of the war imposed by their aggression. It was a legal basis for reparations, but Germans read it as an admission of guilt and called it the "war-guilt lie".
Q: What were the London Schedule of Payments and the A, B and C bonds?
A: On 5 May 1921 the Allies fixed reparations at 132 billion gold marks in three classes of bonds: A (12 billion), B (38 billion) and C (82 billion). Only A and B (50 billion) were to be paid in the foreseeable future; C was a paper claim.
Q: What was the "stab-in-the-back" myth?
A: The claim that the German army had not been defeated but was betrayed by politicians and others at home; it made the republic's leaders targets of hatred.

The settlement was neither a Carthaginian peace nor an effective containment. This fits MacMillan's argument that the peacemakers could not be blamed for what others later did.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ John Maynard Keynes (Source D in the worked example) was a member of the British delegation. How far does this make his book reliable evidence about the Treaty of Versailles?
H: What did he know that outsiders did not? What were his reasons for writing the book?
H: How did he treat Wilson, Clemenceau and Lloyd George, and what have historians said about his estimate of Germany's capacity to pay?
A: Strengths: an expert with inside knowledge of the Treasury and the reparations discussions; his main figures were carefully worked out; his argument that Europe's economies were linked was original and he resigned in protest on 7 June 1919, so he had acted on his views. Weaknesses: he wrote angrily within months, as a participant who had lost the argument; his portraits of the leaders are vivid and unfair (Wilson as a gullible idealist); he based his view that Germany could pay only about $10 billion on assumptions that later work has questioned; the book was meant to persuade and succeeded, so it shaped British and American opinion rather than merely recording it. Judgement: reliable evidence of what an informed British critic thought in 1919 and of how the treaty was seen, less reliable on what Germany could in fact pay.
:::

:::recap
- **Conference**: opened 18 January 1919; Council of Ten, then Council of Four from 24 March; Germany, her allies and Russia excluded; terms handed to Germany on 7 May.
- **Aims**: Wilson (Fourteen Points, self-determination, League), Clemenceau (security, reparations), Lloyd George (compromise, trade, balance), Orlando (Treaty of London, Fiume); Japan (racial equality, Shandong).
- **Treaties**: Versailles (28 June 1919), Saint-Germain (10 September 1919), Neuilly (27 November 1919), Trianon (4 June 1920), Sèvres (10 August 1920; replaced by Lausanne, 24 July 1923).
- **Versailles**: 13 per cent of territory, colonies as mandates, army 100,000, Rhineland demilitarised and occupied, Anschluss banned, Article 231, reparations.
- **Difficulties**: new minorities (Sudeten Germans, Hungarians in Romania and Czechoslovakia, South Tyrol); self-determination applied unevenly.
- **Reactions**: Germany (*Diktat*, war-guilt lie, stab in the back); France (too soft, guarantee lapsed); Britain (Keynes); USA (Senate votes 19 November 1919 and 19 March 1920); Italy (mutilated victory); Japan (racial equality, Shandong); Russia (excluded).
- **Reparations**: Article 231; 132 billion gold marks (5 May 1921) in A (12), B (38) and C (82) bonds; real burden 50 billion; transfer problem.
- **Judgement**: a compromise among the victors that satisfied none of them, resented most by the strongest of the defeated, and fatally weakened by the USA's withdrawal.
