**Paper 1 (Historical Sources)**: 1 hour 15 minutes, 40 marks, **one two-part question** from the one option you studied. It carries 40 per cent of the AS marks (20 per cent of an A Level), and its marks are 75 per cent AO3 (sources) and 25 per cent AO1 (knowledge). There are **four sources, A to D**: at least three are written, up to one is visual (a cartoon, photograph or poster), and together they run to at most 600 words. Each ends with its provenance in italics.
- **(a) [15]**: "Read Sources A and B. Compare these two sources as evidence about …"
- **(b) [25]**: "Read all of the sources. How far do these sources agree that …?" (or a quoted view followed by "How far do these sources support this view?", or "To what extent …?")

Both parts are answered with reference to the sources; part (b) also needs your own knowledge.

:::try Warm-up
Q: What are the four questions you ask of every source, and what is the fifth? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))
A: Nature (what kind of document), origin (who made it, when and where), purpose (why, and for whom), and context (what was happening). The fifth is content: what it says, read against the question you were asked.
Q: A friend says "this leaflet is biased, so it is no use". What is wrong with that? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))
A: A biased source can be excellent evidence of what its author wanted people to believe. "Biased" starts the evaluation; it does not finish it. You must say whose view it is, what it is evidence of, and what it leaves out.
Q: What share of Paper 1 is AO3, and what does AO3 test? (from [History: the course map](#hist.overview))
A: 75 per cent. AO3 is the understanding, analysis, evaluation and interpretation of historical sources in context.
:::

## Paper 1 at a glance

**Four sources, two questions, 75 minutes.** Everything in this chapter is about doing four things well.

| Skill | What it means | Where it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Reading | Understanding what a source says, suggests and leaves out | (a) and (b) |
| Evaluating | Judging how strong it is as evidence **for this question**, from nature, origin, purpose and context | (a) and (b) |
| Comparing | Finding what two sources share and where they differ, and explaining why | (a) |
| Judging | Weighing four sources and your knowledge to answer "how far" | (b) |

**A suggested time plan** (a guide for practice, not a rule of the paper): about 5 minutes reading all four sources and the question; about 25 minutes on (a); about 45 minutes on (b). Marks per minute are almost the same in both parts (15 marks in 25 minutes, 25 marks in 45), so a student who spends 40 minutes on (a) has nothing left for the bigger part.

Examiners mark with two tables. Read them now, because every skill below is aimed at moving you up one of them.

| Part (a) [15] | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| Level 4 | 12–15 | A **developed comparison**: explains why the points of similarity and difference exist, through context and/or evaluation of the sources |
| Level 3 | 8–11 | Identifies similarities **and** differences, supported with source content |
| Level 2 | 4–7 | Similarities **or** differences (one-sided), or both asserted without support |
| Level 1 | 1–3 | Describes each source; very simple comparisons ("one is a letter, one is a speech") |

| Part (b) [25] | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| Level 5 | 21–25 | **Evaluates** the sources to reach a judgement: uses contextual knowledge to decide whether each is stronger or weaker as evidence, and judges which side is stronger, addressing "how far" |
| Level 4 | 16–20 | Developed explanation of how the sources support and challenge; detailed use of all four; explicit links to the question; context used to interpret them |
| Level 3 | 11–15 | Uses sources on both sides, but links are implicit and comments on bias or reliability are **not based on contextual understanding** |
| Level 2 | 6–10 | Valid points on one side only, or an attempt at both with one side misread, or "all the sources agree" |
| Level 1 | 1–5 | Describes content, or writes an essay on the topic with the sources as illustration |

Notice the wording at Level 3 of part (b): comments on bias or reliability that are not based on contextual understanding. That one phrase explains most of what follows.

## Reading a source closely

Before you judge a source, you must understand it. Most weak answers misread, or half-read, one source in four. Read every source three times, with a pencil.

1. **What does it say?** Put the message in your own words in one line, in terms of the question.
2. **What does it suggest?** An **inference** is something the source shows without saying it outright.
3. **What does it leave out?** The silences are evidence too.

:::def Inference
A conclusion you draw from a source that it does not state directly, supported by its words and its context. "Bismarck is speaking to men he expects to oppose him" is an inference from a speech; it is not written in the speech.
:::

Here is a short extract to practise on.

**Source**

… the great questions of the time will not be decided by speeches and majority decisions … but by iron and blood.

*Adapted from a speech by Otto von Bismarck, Minister-President of Prussia, to the budget committee of the Prussian House of Representatives (the elected lower house of the Prussian parliament), 30 September 1862. The committee had refused the money the government wanted for the army.*

**Says.** Political questions will be settled by force and not by votes and debate.

**Suggests.** Bismarck does not think parliament should settle the great questions; that he expects his listeners to disagree (he is using a shocking phrase on purpose); and that he is threatening, since he is the head of the government speaking to a committee that had refused money for the army (the provenance line tells you this). The suggestion comes from the words "speeches and majority decisions" and from the setting, a committee that had already refused him.

**Leaves out.** Which "great questions" he means, and how he will act. It never mentions unification. It does not say he will govern without a budget, though he did. A student who writes "Bismarck says he will unify Germany by war in this speech" has read something into the source that it does not contain. This is the commonest misreading in Paper 1.

### Tone and language

Words carry attitudes. When a source is persuasive, the choice of words is part of the message. Look for:
- **Loaded words** that judge while they describe ("stolen", "sullen", "disgraced").
- **Metaphors**: a "place in the sun", a "burden", an "iron curtain". A metaphor presents an opinion as if it were a picture.
- **Strength of verbs**: "we demand", "we regret", "we shall consider". "Must" and "will" are the language of command; "hope" and "trust" are the language of appeal.
- **Who is "we" and who is "they"?** A speech that says "we" includes its audience on its side.
- **Register**: legal and official language ("is hereby declared to be illegal") is cool and exact; a poem or a platform is emotional.

### Silences and omissions

What a source leaves out can matter as much as what it says. The Munich Agreement of 29 September 1938, signed by Germany, the United Kingdom, France and Italy, regulates the handing over of the Sudeten German territory of Czechoslovakia. Its text says "Germany, the United Kingdom, France and Italy" and lists the dates of evacuation. It does not say that Czechoslovakia was not at the conference table. You know that from other evidence: Czechoslovak representatives were not admitted to the talks and were told the result afterwards. A good answer notices the silence and uses it: the document shows what four powers decided **about** Czechoslovakia, not what Czechoslovakia agreed.

:::warn Do not read in more than is there
Three common errors: treating a mood as a fact ("the crowd was furious" from one angry sentence); treating one person's words as everyone's ("the Americans feared trusts" from one platform); and answering from the topic instead of the source ("the Treaty of Versailles was harsh" when the extract is about reparations in 1921). If a point is not in the source, it belongs to your **knowledge**, and you must say so.
:::

## Provenance: nature, origin and purpose

The provenance line in italics under each source is the examiner's gift. It tells you who made the source and when, and it is there so that you will use it. [History from zero](#hist.f.basics) taught the four questions. This section shows how they work together, because the best evaluations make a **chain**: this is the kind of source, made by this person, at this time, for this audience and purpose, so we should expect it to be strong for X and weak for Y.

### Nature: what kind of source is it?

Each kind has typical strengths and limits. These are only starting points, never conclusions.

| Nature | Usually good for | Usual limits |
|---|---|---|
| Law, treaty, decree | What the authorities decided and intended | Not whether it was obeyed, enforced or accepted; says little about reasons |
| Public speech | The position a leader wanted to be seen to take; the arguments used on a particular audience | Written for effect; shaped by the audience; may have been polished before it was printed |
| Private diary or letter | Candid thoughts, written for oneself or one reader | One person's partial view; may be self-justifying; may have been edited when it was published later |
| Official report, statistics | Wide coverage, apparent precision | Chosen and collected for the compiler's purposes; what is counted and what is left out |
| Newspaper, pamphlet, platform | What a group wanted the public to think and feel; the arguments in circulation | Owned, written and funded by someone with an aim |
| Memoir, later account | The author's view in hindsight | Memory fades; the author defends a reputation; knows how things turned out |
| Cartoon, poster | The message a maker hoped a public would understand at a glance; shared symbols and fears | Exaggerates and simplifies on purpose |
| Photograph | What was in front of the lens | What was left out of the frame; staging, selection, captions |
| Poem, song | Mood, values, the words people liked | Not a record of events; emotional |

### Origin: who, when, where, and how close?

Ask three things. **Who** made it, and what did they know and want? **When**: before, during or after the events, and with what hindsight? **Where**: inside the country, at the centre of power or at the edge, or an outsider looking in? Closeness to events gives knowledge but also an interest. A minister knows what the government intended and has every reason to explain it well. A foreign visitor sees the street but not the council chamber.

### Purpose and audience

Why was it made, and for whom? The same fact appears in a different light when told to a different audience. A government statement to its own parliament is not the same as the same government's note to a foreign power. Keep these in mind: **persuade**, **justify** (defend one's own actions), **protest or demand**, **command**, **record or inform**, **mock**.

:::def Audience
The person or group a source was made for. The audience shapes what is said, what is left out and how it is put, so it is a central part of purpose.
:::

### "As evidence" means "as evidence of what?"

Examiners write "as evidence about …" on purpose. A source is not strong or weak on its own; it is strong or weak **for one question**.

:::def Stronger or weaker evidence
A judgement, made for a particular question, about how far a source can be trusted to show what the question asks about, based on its nature, origin, purpose and context, and tested against other evidence. The same source can be strong evidence for one question and weak for another.
:::

:::eg The same source, two questions
**Source**

Take up the White Man's burden— / Send forth the best ye breed— / Go bind your sons to exile / To serve your captives' need; / To wait in heavy harness / On fluttered folk and wild— / Your new-caught, sullen peoples, / Half-devil and half-child.

*From "The White Man's Burden", a poem by Rudyard Kipling, the British writer, first published in McClure's Magazine (New York), February 1899, with the subtitle "The United States and the Philippine Islands".*

**Question 1.** How useful is this as evidence of the way many Europeans and Americans justified empire in the 1890s?

*Answer.* The source is a poem (nature) by one of the best-known writers in the English-speaking world (origin), published for a mass magazine audience (purpose: to persuade). Its message is that ruling other peoples is a **duty** and a sacrifice, and its language, "burden", "serve", "exile", presents conquest as unselfish service. The words "sullen", "half-devil and half-child" show the assumption that the peoples being ruled were inferior and needed guidance. That is strong evidence of one popular justification, the idea of a civilising mission, because a poem aimed at a mass audience works only if its readers already half-believe it. Kipling's fame and the poem's wide reprinting show that. It is evidence of the language of justification, not of the full range of motives.

**Question 2.** How useful is it as evidence of why the United States took the Philippines in 1898–99?

*Answer.* Here it is much weaker. Kipling was a British poet, not an American decision-maker, and the poem is an **appeal** to Americans, not a record of their reasons. It tells you what one outsider hoped the Americans would feel, and the poem's popularity shows that the idea appealed to some Americans. But the decision had commercial and strategic motives (a base in the Pacific, a stepping stone to China) that a poem about duty ignores. For this question the source is best used as evidence of the arguments in circulation, with other sources for the actual motives.

*Lesson.* The poem did not change. The question did, and the verdict changed with it.
:::

### Why "bias" earns so little

Level 3 of part (b) is the home of "this source is biased". The examiners' wording is exact: the comment is not based on contextual understanding. Turn every "bias" comment into three moves.

1. **Whose view is this, and in what position?** (origin)
2. **What would that person want the reader to think, and what would they leave out?** (purpose, with context)
3. **So what does it follow for THIS question?** It is strong evidence of X, and weak evidence of Y.

| Weak comment | Strong comment |
|---|---|
| "Carnegie was rich, so he is biased." | "Carnegie was the head of the largest steel company writing to defend great fortunes, so his essay is strong evidence of how a successful business leader wished to be seen, but not of what business leaders generally did with their money." |
| "The Populists were angry, so it is exaggerated." | "The platform was written to unite farmers and workers at the new party's first national convention, so its word 'stolen' is rhetoric; but a party whose candidate won over a million votes in 1892 shows that the anger was widespread, which makes it strong evidence of discontent." |
| "A cartoon is only an opinion." | "The cartoon appeared in a magazine that campaigned against trusts, so it exaggerates on purpose; it is good evidence of what critics said, and its picture of a Senate under the control of business matches the fact that senators were then chosen by state legislatures that business could influence." |
| "It is a speech, so it is unreliable." | "Hoover was a candidate speaking two weeks before an election, so he stressed the American way and attacked a European one to win votes; the speech is excellent evidence of the philosophy he campaigned on." |

The second column of every row names a position, a likely emphasis, a purpose and a verdict about use. That is what "evaluate" means.

### Reliability, utility and corroboration

Two words that sound alike. A source is **reliable** when what it says can be trusted as true; it is **useful** when it helps with a question. A false statement can be useful: a propaganda lie tells you what a regime wanted people to believe. A true statement can be useless for the question set. In the exam, think about use first.

:::def Corroboration
Checking a source against another source or against secure knowledge. If two sources of different kinds and origins say the same thing, the case is stronger; if they conflict, you ask why, using provenance. Examiners call this cross-referencing.
:::

## Context: using your own knowledge

Examiners want your own knowledge **to interpret the sources**. In part (b) the mark scheme speaks of "specific contextual knowledge used to evaluate" the sources. Knowledge does three jobs.

1. **Place the source.** When was it made, and what was happening? Kipling's poem appeared in February 1899, when the United States Senate was about to vote on the Treaty of Paris. The treaty, signed on 10 December 1898, ended the war with Spain and gave the Philippines to the United States for a payment of $20 million. Fighting between American troops and Filipino forces broke out on 4 February 1899, and the Senate approved the treaty on 6 February 1899 by 57 votes to 27, one more than the two-thirds it needed. That context shows the poem is not a general reflection; it is a contribution to a live political argument.
2. **Test the source.** Does what you know support what it says, or contradict it? Corroboration is the best test.
3. **Fill the gap.** What does the source not tell you that the question needs? In part (b) an answer that only repeats the sources cannot reach the top levels.

:::exam Knowledge is not an essay
A very common error at Level 1 and 2 is to write an essay on the topic and use the sources as illustrations. Always start from a source and say what the knowledge does to it: "this is supported by", "this is challenged by", "this adds that …".
:::

## Visual sources: cartoons, photographs and posters

At most one source in four is visual. A cartoon or poster is a **message** designed to be understood quickly; a photograph is a **record** that was framed by someone. Use this checklist.

:::steps
1. **Read the words.** Title, caption, labels, speech, the name of the publication and its date.
2. **Name the symbols.** Who or what does each figure, animal or object stand for? Is the symbol one the audience of the time would recognise?
3. **Notice scale and position.** What is large, what is small, who is above or below?
4. **Notice how people are drawn.** Caricature (exaggerated features) tells you the maker's attitude.
5. **Ask who published it, and for whom.** A magazine has readers and a line of its own.
6. **Say what it leaves out** and what a rival publication might have drawn.
:::

:::eg A cartoon read step by step
*"The Rhodes Colossus: Striding from Cape Town to Cairo", a cartoon by Edward Linley Sambourne in Punch, a London weekly humorous magazine, 10 December 1892. (A visual source is described in words in the exam booklet; here it is described in the same way.)* Cecil Rhodes, shown as a giant, stands with one foot at Cape Town and the other at Cairo, holding a line of telegraph wire that stretches across the whole of Africa.

*Words.* The title tells you the subject (Rhodes, a British businessman and politician in southern Africa) and the idea (he spans a continent). The publication is Punch, a magazine for well-off British readers, in 1892.

*Symbols.* A **colossus** is a statue taller than anything around it; the title recalls the ancient Colossus of Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders of the world, which gives the pun "Rhodes Colossus". It tells you that the artist sees Rhodes as huge, important and perhaps a little absurd. The telegraph wire represents Rhodes's plan to link Africa from south to north by telegraph, and, by extension, to link it by British influence.

*Scale.* Rhodes towers over the continent, and Africa and its peoples are tiny: the continent is a stage for one man's ambition.

*What a historian can say.* It is strong evidence of the **image** of Rhodes and of imperial ambition in Britain in 1892: a magazine read by the middle and upper classes found a link from the Cape to Cairo exciting enough to make a joke of. It says nothing about what Africans felt, and it cannot show whether the plan worked.
:::

**Photographs.** A photograph shows what was in front of the lens, but somebody chose where to stand, when to press the shutter, what to crop and how to caption it. Ask what is outside the frame. The best-known photograph of the end of the Second World War in Europe, the Soviet flag over the Reichstag in Berlin, was taken by Yevgeny Khaldei on 2 May 1945 with soldiers who posed for the camera, and he later altered the print. A source can be a real photograph of a real day and still be a **staged** one. **Posters** are made to be read at a glance, so they simplify. Their purpose (recruiting, selling, warning) is usually written on their face.

## Part (a): comparing two sources [15]

**The task.** Compare two sources as evidence about an issue, saying what is similar, what is different and **why**. Level 4 requires the explanation: the context or provenance of each source accounts for the points of similarity and difference.

:::key The method for part (a)
1. **Fix the issue.** Underline what the question says ("as evidence about the justifications for empire").
2. **Make a grid** of two or three **themes** that matter for that issue, with a row for each source.
3. **Find the similarities as well as the differences.** Many students look only for differences.
4. **Compare by theme, not source by source.** Write "Both sources …", "However, whereas A …, B …". An answer that describes A and then B has not compared them.
5. **Explain.** For each main point of similarity or difference say why, using provenance (nature, origin, purpose) and context.
6. **Finish with a short verdict** on which is the stronger evidence **for the issue named** and why.
:::

Use quotations, kept short, to prove each point. Do not copy the sources out.

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

… the times when the German left the land to one of his neighbours, the sea to the other, and kept for himself the heavens, where pure doctrine reigns, those times are over. We regard it as one of our foremost tasks to promote and cultivate the interests of our shipping, our trade and our industry, in East Asia in particular. … In a word: we do not wish to put anyone in the shade, but we also demand our place in the sun.

*Adapted from a speech in the Reichstag by Bernhard von Bülow, German Foreign Secretary, 6 December 1897, shortened and translated for this course from the German text in Fürst Bülows Reden (1907). He was replying to a deputy who feared that Germany was drifting into a risky adventure in East Asia, a few weeks after German ships occupied Kiautschou Bay in China.*

**Source B**

Take up the White Man's burden— / Send forth the best ye breed— / Go bind your sons to exile / To serve your captives' need; / To wait in heavy harness / On fluttered folk and wild— / Your new-caught, sullen peoples, / Half-devil and half-child.

*From "The White Man's Burden", a poem by Rudyard Kipling, published in McClure's Magazine (New York), February 1899, with the subtitle "The United States and the Philippine Islands".*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the justifications given for acquiring colonies at the end of the nineteenth century.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities or differences only, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): both, supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that explains why they agree and differ.

**Plan (about 3 minutes).**
- Theme 1, what is claimed: A, a right and an interest (a "place in the sun", trade and shipping); B, a duty and a sacrifice ("burden", "serve").
- Theme 2, how other peoples are seen: A, not mentioned at all; B, central, and condescending.
- Theme 3, what they share: both treat expansion as something a great power should do and speak confidently for their nation.
- Explain with: A, official speech to a parliament after a crisis; B, a poem, addressed to Americans, as the Senate decided whether to take the Philippines.

**A Level 2 answer (about 5 marks).** "Source A says that Germany wants a place in the sun and wants to promote its trade in East Asia. Source B says that white people have a burden to look after other peoples. So A is about trade and B is about helping people. They are different because A is a speech and B is a poem. A is a speech by Bülow and B is a poem by Kipling."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarities. The only explanation is that the sources are different kinds, a Level 1 observation that does not use the provenance to explain anything. **Level 2.**

**A Level 3 answer (about 10 marks).** "Both sources justify taking control overseas and speak confidently for their nation. Source A says Germany must 'promote and cultivate the interests of our shipping, our trade and our industry, in East Asia in particular' and demands 'our place in the sun'. Source B tells Americans to 'take up the White Man's burden'. However, they give very different reasons. In A the reasons are national interest and status: Germany wants what other powers already have. In B the reason is duty: the peoples are 'half-devil and half-child' and need to be served by those who are better. A does not mention the peoples being ruled at all, but B is mainly about them. So the sources agree that expansion is right but disagree about why."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations. But the answer only says **that** they differ; it does not say **why**. That is the step from Level 3 to Level 4.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"Both sources justify expansion and both write with the confidence of a great power: Bülow 'demands' a place in the sun, and Kipling tells his readers to 'take up' a burden as if the decision were a matter of course. Neither suggests that the people who would be ruled should be asked.

The central difference is the **reason** given. Bülow's is national advantage and equality of status: German 'shipping, trade and industry' must not be shut out of East Asia, and Germany must not be left, as in the past, with 'the heavens' while others take the land and the sea. Kipling's is moral duty: ruling is a 'burden', a sacrifice made 'to serve your captives' need'. A second difference is how the ruled are treated. Bülow never mentions the Chinese; Kipling describes the Filipinos at length, but as 'sullen' and 'half-devil and half-child', a picture of people to be managed.

The provenance explains both differences. Source A is an official speech by a minister to the Reichstag, made weeks after German ships had seized Kiautschou Bay, and in reply to a deputy who feared an adventure. Bülow's purpose was practical: to reassure the Reichstag that the action served German interests, so he talks in terms of trade, shipping and rights, the language of national interest. Source B is a poem published in an American magazine in February 1899, in the days before the Senate voted on the treaty giving the Philippines to the United States. Kipling was a British writer, not a decision-maker, and his purpose was to persuade Americans that taking the islands was a duty, not a greedy act. A poem works by moral appeal, which is why it speaks of burden and service and not of markets.

As evidence of the **justifications** for empire, therefore, the sources are complementary. A is the stronger evidence of the arguments used by a government to defend a specific action, because it comes from the minister responsible and was delivered to the body that had to approve. B is stronger evidence of the language of 'civilising mission', which was often used alongside the language of interest, though it is much weaker evidence of what actually moved governments, since Kipling's own country had no say in American policy."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, supported by quotations; **both explained through nature, origin, purpose and context** (a minister's speech after a crisis; a poem aimed at an American audience before a vote); and a closing comparison of what each source is good evidence **of**. A **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::warn Five ways to lose marks in part (a)
1. **Describing A, then B**, with no links between them.
2. **Differences only.** Every pair of sources shares something; look for it.
3. **Listing provenance** ("A is a speech, B is a poem") without saying what difference it makes.
4. **Comparing the topic** instead of comparing the sources "as evidence about" the issue named.
5. **Overlong writing.** Fifteen marks need about 350 to 450 words; four paragraphs, each a theme, is plenty.
:::

## Part (b): using four sources and your own knowledge [25]

**The task.** Decide how far four sources support or challenge a claim, and reach a judgement about "how far". It is worth 25 of the 40 marks, so it deserves more than half your time.

:::html
<figure class="fig small"><svg viewBox="0 0 340 433" role="img" aria-label="Five stacked steps for a Paper 1 part (b) answer joined by downward arrows. Step 1, decode the claim: what exactly must the sources show, underline the key words. Step 2, one line per source: does it support, challenge or do both, quote the words that show it. **Step 1: decode the claim.** Questions say "How far do these sources agree that …?" or give a quoted view: "'Business leaders used their wealth for the good of society.' How far do these sources support this view?" Read the claim slowly. Which words limit it ("main", "all", "only")? Which dates and places does it cover? A source can support part of a claim and not the rest.

**Step 2: one line per source.** Next to each source write whether it supports, challenges, or does both, and the words that show it. Many sources do **both**: a source can give support on one point and challenge on another, and the best answers say so. Do not assume that three sources must be on one side and one on the other.

**Step 3: weigh each source.** For each, ask whether it is stronger or weaker evidence **for this claim**, and why: use nature, origin, purpose and context. This is the step that reaches Level 5.

**Step 4: bring your knowledge.** Use it in three ways: to **test** a source ("this is supported by …"), to **explain** it (the event it comes from), and to **add** (a point the sources miss). Keep each piece short and attached to a source.

**Step 5: group and judge.** Organise your paragraphs by **argument**, not by source. For example: one paragraph on the sources that support (A and D) with their evaluation, one on those that challenge (B and C), and one that weighs the two. Open with a one-sentence line of argument. Close with a judgement that **answers "how far"** and says which side is stronger **because of** the evaluation, not despite it. A good test: if you could write your paragraph without the word "because", you have described instead of evaluated.

:::key Source by source or by argument?
Writing "Source A says … Source B says … Source C says …" is a **list**. It cannot reach beyond Level 3, because it makes no judgement. Group the sources by what they **show** about the claim. Within each group, order the sources from strongest to weakest evidence, and say why. The judgement comes from that ranking.
:::

### Worked example: how far business leaders used their wealth for society

You need only a little background to follow this example, which draws on the American option (the Gilded Age is the Paper 1 topic in 2027). Between about 1870 and 1900 the United States industrialised very fast, and a few men made huge fortunes: Andrew Carnegie in steel, John D. Rockefeller in oil. Giant firms joined into **trusts** (combinations that controlled a whole industry). Prices for crops fell, and farmers in the South and West organised in protest. In 1892 a new party, the People's or Populist Party, held its first national convention in Omaha. US senators were not elected by voters but chosen by state legislatures until 1913.

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth, so that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship. … the man of wealth thus becoming the mere agent and trustee for his poorer brethren, bringing to their service his superior wisdom, experience, and ability to administer, doing for them better than they would or could do for themselves.

*Shortened from "Wealth", an essay by Andrew Carnegie, the steel manufacturer, North American Review, June 1889 (later known as "The Gospel of Wealth"). The phrases are the essay's own.*

**Source B**

… we meet in the midst of a nation brought to the verge of moral, political, and material ruin. … The fruits of the toil of millions are boldly stolen to build up colossal fortunes for a few, unprecedented in the history of mankind; and the possessors of those, in turn, despise the Republic and endanger liberty.

*Shortened from the preamble to the platform of the People's (Populist) Party, adopted at its first national convention at Omaha, Nebraska, 4 July 1892. The phrases are the platform's own.*

**Source C**

*A cartoon: "The Bosses of the Senate", by Joseph Keppler, published in Puck, a New York weekly satirical magazine, 23 January 1889. In the Senate chamber, giant figures whose bodies are money bags, labelled with industries such as steel, copper, oil, iron, sugar, tin and coal, tower over the small senators at their desks.*

**Source D**

… every contract, combination in the form of trust or otherwise, or conspiracy, in restraint of trade or commerce among the several States, or with foreign nations, is hereby declared to be illegal.

*Shortened from Section 1 of the Sherman Antitrust Act, passed by Congress and signed by President Benjamin Harrison on 2 July 1890. The phrases are the Act's own.*

**Question.** "Business leaders in the United States used their wealth for the good of society in the 1880s and 1890s." How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but comments on bias are not based on context. Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, explicit links to the question, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: the claim.** "Used their wealth for the good of society" asks about what business leaders **did** and its **effect on society**, not about what they said or whether they were rich. Dates: the 1880s and 1890s.

**Step 2: one line each.**
- **A**: supports, since it says wealth should be used as a trust for the poor ("agent and trustee for his poorer brethren"); but it is a duty, and the last words ("better than they would or could do for themselves") are paternalist: they assume that the poor cannot manage for themselves.
- **B**: challenges, since wealth was "stolen" and "endanger[s] liberty"; it does not say all business leaders did nothing good.
- **C**: challenges: business wealth buys power over government for the few; it says nothing about charity.
- **D**: challenges by implication: Congress treated trusts as a danger to trade; but it is a law, not an opinion about charity.

**Step 3: weight each source, with context.**
- **A.** An essay by the best-known industrialist of the day, written to answer critics and to persuade other rich men. It is the strongest evidence of the **claim that wealth carries a duty**, because it comes from the man who followed it: Carnegie gave away the great bulk of his fortune, roughly 350 million dollars, and paid for more than 2,500 public libraries. But it is a statement of **intention and principle**, not a description of what business leaders generally did, and Carnegie was defending his own position. His company's steelworkers worked twelve-hour days, and in 1892 at Homestead, Pennsylvania, a lockout and a battle with armed guards cost the lives of seven workers and three guards. So A is strong evidence that **some** leaders thought wealth had a social purpose, weak evidence that they acted on it **for society** in how they earned it.
- **B.** A party platform written for a convention of angry farmers and workers, to win votes. "Stolen" is rhetoric, a deliberate generalisation. But the words fit the context of falling crop prices, debts to banks and high railway rates, and the Populist candidate, James B. Weaver, polled over a million votes in 1892 and won 22 electoral votes. As evidence of what a large part of society **thought** of the wealthy, it is strong. As evidence of what business leaders did, it is the partisan view of their opponents.
- **C.** A cartoon in a magazine that campaigned against trusts, drawn to exaggerate. Its picture of senators as dwarfs is satire. But it matches what we know: senators were chosen by state legislatures, which business could influence. It is strong evidence of **what critics believed**, and weaker evidence of how business leaders in fact behaved. It says nothing for or against charity.
- **D.** A law passed by the Senate 51 to 1 and the House 242 to 0, so almost every member of Congress thought the trusts were a problem. It is the most official and least partisan of the four. But it was a weak tool (the Supreme Court in 1895 ruled that the sugar trust, which controlled most of the country's sugar refining, was not covered, because manufacturing was not "commerce"), and in 1894 it was used against strikers in the Pullman railway strike. So it shows concern about the power of big business, but it says little about whether wealth was used for good.

**Step 4 and 5: group and judge. A Level 3 answer (about 13 marks).** "Source A supports the view because Carnegie says the rich should be trustees for the poor. Source B challenges it because the Populists say the rich have stolen from the people. Source C also challenges it because it shows business men controlling the Senate. Source D challenges it because it shows that Congress passed a law against trusts. A is biased because Carnegie was rich, and B is biased because the Populists were angry, so we cannot trust them. C is only a cartoon. Overall, the sources do not support the view very much because three of the four are against it."

*Commentary.* All four sources are used, sides are identified, and a conclusion is drawn. But the comments on bias are generic ("rich", "angry", "only a cartoon") and are not used to decide anything; the sources are taken one at a time, and the final count ("three of the four") is an arithmetic judgement. No knowledge is used. **Level 3.**

**A Level 5 answer (about 23 marks).**

"The sources support the view only to a limited extent. The only direct support is Source A, and it is the weakest kind: a statement of what a rich man believes a rich man should do.

Source A is Carnegie's own essay, written to persuade other wealthy men and to answer critics, and its claim that wealth is a 'trust' for the poor is strong evidence of one important attitude among business leaders. Carnegie also acted on it, giving away the bulk of his fortune and building more than 2,500 libraries. But the essay is about what ought to happen to wealth **after** it has been made, and says nothing about how it is made. In 1892 Carnegie's own company locked out its steelworkers at Homestead, and ten people died in the fighting that followed. And the claim that the rich can do for the poor what 'they would or could' not do for themselves tells us that Carnegie expected to control how society was helped. A therefore supports the view only for the later use of a few very large fortunes.

The other three sources challenge the view, from different directions, and they do not all carry the same weight. Source B, the Populists' platform, accuses the wealthy of using their fortunes to endanger liberty. It was written to win votes, and 'stolen' is an exaggeration. But a party that won over a million votes and 22 electoral votes in 1892 spoke for a large part of society, so B is strong evidence that many Americans, especially farmers whose crop prices were falling, did not believe that wealth was serving them. Source C, Keppler's cartoon, makes the same point about politics: money bags towering over senators. It is satire and exaggerates, but it is corroborated by the fact that senators were chosen by state legislatures that business interests could influence. Source D, the Sherman Act, is the most official of the sources. A near-unanimous Congress agreed that the combinations of business were a danger to trade and competition. Its weakness is that it said little about wealth and did little in practice: the Supreme Court limited it in 1895 and the government used it against strikers in 1894, so it shows concern rather than effect.

Taken together, B, C and D show that influential critics thought great business wealth was used for the few; they are better evidence of **opinion** than of what business leaders did. A shows that some leaders thought differently and acted on it. My own knowledge points the same way: the philanthropy of Carnegie and others was real and large, but the same fortunes rested on low pay, long hours, and trusts that cut competition. The sources therefore support the view only for the way that a few great fortunes were **spent**, and not for how business leaders **used their power**."

*Commentary.* The sources are **grouped by argument** (A for; B, C and D against); each is weighed using nature, origin, purpose and context (a rich man defending wealth; a party platform at a convention; satire; a law passed almost unanimously); knowledge is used to test and extend the sources (the Homestead lockout, the Senate's election, the 1895 ruling); and the judgement answers "how far" by distinguishing between **spending** wealth and **using power**. **Level 5.**
:::

### Spot the weak paragraph

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question (from the example above): "How far do these sources support the view that business leaders used their wealth for the good of society?" [25]

A student wrote:

"Source C is a cartoon, so it is biased. It was drawn by Joseph Keppler for Puck magazine and shows giant money bags standing over the senators in the Senate. This shows that business men had too much power in the Senate. The cartoon is only one man's opinion and he may have been paid to draw it, so it is not reliable. This means that Source C challenges the view, but it is not very useful."

**The level.** Part of Level 3 at best, and low in it. The paragraph reads the source correctly, but its evaluation is exactly what the descriptor describes: a comment on bias "not based on contextual understanding". "Biased" is asserted, not explained; "he may have been paid" is a guess with no basis in the provenance; and the verdict, "not very useful", is not tied to the question. It also says nothing about what the cartoon does **not** show, which is any use of wealth for society at all.

**The fix.** "Source C, a cartoon in a magazine that campaigned against the trusts, shows business money bags towering over the Senate. Its picture is deliberately exaggerated, but it matches what we know: senators were chosen by state legislatures, which business could influence, so it is good evidence that critics believed wealth was used to buy political power. That challenges the view that business leaders used their wealth for society, though it says nothing about charity. It is therefore stronger evidence for the **power** of business than for how its wealth was **spent**." Now the comment names a position, a purpose and a limit, and uses knowledge to test the source.

## Doing it under time pressure

A sensible routine for the 75 minutes:

| Time | Do |
|---|---|
| About 5 min | Read the two questions, then all four sources. Underline the key words of (b). Put a tick (supports), a cross (challenges) or both beside each source. |
| About 25 min | Part (a): grid of themes (3 min), then write (about 22 min): similarities, differences, explanation, verdict. |
| About 45 min | Part (b): plan (5 min: groups, evaluation, knowledge, judgement), then write (about 38 min), leaving 2 minutes to check that the last paragraph answers "how far". |

:::exam Technique for Paper 1
- **Quote briefly.** Two to six words in quotation marks, built into your sentence, prove a point; copying a line does not.
- **Name the source.** "Source B suggests …", never "the source" or "it".
- **Evaluate as you go.** Put the evaluation next to the point it affects, not in a block at the end.
- **Use the provenance line in every source.** If you have ignored one, you have probably missed the point of that source.
- **Answer the question as set.** "As evidence about X" and "how far do the sources agree that Y" are different tasks.
- **Check "how far".** The last sentence of (b) should be a judgement with a reason, not "so the sources are mixed".

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What is the difference between reading what a source says and making an inference?
A: What it says is its direct message. An inference is something it shows without stating it, drawn from its words and its context (for example, that a speaker expects to be opposed).
Q: Give two things to look for when you read the tone of a source.
A: Loaded words and metaphors; the strength of verbs (demand, regret, hope); who "we" and "they" are; the register (legal, emotional, official).
Q: Why is the comment "this source is biased" worth little on its own?
A: It does not say whose view it is, what that person wanted people to think, or what the source is therefore good and bad evidence of for the question. The mark scheme describes this as a comment not based on contextual understanding.
Q: What are the three jobs of your own knowledge in part (b)?
A: To place the source in its context, to test it against what you know (corroboration), and to fill a gap the sources leave.
Q: Why are Paper 1 (a) answers organised by theme, not by source?
A: Because the task is to compare. Describing A then B leaves the comparison undone; writing about a theme in both sources, and explaining why they agree or differ, is the comparison.
Q: What makes a cartoon different from a photograph as evidence?
A: A cartoon is a deliberate message built from symbols and exaggeration for a public; a photograph records what was in front of the lens but was framed, timed and captioned by someone.
Q: Why was Kipling's poem a weak source for the American motives for taking the Philippines but a stronger source for ideas of "civilising mission"?
A: Kipling was a British poet writing an appeal, not an American decision-maker explaining policy, so it is weak on motives; but a poem aimed at a mass audience shows what arguments were thought persuasive.

H: Compare Source C (October 1905) with Source D (April 1906). What does D keep that C appeared to give away?
H: Evaluate the diary (Source B) for what it shows about how the Tsar saw his people, then use your knowledge of the Duma (April to July 1906).
A: Support: C promises civil freedoms and that no law will take effect without confirmation by the State Duma, a real limit on autocracy; D (Article 86) confirms that a law needs the Duma's agreement; A shows what the people demanded and B shows the cost of ignoring them, so the Tsar was under great pressure. Challenge: D keeps "the supreme autocratic power" in the Tsar and says obedience is God's command, and the Tsar's ratification is still needed; B shows no sign that Nicholas understood the workers' demands. Evaluation with knowledge: C was issued after the general strike of October 1905, on the advice of Sergei Witte, and shows a concession under pressure, not a conviction; D was issued days before the first Duma met on 27 April 1906 (Old Style) and, with the Tsar's powers over emergency decrees, the power to dismiss the Duma and a State Council half of whose members he appointed, made the power-sharing limited; the first Duma was dissolved on 8 July 1906 (Old Style) after 72 days. Level 5 judges that the sources support the view only to a limited extent: the Tsar was willing to **concede under pressure**, but D shows that he never gave up the principle of autocracy.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B in the box above (the American sources). Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to the role of government in the economy.
A: Similarities: both are public speeches by men who sought or held the highest office, both speak in broad principles, and both reflect the economic anxieties of their time. Differences: A (Hoover, 1928) praises "rugged individualism" and calls government direction of economic life "paternalism and state socialism"; B (Roosevelt, 1933) blames "money changers" for the crisis and speaks of restoring "that temple", which prepares the way for strong government action; A sees the danger in too much government, B in too little control of finance. Explanation: A is a campaign speech made on 22 October 1928, in a boom, when Hoover was defending the Republican record; B was made on 4 March 1933, after more than three years of Depression, at the start of the Roosevelt presidency, when millions were unemployed and banks had closed. Level 4 explains the difference through the contrast of boom and slump, and through purpose (a candidate rallying support against a rival; a new President preparing the public for action), and gives a verdict on what each is better evidence of.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and C in the box above (the Munich sources). Compare these two sources as evidence about British attitudes to the settlement of the Czechoslovak crisis in 1938.
A: Similarities: both see the crisis as a threat of war and both speak to a large audience (a broadcast to the nation, a House of Commons debate); both say something negative: A is horrified at the prospect of war, C at the outcome. Differences: A (27 September, before Munich) shows a Prime Minister reluctant to fight "because of a quarrel in a far-away country", language that treats Czechoslovakia as remote; C (5 October, after) calls the settlement "a total and unmitigated defeat". Explanation: Chamberlain was the head of the government, speaking before the conference to prepare the public for a compromise and to justify his search for peace; Churchill, a backbencher out of office, was a critic of appeasement who had warned about German rearmament, and spoke to attack the government's policy in the debate. Level 4 explains why the same crisis produced opposite responses and judges that A is stronger evidence of the attitudes of the government and a war-weary public, C of the critics' position and not of what most people felt in October 1938.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources in the Munich box. "The Munich Agreement was a success for British policy." How far do these sources support this view?
H: Decide what would count as "success" for Britain: avoiding war, keeping Czechoslovakia secure, or reaching lasting peace with Germany?
H: Group the sources by what they show: the aim (A), the settlement (B, D), the verdict of a critic (C).
H: Test B with your knowledge of what happened to Czechoslovakia in March 1939 and to Poland in September 1939.
A: Support: A shows the aim of avoiding war; B shows an agreement with Hitler "never to go to war with one another again"; D shows a settlement signed by four powers, with a timetable for evacuation, and so a war was avoided in September 1938. Challenge: C says the result was a "total and unmitigated defeat"; D's terms handed the Sudeten German territory to Germany without Czechoslovakia at the conference, a point you can use from knowledge; A reflects reluctance to fight, not success. Evaluation with knowledge: B is a document signed by Chamberlain at the time and is therefore strong evidence of what he hoped, but it was signed on 30 September, and Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939 and attacked Poland in September 1939, so what it promised was not achieved; C is a critic's speech, partisan in the sense that it came from an opponent of the government, but the events of 1939 support its main point. Level 5 judges, for example, that the sources support the view only to a limited extent: Munich did avoid war in 1938, which was Chamberlain's aim, but the evidence suggests it gave Hitler more than it gained for Britain.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read Source A (Bülow, 1897), Source B (Kipling, 1899) and the Rhodes Colossus cartoon, all in this chapter. How far do these sources agree that empire was a matter of duty and not of interest?
H: Which source speaks of duty, and which of interest? Where does the cartoon fit?
H: Think about what each source was written for. Can a source that talks of duty still be evidence for interest?
A: Support for duty: B ("burden", "serve your captives' need") makes duty the central idea; A refers to "tasks", but of promoting Germany's own interests. Challenge: A is the language of interest ("shipping, trade and industry") and status ("our place in the sun"); the cartoon shows ambition and power more than service: Rhodes towers over a continent. Evaluation: Kipling's poem was an appeal to Americans to take a decision that was disputed; the language of duty was a way to make acquisition respectable, and one could argue it **disguised** interest, which a historian can show from the American strategic and commercial aims of 1898; the cartoon is a British magazine's joke about a businessman-politician, so it shows how ambition was seen in Britain, not what motivated governments. A* judgement: the sources agree only that empire was a **respectable** thing for a great power to want; they differ on the reason, and the strongest evidence for interest is the speech by the official responsible, while the strongest evidence for the language of duty is the poem. The sources do not support the view that empire was about duty **and not** interest.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ A student says: "The Sherman Act (Source D of the Gilded Age sources) is the strongest source, because it is a law and laws are official." Explain why this is not enough, and say what you would want to know about the Act before deciding how far it shows that business leaders harmed society.
H: Think about what a law is evidence of: intention, or effect?
H: Ask who voted for it, what it said, and what happened when it was tested.
A: A law is strong evidence of what a legislature intended and of what it thought was a problem, which makes it a good source for the **fear** of trusts. But it is not evidence that business leaders harmed society, and still less of how far. A law can be vague (this one used general words such as "combination" and "restraint of trade"), passed for political reasons (near-unanimous votes may show that no member wanted to appear on the side of the trusts), and weakly enforced: the Supreme Court limited its use against the sugar trust in 1895, and it was used against strikers in 1894. To judge it you would want to know why it was passed, how the courts read it, and what the government actually did under it. A* answers say that **official** does not mean **stronger**: the best evidence comes from combining sources of different kinds that point the same way.

:::recap
- **Paper 1**: 1 h 15 min, 40 marks; four sources (at most 600 words in all), at most one visual; (a) [15] compare A and B; (b) [25] all four sources and your own knowledge; 75 per cent AO3.
- **Read** each source for what it says, what it suggests (inference) and what it leaves out; notice tone, loaded words and metaphors.
- **Provenance** is nature, origin and purpose, with context; **as evidence** means for one question, so a source can be strong for one and weak for another.
- **"Biased" is not enough** (Level 3): say whose view it is, what they wanted, and what the source is therefore strong and weak evidence of.
- **Knowledge** places, tests (corroborates) and extends sources; it is never an essay.
- **Part (a)**: compare by theme, find similarities as well as differences, explain with provenance and context; Level 4 is a developed comparison (12–15).
- **Part (b)**: decode the claim, one line per source, weigh each, use knowledge, group by argument, judge "how far"; Level 5 evaluates (21–25).
- **Visual sources**: read the words, symbols, scale, publication and audience; photographs are framed and may be staged.
- **Timing (a guide)**: about 5 minutes reading, 25 on (a), 45 on (b).
