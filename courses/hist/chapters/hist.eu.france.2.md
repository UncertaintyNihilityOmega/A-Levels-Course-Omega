In 1789 almost nobody in France wanted a republic. Even in June 1791 the deputies pretended that the king had been kidnapped so that they could keep him. Fifteen months later he was a prisoner in the Temple, the Tuileries had been stormed, and the Convention had abolished the monarchy. How a country that wanted a reformed king ended up with no king at all is one of the great questions of this course. ## From Versailles to Paris: the Constituent Assembly's task

After the October Days of 1789 the king and the National Assembly both sat in Paris (the Assembly moved on 19 October). The deputies, now called the **Constituent Assembly** because their job was to write a constitution, set out to rebuild France from the ground up. Between October 1789 and September 1791 they changed the tax system, the map, the courts and the Church. They also quarrelled about what the king's powers should be, and a large part of France disliked what they did to the Church.

Remember this running theme as you read. The Assembly's aim was a **constitutional monarchy**: a king whose power is limited by a written constitution and an elected assembly. For two years almost everybody accepted that aim. The story of this chapter is how it collapsed.

:::def Constitutional monarchy
A system in which the king remains head of state but his powers are limited by a written constitution and by an elected law-making assembly.
:::

## Revolutionary and counter-revolutionary groups

### Clubs, factions and parties

In this chapter "left" and "right" describe where deputies sat in the Assembly hall and what each side wanted: those who wanted to go further sat to the left of the president, and defenders of the king and the old order sat to the right. The labels stuck, and they are still used in politics today.

There were no political parties in 1789 in the modern sense, with membership cards and programmes. Politics was done in the Assembly, in the newspapers (the press was free from 1789) and above all in **clubs**, societies where people met to discuss and plan.

The **Jacobin** club began as the Breton Club at Versailles in 1789, when deputies from Brittany met to agree tactics. After October it moved to Paris and took over a former Dominican convent in the rue Saint-Honoré (the Dominicans were called Jacobins in Paris), under the name Society of Friends of the Constitution. It had two roles. It was a place where deputies agreed what to do before votes, and it was a network: by 1791 hundreds of local societies across France were affiliated to it and exchanged letters. At first it was broad and respectable. Its aim was to defend the constitution and watch for plots against it.

The **Cordeliers** club (Society of the Friends of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, founded in April 1790) was more radical and more popular: its fees were low and women and "passive" citizens could attend. Georges Danton, Camille Desmoulins and Jean-Paul Marat, whose newspaper *L'Ami du peuple* ("The Friend of the People") attacked ministers and generals, were associated with it. Do not confuse the two clubs. You need to know the Cordeliers mainly as the group that pushed for a republic after Varennes.

The **Feuillants** appeared on 16 July 1791. After the king's flight, the leaders of the Jacobins who wanted to keep the king (Antoine Barnave, Alexandre de Lameth, Adrien Duport, and the Marquis de Lafayette, commander of the National Guard) walked out and set up their own club in the former convent of the Feuillants. They believed the Revolution had achieved its aims and that going further, towards a republic, would let in disorder and attacks on property. Their aim was to **end the Revolution** by making the constitution work with Louis XVI as king.

The **Girondins** were not a party at first. They were a group of Jacobin deputies in the Legislative Assembly led by the journalist **Jacques-Pierre Brissot** (so they were first called Brissotins), with orators such as Pierre Vergniaud, Marguerite-Élie Guadet and Armand Gensonné, several of whom came from the department of the Gironde around Bordeaux, which gave them their later name. They were supported by the salon of Madame Roland and the ministers Jean-Marie Roland and Étienne Clavière. Their aim in 1791–92 was to take the Revolution forward by **war**: war against the émigrés and the kings who sheltered them would, they said, expose the king's loyalty, unite the nation and spread liberty. Many of them were not yet republicans.

:::def Jacobins
The Society of Friends of the Constitution, a political club meeting in a former Jacobin (Dominican) convent in Paris, with a national network of affiliated clubs. Before July 1791 it contained most of the Assembly's patriots; afterwards it was the club of the left, led in 1792 by Robespierre against the Girondins on the question of war.
:::

:::def Feuillants
The club founded on 16 July 1791 by moderate former Jacobins (Barnave, the Lameths, Duport, Lafayette) who wanted to stop the Revolution and make the constitutional monarchy work with Louis XVI.
:::

:::def Girondins
The group around Brissot, Vergniaud and other deputies of the Legislative Assembly (named later after the Gironde department, home of several of them) who wanted war in 1791–92 to expose the king and spread the Revolution. They controlled the ministries from March to June 1792.
:::

:::key Aims of the revolutionary groups, 1791–92
1. **Feuillants**: stop the Revolution; a strong constitutional monarchy under Louis XVI; the vote limited to property owners.
2. **Girondins**: a forward policy: war to expose the king and spread liberty; at first they wanted to work with the king's ministers, not overthrow him.
3. **Radical Jacobins and Cordeliers**: a wider vote and more power for the people; suspicion of the king; by 1792 many wanted him removed.
:::

### The counter-revolution and why it failed

Not everybody accepted the changes. **Counter-revolutionaries** wanted to undo some or all of the Revolution. They came in several kinds.

- **Émigrés**. The Comte d'Artois left France on 16 July 1789, two days after the Bastille, and other nobles followed after the October Days and after Varennes. By 1792 there were tens of thousands outside France (historians estimate about 150,000 over the whole Revolution). Artois set up a court in exile, first at Turin and then in the Rhineland towns of Koblenz and Worms, where the Prince de Condé raised a small army of émigré officers. They lobbied the monarchs of Europe for an invasion.
- **Plots inside France.** The Marquis de Favras planned to free the king and was hanged in February 1790. In the south, royalist gatherings such as the *camp de Jalès* in 1790–91 had thousands of followers but were dispersed. On 28 February 1791, the "Day of the Daggers", about 400 nobles who came to defend the king at the Tuileries were disarmed by the National Guard.
- **Refractory priests**. The priests who refused the oath of 1790 (below) were a counter-revolutionary force in many parishes, especially in the west.
- **The king and queen**, from Varennes onward, in secret. Marie Antoinette appealed to her brother Leopold, the Austrian emperor, for help.

:::def Émigrés
Nobles, clergy and others who fled France after 1789 to oppose the Revolution from abroad; their lands could later be confiscated.
:::

:::def Refractory priests
Priests who refused to swear the oath of loyalty to the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (also called non-jurors); those who swore were "constitutional" or "juring" priests.
:::

Why did counter-revolution fail before 1792?

:::key Reasons why the counter-revolution failed, 1789–92
1. **Divided and disorganised.** Émigrés quarrelled over aims: some wanted the pre-1789 monarchy back, others a deal with the constitutionalists. Their plots were small, badly kept secrets and easily broken up.
2. **Based abroad.** The émigrés were far from France, were mocked as people who had deserted their country, and had no popular following inside it.
3. **Foreign help was slow and self-interested.** Leopold II preferred to avoid war and the powers disagreed. Austria and Prussia were distracted by Poland and by their own rivalry. The Declaration of Pillnitz promised nothing unless every power joined, and Britain would not.
4. **The Revolution had real beneficiaries.** Peasants who had gained from the August Decrees and, from 1790, those who bought Church land had a stake in not going back. National Guards and elected officials ran the new local government.
5. **The king would not be rescued openly.** He accepted the constitution in September 1791, which took away the counter-revolution's legal claim to be acting for him.
6. **Counter-revolution helped the Revolution.** Each emigration, plot and foreign threat made the patriots more suspicious of the king and made war look unavoidable.
:::

So counter-revolution was too weak to defeat the Revolution before 1792, yet strong enough to frighten it. That is why it matters here. Fear of a plot between the king, the émigrés and foreign monarchs is one of the strongest causes of what happened in 1791 and 1792.

## The reforms of the Constituent Assembly, 1789–91

### Finance: Church lands, assignats and taxes

The Assembly inherited the bankruptcy that had called the Estates-General. Its solution was to take the Church's land. On **2 November 1789** it voted that Church property was "at the disposal of the nation", on condition that the state paid the clergy's salaries and looked after the poor and schools. The land was estimated to be worth between two and three thousand million livres, more than enough to pay off the debt. In February 1790 it also ended the monasteries' solemn vows, and the monks and nuns were free to leave.

The sale of land took time, so in **December 1789** the Assembly authorised **assignats**: bonds that could be exchanged for Church land, with a ceiling of 400 million livres. Because the sale was slow and creditors wanted cash, the Assembly turned the bonds into paper money in 1790. By September 1790 it had agreed to issue up to 1,200 million livres of assignats that paid no interest and could be used to pay debts. It promised to burn the assignats as the land was sold, so that the money supply would shrink and the value would hold.

:::def Assignats
Paper securities issued from December 1789 and backed by the value of confiscated Church lands. From 1790 they circulated as paper money. They lost value as more were printed to pay for the war.
:::

The scheme had four effects:
- It **solved the immediate debt problem** and gave the state credit.
- It **created a class of buyers** of Church land (peasants, bourgeois, some nobles) who had a stake in the Revolution succeeding.
- It **tied the Revolution to the Church question**, because land sales meant the state could never give the Church its property back.
- It **led to inflation**. From 1791 the Assembly and then the Legislative Assembly printed more assignats, partly to pay for the army, and prices rose. The paper's value fell steadily. The final collapse came after 1792, in the next chapter's story.

**Taxation.** The Assembly abolished the taxes that had been so unequal. The *gabelle* (salt tax) went in March 1790, the internal customs barriers in the autumn of 1790, and the tithe was already abolished in August 1789 without compensation. The *taille* was replaced by new direct taxes on everyone: a **land tax** (*contribution foncière*, law of November 1790) on all landowners, a tax on movable property and income (*contribution mobilière*, 1791), and a licence tax on trades (*patente*, 1791). On paper this was the equality that Calonne had asked for in 1786. In practice assessment was slow, many peasants paid late or not at all (they had just been told the old taxes were gone), and tax receipts in 1790–91 were far below what the budget needed. The Assembly's fiscal revolution was therefore a **success in principle and a failure in practice**: the Crown's financial problems had been moved from a tax crisis into a paper-money problem.

The Assembly also reformed business. The **d'Allarde law** (March 1791) abolished the guilds and opened all trades to anyone who paid for a licence. The **Le Chapelier law** (14 June 1791) banned associations of workers and employers and made strikes illegal. These were Enlightenment, free-market laws, but the Le Chapelier law left workers with no legal way to bargain over wages, and so it divided the Third Estate.

### Local government: the departments

Before 1789 France was a patchwork of provinces, intendancies and church and legal districts, each with different rules. The Assembly replaced it with a regular system. By the law of **15 January 1790** France was divided into **83 departments**, named after rivers and mountains rather than old provinces, and each was subdivided into districts, cantons and communes. The departments were sized so that every citizen could reach the capital of their department in about a day.

All local officials were **elected** (by active citizens, below). Each department had an elected council and executive; each commune (the old parish, town or village) had an elected mayor and council. The royal intendants were abolished. The national government now appointed almost nobody locally.

Effects: citizens at last had **uniform laws and rights**; the old provincial privileges and local loyalties were weakened; and thousands of men gained experience of office, which made the Revolution a mass participation in government. But there were two problems. The local councils could ignore or slow orders from Paris, so the central government had little control. And the system depended on the vote, which was limited.

:::def Active and passive citizens
The Assembly's division of male citizens in 1789–91. **Active citizens** (men over 25 paying direct tax equal to at least three days' labour) could vote in primary assemblies. **Passive citizens** (the poor, servants, and all women) had civil rights but no vote.
:::

The deputies defended the distinction: the vote, they said, belonged to those with a stake in the nation, as owners of property. Robespierre, in a speech of 1791, answered that it betrayed the Declaration's first article, "men are born and remain free and equal in rights". Roughly three in five adult men were active citizens (the estimates vary), but women, servants and the poor had no vote, and for the elections of deputies there was a second stage, in which active citizens chose electors who had to meet a higher property test.

### Justice

The Assembly dismantled the old system, symbolised by the Parlements. Their judges had been on forced "vacation" since November 1789, and the courts were abolished in September 1790. **Lettres de cachet** were abolished in March 1790. The law of **16–24 August 1790** created a new, uniform system:
- elected **justices of the peace** in every canton to settle small disputes and try to reconcile parties;
- a civil court in every district, with **elected judges**, who had to be lawyers and served for fixed terms;
- **juries** for serious criminal trials, the first in French history, as in England;
- public trials, and a right to a defence lawyer.

The **penal code of September 1791** fixed punishments in law (judges could no longer choose them) and was milder than the old one: branding and breaking on the wheel disappeared, and the same punishment applied whatever the rank of the offender. Decapitation by the **guillotine**, proposed by the deputy Dr Joseph-Ignace Guillotin as a quick and equal form of execution, was first used in April 1792.

The new justice system satisfied the demands of the cahiers for an end to arbitrary imprisonment and the sale of judicial offices. Its main weakness was that it **cost a great deal and was untried**. Elected judges lacked experience, and in some areas juries were afraid to convict neighbours.

### The Church: the Civil Constitution of the Clergy

Having taken the Church's land, the Assembly decided that the Church should be reorganised by the state. The **Civil Constitution of the Clergy** was passed on **12 July 1790** (the king reluctantly signed it on 24 August). It:
- reduced the dioceses from about 130 to 83, **one for each department**;
- ordered that **bishops and parish priests be elected** by the active citizens of the department or district, even if they were Protestants or Jews;
- made all clergy **paid by the state**, with higher salaries for poor priests and lower ones for bishops;
- forbade the Pope to confirm bishops: a new bishop would write to him only as a sign of unity.

Many of the deputies were Catholics and some of them were priests. They saw this as a reform of the abuses in the Church that would bring it into line with the nation. But Catholics saw the state overriding the Pope on a spiritual matter. Church doctrine had never before allowed laymen to choose a bishop in this way.

At first Louis XVI and the clergy tried to wait for the Pope's reply. Pius VI delayed. In November 1790 the Assembly lost patience and ordered (decree of **27 November 1790**) that every priest in a public post must swear an **oath** to "maintain with all his power the constitution". The king, after weeks of delay, assented on 26 December. About half the clergy refused. Roughly half of parish priests took the oath, but only seven bishops did, and the proportion of jurors varied widely by region. In March and April 1791 **Pope Pius VI condemned** the Civil Constitution and the Revolution's principles.

So the Revolution now had two Churches: the **constitutional Church** (priests who had sworn) and the **refractory Church** (priests who had not). The split was geographic as well as personal. The oath was taken by a majority in the Paris basin and the south-east and rejected by a majority in the west (Brittany and the Vendée), the north-east and the Massif Central. Peasants who had welcomed the end of the tithe were in many places furious at the removal of the priest they knew.

:::def Civil Constitution of the Clergy
The law of 12 July 1790 by which the Assembly made the French Church a state-controlled institution: dioceses matched to departments, bishops and priests elected and paid by the state, and no papal confirmation. Priests were ordered to swear an oath to it in November 1790.
:::

:::key Why the Civil Constitution was so divisive
1. It was passed **without the Pope or the Church's own consent**, so Catholics were asked to choose between their religion and the nation.
2. The **oath** forced every priest, and then every parishioner, to take a side.
3. The **king** had a personal religious objection and was pushed from reluctant compromise into opposition.
4. It created **refractory priests** who became the leaders of counter-revolution, especially in the west.
5. The Revolution, which in 1789 had united almost all Frenchmen, now split them along a line (religion) that did not follow the line between rich and poor.
:::

:::warn Do not call all the Assembly's reforms anti-religious
The Assembly granted Protestants full civil rights in December 1789 and Jews' rights in 1790–91, and most deputies were practising Catholics. The Civil Constitution tried to reform the Church, not to destroy it. The attack on Christianity itself belongs to 1793–94 (the next chapter). A top-level answer says that the *effect* of the reform was to divide Catholics even though the *intention* was not hostile.
:::

:::note Historians on the Church question
John McManners, *The French Revolution and the Church* (1969), made the case that the religious quarrel was one of the most damaging errors of the Revolution: it turned a large part of the country against the new order. You can use that as a stretch point: the Assembly settled its finances by taking Church land, and then had to settle the Church itself, and the second job went much worse than the first.
:::

### What the reforms achieved

| Area | What changed | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Finance | Church lands sold; assignats; land tax for all | Inflation; slow tax collection |
| Local government | 83 departments; elected officials | Weak control from Paris; vote limited |
| Justice | Elected judges; juries; uniform penal code | Costly; untrained judges |
| Church | State-run, elected clergy | Oath split France; Pope condemned |
| Citizenship | Equality before the law; Protestants, Jews | Active and passive citizens; no vote for women |

## Political instability: the constitution and the king, 1789–91

### Disagreement on the terms of the new constitution

The Assembly spent two years on the constitution, and the debates showed deep disagreement about two things: how much power the king should keep and who should vote. Not all of the time was quarrel. On 14 July 1790, the first anniversary of the Bastille, the **Festival of the Federation** was held on the Champ de Mars, a great open parade ground in Paris: delegates of the National Guards from all over France gathered there, and the king swore to uphold the constitution that the Assembly was writing. It was the high point of national unity before the flight to Varennes.

On the king, there were three views. The **monarchiens** (Jean-Joseph Mounier, Pierre-Victor Malouet) wanted a British-style system with two chambers and an absolute royal veto; they lost in September 1789 and Mounier left the Assembly after the October Days. A large centre, including the "triumvirate" of Barnave, Duport and Alexandre de Lameth, voted for a single chamber and a **suspensive veto** that let the king delay a law for two legislatures. A small left, led by Robespierre, Jérôme Pétion and others, wanted to cut the king's power further and widen the vote.

The **Constitution of 1791**, completed on 3 September, made France a constitutional monarchy:
- the king (now "King of the French", not "King of France") headed the executive, chose his ministers (who could not sit in the Assembly), and had a suspensive veto;
- a single-chamber **Legislative Assembly** of 745 deputies, elected for two years by a two-stage vote of active citizens;
- the Declaration of 1789 as its preamble;
- members of the Constituent Assembly were forbidden to stand again (a proposal of Robespierre's, passed in May 1791), so the new Assembly was entirely inexperienced.

The construction was fragile. The king had a veto and chose the ministers but had no way to make the Assembly pass laws. The Assembly had no way to control the ministers. If king and Assembly disagreed, as they did from November 1791, there was nothing to settle it except force. The constitution only worked if the king **wanted** it to work.

### The behaviour of the King and the flight to Varennes

Louis XVI never accepted the Revolution in his heart. He agreed to its measures under pressure, but privately he regarded the Assembly's decisions as forced on him. After October 1789 he and his family lived in the Tuileries Palace in Paris, protected, but also watched, by the National Guard. In April 1791 two things deepened his anger. His adviser the Comte de **Mirabeau**, who had been secretly paid to advise him and had tried to keep the monarchy and the Revolution together, died on 2 April. And on 18 April a crowd stopped the family's carriages leaving for **Saint-Cloud**, where the king meant to take Easter communion from a priest who had refused the oath, while the National Guard, which Lafayette could not make obey him, sided with the crowd. Louis was a prisoner, and as a Catholic he was being forced to receive the sacraments from priests he thought were illegitimate.

So he decided to escape. In the night of **20–21 June 1791** the royal family left the Tuileries in disguise, the children and queen as the household of "Baroness de Korff", a Russian widow, with the king as her valet. They aimed for **Montmédy** near the eastern frontier, where the Marquis de Bouillé had loyal troops. The plan was to gather a force, issue a statement and negotiate a better constitution with Austrian support as a threat.

It went wrong through delays and slowness. The heavy carriage was late, the escort of soldiers was in the wrong places, and at Sainte-Menehould the postmaster Jean-Baptiste Drouet is said to have recognised the king from his portrait on an assignat note and followed. The family was stopped at **Varennes** on the night of 21 June. They were brought back to Paris, arriving on **25 June** through crowds that, on the Assembly's orders, were silent.

Louis had left a long **declaration** in the Tuileries. It was not the work of a man who had willingly accepted the Revolution. He listed his complaints: the insults he had suffered since October 1789, his loss of authority, the Civil Constitution forced on his conscience, and the disorder in the country. He asked the French to rally round their king. Paris read it on the morning of 21 June.

:::def Flight to Varennes
The attempt of Louis XVI and his family to escape from Paris on 20–21 June 1791 to the loyal army at Montmédy, which ended when they were recognised and arrested at Varennes. It revealed that the king did not accept the Revolution.
:::

**The consequences of Varennes.** The Assembly decided to save the monarchy. It declared that the king had been **kidnapped** (*enlevé*), a fiction no one believed, because the declaration he left behind proved that he had gone by choice. The deputies had little choice: the alternatives were a republic, a regency or a new king, and none of these had support. On 15 July 1791 the Assembly declared the king **inviolable**, that is, not to be tried for the flight. But the damage could not be undone. After Varennes:
- the king's word was **no longer trusted**: the patriots now believed that he would betray the Revolution if he could;
- the idea of a **republic** was for the first time publicly discussed (the Cordeliers and newspapers called for the king to be removed), though only a few wanted it;
- **fear of a conspiracy** between the king, the émigrés and foreign powers became a political fact;
- the Jacobin club **split**.

Timothy Tackett, *When the King Took Flight* (2003), argues that Varennes was a turning point in how the French saw the Revolution: it turned vague suspicion of a plot into the conviction that the king was an enemy, and the sense of betrayal outlasted the monarchy.

### The Champ de Mars massacre

During the first days of July the Cordeliers and radical Jacobins campaigned for the king to be removed. On Sunday **17 July 1791** a large crowd (many of them women and artisans) gathered at the **Champ de Mars**, where the Festival of the Federation had been held a year earlier, to sign a **petition** on the "altar of the fatherland", demanding that the Assembly judge the king's flight and replace him. Earlier that weekend two men found hiding under the altar had been lynched, which gave the authorities a reason to act.

The Assembly had ordered the mayor, Jean-Sylvain Bailly, to keep order. He proclaimed **martial law** (red flag) and the National Guard under Lafayette arrived. After the crowd threw stones, the Guard fired. Estimates of the dead vary from about a dozen to perhaps fifty; the number is uncertain because the authorities and the radical press gave very different figures.

:::def Champ de Mars massacre
The shooting of petitioners calling for the king's removal on 17 July 1791 by the National Guard under Lafayette, under martial law ordered by the mayor of Paris.
:::

**Consequences.** The radical movement was driven underground: the Cordeliers were harassed, and Danton and Marat went into hiding. The Jacobins split (the Feuillants had walked out the day before). The popular movement and the middle-class National Guard that had made the Revolution together in 1789 now stood on opposite sides, and the memory of the shooting was bitter. The constitutionalists had saved the monarchy, but they had done so by fighting the people who had made the Revolution, and by supporting a king who had tried to flee.

:::key Why the constitutional monarchy was unstable by autumn 1791
1. A **king who did not believe in it**: the flight showed that Louis accepted the constitution only for lack of choice.
2. A **constitution with no way to settle disputes** between king and Assembly except the veto.
3. **Divisions among patriots**: Feuillants against Jacobins, with the republicans driven underground.
4. **The Church**: thousands of refractory priests and their followers saw the Revolution as an enemy.
5. **Fear of foreign invasion and a royal plot**, ready to explode as soon as war began.
:::

## From constitutional monarchy to republic, 1791–92

### The Legislative Assembly

On 13 September 1791 Louis accepted the constitution and on 14 September swore to it; the Constituent Assembly dissolved on 30 September, and the **Legislative Assembly** met on **1 October 1791**. Of the 745 deputies, roughly 260 sat with the Feuillants on the right, roughly 135 with the Jacobins and Cordeliers on the left, and about 345 were uncommitted in the centre (the Plain). The newcomers were young, mostly lawyers and local officials, and they had no experience.

Two problems quickly appeared. The first was **émigrés and refractory priests**. The Assembly decreed on 9 November that émigrés who did not return by 1 January 1792 would be treated as suspected conspirators, and on 29 November that priests who refused the oath would be treated as suspects and could be deported. Louis **vetoed** both decrees in December. Within three months the king was using his constitutional power against the Assembly's measures to defend the people it saw as enemies. The second problem was **war**.

### Declaration of war on Austria, April 1792

By the end of 1791 the idea of war was popular for different reasons.
- **The Girondins** (Brissot, Vergniaud) argued that the émigrés' armies at the frontier were a threat and that France should strike first. War would also force the king to show his hand: if he supported it, the country would be united; if he sabotaged it, he would be exposed. They also saw it as a crusade for liberty against tyrants.
- **Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette** wanted war for the opposite reason. They expected the French army to be beaten, since many of its noble officers had emigrated, and hoped that an Austrian victory would restore the king's power.
- **Lafayette and the Feuillant generals** hoped that a quick victory would give them glory and strengthen the constitution.
- **Robespierre** was almost alone in opposing it. In speeches to the Jacobins in December 1791 and January 1792 he warned that the army was unready, that the generals and the court could not be trusted, and that people do not welcome "armed missionaries". He feared that war would bring a military dictator.

The foreign powers were slower to act. The **Declaration of Pillnitz** (27 August 1791), signed by Leopold II of Austria and Frederick William II of Prussia, said that the position of the French king was of common interest to all sovereigns and that they would act if all the great powers agreed. Since Britain would not, it was a gesture, but the émigrés celebrated it and the French took it as a threat. Leopold died on 1 March 1792, and his successor Francis II was more willing to fight. In March Louis dismissed his Feuillant ministers and appointed a Girondin ministry (Roland, Clavière, General Dumouriez). On **20 April 1792**, on the king's proposal, the Assembly declared war on Austria ("the King of Bohemia and Hungary") by an almost unanimous vote. Prussia soon joined Austria.

:::def Declaration of war on Austria (20 April 1792)
The decision of the Legislative Assembly, proposed by Louis XVI on the advice of his Girondin ministers, to go to war with Austria. It was supported for opposite reasons by those who wanted to save the Revolution and those who hoped to see it defeated.
:::

The war went badly at once. French troops fled at the first contact with the Austrians in the Netherlands in late April, and a general was murdered by his own men. Soldiers distrusted their officers, and many of the officers were hostile or had left. The war thus produced precisely what the Girondins and Robespierre had both feared: **defeat, suspicion of traitors and the need for strong action** at home.

### Vetoes, the Tuileries and the Brunswick Manifesto

Inside France the war made the king's position impossible. In June 1792 he vetoed two more decrees: one on refractory priests, and one for a camp of 20,000 volunteers (*fédérés*) near Paris. When his Girondin ministers protested, he dismissed them on **13 June**, and appointed Feuillants. On **20 June** a crowd of Paris working people, armed with pikes, marched into the Tuileries; the king was made to wear the red cap of liberty and to drink to the nation. He did not withdraw his vetoes. The attempt to frighten the king into submission had failed, but he had also been humiliated.

On **11 July** the Assembly proclaimed "the fatherland in danger" and called for volunteers. In late July battalions of fédérés from the provinces reached Paris, among them the men from Marseille who sang the war song written in April by Claude Joseph Rouget de Lisle in Strasbourg, which Paris came to call the **Marseillaise**.

Then on **25 July** the Duke of Brunswick, commander of the Austrian and Prussian armies, issued a **manifesto**, drafted with the help of émigrés. It threatened that if the royal family were harmed or the Tuileries attacked, Paris would be given up to "military execution and total destruction", and that National Guards who resisted would be treated as rebels. It was published in Paris at the start of August. It was meant to frighten Paris into obeying the king. It had the opposite effect: it seemed to prove that the king and the foreign armies were in league against the nation, and Parisians were more ready to act than before. Petitions to depose the king came from dozens of Paris sections (the 48 districts of the capital).

:::def Brunswick Manifesto
The declaration of 25 July 1792, issued in the name of the Duke of Brunswick, commander of the Austrian and Prussian armies, threatening Paris with destruction if the royal family was harmed. It strengthened the belief that the king was allied with France's enemies.
:::

### The fall of the monarchy, 10 August 1792

On the night of 9–10 August the leaders of the Paris sections set up an **Insurrectionary Commune** to replace the legal city council. In the morning, thousands of National Guards from the sections and fédérés from the provinces marched on the **Tuileries**, defended by about 900 Swiss Guards and some National Guards. The king left the palace to take refuge with the Assembly. In the fighting that followed, the Swiss Guard, ordered by the king to stop firing, lost most of its men; hundreds of attackers were killed too (estimates vary), and many Swiss were killed after surrendering.

The Assembly, with the king in its hall and the palace in the people's hands, **suspended the king** and called a **National Convention**, to be elected by almost all adult men, with no distinction between active and passive citizens (servants were still excluded), to write a new constitution. The royal family was imprisoned in the Temple on 13 August. A provisional **Executive Council** ruled in the meantime, with Danton as Minister of Justice. Lafayette, who tried to turn his army against the Paris insurgents, failed and fled to the Austrians on 19 August.

The 10 August was a revolution made by Paris, not by the Assembly. It also shows who held power in 1792: the sections and the armed crowd, not the deputies. The people who had been "passive" citizens, and who after the 1791 constitution had no vote, now acted as the force that decided.

### The September Massacres

In late August the Prussian army took Longwy (23 August) and Verdun (2 September), the last fortress on the road to Paris. Volunteers were being enrolled to march to the frontier, and Paris feared that when they left, the prisons, full of people held for crimes and some for political offences, would be broken open by counter-revolutionaries who would then attack the families left behind. Marat and other journalists stirred this fear.

Between **2 and 6 September 1792** bands of Parisians, among them National Guards and the militants of the sections, entered the prisons (the Abbaye, the Carmes, La Force, the Conciergerie, the Châtelet, the Bicêtre and the Salpêtrière) and held improvised "trials" in which many prisoners were released and others were killed at once. About **1,100 to 1,400 prisoners** were killed (estimates differ, and so do the numbers of those held), including 200 or more priests who had refused the oath; most of the victims, however, were ordinary criminals, among them many women and teenagers. The Princesse de Lamballe, a friend of the queen, was killed on 3 September. The authorities did not stop it; the Assembly and the Commune did little. A circular of 3 September from the Paris Commune's surveillance committee, to which Marat belonged, urged other towns to do the same, and similar killings took place in some provincial cities.

The massacres matter for this key question for three reasons: they showed how weak the new government's control of Paris was; they deepened the split between the Girondins, who were horrified, and the Paris radicals, who saw them as terrible but necessary; and they proved that the Revolution would meet fear of invasion with violence at home. They were not a decision of the government, but a mass panic.

### The National Convention and the abolition of the monarchy

Elections to the Convention were held in late August and early September in two stages; turnout was low. On **20 September** the Convention met in Paris; the same day, at **Valmy**, a French army under Dumouriez and Kellermann halted the Prussian advance after a long artillery exchange, the first French success of the war. (The battle itself is not needed in detail, but its effect is: it removed the immediate danger.)

On **21 September 1792** the Convention, on a proposal by Jean-Marie Collot d'Herbois, voted unanimously to abolish the monarchy. The next day, 22 September, documents were dated **Year I of the Republic**. The Republic was not set up by a single law or an announcement of a new constitution; it was declared in practice by the abolition of the monarchy on the 21st and by the new calendar of the 22nd (a new calendar that counted years from the Republic), and on 25 September the Convention declared the Republic "one and indivisible". The king's own trial and the question of the new constitution belong to the next chapter.

:::def National Convention
The assembly elected by almost all adult men in August–September 1792 to write a republican constitution. It met on 20 September 1792, abolished the monarchy on 21 September and governed France until 1795.
:::

## Why did France become a republic by 1792? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | The king's reluctance to accept limits on his power; the lack of a republican tradition | Made a stable constitutional monarchy hard, but nobody wanted a republic in 1789 |
| Medium-term | The religious split (1790–91); the flaws of the 1791 constitution; counter-revolution and emigration | Divided France and made the king and the Revolution enemies |
| Short-term | Varennes (June 1791); the Champ de Mars (July 1791); the war (April 1792); the vetoes | Destroyed trust in the king; made him seem a traitor |
| Triggers | Brunswick Manifesto (25 July); the fédérés in Paris; defeat and the fall of Verdun | Set off the insurrection of 10 August and the massacres |

:::key A judgement you can defend
**Varennes** was the necessary cause: it destroyed trust in Louis XVI and made a republic imaginable. But the Assembly rescued the monarchy afterwards, and most deputies still wanted a king in September 1791. What turned distrust into overthrow was the **war**, because military defeat made the king's vetoes and his foreign connections look like treason, and because the Brunswick Manifesto and the arrival of the fédérés gave Paris a reason and a force to act on 10 August. The Republic was made by war and by Paris, more than by anyone's plan for a republic.
:::

:::exam Linking the causes
The strongest answers explain the **chain**: religion made the king an opponent of the Revolution; Varennes proved it; the Champ de Mars split the patriots; the war turned the king's hostility into a question of national survival; 10 August answered it. A weaker answer lists the events. Also separate **what the deputies wanted** (a constitutional monarchy, until the end) from **what happened** (a republic). The Republic was the unplanned result of a series of crises.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **William Doyle**, *The Oxford History of the French Revolution* (1989), is a clear narrative which shows how the war of 1792 wrecked the constitutional monarchy.
- **Peter McPhee**, *The French Revolution 1789–1799* (2002), stresses the role of ordinary people, in the countryside and in Paris, in shaping the Revolution's direction.
- **Timothy Tackett**, *When the King Took Flight* (2003), argues that Varennes was a turning point in people's belief in a conspiracy against the Revolution.
- **John McManners**, *The French Revolution and the Church* (1969), argues that the religious split was among the most damaging errors of the Constituent Assembly.
You can use these at AS to show that causes are weighed differently, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Louis XVI tried to flee from Paris in June 1791.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Louis XVI did not like what the Revolution had done to him. He was a prisoner in the Tuileries and the Civil Constitution of the Clergy had upset him because he was religious. So he escaped on 20 June 1791 with his family and went towards the border but he was recognised at Varennes and brought back."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it describes his feelings and then the journey. "Did not like" is not explained, and no factor has any detail. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was religion. The Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790) and the oath of November 1790 forced priests to swear to the state, and the Pope condemned it in March and April 1791. Louis was a devout Catholic and believed he was being made to betray his conscience. On 18 April 1791 a crowd stopped his family leaving for Saint-Cloud, where he meant to take Easter communion from a priest who had refused the oath. A second reason was his loss of power. Under the constitution being finished in 1791 he would have only a suspensive veto, and he had lived under the eye of the Paris crowd and the National Guard since the October Days of 1789."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add a third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was that he believed he had a chance. The Marquis de Bouillé had loyal troops at Montmédy, near the frontier, and Marie Antoinette's brother, the Emperor Leopold, might support a restoration. The death of Mirabeau on 2 April removed the one adviser who had tried to keep the king and the Revolution together, and made the king more willing to take the risk.

These reasons were linked. The loss of power made the king feel a prisoner; the religious quarrel turned that resentment into a matter of conscience, so that submission seemed a sin and not only a defeat; and the chance of help made escape look possible. The decisive reason was the religious one. Louis had accepted every earlier reduction of his power, including the loss of his authority in October 1789, but the stopping of his journey on 18 April 1791 showed him that he could not even practise his religion, and he fled two months later. He did not mean to leave France, only to negotiate from strength, which is why his declaration left in the Tuileries attacked the Assembly while calling on the French to return to their king."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected**, with a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

Their Majesties regard the situation of the King of France as a matter of common interest to all the sovereigns of Europe. They hope that the powers whose help is asked will not refuse to use, together with them, the most effective means to put the King of France in a position to lay the foundations of a monarchical government. Then, and only then, they are resolved to act promptly and together with the forces needed. In the meantime they will order their troops to be ready for active service.

*Adapted from the Declaration of Pillnitz, issued by Leopold II, Holy Roman Emperor, and Frederick William II, King of Prussia, 27 August 1791.*

**Source B**

The allied courts have taken up arms to end the anarchy inside France, to stop the attacks on the throne and the altar, and to restore to the King the security and freedom he has lost. The inhabitants of Paris are to submit at once to the King. If the Tuileries Palace is attacked, or the least violence or outrage is done to the King, the Queen or the royal family, the allied sovereigns will take an exemplary and unforgettable vengeance by giving up the city of Paris to military execution and total destruction.

*Adapted from the Brunswick Manifesto, issued in the name of the Duke of Brunswick, commander of the Austrian and Prussian armies, 25 July 1792.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about foreign attitudes to the Revolution in France.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 5 marks).** "Both sources are from foreign rulers who want to help Louis XVI. Source A says the powers will act together and Source B says Paris will be destroyed. Source B is more threatening. They are both written by kings so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One similarity and one difference, but unsupported by quotation and the point about bias is a generalisation. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources agree that the foreign monarchs claim to be acting on behalf of the French king and that his position is a concern of all of them. Source A calls his situation 'a matter of common interest to all the sovereigns of Europe' and speaks of restoring a 'monarchical government'; Source B says the allies have come to 'restore to the King the security and freedom he has lost'. Both also threaten force: Source A tells the troops to be 'ready for active service', and Source B promises 'military execution'.

They differ sharply in how definite the threat is. Source A is **conditional**: the sovereigns will act 'then, and only then', if the other powers agree to help. Source B is **unconditional and specific**: it orders the people of Paris to submit, and names the punishment, 'total destruction', for any harm to the royal family. Source A is a statement of concern, Source B an ultimatum.

The context explains the difference. Source A was issued after Varennes, when the Emperor Leopold wished to help his sister but did not want a war, and so he made action depend on all the powers joining. He and the Prussian king knew that Britain would not, so the declaration promised in effect nothing, though it pleased the émigrés, who were pressing for intervention. By July 1792 the position had changed: France had declared war on Austria in April, and Source B is the work of an army commander whose forces were about to invade; it was drafted with the help of émigrés, who wanted to frighten Paris into submission. It was addressed to the French as well as to the allies' own side, which explains its threatening tone.

As evidence, Source A is better evidence of the **caution** of the great powers in 1791, and shows that the Revolution was not yet in serious danger from abroad. Source B shows how far hostility had grown by 1792, but it is poor evidence of the allies' real intentions: it was a threat designed to frighten, and it was written by men who underestimated Paris. Its real importance was in its effect on the French, who read it as proof that the king was allied with the invaders."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained by dating and purpose (Leopold's caution in 1791, war and émigré influence in 1792); and a final comparison of the sources' value for the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The flight to Varennes was the main reason why France became a republic by 1792.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the declaration left in the Tuileries; the king's word no longer trusted; Cordeliers petition for a republic (July 1791); the idea of a republic discussed for the first time; Tackett.
- Against 1, the Assembly saved the monarchy: the "kidnap" fiction, inviolability (15 July), the Feuillants, the constitution accepted on 13–14 September 1791.
- Against 2, the war: April 1792, defeat, vetoes, 20 June, Brunswick Manifesto, 10 August.
- Against 3, Paris and the sections: the Insurrectionary Commune, the fédérés, the Swiss Guard.
- Judgement: Varennes was the necessary cause; the war was the decisive one. Criterion: what turned distrust into overthrow.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The flight to Varennes was the main reason. Louis XVI left a declaration in the Tuileries in June 1791 attacking the Revolution and he was arrested at Varennes and brought back. The Assembly said he had been kidnapped, but nobody believed it. After this people did not trust the king and the Cordeliers asked for a republic. So it was the main reason France became a republic."

*Commentary.* Accurate, but it stops at the effect of Varennes. The last sentence is an assertion: distrust is not the same as the overthrow of the monarchy, and the paragraph does not say what else was needed. An answer made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet Varennes did not make France a republic in 1791, and that is the strongest argument against the statement. The Assembly rescued the monarchy: it declared that the king had been kidnapped, made him inviolable on 15 July, and the Feuillants split from the Jacobins on 16 July to defend a constitutional monarchy. The Champ de Mars petition for his removal on 17 July was shot at, and radicals were driven underground. In September 1791 the king swore to the constitution, and the new Legislative Assembly had about 260 Feuillants and no republican party at all. Varennes damaged trust, but in September 1791 most deputies still wanted a king.

What changed between September 1791 and August 1792 was the war. Declared on 20 April 1792, it went wrong at once, and the king's vetoes of the decrees on refractory priests and on a camp of volunteers, and his dismissal of the Girondin ministers in June, seemed to many to be the acts of a man working with the enemy. The Brunswick Manifesto of 25 July, with its threat to Paris, appeared to confirm it. The sections of Paris, which had the means (armed National Guards and the fédérés who had marched from Marseille and elsewhere) and now had a motive, set up the Insurrectionary Commune and attacked the Tuileries on 10 August. The Assembly suspended the king only because the people already held the palace.

Overall, Varennes was a necessary cause of the Republic, because it destroyed the trust that made a constitutional monarchy workable, and because after it the idea of a republic could be spoken. But it was not the main reason. The monarchy survived Varennes with the support of most deputies and the National Guard. What destroyed it was the war, which turned the distrust Varennes had created into the belief that the king was a traitor in a national emergency, and gave Paris both a reason and the force to act. The statement is therefore true of the origins of the king's fall but not of its timing and cause."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (what turned distrust into overthrow) and separates the necessary cause from the decisive one. With an opening paragraph on the case for Varennes, this is Level 5 (19–20).

**Question.** 'The war of 1792 destroyed the French monarchy.' How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: D (the Assembly itself blames the king's conduct "in a war undertaken in his name"); A (war was meant to test and purge, so it was expected to change the regime). Qualify: B (Robespierre foresaw that war would be dangerous, but says nothing of the monarchy). Challenge: C (the king's hostility and the break with the Revolution were plain nearly a year before the war).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a Girondin leader arguing for a policy before it happened. It is strong evidence of **why war was wanted** and of the Girondin belief that war would shake the regime, but it is persuasive and forward-looking, so it cannot show what war actually did. Its words about "despotism" show the Girondins thinking of kings in general, not yet of a republic.
- **B**: an opponent's warning, in a debate, written for the Jacobin audience. It is useful evidence that not every patriot wanted war, and that the war's dangers were foreseen, but it concerns the invasion of foreign countries, not the king.
- **C**: the king's own account of his grievances, written to justify his flight, so it is one-sided and self-pitying. As evidence it is powerful for a different reason: it was **his own** declaration, and it shows he had rejected the Revolution before the war. It was left behind by someone whose purpose was to rally support, but it had the opposite effect.
- **D**: an official act of the Assembly passed on 10 August, with the Tuileries already in the hands of the insurgents. It was drafted to give legal form to a revolution the deputies had not made, and to cast the blame on the king. That makes it a source for **how the Assembly justified** the overthrow, and it is good evidence of what the deputies *said* the cause was. It may play down the role of the crowd, the fédérés and the Commune.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A and D support the view that war destroyed the monarchy, but from opposite ends. Brissot in Source A expected war to 'purge' France of 'the vices of despotism'; Source D, eight months later, says the 'distrust' inspired by the king's conduct 'in a war undertaken in his name' was the main source of the nation's evils. Our knowledge confirms D: defeats from April 1792, the king's vetoes of the decrees on priests and on a camp of volunteers, and the Brunswick Manifesto of 25 July made him look like an ally of the enemy. Source B does not mention the king, but its warning that war could be dangerous for those who start it is borne out by what followed. Source C challenges the view: in June 1791 the king was already declaring that the Revolution had stripped him of power and the Civil Constitution had violated his conscience, so the trust that made a constitutional monarchy possible had gone long before April 1792. Source D must also be read with care, since it justified an insurrection the Assembly did not control. Overall the sources support the view to a large extent, because war was what turned the distrust of Source C into the overthrow described in Source D. But C shows that the war was not the only cause: it worked on a trust already broken by Varennes. The sources therefore agree that war was the decisive cause of the monarchy's fall, but not that it was the only one."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1791–92; the answer explains where sources support, qualify and challenge; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the Civil Constitution of the Clergy the most divisive reform of 1789–91?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Civil Constitution of the Clergy was passed on 12 July 1790. It reduced the number of dioceses to 83, one for each department, and said that bishops and priests would be elected by the voters and paid by the state. The Pope was not allowed to confirm bishops. In November 1790 all priests had to swear an oath to the constitution. About half of the priests refused. The Pope condemned the Civil Constitution in 1791. Louis XVI signed it in August 1790 but he did not like it. So the Civil Constitution was very divisive."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it lists what the law did and then asserts the conclusion in the last sentence. It never says *who* was divided, *why*, or how this compares with other reforms such as the assignats or the active and passive citizens. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description, few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The Civil Constitution was the most divisive reform because it split a country that had been united in 1789 along a line, religion, that touched every parish. The oath of November 1790 forced each priest to choose, and the half who refused (in the west and north-east, most of them) became leaders of resistance, so that in some places the Revolution met open hostility for the first time. By comparison, the division between active and passive citizens separated rich from poor, but it did not force ordinary families to take sides in the way the oath did. The Le Chapelier law angered workers but did not threaten the faith of a whole region. It also turned the king from a reluctant partner into an opponent, as the stopping of his journey to Saint-Cloud on 18 April 1791 showed." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name the three political groupings of 1791–92 and say what each wanted.
A: The Feuillants (from July 1791) wanted to end the Revolution and keep a constitutional monarchy under Louis XVI. The Girondins (Brissot, Vergniaud) wanted war to expose the king and spread liberty. The radical Jacobins and Cordeliers (Robespierre, Danton, Marat) wanted a wider vote, distrusted the king and, by 1792, many wanted him removed.
Q: Give three reasons why counter-revolution failed before 1792.
A: Any three of: the émigrés were divided and abroad; foreign help was slow and self-interested (Pillnitz was conditional); many peasants and buyers of Church land had gained from the Revolution; the king would not openly lead a counter-revolution; their plots were small and easily broken up.
Q: What were assignats, and why did they cause problems?
A: Paper securities backed by Church land, authorised in December 1789 and used as paper money from 1790. They solved the immediate debt, but as more were printed (especially to pay for the army) they lost value and prices rose.
Q: How did the Assembly reorganise local government and justice?
A: France was divided into 83 departments (15 January 1790) with elected councils and officials, and the intendants were abolished. Justice was reformed in 1790: elected judges, justices of the peace, juries for criminal trials, the parlements and lettres de cachet abolished.
Q: What was the oath of 1790, and what did it do to France?
A: Priests in public posts had to swear to maintain the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (decree of 27 November 1790). About half refused. France then had constitutional and refractory clergy, split geographically (the west and north-east refused most), and the Pope condemned the reform in 1791.
Q: Why did Louis XVI flee in June 1791, and what were the results?
A: He had lost power, was a virtual prisoner, and was angry about the Civil Constitution (a crowd stopped him leaving for Saint-Cloud in April). He hoped to reach loyal troops at Montmédy. He was arrested at Varennes. The Assembly said he had been kidnapped, but he was no longer trusted and the republic was now openly discussed.
Q: What happened on the Champ de Mars on 17 July 1791?
A: The National Guard under Lafayette fired on a crowd signing a petition for the king's removal, after martial law was declared. Estimates of the dead vary from about a dozen to perhaps fifty. The radicals were driven underground and patriots were split.
Q: Why did France declare war on Austria on 20 April 1792, and why did the king agree?
A: The Girondins wanted war to expose the king and spread liberty; the Feuillant generals wanted glory; Louis and Marie Antoinette hoped for defeat that would let the Austrians restore the king's power. Only Robespierre argued against it.
Q: What was the sequence from the Brunswick Manifesto to the Republic?
A: Manifesto (25 July) → fédérés and sections form the Insurrectionary Commune → Tuileries stormed (10 August) → king suspended, Convention called → September Massacres (2–6 September) → Convention meets (20 September) → monarchy abolished (21 September) → Year I (22 September).

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Civil Constitution of the Clergy divided France.
A: Indicative content: the Assembly reorganised the Church without the Pope or the clergy's consent (elected bishops, no papal confirmation, state salaries); the oath of November 1790 forced every priest to choose, and about half refused; the Pope condemned the reform in March and April 1791, so Catholics had to choose between loyalty to Rome and to the nation; refractory priests became leaders of resistance, especially in the west; the king's conscience made him an opponent. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: the oath was the mechanism, the Pope's condemnation made refusal a matter of faith, and the geography of refusal shows the reform divided regions as well as individuals. It ends with a judgement on the main reason (e.g. the oath, because it turned a disagreement over organisation into a test of loyalty for every parish).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Champ de Mars massacre of 17 July 1791 weakened the constitutional monarchy.
A: Indicative content: it came after Varennes, when the Assembly had protected the king; the Cordeliers and radical Jacobins had called for his removal; the National Guard under Lafayette and Bailly's martial law killed unarmed petitioners (a dozen to perhaps fifty, estimates differ); the radicals were driven underground and the popular movement came to see the National Guard and the constitutionalists as enemies; the Jacobins split (Feuillants against Jacobins); the constitutionalists were left defending a king nobody trusted with the support of a narrow group. Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why counter-revolutionary groups failed to overthrow the Revolution before 1792.
A: Indicative content: the émigrés were divided, abroad and without a following; foreign powers were distracted and self-interested (Pillnitz was conditional and Britain refused); royalist plots at home (Favras, the Day of the Daggers, Jalès) were small and easily broken; those who had gained from the August Decrees and bought Church land had a stake in the Revolution; the king would not lead a rising in person until Varennes, and then failed. A Level 4 answer connects: the counter-revolution was weak in France but still frightened the revolutionaries, which is why its failure did not end the danger.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Civil Constitution of the Clergy was the most divisive reform of 1789–91.' How far do you agree?
A: For: it split Catholics and parishes; the oath forced a personal choice; the Pope's condemnation; refractory priests led resistance in the west; it drove the king into opposition (April 1791). Against: the assignats and land sales divided buyers from others and caused inflation; active and passive citizens separated rich from poor and left women and servants without a vote; the Le Chapelier law angered workers; the new taxes were resisted; the reforms of local government and justice were widely welcomed. Level 5 (17–20) sets a criterion, e.g. "which reform divided people who had previously been united, and for the longest?", and judges that the Church reform was the most divisive because it cut across class lines and made opponents out of people who accepted the rest, though social divisions caused by the vote and the Le Chapelier law were deeper in towns.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources B and C (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about criticism of the Revolution in 1790–91.

Differences: B is a conservative critic, who thinks the Revolution has gone too far and destroyed the old order; C is a radical critic, who thinks it has not gone far enough, because the Declaration of 1789 excluded women. B is by a British statesman, writing from abroad in response to the events of October 1789; C is by a Parisian participant, writing for French readers in 1791. Explanation: the different positions come from different aims and different distance from events. Level 4 explains these through context and judges which is better evidence of the criticism the Revolution faced (e.g. B shows the foreign conservative reaction that fed the fear of intervention, C shows internal criticism from supporters).
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources agree that the reforms of 1789–91 gave the people of France more freedom?

H: Use your own knowledge of the Assembly's other reforms to judge A: what else did the Church reform change?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: A gives active citizens a say in choosing their clergy, a real change from appointment by the Crown and Pope; B, though hostile, shows that contemporaries saw the old order swept away. Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How successful were the Constituent Assembly's financial reforms of 1789–91?

Level 5 judges against a criterion, e.g. "did it solve the problem that had caused the Revolution?": yes in principle (equal taxes, debt cleared by land sales), no in practice (revenue remained short and paper money would fuel inflation).

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Timothy Tackett argued that the flight to Varennes changed how the French saw the king and the Revolution. How far does the evidence of 1791–92 support this view?

:::recap
- **Groups**: Jacobins (club of the patriots, then of the left), Feuillants (July 1791, keep the constitutional monarchy), Girondins (Brissot, war party), Cordeliers (Danton, Marat, popular and radical). Counter-revolution (émigrés, Artois, refractory priests) failed because it was divided, abroad and badly supported, but it kept the revolutionaries afraid.
- **Reforms**: Church lands nationalised (2 November 1789); assignats (December 1789; paper money 1790; inflation); new taxes on all (land, property, trades); 83 departments (15 January 1790); elected judges, juries, new penal code; Civil Constitution of the Clergy (12 July 1790); oath (27 November 1790): about half the clergy refused; Pope condemned (1791).
- **Constitution of 1791**: constitutional monarchy; single-chamber Legislative Assembly of 745; suspensive veto; active and passive citizens.
- **Varennes** (20–21 June 1791): the king fled to Montmédy and was arrested; the Assembly said he was kidnapped; he lost the nation's trust. **Champ de Mars** (17 July 1791): National Guard fired on petitioners; patriots split.
- **War**: declared on Austria 20 April 1792 (Girondins to expose the king, Louis hoping for defeat); defeats; vetoes; 20 June; Brunswick Manifesto (25 July); **10 August**: Tuileries stormed, king suspended.
- **Republic**: September Massacres (2–6 September; about 1,100–1,400 killed); Valmy and the Convention (20 September); monarchy abolished (21 September); Year I (22 September).
- **Judgement**: Varennes was the necessary cause (trust broken); the war was the decisive cause (distrust turned into treason, and Paris acted).
:::
