## What is the question really asking?

*What factors led to the formation of Indonesia and what challenges did it face up to the mid-1960s?* is two questions in one. The first is a question about **causes**: why an independent Indonesia came into existence in 1945–49. The second is about **problems and outcomes**: what went wrong for the new state, and why its first republic, built on parliamentary democracy and then on Sukarno's Guided Democracy, ended in 1965–67. Keep four questions in mind.
1. **Who made independence?** Was it the proclamation of 1945 by Sukarno and Hatta, the pressure of the young, the revolution, or the Dutch decision to hand over in 1949? Each answer gives a different date and a different hero.
2. **Why did the Dutch lose?** The Netherlands had more soldiers, money and weapons than the Republic. Was it beaten by Indonesian resistance, by international pressure, or by its own weakness?
3. **What problems did the state face?** Was the real trouble political (the constitution and Sukarno), economic, regional or social?
4. **Why did Sukarno fall?** Was it the army, the failure of Guided Democracy, the economy, Konfrontasi or the 30 September Movement?

The last two sections, "Why did the Dutch fail to re-establish control?" and "Why did the New Order replace Guided Democracy?", come back to these.

:::def Pancasila
The "five principles" that became the philosophical basis of the Indonesian state. Sukarno first set them out in a speech on 1 June 1945; the version in the preamble to the 1945 constitution reads: belief in one God; just and civilised humanity; the unity of Indonesia; democracy guided by wisdom in consultation and representation; and social justice for all Indonesians.
:::

:::def Indonesian Revolution
The armed and diplomatic struggle between the Republic of Indonesia and the Netherlands from 17 August 1945 to the transfer of sovereignty on 27 December 1949. It combined a war (the Dutch called their two campaigns "police actions"), negotiation, and a social upheaval in which many Indonesians seized power at local level.
:::

## Securing independence: the Japanese, the nationalists and the young, 1944–45

### Japan's promises

By 1944 Japan was losing the war in the Pacific. After the fall of Saipan in July, General Tojo resigned, and his successor as Prime Minister, **Koiso Kuniaki**, announced on **7 September 1944** that the East Indies would be granted independence "in the future" (see [The route to independence (3)](#hist.d.malaysia.3)). No date was given, and Java and Sumatra were controlled by different Japanese armies and the eastern islands by the Navy, but the promise changed politics. The Indonesian flag was allowed to be flown, *Indonesia Raya* could be sung, and the nationalists now had something to hold the Japanese to.

### The Investigating Committee and the Preparatory Committee

The Japanese made the promise concrete in 1945. The syllabus names a "1944 Preparatory Committee for Indonesian Independence". Be careful with the names and dates, because two different bodies were set up, in 1945 and not 1944, and the first step was the 1944 promise.

| Body | Date | Members and chair | What it did |
|---|---|---|---|
| **BPUPK**, the Investigating Committee for Preparatory Work for Independence | Announced 1 March 1945; members named 29 April; inaugurated 28 May 1945 | About sixty members, among them Sukarno, Hatta, Mohammad Yamin and Supomo, chaired by Dr Radjiman Wediodiningrat; a few Japanese members | Debated the philosophy of the state (first session 29 May–1 June) and the constitution (second session 10–17 July) |
| **PPKI**, the Preparatory Committee for Indonesian Independence | Formed 7 August 1945 | A smaller body under Sukarno as chairman and Hatta as vice-chairman | Adopted the constitution and chose the president and vice-president on 18 August 1945 |

If an exam question mentions the "1944 Preparatory Committee", treat it as referring to this process: the promise of September 1944, the BPUPK of 1945 and the PPKI of August 1945. Describe the committees accurately and say which came first.

### Pancasila

At the BPUPK's first session the question was what kind of state Indonesia should be, and in particular whether it should be an Islamic state. On **1 June 1945** Sukarno answered in a speech in which he proposed **five principles**: (1) Indonesian nationalism, (2) internationalism or humanity, (3) consensus or democracy, (4) social welfare and (5) belief in God, with tolerance. He called the five together **Pancasila** (from the Sanskrit for "five principles"). It was a compromise designed to hold together secular nationalists, Muslims, Christians and Hindus.

A committee of nine, the *Panitia Sembilan*, met in June to turn the speech into a preamble, and on **22 June** produced the text known as the **Jakarta Charter**. It put belief in God first and added seven words: "with the obligation for Muslims to follow Islamic law". On **18 August 1945**, the day after the proclamation, the PPKI **deleted** those words, because, according to Hatta's later account, Christian representatives from eastern Indonesia objected that the clause would discriminate against them. Hatta persuaded the Muslim delegates to accept the change for the sake of unity. The five principles in the preamble of the 1945 constitution are the version quoted in the definition above.

:::key Why Pancasila mattered
1. It gave the new state an **ideology** that was neither Islamic nor wholly secular, so that all the main groups could accept it.
2. It was a **compromise**, and the argument over the Jakarta Charter and the place of Islam continued: it came back in the Constituent Assembly of the 1950s and in the Darul Islam rebellions.
3. Sukarno, and later Suharto, used Pancasila to **define loyalty**: Suharto's New Order made it the only basis of every organisation and called opponents "anti-Pancasila".
:::

### The surrender, the young and the proclamation

The final days show how independence was proclaimed. They are one of the most examined parts of the story.

On **9 August** Sukarno, Hatta and Dr Radjiman flew to Dalat, in Vietnam, to see Field Marshal **Terauchi**, the Japanese commander in South-East Asia. On 12 August Terauchi told them that Japan would grant independence and that the PPKI should prepare it. They returned to Jakarta on **14 August**. The next day, **15 August**, Japan announced its surrender.

The **pemuda** (the young men), many of them students, had been waiting for this. Groups of young activists, among them Sukarni, Chairul Saleh and Wikana, based at student hostels such as Menteng 31 and Prapatan 10, had been in touch with Sutan Sjahrir, who had heard the news by secret radio and who urged an immediate proclamation, before the Allies arrived. They believed that independence **declared by Indonesians**, and not **granted by Japan**, was the only independence that would be respected. Sukarno and Hatta hesitated. They wanted the PPKI, a body set up by the Japanese, to proclaim independence in an orderly way, partly because they feared a bloody confrontation with the Japanese troops still in Java and partly because they did not trust the young to control events.

In the early hours of **16 August** a group of the young men, with a Peta officer, took Sukarno and Hatta from Jakarta to the town of **Rengasdengklok**, east of the capital, intending to put them out of reach of Japanese influence and to force them to declare independence. This is usually called a "kidnapping". Sukarno and Hatta refused to be forced, however, and that evening Ahmad Subardjo came from Jakarta to negotiate, with an assurance from Admiral **Maeda Tadashi**, the Japanese Navy's liaison officer, that the Japanese would not stop them. They returned to Jakarta that night.

The proclamation text was drafted that night in Maeda's house, since the Admiral's Navy status gave some protection. The young men's more radical draft was rejected, and Sukarno wrote the final version, with Hatta and Subardjo. The meeting adjourned at about 5 a.m. on **17 August**. At 10 a.m., at Sukarno's home at Jalan Pegangsaan Timur 56 in Jakarta, Sukarno read the proclamation, with Hatta beside him, and the red-and-white flag was raised.

:::note Source: the Proclamation of Independence
*Adapted from an English translation of the Proclamation of Independence, read by Sukarno at Jalan Pegangsaan Timur 56, Jakarta, 17 August 1945.*

We, the people of Indonesia, hereby declare the independence of Indonesia. Matters concerning the transfer of power and other matters will be executed in an orderly manner and in the shortest possible time. Jakarta, 17 August 1945. In the name of the people of Indonesia. Sukarno, Hatta.

**What it shows.** It is **short** and legalistic, signed by two men "in the name of the people". It says **"transfer of power"**, a phrase acceptable to the Japanese, because they understood it as an administrative handover and not as a grant of sovereignty. It makes no claim about the form of the state, which shows that the constitution had yet to be decided, and it was written within days of Japan's surrender, which explains its speed.
:::

On **18 August** the PPKI adopted the **1945 constitution**, which set up a strong presidency and removed the seven words from the Jakarta Charter, and elected **Sukarno as president and Hatta as vice-president**. A Central Indonesian National Committee (KNIP) was set up to assist the president.

:::key Who made independence on 17 August 1945?
1. **Sukarno and Hatta**: they wrote and read the proclamation, and had the standing to make it count; but they hesitated and wished to act through the Japanese-sponsored PPKI.
2. **The pemuda and students**: they forced the pace, by their pressure on 15–16 August and the Rengasdengklok affair, and made the proclamation an Indonesian act and not a Japanese gift.
3. **The Japanese**: they created the committees, the army and the promise; and the Navy officer Maeda gave protection. The surrender of 15 August, not the Japanese promise, created the opportunity.
4. **Nationalism before 1942**: the flag, the anthem, the language and the leaders who made the proclamation were all older than the Japanese.
:::

:::note The role of Japan in the proclamation
Benedict Anderson's *Java in a Time of Revolution* (1972) stresses the pemuda, their training and their impatience, and describes a generation shaped by the occupation. George Kahin's *Nationalism and Revolution in Indonesia* (1952) places the proclamation within a long nationalist movement. M. C. Ricklefs, *A History of Modern Indonesia since c.1200* (4th edition 2008), emphasises that the older leaders were cautious and the young forced the issue, but that all of them were responding to the sudden Japanese collapse. You should present the proclamation as the result of all these things, and be ready to rank them.
:::

## The Indonesian Revolution, 1945–49

### Reactions to the proclamation

The news of independence travelled by radio and word of mouth, and the Japanese tried to stop it. Across Java and then Sumatra the reaction was fast. Young men took over government offices, railway stations and radio stations, seized Japanese weapons (from August to October, in many places the Japanese surrendered arms to the young), and formed fighting groups, as well as a Republican army, set up in stages and led, from 12 November 1945, by the Peta teacher **Sudirman**. A civilian government was formed in Jakarta, under a parliamentary system in which **Sutan Sjahrir** became Prime Minister in November 1945. In January 1946 the Republic moved its capital to **Yogyakarta**, to be safe from the Dutch and the Allies in Jakarta.

The **Dutch** refused to recognise the Republic. They regarded it as a creation of the Japanese and Sukarno as a collaborator. Their policy, directed by the Lieutenant Governor-General **Hubertus van Mook**, was to restore Dutch authority and to give the Indies a new form: Queen Wilhelmina had promised in a broadcast of December 1942 a partnership within the Kingdom. The Dutch army, however, was in the Netherlands, and the Allies, who had to accept the Japanese surrender, controlled the islands.

The first months were violent. Between October and December 1945 a period known as **Bersiap** ("be ready") saw attacks on Dutch and Eurasian civilians who had been released from Japanese camps, and on Chinese and others who were suspected of siding with the Dutch. Estimates of those killed range widely, from about 3,500 to about 30,000, and the Netherlands Institute for War Documentation (NIOD) puts the Dutch casualties at about 5,500. These figures are disputed and the causes (fear, revenge, disorder, and the movement of people) were complicated: the point for this chapter is that the early revolution was a time of great disorder in which the Republic's leaders struggled to control the armed groups.

### The British, Surabaya and the re-occupation

British forces under South-East Asia Command (Admiral Mountbatten) were given the task of disarming the Japanese and freeing the Allied prisoners and civilian internees, and they landed in Java and Sumatra in late September and October 1945. They were not sent to put the Dutch back in power, but their commanders were soon drawn into the conflict, since the Dutch administration, the Netherlands Indies Civil Administration (NICA), followed them. The most famous clash was at **Surabaya**, in East Java. After the death of the British commander Brigadier Mallaby on 30 October, the British issued an ultimatum, and from **10 November 1945** the city was bombarded and fought over for about three weeks. Thousands of Indonesian fighters and civilians were killed (estimates run from about 6,300 to 15,000). The Indonesians lost the city but showed that they would fight. Indonesians celebrate 10 November as Heroes' Day.

Britain's role matters for the question of why the Dutch lost. The British **would not fight a colonial war** for the Netherlands, and their Labour government encouraged the Dutch to negotiate. By the end of November 1946 British troops had left, and the Dutch had to bring in their own, which they did through 1946 and 1947, building an army of well over 100,000 men.

### Peace talks and the attempted coup, 1946

The two sides first negotiated under British chairmanship, then on their own. The talks held at Hoge Veluwe in the Netherlands in April 1946 failed. Inside the Republic, there was a bitter quarrel between those who wanted to negotiate (Sjahrir, the Prime Minister, and Hatta) and those who wanted a total struggle, "100 per cent *merdeka*" (independence). The opposition front, the *Persatuan Perjuangan* (Struggle Front), was led by **Tan Malaka**, the old communist who thought Sjahrir was selling out. In late June 1946 officers kidnapped Sjahrir, and on **3 July 1946** Major-General Sudarsono tried to force Sukarno to dismiss the government and put power in the hands of the opposition. The attempted coup failed, because Sukarno refused and the army stayed loyal. The affair showed that the Republic was divided and that the negotiating line would be tested by its own radicals.

### The Linggadjati Agreement

Negotiations resumed under the British diplomat Lord Killearn and a Dutch commission headed by **Willem Schermerhorn**. At the hill resort of Linggadjati, near Cirebon, the two sides **initialled** an agreement on **15 November 1946**, and it was signed in Jakarta on **25 March 1947**. Its main terms were these.
- The Netherlands recognised the Republic's **de facto authority** over **Java, Madura and Sumatra**.
- The two sides would work for a federal **United States of Indonesia** by 1 January 1949, of which the Republic would be one part, with Borneo and a "Great East" as the others.
- The United States of Indonesia and the Netherlands would form a **Netherlands–Indonesian Union** headed by the Dutch Crown.
- Disputes would be settled by arbitration.

:::def Linggadjati Agreement
The agreement initialled on 15 November 1946 and signed on 25 March 1947, by which the Netherlands recognised the Republic's authority over Java, Madura and Sumatra and the two sides agreed to create a federal United States of Indonesia within a Netherlands–Indonesian Union. It collapsed within months because the sides read it differently.
:::

**Why it failed.**
1. **Different readings.** The Dutch saw Linggadjati as a "programme of principles": the Republic would be a part of a Dutch-led federation, and the Dutch would keep control of defence, finance and foreign policy during the transition. The Republic saw it as recognition of a state that would be sovereign and as the first step to an equal partnership. The words "cooperation" and "federal" were read in opposite ways.
2. **Dutch federal policy.** The Dutch set up separate states of their own making, beginning with the State of East Indonesia at a conference at Denpasar in December 1946, so as to surround and weaken the Republic. Republicans saw this as proof that the Dutch wanted to divide them.
3. **Opposition in both camps.** In the Republic, the Persatuan Perjuangan, the PNI and the Masyumi denounced the agreement as a betrayal, and in the Netherlands many politicians believed that the government had given too much; Dutch public opinion was split (a survey showed 38 per cent in favour and 36 per cent against, by one account).
4. **Economic pressure.** The Dutch blockade and the Republic's lack of arms and revenue worsened the mood. The Dutch wanted the plantations and oil of Java and Sumatra back, which were vital for the Dutch economy after the war.

In late May 1947 the Dutch Commission-General offered proposals for an interim federal government, which the Republic rejected. Sjahrir resigned on 27 June, and **Amir Sjarifuddin** became Prime Minister. On 20 July 1947 the Dutch announced that they no longer considered themselves bound.

### The first police action, July 1947

At midnight on **20–21 July 1947** the Dutch launched **Operation Product**, which they called a "police action" to restore order within a Dutch territory, so as to avoid the word war. Tens of thousands of Dutch soldiers attacked, and in about two weeks they took the rich plantation, oil and port areas of Java and Sumatra. The Republic held the interior of central Java and the poorer parts of Sumatra.

### The United Nations and the USA

This was the point at which the **international dimension** began. India and Australia, both newly active in world politics, brought the conflict to the **United Nations Security Council** at the end of July 1947, and the Council called on 1 August 1947 for a ceasefire. The Dutch insisted that the matter was internal, but the Council set up a **Committee of Good Offices** to mediate. It had three members: one chosen by the Dutch (Belgium), one by the Republic (Australia) and a third chosen by both (the USA, represented by Frank Graham). The USA thus entered the dispute as a mediator and a power that the Republic wanted to attract.

### The Renville Agreement

Under American chairmanship, negotiations took place aboard the American naval vessel **USS Renville** in Jakarta Bay, and the agreement was signed on **17 January 1948**. It accepted the ceasefire line, known as the **Van Mook Line**, that connected the most advanced Dutch positions, which left the Republic with only about a third of Java and most of Sumatra. It required the Republic to withdraw its forces from the territory behind the line. It promised a referendum within six months in each area to decide whether it would join the Republic or the United States of Indonesia, and a constitutional convention to draft a federal constitution. It was a **bad deal for the Republic**, which accepted it because it lacked ammunition and feared further attacks. It lost food-producing regions, was blockaded by the Dutch, and the Sjarifuddin cabinet, which had signed it, fell in January 1948. **Mohammad Hatta** became Prime Minister, and he led a government that cut the size of the army by a "rationalisation" that hit the left-wing units hardest. It was one of the quarrels that led to Madiun.

### The Madiun Affair, 1948

In August 1948 the veteran communist **Musso**, who had spent years in Moscow, returned to Java. He took control of the PKI, and the left-wing parties formed a People's Democratic Front under Amir Sjarifuddin, which criticised the Hatta government for compromise. On **18 September 1948**, in the town of **Madiun**, in East Java, communist and left-wing units seized government buildings and the local army headquarters, and a leader announced that the "victory" had begun. The next day Sukarno broadcast a challenge to Indonesians: to choose between himself and Hatta, or Musso and the communists. The Republic's army crushed the rising in a few weeks. Musso was shot in October, Amir Sjarifuddin and other leaders were executed in December, and tens of thousands of left-wing people were arrested, with thousands killed (the figures in accounts of the affair differ).

Madiun had a major effect on the Cold War context of the revolution. It showed that the Republic could and would suppress communism with its own forces. The Dutch had portrayed the Republic as a danger of communism; now the Republic had fought communists, and in the United States, where the Truman administration and Congress were worried about communism in Asia, many began to see Sukarno and Hatta as possible allies and the Dutch as an obstacle. Madiun is also the first confrontation between the Republic and the PKI, a conflict that returns in 1965.

### The second police action, December 1948

In December 1948 the Dutch decided to destroy the Republic. On **19 December 1948** they launched **Operation Kraai** (the "second police action"), with an air attack and parachute landing at Maguwo airfield outside **Yogyakarta**, and took the capital within hours. They captured Sukarno, Hatta, Sjahrir and other leaders, and exiled them to the island of Bangka. The Dutch believed that this would end the Republic.

It did not. **General Sudirman**, though ill, took to the countryside and led a guerrilla war, and General Nasution set up a system of "total people's defence" in Java. An **Emergency Government** under **Sjafruddin Prawiranegara** was declared in Sumatra to carry on, and it did. On **1 March 1949** Republican troops under Lieutenant-Colonel **Suharto** attacked Yogyakarta and held it for about six hours: a small military success, but it showed that the Republic still existed and could fight, while the UN was debating.

The world's reaction was fierce. The Security Council called for a ceasefire on 24 December 1948 and in January 1949 demanded the release of the Republic's leaders and the restoration of the Republican government. India held a conference of Asian and other states in New Delhi, which condemned the Dutch action. Most important, the **United States** threatened to cut off **Marshall Plan** aid to the Netherlands, which had been receiving it for its economic recovery. The USA had grown more sympathetic to the Republic since Madiun, and now many in Congress and the public were outraged, because they saw the Dutch attack as a colonial war waged with American money.

### The Roem–Van Roijen Agreement and the Round Table Conference

Under this pressure the Dutch gave way. In the **Roem–Van Roijen Agreement** of **7 May 1949**, named after its negotiators, the Republic agreed to stop guerrilla warfare and to attend a conference, and the Dutch agreed to return the Republican leaders to Yogyakarta, which they did in July. The **Round Table Conference** met at The Hague from **23 August to 2 November 1949**, with delegations from the Netherlands, the Republic and the Dutch-created federal states. It agreed that:
- the Netherlands would transfer **sovereignty** to a **United States of Indonesia**, which would be joined with the Netherlands in a **Netherlands–Indonesian Union**, a body with few real powers, headed by the Dutch Queen;
- the status of **Dutch New Guinea** (West Irian) would be decided by negotiation within a year;
- Indonesia would take over about **4.5 billion guilders** of the debt of the Dutch East Indies government, and would not discriminate against Dutch property and firms.

Sovereignty was transferred on **27 December 1949**. Within a year the federal system had gone. The United States of Indonesia was dissolved into a unitary Republic on 17 August 1950, and Indonesia soon repudiated the Union and the debt, and began nationalising Dutch businesses. The status of West Irian was left unsolved and was a bitter dispute throughout the 1950s.

## Why did the Dutch fail to re-establish control?

The question is one of the most likely essay titles on this topic, and the answer is a balance of several factors. Sort them into **Indonesian**, **international** and **Dutch** causes.

**Indonesian resistance and the survival of the Republic.** The Republic had popular support, a large army that grew out of Peta and the local fighting groups, and leaders who had survived. It could not defeat the Dutch army in the open, but it could prevent the Dutch from controlling the countryside. The Dutch held the towns and the plantations, but could not hold the villages, and the guerrilla war after December 1948 (Sudirman, Nasution, the attack on Yogyakarta on 1 March 1949) showed it. The Republic also showed that it could suppress communism (Madiun), which won American respect, and it showed resilience after the capture of its leaders, since an Emergency Government carried on.

**International pressure.** This was the factor that decided the end. The **United Nations** turned the dispute into a world question and mediated through the Committee of Good Offices. **India and Australia** raised it in 1947, and Asian governments condemned the second police action. The **USA**, after Madiun, saw the Republic as a possible ally in the Cold War, and the Dutch attack of December 1948 led it to threaten Marshall Plan aid, the one lever that the Netherlands could not ignore.

**Dutch weaknesses.** The Netherlands was exhausted and poor after the German occupation. The war required a huge army, with more than 100,000 men, far from home, at a time when the country needed to rebuild. Dutch opinion was divided. The Dutch **federal policy** failed, because the states they created had little support and the Republic was the only body with real legitimacy. The British had refused to fight for them in 1945–46, and the Dutch could not get the Republic to bend.

:::key Three reasons why the Dutch failed
1. **Indonesian resistance**: popular support, an army that could not be destroyed and a Republic that survived the loss of its capital and leaders.
2. **International pressure**: UN mediation, India, Australia and above all the US threat to Marshall aid after the second police action.
3. **Dutch weakness**: an exhausted country, a costly army, a divided public and a federal policy that did not work.
These worked together: the Republic's resistance gave the outside world something to support, and the outside world's pressure made the cost of continuing too high for the Dutch.
:::

:::warn Do not call the police actions "wars" without explaining
The Dutch called their campaigns "police actions" to treat the revolution as a domestic matter. In exams use the term "police action" in inverted commas, or "the first (or second) police action", and say what they were: major military offensives. Do not describe Linggadjati as a "treaty of independence": it recognised the Republic's authority only over Java, Madura and Sumatra, in a federal Dutch-led union.
:::

## The challenges of the new state in the 1950s

Independence on 27 December 1949 did not end the Republic's troubles. The new state was a union of thousands of islands, with about 70 to 80 million people, no tradition of self-government, a wrecked economy and an army that had been made by a revolution and not by a constitution. This section follows the **political**, **economic** and **social** challenges in turn. The point to keep in mind is that they were linked: the political weakness of the parliamentary system made it hard to deal with the economy, and the economic problems fuelled the regional and social tensions.

### Political challenges: the constitution and the form of government

**Federal or unitary?** The settlement of 1949 created a **federal** United States of Indonesia, with the Republic as one of sixteen units and the rest the states created by the Dutch. Almost no Indonesian nationalist liked it, since it looked like the Dutch plan for dividing the country. The federal states collapsed quickly, and on **17 August 1950**, five years after the proclamation, Sukarno announced a **unitary** Republic of Indonesia with a new **Provisional Constitution**. A rebellion in the South Moluccas, which proclaimed a "Republic of the South Moluccas" in April 1950, was crushed in the same year.

**Parliamentary democracy.** The Provisional Constitution of 1950 made Sukarno president but only as a **figurehead**. Real power lay with a Prime Minister and cabinet answerable to a parliament, which was chosen, until 1955, by appointment. There were dozens of parties, none with a majority. **Cabinets rose and fell quickly**: between September 1950 and March 1957 there were six (under Natsir, Sukiman, Wilopo, Ali Sastroamidjojo twice, and Burhanuddin Harahap), and most lasted a year or less (Ali's first, at about two years, lasted longest). Each coalition depended on the support of Islamic parties (the Masyumi and Nahdlatul Ulama) and the secular nationalists (the PNI), and the PKI was never given a place in a cabinet. Instability prevented long-term economic plans.

**The army and politics.** The army was divided between officers who had been trained by the Dutch and those who had grown up in the revolution and the Japanese Peta, and it resisted the government's plan to cut its size. On **17 October 1952** officers pointed tanks and guns at the presidential palace and demanded that parliament be dissolved. Sukarno refused, and the army chief, Nasution, who had backed the demand, lost his post. The affair showed that the army was ready to intervene in politics.

**The 1955 elections.** The first general election was held on **29 September 1955**, with a turnout of about 91.5 per cent of the registered voters. It was fair and peaceful, but it did not give anyone a mandate. The four biggest parties won almost four-fifths of the votes between them (78 per cent), and the result showed that Indonesia was divided by **religion and region**.

The PNI (secular nationalists, strong in Java), Masyumi (modern Islamic party, strong in Sumatra and the outer islands), the NU (traditional Islamic, strong in rural Java) and the **PKI** (communists, strong in Java) were each in the range of 16 to 22 per cent. In the 257-seat parliament the PNI and Masyumi each won 57 seats, the NU 45 and the PKI 39, and 28 parties won at least a seat. The PKI's fourth place, with over six million votes, made it a major party and frightened its enemies. A second election, on 15 December 1955, chose a **Constituent Assembly** to write a permanent constitution, but its members were divided between those who wanted an **Islamic basis** for the state and those who wanted **Pancasila**, and it never agreed.

**Sukarno's challenge to the system.** Sukarno, who had no power in the parliamentary system, disliked it. He said that Western democracy, with its quarrelling parties, did not suit Indonesia, and on 28 October 1956 he called for the parties to be "buried". On 1 December 1956 **Hatta**, the vice-president and the leader who believed most in parliamentary government, resigned in protest, breaking the partnership of 1945. In February 1957 Sukarno announced his *Konsepsi*, a plan for a "mutual cooperation" cabinet containing all the main parties, including the PKI, and a National Council representing "functional groups". In March 1957 the army, under Nasution, persuaded him to proclaim **martial law**, and in April a non-party cabinet under Djuanda took office.

:::key The political challenges of the 1950s
1. **The form of the state**: a federal structure imposed in 1949 and replaced by a unitary republic in 1950; the argument between an Islamic and a Pancasila basis for the state.
2. **Instability**: six cabinets in under seven years, and an election in 1955 that gave no party a majority.
3. **The army**: a political force from the revolution, which showed its strength in 1952.
4. **Sukarno**: a president without power who wanted more and was ready to attack the system.
:::

### The leadership of Sukarno

Sukarno was the one national figure who could unite the Republic. He was a hero of the independence movement, a brilliant orator and a man whom Muslims, nationalists and communists all accepted. His method was to **balance** the groups, and above all the **army** and the **PKI**, so that neither could dominate him. His strengths were his appeal and his sense of the nation's symbols; his weaknesses were his lack of interest in economics (he said little about the daily needs of the people) and his habit of replacing institutions with slogans. From 1957 he used a series of slogans to justify his control: **Guided Democracy**, **Manipol-USDEK** (the "political manifesto" with the 1945 Constitution, Indonesian socialism, Guided Democracy, Guided Economy and Indonesian identity) and **Nasakom**, the union of nationalism (*Nasionalisme*), religion (*Agama*) and communism (*Komunisme*).

### Regional challenges: the rebellions

A basic political problem was that the **islands outside Java** earned most of the foreign currency (from rubber, oil and copra) but felt that Java, with the capital and the bulk of the people, took the benefit. Regional army commanders, who often ran the local economy by smuggling, resented Jakarta's control. From December 1956 army commanders in parts of Sumatra took over local government, and in March 1957 the commanders in Sulawesi did the same, taking the name **Permesta**. On **15 February 1958** a group of army officers and politicians, including several Masyumi leaders such as Sjafruddin Prawiranegara, proclaimed a rival **Revolutionary Government of the Republic of Indonesia (PRRI)** in West Sumatra, and Permesta joined. The rebels' complaints were regional autonomy, the sharing of revenue, resentment of Java and fear of communist influence in Sukarno's circle. The central army crushed them with a combined operation, and the last major rebel leaders surrendered in 1961. According to Nasution's figures there were about 7,100 military deaths on both sides, most of them on the rebel side. The United States, through the CIA, secretly helped the rebels in 1958, but the shooting down of the American pilot Allen Pope in May 1958 ended it, and the USA switched to helping the central army. The consequence was that the army's power increased, and the Masyumi was banned in 1960.

There was also a long rebellion in the name of **Islam**: **Darul Islam**, led in West Java by S. M. Kartosuwiryo, who in August 1949 proclaimed an Islamic State of Indonesia, and with branches in Aceh and South Sulawesi. It was put down in 1962, when Kartosuwiryo was captured and executed. These rebellions show how far the new state's authority was contested.

### Economic problems

The state inherited an economy shaped to benefit the Dutch.
- **War damage and dependence on exports.** Four years of Japanese rule and four of revolution had damaged plantations, transport and industries. Indonesia earned its income by exporting rubber, oil, tin and other raw materials, so that its income rose and fell with world prices. The Korean War (1950–53) raised the price of rubber and gave a brief boom, and its end brought a slump.
- **Dutch control.** The Dutch kept control of most large businesses, banks and shipping, which were protected by the Round Table agreements, and of most of the senior management. Indonesians had little training or capital. Governments tried to promote indigenous importers (the "Benteng" policy) but this often produced firms in name only, with Chinese or other partners running them.
- **Budget deficits and inflation.** Governments spent more than they raised, on the army, the civil service and subsidies, and printed money. Prices rose steadily. On 25 August 1959 the government devalued the currency by 75 per cent, and prices went on rising through the early 1960s.
- **West Irian and the Dutch firms.** The Dutch kept West New Guinea, and when the United Nations failed in November 1957 to pass a resolution on it, trade unions began to seize Dutch businesses on 3 December 1957, and the army stepped in to run them. The takeover broke the Dutch hold on the economy, but it also removed the managers who knew how to run it, and gave **army officers** a share in the economy and a motive to oppose change.
- **Population.** Java was very crowded, and population growth made it harder to raise living standards.

The result was an economy that grew little and was badly managed. These problems would worsen under Guided Democracy: by 1965 inflation was about 600 per cent, on one measure, and the state owed about 2.4 billion US dollars to foreign creditors.

### Social issues

- **The role of Islam.** Indonesia had the biggest Muslim population in the world, but the founding compromise (Pancasila, with no reference to Islamic law) disappointed many Muslims. The tension between a secular and an Islamic state gave the Darul Islam rebellions, the Constituent Assembly's deadlock and later bitter feelings between Muslim groups and the PKI.
- **The Chinese minority.** Chinese Indonesians were about 2 per cent of the population and dominated much of trade. Resentment of them was strong and the government encouraged indigenous business at their expense; in 1959 it banned foreigners from retail trade in rural areas, which hit non-citizen Chinese, and many left. Their position, as a minority linked in many minds with China and with wealth, would make them vulnerable in later crises.
- **Land and poverty.** Most Indonesians were poor farmers, many with little or no land, especially on Java, and peasants hoped for the land reform that the revolution seemed to promise. The land reform law of 1960 was not carried out, and from 1963 the PKI's peasant organisation supported "unilateral actions" by peasants to seize land. This set landowners, many of them religious leaders and army families, against the PKI.
- **Education and ideas.** The new state spread schools, and literacy rose rapidly. It also produced a generation of students who were politically alert, who would play a decisive part in 1966.
- **Regional and ethnic divisions.** Java against the outer islands, Muslims against Christians and Hindus, and the many ethnic groups, were all issues for a new state with a single language but hundreds of local ones.

:::key The challenges of the 1950s: how they linked
1. **Political**: an unstable parliament and a constitution in dispute left the state without strong leadership.
2. **Economic**: an export economy, inflation and the loss of Dutch managers made reform hard.
3. **Social and regional**: Islam, land, the Chinese, and Java against the outer islands, divided the country.
4. **The answer of Sukarno and the army**: more central control, martial law (1957) and Guided Democracy (1959), at the cost of parliament.
:::

## Guided Democracy, 1957–65

Sukarno and the army, in alliance, brought the parliamentary system to an end. On **5 July 1959**, after the Constituent Assembly failed to agree, Sukarno issued a decree that **restored the 1945 constitution**, with its strong presidency, and dissolved the Assembly. It gave him the powers of president and prime minister, and from 1960 he ruled with a parliament that he appointed. In 1960 the Masyumi and another party were banned. In March 1963 the provisional People's Consultative Assembly named him **president for life**.

The system worked by **balance**. Sukarno gave the army a major part in government and the economy, and the army (under Nasution, then Ahmad Yani) was the strongest force in the state. He gave the **PKI** a place, too, under the slogan of Nasakom, and the PKI, which was now the largest communist party outside the communist states, with over two million members by 1962 and many more in its trade-union, peasant, women's and youth bodies, supported Sukarno's policies as the best protection against the army. Sukarno moved the country to the left in foreign policy: in 1961 he helped found the Non-Aligned Movement, in 1962 he forced the Dutch to give up West Irian, and from 1963 he turned against the "neo-colonial" West. The balance was always **unstable**, since the army and the PKI both knew that the president could not live for ever, and each feared that the other would take power on his death.

:::def Guided Democracy
The system Sukarno introduced between 1957 and 1959, in which, he said, the parties' quarrels were replaced by leadership "guided" by the president, with the 1945 constitution restored on 5 July 1959. In practice it rested on the support of the army and the PKI, and on Sukarno's ability to balance them.
:::

**West Irian.** The one great success of Sukarno's foreign policy was the end of the Dutch hold on West New Guinea. He launched a campaign (the "Trikora" of December 1961) with Soviet arms, and the United States, anxious about the Cold War, pressed the Dutch to compromise. Under the New York Agreement of 15 August 1962 the Dutch handed West Irian to a UN administration, which passed it to Indonesia in 1963. The victory made Sukarno popular at home, and it made him believe that confrontation worked.

## The Konfrontasi with Malaysia, 1963–66

Sukarno's next target was the plan to unite Malaya, Singapore, Sabah and Sarawak, described in [The route to independence (2)](#hist.d.malaysia.2). He and his advisers, the Foreign Minister **Subandrio** and the PKI, called Malaysia a **neo-colonial plot**, a British scheme to keep control and to encircle Indonesia. In reality there were other motives, too: he wanted to keep up the nationalist momentum after West Irian, to deflect attention from economic problems, and perhaps to expand Indonesian influence over Borneo.

:::def Konfrontasi
The undeclared conflict between Indonesia and Malaysia from 1963 to 1966, called "Confrontation" in English, in which Indonesia used diplomacy, propaganda, raids and infiltration, mainly in Borneo, to oppose the creation of Malaysia, and Britain, Australia and New Zealand helped Malaysia.
:::

The steps were these.
- **Brunei, December 1962.** A revolt in the British protectorate of Brunei by A. M. Azahari's party, which opposed the Malaysia plan, was put down within about 30 hours by British Gurkha troops. Indonesia had sympathised with it.
- **20 January 1963.** Subandrio announced a policy of "confrontation" with Malaysia. In April the first Indonesian raid on a Sarawak police post occurred at Tebedu.
- **Manila, July–August 1963.** A meeting of Sukarno, the Tunku and the Philippine president agreed that the UN would ask the peoples of Sabah and Sarawak whether they wanted to join Malaysia. When Malaysia was proclaimed on **16 September 1963**, before the UN's report was out, Sukarno said that the agreement had been broken. Two days later crowds in Jakarta burned the British embassy.
- **1964.** Indonesia sent small forces across the border in Borneo, and in August landed troops on the Malay peninsula itself. The British, with Australian and New Zealand troops, fought a mainly defensive war and in 1964 began secret raids across the border into Indonesian Borneo.
- **7 January 1965.** When Malaysia took a seat on the UN Security Council, Indonesia left the United Nations (it returned in September 1966).

The effects on Indonesia were serious. The confrontation **cost money** in a collapsing economy and **isolated Indonesia** from the West, and made it dependent on the Soviet Union and increasingly on China. It also **raised the PKI's stock**, since the PKI was the most enthusiastic supporter, and it **raised tension** between the army and the PKI, since many army leaders disliked the war and the PKI's demand for an armed "fifth force" of workers and peasants. Konfrontasi was not a direct cause of the 30 September Movement, but it was part of the setting. It ended only after Suharto took power: peace talks began in May 1966, and a final agreement, signed in Bangkok on **11 August 1966**, recognised Malaysia.

:::key The impact of Konfrontasi on Indonesia
1. **Economic**: military spending and the loss of Western aid and trade made the economy worse.
2. **Political**: the war polarised the army and the PKI and made Sukarno more dependent on the left.
3. **International**: it isolated Indonesia, took it out of the UN and pushed it towards China.
4. **Ending**: Suharto's government ended it within a year of taking power, which was a sign of the new priorities.
:::

## The 30 September Movement

### The setting in 1965

By 1965 the system of balance was under strain. Inflation was out of control, the rice price was high and the government could not pay its debts. The **PKI** was bigger and bolder, with its peasant organisation, in 1963–64, seizing land and its leaders demanding a "fifth force" of armed workers and peasants and the "Nasakomisation" of the army. Many **army leaders**, led by the Army Commander Ahmad Yani and the Defence Minister Nasution, opposed it, and the army and Muslim organisations were increasingly hostile to the PKI. In August 1965 rumours spread about **Sukarno's health**, and with them fears of what would follow his death. There were also rumours of a "Council of Generals" that was plotting to overthrow Sukarno. The tension was great, and every group thought that the others were preparing to strike.

### The events of 30 September–1 October

In the early hours of **1 October 1965** a group calling itself the **30 September Movement** (*Gerakan 30 September*, G30S) set out to kidnap seven senior army generals in Jakarta. The unit used was largely from the presidential guard (Cakrabirawa) and other battalions, under **Lieutenant-Colonel Untung**. The army commander, **General Ahmad Yani**, and two other generals, **M. T. Haryono** and **D. I. Panjaitan**, were shot at their homes. Three more generals, **S. Parman**, **Suprapto** and **Sutoyo**, were taken alive to a place called **Lubang Buaya** ("Crocodile Hole") on the edge of the Halim air force base and killed, and the bodies of all six, with that of the aide described below, were thrown into a well. The Defence Minister, General **Nasution**, escaped over a wall, but his young daughter Ade Irma Suryani was shot and died later, and his aide Lieutenant Pierre Tendean, who was mistaken for him, was taken and killed. A police officer, Karel Sadsuitubun, was also killed that night.

At about 7 a.m. the state radio broadcast a statement by Untung: the Movement, it said, had acted to protect President Sukarno from a coup by a CIA-backed "Council of Generals". Later that day it announced a **Revolutionary Council** that would replace the cabinet. In Central Java some army units joined the Movement, and in Yogyakarta two officers who opposed it were killed.

The Movement collapsed in about a day. It did not kidnap **General Suharto**, the commander of the army's strategic reserve, **Kostrad**, who was in Jakarta. Suharto took command of the army that morning, won over the units around the capital, and by the evening of 1 October took back the radio station and the main sites. Halim air base was captured in the early hours of 2 October. **Aidit**, the PKI chairman, who had been at Halim, fled to Central Java. The seven officers were buried on **5 October** as Heroes of the Revolution, and the funeral, which Sukarno did not attend, became the start of the army's campaign.

:::def 30 September Movement
The group of army officers and others who, in the early hours of 1 October 1965, kidnapped and killed six senior generals in Jakarta and announced a Revolutionary Council. It was crushed within a day by General Suharto. The army and the New Order blamed the Indonesian Communist Party for it, and the question of who was really responsible is still debated.
:::

## The destruction of the PKI, 1965–66

This is one of the most terrible and most debated events of the twentieth century in South-East Asia. It must be described factually and without exaggeration, and with respect for those who died. The killings are not in doubt; what is debated is how many died, who organised them and why.

### The army's response: blaming the PKI

Within days Suharto and the army leadership declared that the **PKI** had planned the whole affair. The army's newspapers, now the only ones allowed, published the story of a communist coup, and of the torture and mutilation of the generals at Lubang Buaya by women of the PKI's women's organisation, Gerwani. The post-mortem reports on the bodies, which Benedict Anderson later analysed, showed that the generals had been shot and beaten, not mutilated, and John Roosa has argued that the story was a fabrication by the army's psychological-warfare specialists. At the time the story was believed by many and had a great effect: it presented the PKI as inhuman, and justified action against it.

The **PKI leaders** were pursued. Aidit was captured in Central Java and was killed in November 1965, and other leaders were arrested or killed. The party was banned for good on **12 March 1966**, after Supersemar.

### The killings

The mass killings began in October 1965. According to the account that scholars such as Anderson and McVey gave in 1966, there was a gap of about three weeks between the failure of the Movement and the start of the violence, during which the army prepared the ground. In **Aceh** the killings began in October. In **Central Java**, where PKI support was strongest, paratroopers of the **RPKAD** under Colonel **Sarwo Edhie Wibowo** arrived in October and the killing spread through the province. It then spread to **East Java** and, in December 1965, to **Bali**. In other places, including parts of Sumatra and Sulawesi, there were smaller outbreaks. The worst of the violence took place between October 1965 and early 1966, and it faded by about March 1966.

The killers were of several kinds. In many places the **army** organised, trained and armed **civilian groups**, and the killings seldom started before units of the army had approved. The civilian groups included Muslim youth organisations (in East Java above all the youth wing of the Nahdlatul Ulama), local militias linked to other parties, and local gangs, and in some places they acted on their own. The reasons for taking part were mixed: fear of a communist takeover, which the army's propaganda made seem real, hostility to the PKI's atheism and land seizures, old local quarrels, a wish to show loyalty, and in some places simple greed. The victims were **members and supporters of the PKI** and of its associated organisations (trade unions, peasant groups, teachers, women's and youth groups), and also people who were on the left or were simply named by their neighbours. In some places **ethnic Chinese** people were attacked, too, since China was seen as the PKI's ally. Many victims had no connection with the Movement at all.

Those who were not killed were **arrested**. Robert Cribb and Michele Ford write that more than a million people passed through detention camps, and some were held for ten years or more. Amnesty International in 1977 estimated that about a million had been detained. The best-known detainee was the novelist **Pramoedya Ananta Toer**, who spent years in prison, including time on the island of Buru. For a generation after 1966 former prisoners and their families were barred from public jobs and marked on their identity cards.

### How many died? The estimates, and why they differ

| Source | Estimate | Note |
|---|---|---|
| An Indonesian army figure, given before the killings had ended | 78,500 | Too low, because it was given before the killings ended |
| Armed forces security command (Kopkamtib), December 1976 | 450,000 to 500,000 | An official figure, given by the regime that was responsible for the army's role |
| Benedict Anderson | 200,000 in 1966; by 1985 500,000 to 1 million | A leading scholar who revised his figure as evidence came in |
| Robert Cribb and Michele Ford | "about half a million" | A range of 200,000 to 800,000 has also been given in Cribb's name in one article |
| Colonel Sarwo Edhie Wibowo (reported claim of 1989) | about 3 million | Quoted shortly before his death; not accepted by most historians |
| Most scholars | at least 500,000; up to a million or more | The usual range in textbooks |

The estimate you should use is **from about 500,000 to 1 million**, with the **qualification** that no figure is certain and that figures of 200,000 or of 3 million have been put forward.

:::key Why the estimates of the dead vary so much
1. **No census or records.** The killings were not recorded by any authority, and the bodies were often buried in unmarked graves that have not been opened.
2. **Many places, many killers.** The violence was spread over a few months across several islands, and it was carried out by army units, militias and local groups, so that no one counted it.
3. **Silence.** Under Suharto the subject was forbidden for more than thirty years: survivors were afraid to speak, and most killers stayed silent; Cribb and Ford note that witnesses are dying and evidence is decaying.
4. **Political uses of the figures.** The army had reasons to give a low count (to hide its part) or a high one (to show its success); the PKI and its supporters gave other figures; each foreign government had its own view.
5. **Method.** Estimates are made by taking local studies (of parts of Java, Bali, Sulawesi and Sumatra) and extending them to the whole, which is uncertain. Definitions differ over who counts (those killed, those who disappeared, deaths in detention) and over the period covered.
:::

### The debate on responsibility

There are two separate questions, and you should keep them apart: **who was responsible for the 30 September Movement**, and **who was responsible for the killings that followed**? There is a third: the part played by **outside powers**.

**1. The Movement.** Historians and others give several explanations.
- The **New Order's official account** (for example *The Coup Attempt of the "September 30th Movement" in Indonesia*, by Nugroho Notosusanto and Ismail Saleh, 1968) said that the **PKI planned and led a coup**. It was supported by confessions at the military trials of 1966–68, in particular that of **Sjam Kamaruzaman**, the head of the party's secret Special Bureau, and it was told again in the 1984 film *Pengkhianatan G30S/PKI*, which was shown on television every year until the fall of Suharto in 1998.
- The **"Cornell Paper"** of **Benedict Anderson and Ruth McVey** (written in January 1966 and circulated later, with the title *A Preliminary Analysis of the October 1, 1965, Coup in Indonesia*) argued that it was an **internal army affair**, the work of junior officers from Central Java who had grievances against the generals in Jakarta, and that the PKI leaders had little part. Anderson later argued that some generals knew of the plot.
- **John Roosa**, in *Pretext for Mass Murder: The September 30th Movement and Suharto's Coup d'État in Indonesia* (University of Wisconsin Press, 2006), argues that the Movement was organised by a **small group**: Sjam, the head of the Special Bureau, Aidit and some officers, and not by the PKI as a party, whose members were mostly unaware. He argues that it was poorly planned and quickly collapsed, and that Suharto used it as a **pretext** to destroy the PKI and take power. The title of the book states the thesis.
- Other writers have given more weight to Sukarno's part, or to **Suharto's foreknowledge** (one of the officers, Colonel Latief, later said that he had told Suharto the night before, which Suharto denied) or to outside powers.

**2. The killings.** On this the main question is **how far they were planned and directed** and how far they were local and spontaneous.
- Robert Cribb (editor), *The Indonesian Killings 1965–1966: Studies from Java and Bali* (1990), collected regional studies and showed that the violence varied: in some places the army began it, and in others local groups did, with the army's approval. Anderson and McVey observed that the army began its campaign only after the Movement had collapsed.
- **Geoffrey Robinson**, *The Killing Season: A History of the Indonesian Massacres, 1965–66* (2018), argues that the **army led and directed** the killings, using its command structure and civilian militias, and that foreign governments supported it. **Jess Melvin**, *The Army and the Indonesian Genocide* (2018), uses army documents from Aceh to argue that the killings were a planned campaign directed from above, and calls them a genocide. Other scholars say that the term "genocide" does not fit because the legal definition does not cover political groups, and use terms such as "politicide".
- No serious historian argues that the killings did not happen, or that the victims were responsible for their own deaths: the debate is about **organisation, scale and intention**.

**3. Outside powers.** The United States, Britain and others wanted the end of Sukarno's leftward drift. Declassified US documents show that the US government knew of the killings and gave the army support in the form of money and radio equipment. A US Embassy officer, Robert Martens, said in 1990 that he helped to draw up lists of about 5,000 communist leaders that were passed to the army. Cribb (2002) concluded that there was considerable evidence that the USA encouraged the killings, but no evidence that its involvement significantly increased their scale; Robinson and Bradley Simpson argue for a larger role. Britain, then fighting Konfrontasi, also had reasons to want Sukarno gone. The CIA has denied any active involvement.

**4. Memory.** The killings were not discussed in Indonesia for decades. School textbooks passed over them. In July 2016 an International People's Tribunal, which had heard evidence in The Hague in November 2015, found the Indonesian state responsible for crimes against humanity; Indonesia rejected its ruling, and the tribunal had no legal force. In January 2023 President Joko Widodo said that he "strongly regretted" the gross violations of human rights in the past, including the killings of 1965–66, but he did not offer a formal apology or promise trials.

:::key The debate on responsibility: what is agreed and what is not
- **Agreed:** six generals were killed on 1 October 1965; the army under Suharto crushed the Movement; between about half a million and a million people were killed and many more were arrested in the following months; the army had a major part in the killings.
- **Debated:** who planned the Movement (the PKI leadership, a small group within it, army officers, or others); how far Suharto knew in advance; how far the killings were directed from above or local; the size of the outside powers' part; whether "genocide" is the right word.
:::

:::warn Describe the killings with care
Say "killed", "arrested" and "detained" and give the range with its source: do not write "the PKI got what it deserved" or "the communists were wiped out" as if it were a neutral summary. Do not use a single number as if it were certain. Do not describe the PKI's guilt in 1 October as established fact, or its innocence. Say which historian holds which view. The victims were men and women of all kinds, and many had nothing to do with the Movement.
:::

## The transition to the New Order, 1966–67

### The students and the Three Demands

Suharto did not take power in a day. He moved step by step between October 1965 and March 1967, with Sukarno still the president, and each step depended on pressure from outside the army.
- **KAMI.** In October 1965 (sources give dates between 22 and 27 October) university students in Jakarta formed the **Indonesian Student Action Front (KAMI)**, with army encouragement, to demand the dissolution of the PKI. Other action fronts (for school pupils, graduates and so on) followed.
- **The Three Demands.** On **10 January 1966** KAMI and other groups put forward the *Tritura*, the Three Demands of the People: **dissolve the PKI**, **purge the cabinet of PKI sympathisers**, and **lower prices**. Student demonstrations, with the army's tacit protection, filled the streets of Jakarta.
- **February 1966.** On 21 February Sukarno reshuffled the cabinet and removed Nasution as defence minister, but kept many left-wing ministers, which the students saw as defiance. On 24 February a student, **Arief Rachman Hakim**, was shot dead in a clash with the presidential guard. His funeral became a demonstration, and he became a martyr of the movement.

:::def KAMI
The Indonesian Student Action Front (*Kesatuan Aksi Mahasiswa Indonesia*), formed in October 1965 by university students, with army backing, to demand the banning of the PKI. Its demonstrations in January–March 1966 (the Tritura, "Three Demands of the People") put pressure on Sukarno and gave Suharto's rise popular support.
:::

### Supersemar, 11 March 1966

On **11 March 1966** troops without insignia surrounded the palace in Jakarta where Sukarno was holding a cabinet meeting. Sukarno left for his palace at Bogor. That afternoon three generals (**Basuki Rachmat**, **Amir Machmud** and **M. Jusuf**) went to see him there and returned with a signed letter, the **Order of Eleventh March** (*Surat Perintah Sebelas Maret*, shortened to **Supersemar**), which told Suharto to take "all measures considered necessary" to restore security and order and to protect the president and the government. Accounts differ about how it was signed (Sukarno may have felt that he was under pressure) and the original document has been lost.

:::def Supersemar
The Order of Eleventh March 1966 (*Surat Perintah Sebelas Maret*), by which Sukarno authorised General Suharto to take whatever measures he thought necessary to restore order. Sukarno meant it as a temporary delegation of authority; Suharto used it as a transfer of power. The original has been lost, and several slightly different versions exist.
:::

Suharto used it at once. On **12 March** he banned the PKI. On 18 March fifteen ministers loyal to Sukarno were arrested. Later in 1966 the People's Consultative Assembly (MPRS) confirmed the order and made it impossible for Sukarno to withdraw it. Sukarno tried to regain control by speeches, but he was by now isolated: the army, the students and the Muslim groups were against him, and the PKI had been destroyed. On **12 March 1967** the MPRS stripped Sukarno of his remaining powers and named **Suharto acting president**, and in March 1968 it elected him president. Sukarno was kept under house arrest and died on 21 June 1970.

## Why did the New Order replace Guided Democracy?

Sort the causes into **long-term**, **medium-term** and **triggers**, and then rank them.

| Type | Cause | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | The weakness of the Republic's institutions; the army's role since the revolution | Left no body but the army that could replace Sukarno |
| Long-term | Sukarno's reliance on a balance of army and PKI | Made the system depend on one man and on a rivalry that could turn violent |
| Medium-term | The economy: inflation (about 600 per cent in 1965), debt, Konfrontasi's costs | Made the government unpopular and gave the army and students a grievance |
| Medium-term | The rise of the PKI and the fear of it among the army and Muslim groups | Set the groups that would unite against it |
| Trigger | The 30 September Movement and the army's response | Gave the army the pretext and Suharto the command |
| Trigger | The destruction of the PKI (October 1965 to 1966) | Removed the one force that could have defended Sukarno |
| Pressure | The students (KAMI), the Muslim groups and the press | Gave the army popular support |
| Consolidation | Supersemar (11 March 1966) and the MPRS (1967) | Gave Suharto legal authority |
| Outside | The USA, Britain and Western aid | Welcomed the change and supported it with money and diplomacy |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The **30 September Movement** was the trigger, but it did not make the New Order by itself: the **army** (Suharto) turned it into a victory by acting fast and by destroying the PKI, the only organisation able to defend Sukarno. The **underlying** causes were the instability of the balance on which Guided Democracy rested, and an economy that was in collapse. The **students and Muslim groups** gave the army popular backing and the legal steps of Supersemar and the MPRS gave Suharto legitimacy. Suharto's rise was therefore the result of a combination, and the army's decision was the decisive factor.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: how and why did Suharto rise?)
- **M. C. Ricklefs**, *A History of Modern Indonesia since c.1200* (4th edition 2008), takes the view that the army's leadership turned a failed movement into a seizure of power over about eighteen months, using the students, the Muslim groups and the legal forms of Supersemar and the MPRS, and that Sukarno was unable to stop it.
- **John Roosa**, *Pretext for Mass Murder* (2006), argues that the Movement was not a PKI coup but was used by Suharto as the pretext for his own, which destroyed the PKI and then Sukarno's authority.
- **Benedict Anderson and Ruth McVey**, the "Cornell Paper" (January 1966), saw the Movement as an affair within the army, and the army's rise as a consequence.
- **Robert Cribb** (editor), *The Indonesian Killings 1965–1966* (1990), and **Geoffrey Robinson**, *The Killing Season* (2018), disagree on how far the violence was directed from above, which affects how you rank the army's role.
These authors give you different weightings of the same factors. Use them to show that you know a judgement is needed, but base it on evidence.
:::

:::note The big picture: what formed Indonesia, and what challenges it faced
- **What led to the formation of Indonesia:** the nationalist movement of 1908–42 (ideas, leaders, language); the Japanese occupation (the end of Dutch power, organisation, arms, the promises of 1944–45); the proclamation of 17 August 1945 (Sukarno and Hatta, pushed by the pemuda); and four years of revolution in which Indonesian resistance and international pressure (the UN and the USA) defeated the Dutch.
- **What challenges it faced:** a federal form rejected in 1950; an unstable parliament; a president who wanted more power; the army's political role; regional and Islamic rebellions; a wrecked, export-dependent economy; and social divisions over Islam, land and the Chinese.
- **How the first republic ended:** Guided Democracy (1959), Konfrontasi (1963–66), the 30 September Movement, the destruction of the PKI, and Supersemar (1966).
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'The Dutch failed to re-establish control mainly because of international pressure.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained and balanced argument, a clear and supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Mainly" needs a test. Criteria: (1) **what stopped the Dutch winning on the ground**; (2) **what forced them to concede in 1949**; (3) **whether either factor could have worked without the other**. For the view: the Committee of Good Offices and the UN (1 August 1947, 24 December 1948, January 1949); India and Australia; the US threat to Marshall Plan aid after the second police action (19 December 1948); the shift of American opinion after Madiun; Roem–Van Roijen (7 May 1949). Against: Indonesian resistance (Surabaya, the guerrilla war of Sudirman and Nasution, the attack on Yogyakarta on 1 March 1949, the Emergency Government), the Republic's survival after the capture of its leaders, popular support; Dutch weaknesses (cost, over 100,000 men, divided opinion, the failure of federal policy), British refusal to fight in 1945–46. Judgement: international pressure decided the final step, but it worked because Indonesian resistance had made the Dutch case unwinnable.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "The Dutch failed to get control back because of international pressure. The United Nations told the Dutch to stop in 1947 and America said it would stop Marshall Aid. The Dutch then had to agree to Indonesian independence at the Round Table Conference. So the view is right."

*Commentary.* True and relevant but a list with no criteria and no test of "mainly". It does not mention Indonesian resistance or Dutch weakness, the conclusion repeats the question, and there are no dates or names to show how the pressure worked. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 (4–6).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If we ask what **forced the Dutch to concede**, international pressure was decisive in the last stage. After the second police action of 19 December 1948 the Security Council demanded a ceasefire and the release of the Republican leaders, and the United States threatened to cut Marshall Plan aid to the Netherlands, which depended on it for recovery. Within five months the Dutch signed the Roem–Van Roijen Agreement (7 May 1949) and by 2 November they had agreed at The Hague to transfer sovereignty. But the criterion also shows a limit. The American threat was a response to the Republic's own actions: it had suppressed the communists at Madiun in September 1948, which made it an acceptable partner for Washington, and its guerrilla war after December 1948 showed that the Dutch capture of Yogyakarta had not ended it."

*Commentary.* A criterion (what forced the concession) is applied with precise detail and tested against the claim. Level 4. For Level 5 it needs the other criteria, the Dutch weaknesses, and a sustained judgement on how the factors fitted together.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is partly valid. International pressure was the decisive factor in the last stage of the revolution, but it was not the main reason why the Dutch failed to re-establish control, because it would not have worked on a Republic that could be defeated, and the Dutch were already failing on the ground and at home. I shall use three criteria: what stopped the Dutch from winning; what forced them to concede in 1949; and whether either factor could have worked without the other.

On the first criterion the main factor was Indonesian resistance. The Dutch could take the towns and the plantations, as they did in the first police action of 21 July 1947, when they seized the main export areas of Java and Sumatra, but they could not hold the countryside. After the Renville Agreement of 17 January 1948, which left the Republic only about a third of Java and most of Sumatra, it survived, and after the second police action of 19 December 1948, when Dutch paratroopers took Yogyakarta and arrested Sukarno and Hatta, it did not collapse. General Sudirman led a guerrilla war, General Nasution set up a system of total people's defence and an Emergency Government under Sjafruddin Prawiranegara carried on in Sumatra; on 1 March 1949 Lieutenant-Colonel Suharto's troops held Yogyakarta for about six hours. The Republic also showed discipline: it crushed the communist rising at Madiun in September 1948 itself. Behind the army there was popular support, and a force of well over 100,000 Dutch troops could not suppress it.

On the second criterion, however, the answer is international pressure, and here the view is strongest. The Netherlands could have gone on fighting a guerrilla war, but not against the United States. India and Australia took the dispute to the Security Council in July 1947, and the Council's call for a ceasefire on 1 August and the Committee of Good Offices turned a 'police action' into a world question. After Madiun, American opinion began to see the Republic as an anti-communist partner and the Dutch as a colonial power that was wasting American money. When the Dutch attacked again in December 1948, the Security Council called for a ceasefire on 24 December and demanded the restoration of the Republic, India held a conference in New Delhi that condemned the Dutch, and the United States threatened to cut Marshall Plan aid. This was the one pressure that the Dutch economy could not bear, and the Roem–Van Roijen Agreement of 7 May 1949 and the Round Table Conference followed.

On the third criterion the factors are linked. The American attitude was not formed in a vacuum: it was the result of Madiun, and of the Republic's survival after December 1948, which showed Washington that the Dutch could not impose a settlement and that continued war would only help the communists. In the same way Dutch weakness made them vulnerable: the Netherlands was poor after the German occupation, a large army overseas cost money it needed for reconstruction, Dutch opinion was divided (a poll at the time of Linggadjati showed 38 per cent in favour and 36 per cent against), and the Dutch federal policy of creating states such as East Indonesia had produced no leaders who could rival the Republic. Britain had refused to fight a colonial war in 1945–46 and withdrew its troops by the end of 1946, which left the Dutch to do it alone.

Overall, international pressure was the factor that decided the timing and the terms of the Dutch withdrawal, and it was therefore the most important cause of the Dutch failure in 1949. But it would not have been effective if the Republic had not survived the Dutch attacks and proved itself an anti-communist government, and if the Dutch had not been weakened by the cost of the war. The fairest verdict is that Indonesian resistance made the Dutch failure possible and international pressure made it certain, so that 'mainly' overstates the importance of the outside world."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): three explicit criteria, a balanced argument that shows how the factors depended on each other, and a clear judgement that tests the word "mainly". Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise, selected detail (dates, names, the Van Mook Line, the dates of the two police actions, the Marshall Plan threat). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 850 words, 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the reasons for the transition to Suharto's New Order. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the reasons" asks you to **weigh** several causes against each other and to say which mattered most, so you need **criteria** (for example how far each was a long-term cause or a trigger, which created the opportunity and which supplied the force, and whether the change would have happened without it).

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) **underlying cause or trigger**; (2) **opportunity or force**; (3) **could it have been avoided?** Evidence: the balance of army and PKI under Guided Democracy; the economy (inflation about 600 per cent in 1965; a debt of about 2.4 billion US dollars); Konfrontasi; the 30 September Movement (1 October 1965); the army's response and the destruction of the PKI; KAMI and the Tritura (10 January 1966); Supersemar (11 March 1966); the MPRS (12 March 1967); outside support. Judgement: the army's use of the Movement was the decisive factor, but the New Order was made possible by long-term causes.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "Suharto took over because of the 30 September Movement. The PKI killed six generals and Suharto stopped them. He then got Sukarno to sign Supersemar and became president in 1967. So the Movement was the reason."

*Commentary.* A sequence of events with one cause. It states that the PKI killed the generals as fact, and ignores the economy, the students and the army's wider aims. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 (4–6).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If we ask what **made Suharto's rise possible**, the answer is the structure of Guided Democracy. Sukarno's power rested on a balance between the army and the PKI, which each feared the other; when one of the two was destroyed, the balance went with it. The 30 September Movement was the trigger, but the army under Suharto chose what to make of it: it blamed the PKI, banned it, and encouraged the mass killings that followed, between about half a million and a million deaths on the usual estimates. Without the PKI, Sukarno had no force to oppose the army, which is why he could be forced to sign Supersemar on 11 March 1966."

*Commentary.* A criterion (what made it possible) is used and tested with precise evidence. Level 4. For Level 5 it needs the economy, the students and the outside powers, and a ranking of all the causes.

**A Level 5 conclusion** (after paragraphs on the balance of Guided Democracy, the Movement and the destruction of the PKI, the economy and Konfrontasi, and the students and Supersemar).

"Overall, the New Order replaced Guided Democracy because of a combination of causes at different levels. The **underlying** causes were the instability of Sukarno's balance between the army and the PKI, which made the system depend on one man, and the economic collapse (about 600 per cent inflation in 1965), which left the government unpopular and the army and the students with a grievance. The **trigger** was the 30 September Movement of 1 October 1965, whoever was responsible for it: its failure gave the army the pretext and Suharto the command. The **decisive** factor was the army's use of that opportunity: it destroyed the PKI, the only organisation able to defend Sukarno, and then used pressure from the students and Muslim groups, and the legal form of Supersemar and the MPRS, to remove the president step by step between October 1965 and March 1967. Outside support (money and diplomatic backing from the West) helped, but did not cause the change. I conclude that the army's decision to exploit the Movement was the most important factor, and that without the earlier weaknesses of Guided Democracy it would not have been possible, since both an army that was ready to act and a political system that could not resist it were needed."

*Commentary.* A clear judgement that separates underlying causes, trigger and decisive factor, and ranks them with a justification. Table A Level 5; Table B depends on the detail in the paragraphs before it.
:::

:::eg Evaluate the view that the political problems of parliamentary democracy were the main reason for Sukarno's introduction of Guided Democracy in 1959. [30]
**Plan.** Criteria: (1) **who gained power from the change**; (2) **what problem each factor explains**; (3) **whether the change was needed or chosen**. For the view: six cabinets in under seven years; no majority in the 1955 election; the deadlock in the Constituent Assembly over Islam and Pancasila; Hatta's resignation (1 December 1956). Against: the army's wish for a political role (17 October 1952; martial law, March 1957); the regional rebellions (PRRI and Permesta, 1957–58); economic problems; Sukarno's own wish for more power and his dislike of Western democracy ("bury the parties", 28 October 1956).

**A Level 4–5 paragraph on the alternative.** "Yet the political problems do not by themselves explain why the system was replaced by Guided Democracy. They explain why it was weak, but the form of the change, the restoration of the 1945 constitution on 5 July 1959, gave power to the two men who had most to gain: Sukarno, whose presidency became strong, and the army, whose commanders had already taken power under martial law in March 1957. The regional rebellions of 1957–58 convinced army leaders that parliamentary government could not hold the country together, and Sukarno, who had said in October 1956 that the parties should be 'buried', used that fear to bring in a system that he had wanted for years. On this view the instability of parliament was an opportunity that two ambitious groups took, and not the cause of the change."

*Commentary.* The paragraph explains an alternative with precise evidence and links it to the question by using the criterion (who gained). Level 4–5. A full answer would give the case for the view first, and a conclusion that the political problems were necessary (they made the change possible) but not sufficient (the army and Sukarno made it happen).

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the importance of the 30 September Movement in bringing Suharto to power." [30]

A student wrote:

"On 30 September 1965 the PKI tried to take over Indonesia by killing six generals. Suharto stopped the coup and then he destroyed the communists, who killed a million people before they were stopped. After this he became the leader of the country. The PKI were evil and wanted to make Indonesia communist, so Suharto saved the country. The 30 September Movement was therefore the only reason why Suharto came to power."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): one cause, asserted, with no criteria, no balance and no test of "importance". Table B Level 1–2 (3–5): there are serious errors of fact and of tone. The writer states as fact that the PKI planned the Movement, which is the New Order's account and one side of a debate; the figure of a million killed is given to the wrong side, since the killings were of PKI members and supporters, mostly by the army and the militias it armed, and the estimate of between 500,000 and 1 million refers to **victims**; and moral language ("evil") replaces argument. There is nothing on Supersemar, the students, the economy or the 1967 transfer of power, and the dates are missing.

**The fix.** Use accurate, neutral language, a range with its source, and a criterion. "If we ask what the Movement **made possible**, it was the pretext. In the early hours of 1 October 1965 six generals were killed in Jakarta; by that evening General Suharto, commanding the army's strategic reserve, had taken control of the capital. The army blamed the PKI, and historians still dispute who was responsible: John Roosa argues that a small group around the PKI leader Aidit and Sjam, the head of the party's Special Bureau, organised it, while the Cornell Paper saw an affair inside the army. Whoever planned it, the army used it to destroy the PKI, whose members and supporters were killed in numbers that most scholars put at 500,000 to one million or more. But it was not the only reason: Suharto also needed the students' Tritura of 10 January 1966, Supersemar of 11 March 1966, and the long decline of Guided Democracy." Now the paragraph describes the events accurately, shows that the responsibility is debated, and begins to rank the causes.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What did Koiso promise on 7 September 1944, and what were the BPUPK and PPKI?
A: Independence for the East Indies "in the future". The BPUPK (Investigating Committee, announced March and inaugurated 28 May 1945) debated the state's philosophy and constitution; the PPKI (Preparatory Committee, formed 7 August 1945) adopted the constitution and chose the president and vice-president on 18 August 1945.
Q: What were the five principles of Pancasila in the final (18 August 1945) version?
A: Belief in one God; just and civilised humanity; the unity of Indonesia; democracy guided by wisdom in consultation and representation; social justice for all Indonesians. (Sukarno's speech of 1 June 1945 put them in another order, with nationalism first and belief in God last.)
Q: What happened at Rengasdengklok on 16 August 1945?
A: Young nationalists (pemuda) took Sukarno and Hatta from Jakarta to Rengasdengklok to put pressure on them to proclaim independence at once. They refused to be forced; Ahmad Subardjo negotiated their return that evening, and the proclamation was drafted at Admiral Maeda's house and read on 17 August.
Q: Why did the British matter in 1945–46, and what happened at Surabaya?
A: British forces under SEAC landed in late 1945 to disarm the Japanese and free prisoners; they did not want to fight a colonial war. After Brigadier Mallaby was killed (30 October), they fought Indonesian forces at Surabaya from 10 November 1945, which Indonesians now commemorate as Heroes' Day.
Q: What were the main terms of the Linggadjati Agreement, and why did it fail?
A: Dutch recognition of the Republic's authority over Java, Madura and Sumatra and a plan for a federal United States of Indonesia in a Netherlands–Indonesian Union. It failed because each side read it differently, the Dutch created rival states such as East Indonesia, and opposition grew on both sides; the first police action followed on 21 July 1947.
Q: What did the Renville Agreement of 17 January 1948 do?
A: It ended the fighting along the Van Mook Line, which left the Republic with about a third of Java and most of Sumatra, and promised referendums and a federal constitution. It was a bad deal for the Republic, and the Amir Sjarifuddin cabinet fell.
Q: What was the Madiun Affair, and why did it matter?
A: A communist-led rising at Madiun on 18 September 1948, crushed by the Republic's army within weeks. It showed the Republic's willingness to suppress communism, and so improved its standing with the USA.
Q: What happened in the second police action (19 December 1948), and what was the response?
A: Dutch paratroopers took Yogyakarta and arrested Sukarno and Hatta. Guerrilla war followed, an Emergency Government was set up in Sumatra, the Security Council called for a ceasefire, and the USA threatened Marshall Plan aid.
Q: What did the Round Table Conference (23 August–2 November 1949) agree?
A: That sovereignty would pass to a United States of Indonesia on 27 December 1949, in a union with the Netherlands, with the status of West Irian left to later talks and a debt of about 4.5 billion guilders taken over by Indonesia.
Q: Why did the parliamentary system of 1950–57 prove unstable?
A: There were many parties and no majority, so cabinets fell often (six between September 1950 and March 1957); the army intervened in politics (17 October 1952); and the Constituent Assembly could not agree on the basis of the state.
Q: Who won the 1955 election, and what was notable about it?
A: No one: the PNI (22.3 per cent) and Masyumi (20.9) each won 57 of 257 seats, the NU 45 and the PKI (16.4 per cent) 39. It showed the divisions of religion and region and the strength of the PKI.
Q: What was Guided Democracy, and when did it begin?
A: Sukarno's system, in which the 1945 constitution was restored (5 July 1959) and the president ruled with the support of the army and the PKI, in place of parliamentary democracy.
Q: What was the 30 September Movement?
A: A group of officers and others who, in the early hours of 1 October 1965, killed six generals in Jakarta and announced a Revolutionary Council. It was crushed within a day by Suharto, and the army blamed the PKI.
Q: What are the estimates of the number killed in 1965–66, and why do they vary?
A: Most scholars give from about 500,000 to 1 million, with figures from 78,500 to 3 million quoted by others. They vary because there is no census or record, the killings were widespread and carried out by many groups, silence was enforced for decades, and the figures were used for political purposes.
Q: What were KAMI, the Tritura and Supersemar?
A: KAMI was the student action front formed in October 1965. The Tritura (10 January 1966) were its three demands: dissolve the PKI, purge the cabinet, lower prices. Supersemar was the order of 11 March 1966 by which Sukarno authorised Suharto to take whatever measures were needed to restore order.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why independence was proclaimed on 17 August 1945.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: which factors created the opportunity and which made the proclamation happen. Japan's promises (7 September 1944), the BPUPK and the PPKI; the Japanese surrender on 15 August; the pressure of the pemuda and the Rengasdengklok affair (16 August); the role of Sukarno and Hatta, who wrote the text with Maeda's protection; the earlier nationalist movement. Level 5 ranks the surrender as the opportunity and the pemuda as the force that made it an Indonesian act, and judges that Sukarno and Hatta gave it authority.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the Linggadjati Agreement failed mainly because the two sides understood it differently.
A: Indicative content. Support: "federal", "cooperation" and "union" were read in opposite ways; the Dutch saw a programme of principles, the Republic a recognition of sovereignty. Challenge: the Dutch federal policy (East Indonesia, December 1946); opposition in both camps; economic motives (plantations and oil); the Dutch decision to use force on 21 July 1947. Level 5 judges that the misunderstanding was a symptom of a deeper conflict of aims, so that it was not the main cause of the failure.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the Madiun Affair for the success of the Indonesian Revolution.
A: Indicative content. For: the Republic crushed the communists with its own forces; the USA's attitude changed; Hatta and Sukarno became possible allies in the Cold War; the Dutch could no longer present the Republic as communist. Against: it weakened the Republic at the time (tens of thousands arrested, the army divided); the American shift came only after the second police action; resistance and the UN also mattered. Level 5 judges that Madiun mattered most by changing American opinion, but that the decisive step came with the second police action.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The attempted coup of 1946 was more dangerous to the Republic than the Dutch.' Evaluate this view.
H: What did the Persatuan Perjuangan want, and who supported it?
H: Compare the danger of division within the Republic with the Dutch military threat in 1946.
A: Indicative content. Support: the 3 July 1946 attempt showed that the army and politicians were divided over negotiation, and a successful coup might have ended the talks and weakened the Republic's legitimacy. Challenge: the Dutch army was the larger threat (the first police action in 1947 took the richest areas); the coup failed because Sukarno refused and the army stayed loyal; the Republic survived internal conflict again at Madiun. Level 5 judges that the internal danger was real but contained, while the external threat was greater.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the political challenges facing Indonesia in the 1950s.
A: Indicative content. The form of the state (federal to unitary, 1950); the unstable parliament (six cabinets in under seven years); the 1955 election without a majority; the Constituent Assembly deadlock; the army's role (17 October 1952); regional rebellions (Darul Islam, PRRI, Permesta); Sukarno's challenge. Level 5 ranks these by how far each threatened the state, and judges that the regional and army challenges were more serious than the party instability.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that economic problems were the main reason for the failure of parliamentary democracy in Indonesia.
H: What did the economy do to the government's popularity?
H: What did the army and Sukarno want, regardless of the economy?
A: Indicative content. Support: export dependence, deficits and inflation; the loss of Dutch managers after 1957; regional resentment over revenue. Challenge: political factors (the party system, the Constituent Assembly deadlock); the army's ambitions; Sukarno's dislike of the system; regional rebellions. Level 5 judges that the economic problems fed the others but were not the main cause, since the key decisions (martial law in 1957, the 1959 decree) were political choices.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the impact of Konfrontasi on Indonesia.
H: Think of the economy, the army, the PKI and Indonesia's place in the world.
H: Did it cause the events of 1965, or only set the scene?
A: Indicative content. Economic cost and isolation; Indonesia's exit from the UN (7 January 1965) and closeness to China; polarisation between the army and the PKI; the "fifth force" demand; the end under Suharto (Bangkok, 11 August 1966). Level 5 judges that it worsened an existing crisis rather than causing it, and that its end was a sign of the New Order's priorities.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the destruction of the PKI in 1965–66 was the main reason why Suharto replaced Sukarno.
H: Which cause came first, the destruction of the PKI or Suharto's rise?
H: What else did Suharto need besides the army?
A: Indicative content. Support: the PKI was the only force that could defend Sukarno; once it was destroyed (killings and arrests from October 1965, the ban on 12 March 1966) he had no support against the army. Challenge: the students and Muslim groups provided popular backing; Supersemar and the MPRS gave legitimacy; the economic collapse had weakened Sukarno; outside support. Level 5 judges that the destruction of the PKI was necessary for Suharto's rise but not sufficient: it needed the army's skill in using it and the legal steps of 1966–67.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Indonesia's independence was won more by diplomacy than by force.' Evaluate this view.
H: What did the guerrilla war achieve, and what did the diplomats achieve?
H: Could the diplomatic result have come without the fighting?
A: Support: the UN, the Committee of Good Offices, Hatta and Sjahrir's negotiations (Linggadjati, Renville, Roem–Van Roijen), and the American pressure that decided the end in 1949. Challenge: Surabaya, the guerrilla war of Sudirman and Nasution and the survival of the Republic, which gave the diplomats something to defend; the Dutch might have won if they had been able to suppress the Republic; the final agreement came after the military stalemate. A* judgement: the two were not alternatives: force made the Republic impossible to destroy and diplomacy turned that survival into recognition; neither would have succeeded alone.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The accounts of who was responsible for the 30 September Movement matter less for the historian of the New Order than the army's use of it.' Evaluate this view.
H: What does a historian need to know in order to explain the rise of Suharto?
H: What would change if Roosa's account were right, or the New Order's?
A: Support: whatever its origin, the army's decision to blame the PKI, ban it and encourage the killings determined the outcome; the New Order's legitimacy rested on the story of the communist coup; the story's use is a historical fact whether or not it is true. Challenge: responsibility matters for judging Suharto's legitimacy and the regime's later claims; if the army had foreknowledge or took part, the army's use of the Movement is a different kind of event; and for the victims' memory the question of truth is central. A* judgement: for explaining how Suharto rose, the use is what matters, but for judging the regime, and for the historical record, who was responsible is also essential.
