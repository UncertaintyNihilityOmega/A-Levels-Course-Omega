:::why
In 1900 the Dutch ruled about forty million people on thousands of islands, and almost nobody, Dutch or Indonesian, spoke of an "Indonesian nation". Forty-five years later, on 17 August 1945, two men proclaimed the independence of a country called Indonesia, with a flag, an anthem, one national language and a name that most of its people had hardly used in 1920. This chapter asks how a set of islands, peoples and religions came to think of itself as one nation, and what the Dutch, the educated elite, the Chinese and Muslim traders and finally the Japanese each contributed. It is the story of how nationalism grew in **ways** (parties, ideas, leaders, a language) and for **reasons** (education, religion, economic grievance, repression, war), which is what key question 9.3 asks.
:::

## What is the question really asking?

*In what ways, and for what reasons, did the nationalist movement develop in Indonesia up to 1945?* has two halves and a trap. The first half, **in what ways**, asks you to describe how nationalism grew: which organisations, which leaders, which ideas, and how fast. The second, **for what reasons**, asks you to explain why. The trap is to tell the story of the parties as a list and never say what pushed them forward. Keep four questions in mind.
1. **What did Dutch rule itself create?** The Dutch built a single colony out of hundreds of kingdoms. They also built schools, a common language of administration, a press and a council, and in doing so gave a few Indonesians the tools to attack them. How far was nationalism a product of the colonial state?
2. **What did Indonesians bring?** Islam, trade, the Malay language, Javanese culture and communism each supplied a different kind of nationalism. How far was the movement united, and why did it divide?
3. **What did the Dutch do in reply?** Did concession or repression shape the movement more?
4. **What did the Japanese change?** Did the occupation of 1942–45 *make* Indonesian nationalism, or only change its speed and its chances?

The last section, "Weighing the causes: what made Indonesian nationalism?", comes back to these.

:::def Nationalism (in the Indonesian case)
The belief that the peoples of the Dutch East Indies formed one nation with the right to a state of its own, and the political movement to win it. In the Indies it had to be **made**, because the territory had no common history of its own: it was a colonial creation, containing Javanese, Sundanese, Minangkabau, Bataks, Balinese, Ambonese and many others.
:::

:::def Dutch East Indies
The Dutch colony in South-East Asia, ruled from Batavia (now Jakarta) and covering most of the territory of modern Indonesia. By 1930 it had a population of about 60.7 million, about two-thirds of them on the island of Java, which was a small part of the land area.
:::

## Colonial rule: the nature of the Dutch East Indies

### How the Dutch ruled

The Dutch had traded in the islands since the early seventeenth century, first through the Dutch East India Company (the VOC), and they ruled directly through the Dutch state from the early nineteenth century. By 1910 they controlled the whole archipelago, after a series of wars in the outer islands (the long war in Aceh, which lasted from 1873 into the early twentieth century, was the most costly). The government at Batavia was headed by a Governor-General appointed in The Hague. Below him a Dutch civil service, the *Binnenlands Bestuur*, supervised the **regents** (*bupati*) and village heads, who were Indonesians and who collected taxes and kept order. This was **indirect rule**: the Dutch ran the top and left the Indonesian aristocracy in place at the middle.

Colonial law divided people into three legal groups: **Europeans**, **Foreign Orientals** (mostly Chinese, with some Arabs) and **natives**. Each group had its own courts, taxes and schools. The Europeans were about 240,000 people in 1930 out of about 60.7 million (about 0.4 per cent), and the Chinese were about 1.2 million (about 2 per cent), according to the 1930 census, the last census of the colonial period. A small group at the top held the wealth and the power, and almost all of the people were poor farmers.

The economy was built for export. Sugar, coffee, rubber, tin and oil went to Europe and brought huge profits to the Dutch. From 1830 to 1870 the **Cultivation System** made Javanese villages grow export crops for the state, and after 1870 private firms took over the plantations. Peasants worked for low wages or paid heavy taxes, while the Netherlands became rich on the proceeds. This was the background to the change of attitude in 1901.

### The Ethical Policy

By 1900 many Dutch people felt uneasy. In 1899 the lawyer and politician **C. Th. van Deventer** had published an essay in the journal *De Gids* arguing that the Netherlands owed the people of the Indies a "debt of honour", a repayment of the wealth taken from them. In her speech from the throne on **17 September 1901**, Queen Wilhelmina announced what became known as the **Ethical Policy**, a duty to raise the welfare of the Indonesian people.

:::def Ethical Policy
The Dutch colonial policy announced in 1901, which accepted a moral duty to improve the welfare of the people of the Indies. It rested on three aims: **irrigation** (waterworks, credit and help for native industries), **migration** (moving people from crowded Java to the outer islands, called transmigration, from 1905) and **education**.
:::

The policy was well meant, but it was underfunded, and its results were modest. The numbers moved by transmigration were tiny compared with the growth of Java's population, and many officials became disillusioned. The Ethical Policy had mostly run out of energy by about 1930, in the Depression. Its **education** programme mattered most for this chapter. In 1900 about 1,500 Indonesians attended European schools, against about 13,000 Europeans. By 1928, about 75,000 Indonesians had completed Western primary school, and nearly 6,500 had completed secondary school. This was a tiny share of a population of tens of millions. But it was a new class of people, and it is a good example of the way in which the colonial state created its own opponents.

### Western education

The Dutch opened schools to train Indonesians as clerks, teachers, doctors and engineers, because the colony needed them. The best of them taught in **Dutch** and led to professional training: the medical school **STOVIA** in Batavia (the "School for the Training of Native Doctors"), the law school (the *Rechtshogeschool*, opened in Batavia in 1924) and the technical college at Bandung (1920), where Sukarno trained as an engineer and graduated in 1926. A few, including Mohammad Hatta and Sutan Sjahrir, went to study in the Netherlands itself.

That education did three things.
1. It gave a small elite a **common language**: Dutch, which let a Javanese doctor, a Minangkabau lawyer and an Ambonese teacher talk to each other.
2. It gave them **ideas**: the ideas of the French Revolution and the Enlightenment, nationalism in Europe, the Russo-Japanese war of 1904–05 (an Asian power had beaten a European one), the Chinese revolution of 1911, Indian nationalism and Marxism.
3. It gave them **a grievance**: they were well educated, yet were paid less than Europeans in the same job and shut out of the highest posts. A Dutch-educated Indonesian doctor was still a "native".

:::key Why Western education created nationalists
1. It created a **new class** of educated Indonesians, with careers that the Indonesian aristocracy did not control.
2. It gave them a **common language** (Dutch) across ethnic and island divisions, and the means to read about nationalism elsewhere.
3. It showed them the **gap** between the Dutch claim of equal opportunity and the colour bar in practice.
Most nationalist leaders before 1945 (Sukarno, Hatta, Sjahrir, Sartono, Soetomo, Amir Sjarifuddin) were products of this education.
:::

### The Volksraad

In response to pressure at the end of the First World War, the Dutch created a council to give a limited voice to local opinion. The **Volksraad** (People's Council) was set up by a law of 1916 and was opened in Batavia on **18 May 1918**. At the start it had 39 members, of whom 15 were Indonesians; some were elected and others appointed by the Governor-General. It could only **advise**. In 1927 it became a partly law-making body, but the Governor-General kept a veto, and by then it had 60 members, 30 of them Indonesians. Only a small fraction of the population could vote, in an indirect system: even in 1939, about one-tenth of the population chose the electors.

:::def Volksraad
The "People's Council", a partly elected body of the Dutch East Indies opened on 18 May 1918. It began with advisory powers only and was given limited law-making powers in 1927, but the Governor-General kept a veto, so it never became a real parliament.
:::

The Volksraad mattered less for what it did than for what it showed. It gave a nationalist a platform to speak in public in a government building, and the Indonesian members, such as Mohammad Husni Thamrin, who led the nationalists in the council in the 1930s, used it as a place to ask for reform. It also divided nationalists: those who sat in it, the **cooperators**, were accused by others of legitimising Dutch rule, and those who refused, the **non-cooperators**, were more likely to be arrested. The Dutch used this as a way of splitting the movement.

### The Chinese and trade

Trade in the Indies was dominated by foreigners. Large firms were European, but in the middle of the colonial economy, as shopkeepers, moneylenders, rice millers and dealers, stood the **Chinese**, who made up only about 2 per cent of the population. The Dutch had encouraged this role for centuries: the Chinese collected taxes, ran licensed monopolies (the notorious opium farms) and stood between the colonial firms and the peasants. The colour-coded legal system made the position stronger by treating Chinese as a separate group.

This had two consequences for nationalism. First, **economic resentment** of Chinese traders gave a spur to Indonesian organisation. The ancestor of the mass movement Sarekat Islam, the Sarekat Dagang Islam (Islamic Traders' Association), was founded by Muslim batik merchants in Surakarta in 1911 to protect themselves from Chinese competition. There were anti-Chinese riots in several towns (at Kudus in 1918, for example), which the Dutch could play on. Second, **the Chinese had their own nationalism**, directed at China: the revolution of 1911 inspired many in the Indies, and the Chinese kept their own schools and press, so that Indonesian and Chinese movements went on largely in parallel. Many nationalist leaders were therefore conscious that the nation they were creating would have to decide whether the Chinese belonged.

:::key The Chinese and Indonesian nationalism
1. Chinese traders were a **target** of economic resentment, which helped to start Sarekat Islam in 1911–12.
2. The colonial system **separated** Chinese from Indonesians in law, school and trade, so that the two communities did not form one movement.
3. This left an unresolved question for the nation: who was "Indonesian"? The Youth Pledge of 1928 said "one people", and later chapters show how far this was true.
:::

### Language and religion

Two things that did **not** come from the Dutch were as important as the schools: a language and a faith.

**Language.** The Dutch East Indies had hundreds of languages. The largest, Javanese, was spoken by roughly four in ten Indonesians, and it had levels of address that expressed social rank. Few Indonesians spoke Dutch, which the Dutch kept for the elite. The language that served all the islands was **Malay**, the trading language of the archipelago for centuries. The Dutch themselves used a form of it for the lower levels of the administration, and from 1917 a government publishing house, **Balai Pustaka**, printed Malay books and magazines. Nationalists saw Malay as the natural national language, because it belonged to no single ethnic group and was easy to learn, and because choosing Javanese would have given one group an advantage. At the second Youth Congress in Batavia, on **28 October 1928**, young nationalists from many regional youth associations made the **Youth Pledge** (*Sumpah Pemuda*): *one country, Indonesia; one people, the Indonesian people; one language, Indonesian*. The anthem *Indonesia Raya* ("Great Indonesia"), composed by W. R. Supratman, was played at the same congress.

:::def Youth Pledge
The declaration made at the Second Indonesian Youth Congress in Batavia on 28 October 1928: one homeland (Indonesia), one nation (the Indonesian nation) and one language (Indonesian, based on Malay). It is remembered in Indonesia as the moment when the idea of a single nation was publicly adopted by the younger generation.
:::

**Religion.** About nine in ten Indonesians were Muslims, although in many places Islam mixed with older beliefs, and there were Christians (in Ambon, North Sulawesi and parts of Sumatra), Hindus (in Bali) and others. Islam did three things for nationalism. It gave the islands a **shared identity** against the Dutch, who were Christians. It created **networks**, as pilgrims returned from Mecca and religious schools (*pesantren*) tied villages together. And Islamic organisations built **modern institutions**. **Sarekat Islam**, which took that name in September 1912, was led by **H. O. S. Tjokroaminoto** and grew into the first mass movement: it claimed 150,000 members by April 1913 and, by its own claim, two million in 1919, although those claims were generous. **Muhammadiyah**, founded in Yogyakarta by Kiai Haji Ahmad Dahlan in November 1912, ran modern schools and clinics and urged Muslims to purify their faith, and **Nahdlatul Ulama**, founded in 1926, spoke for the more traditional village scholars. These movements were mainly social and religious, but they trained organisers and gave millions a sense of belonging.

Religion also **divided**. Secular nationalists, Muslim leaders and communists argued about whether the future state should be Islamic, and Sukarno tried in a famous essay of 1926 to unite "nationalism, Islam and Marxism". The split between Islamic and communist members led Sarekat Islam to expel its communists in 1921 and to decline. These divisions return in the next chapter, when the new state has to decide what kind of country it is.

:::warn Do not describe nationalism as "Indonesian" before it was
Before the 1920s most educated people called themselves Javanese, Minangkabau or Ambonese, or simply Muslims. The word *Indonesia* was first used in a political sense by students in the Netherlands in the 1920s (Hatta's association in Holland was renamed *Perhimpunan Indonesia*, the Indonesian Association, in 1925). Write "the movement became national in the late 1920s" and avoid describing every movement before then as "Indonesian nationalism".
:::

## The growth of nationalism, 1908–42

### The first associations: Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam and the Indische Partij

The movement began with societies that were not yet political parties.
- **Budi Utomo** ("Noble Endeavour") was founded on **20 May 1908** by students of the STOVIA medical school in Batavia, inspired by the doctor Wahidin Sudirohusodo, to improve education and the position of the Javanese. It was elitist and Javanese, and it asked for reform, not independence. Its date is celebrated in Indonesia as National Awakening Day.
- **Sarekat Islam** (1912) was the first mass movement, as described above.
- The **Indische Partij** (1912), founded by the Eurasian Ernest Douwes Dekker with the Indonesians Tjipto Mangunkusumo and Suwardi Suryaningrat (later Ki Hajar Dewantara), was the first party to demand an independent "Indies" for all those born there, including Europeans and Chinese. The Dutch banned it in 1913.

These were small and local, but they established the habit of forming associations, publishing newspapers and holding congresses, and they began the **idea of a movement**.

### The PKI

The Indonesian communists were the first to organise a party that wanted to overthrow Dutch rule. A Dutch socialist, **Henk Sneevliet**, founded the Indies Social Democratic Association in 1914, and in 1920 it took the name **Partai Komunis Indonesia (PKI)**, the first communist party in Asia outside the Soviet Union. It recruited inside Sarekat Islam and the trade unions, and its leaders (Semaun, Darsono, Tan Malaka) were Indonesians, with Moscow's backing through the Communist International.

:::def Partai Komunis Indonesia (PKI)
The Indonesian Communist Party, formed in 1920 from the Indies Social Democratic Association (1914). It organised trade unions and led an unsuccessful rising against the Dutch in November 1926 and January 1927, after which the Dutch banned it. It re-emerged in 1945 and became the largest communist party outside the communist states.
:::

In **November 1926** the PKI rose in Java (Banten) and, in **January 1927**, in West Sumatra. The decision was taken against the advice of Tan Malaka and other leaders who thought the party was too weak. The Dutch crushed the risings within days, arrested thousands of people, and sent many leaders and sympathisers to a prison camp at Boven-Digoel in New Guinea. The party was banned and went underground for the rest of the colonial period.

The consequences were large. The Dutch decided that radical politics was dangerous, and moved to a policy of repression in the 1930s. The PKI's defeat left the field to the **nationalists**, who now had the space as the only broad movement, and who took care to distance themselves from the communists in order to avoid being banned. So the failed rising paradoxically helped the PNI to grow, and it explains why the leaders of the 1930s were cautious about Marxism.

### The PNI and Sukarno

On **4 July 1927** a group of young men in Bandung, led by the engineer **Sukarno**, founded the Indonesian National Association; in May 1928 it became the **Partai Nasional Indonesia (PNI)**. Its first leader was Sukarno, with the lawyers Sartono and Iskaq Tjokrohadisurjo and the older nationalist Tjipto Mangunkusumo among the founders.

:::def Partai Nasional Indonesia (PNI)
The Indonesian National Party, founded in Bandung by Sukarno and others in 1927 (as an association, and as a party from May 1928). It was the first all-Indonesian party that was secular and aimed above all at independence, to be won by **non-cooperation** with the Dutch.
:::

Its aims and methods were these.
- **Independence** (*Indonesia Merdeka*, "Free Indonesia") for the whole archipelago, not for a region or religion. The party tried to unite the three streams of nationalism, Islam and Marxism.
- **Non-cooperation**: its members would not sit in the Volksraad or take Dutch posts, and it would organise the masses through meetings, schools, cooperatives and the press.
- **Marhaenism**, Sukarno's name for his ideology, from the name of a poor farmer he said he had met near Bandung: the people the movement was for were the small peasants and workers who owned a little and were still exploited.
- A **national symbolism**: the red-and-white flag and the anthem *Indonesia Raya*.

Sukarno's skill as a speaker was extraordinary. He spoke in Malay and Javanese, drew on wayang (shadow-puppet) stories and on the Javanese hope for a coming just king, and persuaded his audiences that independence was close. The PNI's membership stayed small and was found mostly in a few cities. Its meetings, however, drew far bigger crowds, and the Dutch took the party seriously.

On **29 December 1929** Sukarno and other PNI leaders were arrested, in a series of raids across Java, after rumours of a rising. His trial in the Bandung court began in August 1930. In his defence speech, called *Indonesia Menggugat* (**Indonesia Accuses**), Sukarno turned the case round: he attacked Dutch rule, as an economic exploitation, and defended the right of the Indonesian people to rule themselves. He was sentenced in December 1930 to four years in prison, later reduced, and he was released at the end of 1931. The speech was published, and it gave the movement one of its founding texts. It also set a pattern: nationalist leaders turned their trials into platforms.

:::key Why the PNI mattered
1. It was the **first all-Indonesian secular party**, aiming at independence, with a single leader who could speak to all groups.
2. It gave nationalism **symbols** (flag, anthem, language) and a **doctrine** (Marhaenism, non-cooperation).
3. It showed that the Dutch would **repress** peaceful nationalism: arrests in 1929 made Sukarno a martyr and the party's leaders a symbol.
:::

### The split of the nationalists: Partindo and the Club Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia

With its leader in prison, the PNI was paralysed. On **25 April 1931** an extraordinary congress dissolved it, to start again free of the court's verdict. It split in two.
- **Partindo (Partai Indonesia)** was founded in 1931 by Sartono, who had been one of Sukarno's defence lawyers, to carry on the PNI's mass politics. When Sukarno was released he joined it in 1932 and became its chairman in July.
- The **Club Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia** (the "Indonesian National Education Club", often called the **PNI-Baru**, the new PNI) was led by two students who came home from the Netherlands, **Sutan Sjahrir** (back in 1931) and **Mohammad Hatta** (back in 1932). They believed that mass meetings were useless against Dutch police, and that the first need was to train a **cadre** of educated leaders who understood politics and could survive repression.

The disagreement was real. Sukarno believed in mass action and the power of the crowd and of inspiring speeches. Hatta and Sjahrir believed in education and organisation, and criticised Sukarno's "leader worship".

:::key Sukarno or Hatta and Sjahrir: two kinds of nationalism
- **Sukarno and Partindo**: mass politics, the nation as a feeling to be awakened, inspiring speeches, a single leader.
- **Hatta, Sjahrir and the Club**: cadre politics, the nation as a political programme to be built, discipline and training, a group of leaders.
The Dutch struck down both: they arrested Sukarno on 1 August 1933 for his writings and exiled him to Ende on the island of Flores in 1934 and then, from February 1938, to Bengkulu in Sumatra; they arrested Hatta and Sjahrir in February 1934 and sent them to Boven-Digoel and then to the island of Banda Neira.
:::

**Mohammad Hatta** (born in Bukittinggi, West Sumatra, in 1902) is the one who completes the picture of leadership. A Minangkabau economist, he studied in Rotterdam from 1921 and chaired the Indonesian Association in the Netherlands in the later 1920s. In 1927 he took part in the Brussels congress of the League against Imperialism, and, arrested in the Netherlands in September 1927, he made a defence speech in The Hague in 1928 (usually called *Indonesia Free*) and was acquitted. He was a systematic organiser, a believer in cooperatives and in education, and an exacting critic. The partnership of Sukarno, the orator, and Hatta, the administrator, would be the leadership of the independence movement.

**Sartono** (1900–68), a lawyer, defended Sukarno in 1930, led Partindo when it was founded in 1931, and was a Volksraad member in the 1930s. Partindo, harassed by the Dutch, dissolved itself in November 1936, and many of its members joined Gerindo.

### The Dutch reply: repression, and a few concessions

From the late 1920s the Dutch response was mostly repression. From 1931 the Governor-General, **B. C. de Jonge**, followed a policy often summed up as "rest and order": meetings were watched, leaders were arrested or exiled, and the Depression, which wrecked the export economy, made the colony poorer and the Dutch less willing to spend on reform. Meanwhile the Ethical Policy faded. The Dutch also relied on the colony's cheap exports, and thus had a strong interest in keeping control.

Repression had two effects. It **removed the leaders** and the parties that wanted independence: by 1934 Sukarno, Hatta and Sjahrir were all in exile. But it also **radicalised a generation** and left the cooperative nationalists as the only legal voice. The movement went in two directions: a minority went underground (Sjahrir wrote of cadre groups that would exist whether or not parties were allowed), and the majority turned to cooperation within the Volksraad and local councils.

### The Soetardjo Petition, 1936

The cooperators' best attempt was the **Soetardjo Petition**. On **15 July 1936** **Soetardjo Kartohadikusumo**, a Javanese member of the Volksraad, introduced a petition asking the Queen and the Dutch government to call a conference between the Netherlands and the Indies to prepare **autonomy** for the Indies within the Kingdom, over a period of ten years. After the ten-year time limit was dropped, the Volksraad voted to accept it, by 26 votes to 20, with six Indonesian members voting against.

The Dutch government, however, left it for more than two years and rejected it on **16 November 1938**, saying that its aims were unclear and in conflict with the existing constitution. The effect of the rejection was powerful. It showed that even the mildest and most legal form of nationalism, an appeal by loyal cooperators to the Dutch Crown, would get nowhere. It strengthened the argument of those who said the Dutch would concede nothing except under pressure, and it prepared Indonesians to accept the Japanese offer in 1942.

:::def Soetardjo Petition
The request put to the Volksraad in July 1936 by Soetardjo Kartohadikusumo for a conference to prepare the Indies for autonomy within the Dutch Kingdom. The Volksraad accepted it by 26 votes to 20, but the Dutch government rejected it on 16 November 1938.
:::

### Gerindo and Gapi, 1937–39

By the later 1930s a new, more flexible nationalism had emerged, partly because of the international situation: **fascism** was growing in Europe, and Japan was at war in China.
- **Gerindo (Gerakan Rakyat Indonesia, the Indonesian People's Movement)** was founded on **24 May 1937**, partly by former members of Partindo and led by Amir Sjarifuddin, A. K. Gani, Mohammad Yamin and Sartono. It was left-wing and nationalist but anti-fascist, and its leaders were ready to cooperate with the Dutch, to the point of taking seats on councils, against the Japanese threat. It sought a full parliament for the Indies and became the only legal party of radical nationalism.
- **Gapi (Gabungan Politik Indonesia, the Indonesian Political Federation)** was founded on **21 May 1939**, at a meeting chaired by Mohammad Husni Thamrin, when eight organisations joined, among them Gerindo, Thamrin's Parindra (the Greater Indonesia Party), the Islamic party PSII and the Pasundan association of West Java. Its chairmen were Thamrin, Amir Sjarifuddin and Abikusno Tjokrosujoso. Its slogan was **Indonesia Berparlemen**, "Indonesia with a parliament". In December 1939 a **Congress of the Indonesian People** attended by about ninety groups called on the Dutch to make the Volksraad a real parliament, and adopted Indonesian as the national language and *Merah Putih* (the red-and-white flag) and *Indonesia Raya* as the flag and anthem.

The Dutch did not respond. They set up a commission (the Visman Commission) in 1940 to hear nationalists, and the Governor-General promised a conference after the war, which Gapi found disappointing. After Germany occupied the Netherlands in May 1940 some Indonesians hoped for change, but the promise of a post-war conference was all that came.

## What nationalism had become by 1941

Put the story together. By 1941 Indonesian nationalism had these features.
- A **name** (Indonesia), a **flag**, an **anthem** and a **language**, adopted in 1928 and repeated in 1939.
- **Leaders** with national reputations (Sukarno and Hatta above all), most of them in prison or exile in the 1930s.
- Several **traditions**: secular nationalism (the PNI), Islam (Sarekat Islam, Muhammadiyah, Nahdlatul Ulama), communism (the PKI, banned), and legal reformism (Parindra, Gapi, the Volksraad).
- A **small base**: educated townspeople, some traders, some students, and a wider audience among villagers who heard of Sukarno and the hope of independence. Most peasants did not belong to any party.
- **No concessions** from the Dutch. Their rule looked unshakeable in 1941, with a strong army and navy, the police, and a loyal civil service.

The central point for the question is that nationalism had **grown**, but it had not yet **won** power or a mass organisation, and without a great shock it might have taken decades more. That shock came on 8 March 1942.

## The Japanese occupation, 1942–45

### Japan's advance and the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere

In 1940, with France and the Netherlands defeated in Europe, the Japanese Foreign Minister, Matsuoka Yosuke, announced a plan for a "**Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere**" (on 1 August 1940). Japan claimed that it would free Asia from European colonialism and unite it in an economic and political bloc led by Japan. In reality it meant **control of resources**: Japan was short of oil, rubber, tin and rice, and the Dutch East Indies had all of them. After the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941, Japan's armies moved south. Japanese troops landed in Borneo in January 1942, and on **8–9 March 1942** (sources give either date) the Dutch commander on Java surrendered at Kalijati. The colonial army of the Indies, which had held the islands for centuries, collapsed within weeks.

:::def Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere
Japan's wartime vision of an Asian bloc, free of Western colonial rule and led by Japan, which promised "Asia for the Asians" but in practice meant that conquered territories such as the Dutch East Indies were run for Japan's military and economic needs.
:::

The effect on Indonesian opinion was dramatic. Many Indonesians welcomed the Japanese, who presented themselves as **liberators**, and an old prophecy attributed to the Javanese king Jayabaya said that a short period of rule by "yellow men" would be followed by freedom. Above all, the myth of Dutch invincibility was broken. Dutch prestige had been the basis of the colonial order, and it could not be restored. The Dutch were interned (about 100,000 European civilians, with some Chinese, were put in camps), the Dutch language and flag were banned, and the Indonesian flag and anthem were at first not allowed for general use either: they were permitted only after Koiso's promise of 1944.

### The administration

The Japanese divided the Indies into three zones of military administration, which reduced the unity that the Dutch had given the archipelago.
- **Java and Madura** under the 16th Army, based in Jakarta (the Japanese reinstated the old name for Batavia). Java, the most populous island, was governed more closely than the rest.
- **Sumatra** under the 25th Army (at first based in Singapore, then in Bukittinggi from 1943).
- **Borneo, Sulawesi and the eastern islands** under the Imperial Navy, whose headquarters was at Makassar.

This division mattered later: the Army was more hostile to independence, and the Navy, which was more interested in preparing eastern Indonesia and in a deal with nationalist leaders, was more sympathetic. Admiral **Maeda Tadashi** in Jakarta would play a key role in 1945.

Within the zones, the Japanese made **big changes in administration**. They removed Dutch officials and interned them, and had to fill their posts with Indonesians, since the Japanese were few. Indonesians were promoted to jobs that the Dutch had kept for themselves, in the civil service, the police, the schools and the railways: this was a real change from Dutch practice, though the top posts were still Japanese and the new officials had little real power. The Japanese abolished all political parties on 20 March 1942, but created new bodies (see below), reorganised villages into **neighbourhood associations** (*tonarigumi*, groups of ten to twenty households, which controlled rice deliveries, labour and security), set up local advisory councils, and in September 1943 created a **Central Advisory Council** for Java with **Sukarno** as chairman. The Japanese language was taught in schools, and **Indonesian** (Malay) became the language of administration and education, because the Dutch language was banned: this gave the national language a much wider use than the Dutch had ever allowed.

:::key What the changes of administration did for nationalism
1. **Indonesians held posts** of responsibility that the Dutch had denied them, which trained a generation of administrators.
2. **Dutch was banned** and Indonesian promoted, so that the national language reached into every school and office.
3. **The village network** (*tonarigumi*) tied remote villages to the centre for the first time.
4. But the Japanese set up **three separate zones**, and everything was done for Japan's war, so these gains came with harsh control.
:::

### The economy: the burden of war

The economic effects were severe.
- **Trade collapsed.** The Indies had exported sugar, rubber, tin and oil to world markets. Allied submarines sank so much Japanese shipping that exports were stopped, and Japan wanted only oil and a few strategic goods. The sugar and rubber plantations were abandoned or planted with food and with crops such as cotton, jute and castor for the war.
- **Rice and prices.** The Japanese army took a fixed share of the rice crop by compulsory delivery, paid at low prices. Cloth, medicine and other imports ran out, and the Japanese printed military currency, so that **prices rose steeply**. By 1944–45 there was **famine** in parts of Java. Estimates of the deaths from famine vary widely: the economic historian Pierre van der Eng has estimated about 2.4 million in Java in 1944–45, and other figures are lower, so the safe statement is that it was very large.
- **Forced labour.** See below.

The economic effect on nationalism was twofold. It created widespread **hardship and resentment**, which turned many Indonesians against the Japanese by 1944. It also **destroyed the old economy** and the Dutch dominance in it, and left the Chinese traders in a weaker position, so that the nationalists inherited a country without a functioning colonial economy.

### Conscription and romusha

The Japanese demanded two kinds of compulsory service, and both mattered.
- **Labour: romusha** (*rōmusha*, "labourers"). The Japanese needed labour for roads, airfields, mines and the Burma–Thailand railway, and they took it from the villages, at first as "volunteers" and then by quotas. Estimates of numbers vary so widely that they should be given as a range. Ricklefs's *A History of Modern Indonesia since c.1200* (4th edition 2008) and the Library of Congress country study give figures from **4 to 10 million** people forced to work, mostly on Java and nearby; of those sent overseas or to other islands (**200,000 to 500,000** from Java), not more than **70,000** are said to have survived the war. Another estimate, by the Japanese historian Shigeru Satō, is that about **270,000** Javanese were sent outside Java, of whom about 15 per cent died or were stranded. The numbers differ because "romusha" was never defined exactly (it can include local unpaid labour as well as men sent overseas) and because records were destroyed or never kept. Sukarno was linked to the recruiting, and he later said that he regretted it, which remains controversial.
- **Military service.** Japan formed the **Heiho** (auxiliaries) in 1943 from Indonesian recruits attached to the Japanese army under Japanese officers, the **Seinendan** (youth corps) and **Keibodan** (vigilance corps) for training and local security, and, from late 1943, **Peta**. By 1945 tens of thousands of Indonesians had had military training. This was **not** formal conscription in the European sense, but it was compulsory in spirit, and it created large groups of young men with discipline and weapons.

:::def Romusha
The forced labourers recruited in the Japanese-occupied Indies, mostly from Java, to build roads, airfields and railways in the Indies and elsewhere in South-East Asia. Estimates of how many were recruited range from several million, and of how many died, from tens of thousands to far more, because the term was never exactly defined and records are poor.
:::

The effect of these demands on nationalism was mixed. The **romusha** system caused fear and hatred, and it turned the village against the Japanese. The **military training** gave nationalism a weapon: the Japanese trained, in effect, the soldiers who would fight the Dutch.

### The Triple A movement, 1942

The Japanese needed Indonesian help in the first weeks, and they set up a propaganda movement. The **Triple A movement** (*Gerakan Tiga A*, from its three slogans: "Japan the Protector of Asia, Japan the Light of Asia, Japan the Leader of Asia") was launched in March–April 1942 under the leadership of an Indonesian, **Mr Samsuddin** (Raden Syamsuddin), and with the help of a Japanese propaganda officer, Shimizu Hitoshi. It put on rallies and posters, and tried to win support for Japan. It **failed**. It was seen as a Japanese puppet, it had no well-known leader (Sukarno and Hatta were not involved), and its slogans were ignored. It was dissolved by early 1943 and replaced by a more serious body.

:::def Triple A movement
The first Japanese propaganda organisation in the occupied Indies (1942), named after its slogans that Japan was the "Protector, Light and Leader of Asia". It had little support because it was run by an unknown Indonesian under Japanese direction, and it was replaced by Putera in 1943.
:::

### Why Japan encouraged nationalism, and how: Putera, Peta and the promise of independence

The Japanese did not set out to create Indonesian independence, since they wished to keep Java's rice, oil and labour for themselves. But they were forced to give ground, for **four reasons**.
1. **To win the war effort.** Japan needed labour, rice and soldiers, and it needed Indonesians to **mobilise the population** and to defend the islands if the Allies invaded. Indonesians would only do that for people they respected.
2. **To remove Dutch influence.** Anti-Western propaganda (the slogan "Asia for the Asians") aimed to turn Indonesians against the Dutch and the West for ever, and to make Dutch return impossible.
3. **Failure of early methods.** The Triple A movement did not work. Japan needed the leaders that Indonesians trusted, notably **Sukarno** (whom they freed from exile in Bengkulu), and **Hatta**.
4. **The military situation.** From 1943, and especially after the fall of Saipan in July 1944, Japan was losing. Japan's leaders hoped to bind Indonesians to the Japanese side by promising a future independence.

The methods were these.
- **Putera (Pusat Tenaga Rakyat, Centre of People's Power)**, launched in **March 1943**, was led by four Indonesians, **Sukarno**, **Hatta**, **Ki Hajar Dewantara** (the educator Suwardi Suryaningrat) and the Muslim leader **K. H. Mas Mansur**. It was a mass organisation intended to mobilise Java for the war, collect rice and recruit labour. It was far more successful than the Triple A movement, since it brought the best-known nationalists, and their speeches reached villages that no party had touched before. Sukarno's speeches, carried by the radio and loudspeakers that the Japanese provided, now reached places that no party had touched before. The Japanese soon felt, however, that Putera served Indonesian aims as much as their own. It was replaced in **January 1944** by the **Jawa Hokokai** (Java Service Association), under closer Japanese control.
- **Peta (Sukarela Tentera Pembela Tanah Air, Volunteer Army of Defenders of the Homeland)**, created on **3 October 1943**, was an army of Indonesian volunteers, with **Indonesian officers** in command of its battalions (*daidan*), unlike the Heiho. It was set up after a petition from Indonesians, usually credited to Gatot Mangkupraja, and in order to resist an Allied invasion. By the end of the war it had **69 battalions**, 66 of them on Java and three on Bali, with about **37,000 men** on Java, Madura and Bali, and about 20,000 more in a similar force in Sumatra. Peta's officers were trained by the Japanese and included men such as **Sudirman**, a teacher, and **Suharto**, who would later be among the leaders of the Indonesian army. Peta provided the core of the national army in 1945.
- **Promises of self-government.** On **7 September 1944**, after the fall of Saipan and the resignation of Tojo, the new Prime Minister, **Koiso Kuniaki**, promised that Indonesia would be given independence "in the future". The Japanese then allowed the Indonesian flag to be flown and the anthem *Indonesia Raya* to be sung. In March 1945 they set up the **Investigating Committee for Preparatory Work for Independence** (BPUPK), which met from 28 May 1945. The next chapter follows this up.
- **Propaganda and mass organisations**: the Japanese also set up Islamic bodies (the Masyumi, a federation of Islamic organisations, in 1943) and youth organisations, which gave the Muslim leaders a role.

:::key Why Japan encouraged nationalism
1. **The war effort**: it needed labour, rice and soldiers, which only trusted leaders could deliver.
2. **Anti-Western feeling**: "Asia for the Asians" was meant to keep the Dutch out for good.
3. **The failure of the Triple A movement** showed that only Sukarno and Hatta could mobilise the population.
4. **Defeat**: from 1943 and especially 1944, Japan promised independence to keep Indonesians loyal.
The effect was that Japan built the **organisation** that Indonesian nationalism had always lacked: mass bodies, a trained army, and the leaders' public voice.
:::

:::note Peta in Java and Peta in Malaya
The Malay nationalists in Malaya also had a Peta (Pembela Tanah Air, "Defenders of the Homeland"), led by Ibrahim Yaacob (see [The route to independence (1)](#hist.d.malaysia.1)), but it was tiny, with about 2,000 men, whereas the Indonesian Peta was large. The difference matters: the Japanese hoped to use the Indonesians for the defence of Java, which they thought would be invaded, and not Malaya. Do not mix them up: the Peta of this chapter is the Indonesian one.
:::

### Resistance to the Japanese

Not all Indonesians cooperated. The resistance took three forms.
- **The underground.** Sutan **Sjahrir**, who stayed out of Putera, led a small group of intellectuals in Jakarta who listened to Allied radio, kept in touch with students and judged that Japan would lose; he did not want independence to look like a Japanese gift. **Amir Sjarifuddin** received money from the Dutch in early 1942 to organise an anti-Japanese underground. He was arrested by the Japanese in January 1943 and sentenced to death, and Sukarno's intervention saved him. The communists (the old PKI) were underground, too, though with little organisation.
- **Revolts.** Villagers and religious leaders rebelled against the Japanese as the occupation grew harsher, in **Aceh** in November 1942 (18 Japanese and over 100 Acehnese were killed, on Ricklefs's figures) and at Singaparna in West Java in February 1944. The most important was the **Peta revolt at Blitar** in East Java on **14 February 1945**, led by a Peta platoon commander, **Supriyadi**, against the abuses of the Japanese and the conditions of the romusha. The Japanese suppressed it and Supriyadi disappeared. This showed that a Japanese-trained army could turn on the Japanese, and the memory of it mattered later.
- **Students and young people (pemuda).** In Jakarta groups of young men such as the Menteng 31 dormitory group, with **Sukarni**, **Chairul Saleh** and **Wikana**, held discussions in dormitories, listened to foreign broadcasts and criticised Sukarno and Hatta for being too cautious. The *pemuda* ("youth") are the main actors of the next chapter.

The importance of the resistance should not be exaggerated: it was small compared with collaboration, and it could not have overthrown the Japanese. But it gave nationalism a second tradition: **independence won by the people**, not given by the Japanese.

## Weighing the causes: what made Indonesian nationalism?

Now bring the two halves together. The question asks you to say **how** nationalism grew and **why**, and to rank the reasons.

| Type | Cause | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Colonial state | Dutch rule, a single colony | Created the territory and the common enemy |
| Colonial state | Western education and the Ethical Policy | Created a new elite, a common language, ideas, and a grievance |
| Economy | Chinese trade, export economy, Depression | Gave economic grievance and a target (Sarekat Islam, the Chinese) |
| Indonesian society | Islam, language | Gave a shared identity, networks and the national language |
| Politics | Parties and leaders (PNI, Sukarno, Hatta) | Gave the movement organisation, symbols and a programme |
| Reaction | Dutch repression and refusal of reform (1927–39) | Radicalised the elite; showed cooperation failed |
| War | Japanese occupation, 1942–45 | Destroyed Dutch power; gave mass organisation, arms, offices and a promise |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The **ideas, leaders, symbols and language** of Indonesian nationalism were largely in place before 1942: the Youth Pledge of 1928, the PNI, Sukarno and Hatta, the flag and anthem. What the Japanese gave was the **opportunity and the means**: the end of Dutch power, mass organisation in the villages, military training, administrative experience for Indonesians, and a public platform for the leaders. So the occupation was the **decisive accelerator** but not the **origin**. Nationalism without the Japanese would probably have been slower; the Japanese without the earlier movement would have had nothing to build on.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: how far did Japan make Indonesian nationalism?)
- **George Kahin**, *Nationalism and Revolution in Indonesia* (1952), the first major study in English, wrote after studying the revolution on the spot and stressed that the independence struggle grew out of a nationalist movement developed under Dutch rule. The Japanese occupation changed the circumstances but not the aim.
- **Benedict Anderson**, *Java in a Time of Revolution: Occupation and Resistance, 1944–1946* (1972), argued that the Japanese occupation had transformed Javanese society, above all by training and arming the young (*pemuda*), who then drove the revolution of 1945. For him the occupation was the formative experience.
- **M. C. Ricklefs**, *A History of Modern Indonesia since c.1200* (4th edition 2008), gives a balanced account: the Dutch repression of the 1930s had left nationalism weak, and the Japanese made it a mass movement and left Indonesians with organisation, weapons and experience, though many suffered terribly under the occupation.
You can use this at Paper 4 to show why the question allows more than one judgement. But Paper 4 rewards your own argument supported by evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If we ask what the occupation **created that did not exist before**, the answer is organisation and arms. Before 1942 the nationalists had ideas and leaders but no mass organisation: the PNI had been dissolved in 1931 and the leaders of the 1930s, Sukarno, Hatta and Sjahrir, were in exile. Under the Japanese, Putera (March 1943) put Sukarno and Hatta in front of the villages of Java, and Peta, created on 3 October 1943, trained about 37,000 men on Java, Madura and Bali under Indonesian officers; two of its officers, Sudirman and Suharto, would command the national army. Neither Sarekat Islam, the PNI nor Gapi had reached so far. But the criterion also shows the limit of the claim: organisation was not the same as ideas. The Japanese provided the machinery, but the flag, the anthem, the language and the demand for independence were all older, adopted at the Youth Congress of 1928."

*Commentary.* A criterion is applied with precise detail, and the answer begins to test the claim against evidence before 1942. Level 4. For Level 5 it needs the other factors weighed explicitly, the harm that the Japanese did to their own cause, and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is partly valid. The occupation was the most important factor in the **speed** of Indonesian nationalism and in giving it the means to win, but it was not the most important factor in its **growth**, which had been made by the Dutch system and by Indonesians over the previous four decades. I shall test it with three criteria: what each factor created; whether nationalism could have grown without it; and what was genuinely new after 1942.

On the first criterion the case for the occupation is strong. By 1941 Indonesian nationalism had a name, a flag, an anthem and a language, but it lacked three things: power, organisation and weapons. The Japanese supplied all three. The surrender of the Dutch on Java on 8–9 March 1942 destroyed the colonial state that had ruled for three centuries and made it impossible for the Dutch to return on the old terms. Putera, formed in March 1943, gave Sukarno, Hatta, Ki Hajar Dewantara and Mas Mansur a platform from which to reach the villages. Peta, formed on 3 October 1943, trained about 37,000 men on Java, Madura and Bali under Indonesian officers, and its veterans, including Sudirman and Suharto, became the army of the Republic. Indonesians were moved into administrative posts that the Dutch had kept for themselves, and the Dutch ban meant that Indonesian became the language of schools and offices. Finally, the promise of independence by Koiso on 7 September 1944 turned a hope into a Japanese commitment that Sukarno and Hatta could later use. Benedict Anderson's *Java in a Time of Revolution* (1972) argues that the young men trained and politicised under the Japanese, the pemuda, were the driving force of the revolution, and the Blitar revolt of 14 February 1945 shows how quickly Japanese training could be turned against the Japanese.

On the second criterion, however, the occupation was not necessary for nationalism to exist. Education made a class of Indonesians who could read about nationalism, and the Ethical Policy's schools, begun in 1901, produced by 1928 about 75,000 primary-school graduates and nearly 6,500 secondary graduates. They did not need the Japanese to find their grievance: the Dutch told them in every post they were refused. Sukarno founded the PNI in 1927, the young nationalists made the Youth Pledge on 28 October 1928, and Sukarno's trial speech *Indonesia Accuses* in 1930 made independence a public demand. Gapi in 1939 united eight organisations behind the cry of Indonesia Berparlemen. George Kahin's *Nationalism and Revolution in Indonesia* (1952) argues that the independence struggle grew from a nationalist movement developed under Dutch rule. And Dutch repression, which exiled the leaders in the 1930s and rejected the Soetardjo Petition on 16 November 1938, was itself a major cause: it showed that peaceful reform would not work and prepared Indonesians to take what the Japanese offered.

On the third criterion the answer is more balanced still. The occupation was not entirely helpful to nationalism. It was harsh: the romusha system took millions of people from their villages, on the estimates of Ricklefs and others, and the famine of 1944–45 killed a very large number; the Triple A movement of 1942 failed because Indonesians did not believe in it. The Japanese also divided the archipelago into three military zones and encouraged nationalism only so far as it served their war. The leaders were divided: Sukarno and Hatta chose to work with the Japanese, while Sjahrir stayed in the underground, and Sukarno's role in recruiting romusha remains controversial. The nationalists were therefore using the Japanese as much as being used by them.

Overall, the occupation was the most important factor in turning a small movement into one that could win independence, but it was not the most important in creating it. The Dutch education system, the nationalist leaders and parties of 1908–41, the Indonesian language and Islam made nationalism; the Japanese gave it the opportunity, the organisation and the army, and Dutch repression made Indonesians ready to take it. The fairest verdict is that the occupation was the decisive accelerator, but that without the earlier movement the Japanese would have had nothing to build on."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): three explicit criteria, a balanced argument with a clear thread (accelerator against cause), historians used as support rather than a list, and a sustained judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise, selected detail (dates, figures, names) that is used for the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 800 words, 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the importance of Sukarno's leadership before 1945. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the importance" asks you to **weigh** Sukarno's contribution against other things (other leaders, social and economic forces, the Japanese), and to say how big it was and why. You need **criteria**: for example what Sukarno did that no one else could, what he failed to do, and how far the outcome depended on him.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) **what Sukarno uniquely contributed** (symbols, mass appeal, unity); (2) **what limited him** (repression, Hatta's critique, his dependence on the Japanese); (3) **whether the movement would have developed without him.** For: the PNI (1927) and Marhaenism, the red-and-white flag and Indonesia Raya, *Indonesia Accuses* (1930), the attempt to unite nationalism, Islam and Marxism (1926), Partindo (1932), Putera (1943) and the Central Advisory Council, the Pancasila speech (1 June 1945), the partnership with Hatta. Against: arrested in 1929 and 1933 and absent in exile for most of the 1930s; the PNI collapsed without him; Hatta and Sjahrir offered a different, cadre-based leadership; much of the groundwork (Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam, the Youth Pledge of 1928) did not depend on him; his collaboration with the Japanese was criticised. Judgement: he was very important as a symbol and mobiliser, but not as the sole creator of the movement.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "Sukarno was the most important leader of Indonesian nationalism. He founded the PNI in 1927 and made good speeches. He was arrested by the Dutch in 1929 and sent to prison. During the Japanese occupation he led Putera. In 1945 he declared independence. So his leadership was very important."

*Commentary.* Accurate, but a sequence of events with no explanation of why they mattered and no other factor. "Very important" is asserted. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 (5–6): correct facts but no precision (no dates for Putera, no detail of the trial).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If we ask what Sukarno contributed that no one else did, the answer is that he made nationalism a **popular cause**. Hatta was the better organiser and Sjahrir the sharper thinker, but only Sukarno could speak to crowds in the language they understood. He gave the movement its symbols, the red-and-white flag and the anthem, and its doctrine, Marhaenism, a belief that independence was for the small farmer and worker. His defence speech at Bandung in 1930, *Indonesia Accuses*, turned a trial into a platform. But the criterion also shows a weakness. A leader who is the movement's voice is also its weak point: when the Dutch arrested him in December 1929 the PNI was paralysed and dissolved itself in April 1931, and when they exiled him in 1934 to Flores, he was out of politics for eight years."

*Commentary.* A criterion (the unique contribution) is used and tested against a counter-point, using precise evidence. Level 4. For Level 5 it needs the other leaders and the Japanese period weighed, and a sustained judgement.

**A Level 5 conclusion** (after paragraphs on his symbols and mass appeal, his limits in the 1930s, his partnership with Hatta, and his role under the Japanese).

"Overall, Sukarno's leadership was very important, but in a particular way. He was the **symbol and mobiliser** of Indonesian nationalism: the flag, the anthem, Marhaenism and the speeches turned an elite movement into a popular one, and in 1943 Putera carried his voice across Java. In that role no other leader could have replaced him, and he and Hatta together were the leadership that took the movement through the Japanese years. But he was not its **creator**: Budi Utomo, Sarekat Islam and the Youth Pledge of 1928 came before or without him, and the education system made the class from which his supporters came. He was not its **organiser** either: the PNI collapsed when he was arrested, and it was Hatta and Sjahrir's cadre idea that survived. And his importance was limited by repression, which kept him in prison or exile for most of the 1930s, and by his later collaboration with the Japanese, which he defended as a means to independence and others condemned as a betrayal. I conclude that Sukarno was the most important leader in making nationalism a mass movement, but that the movement was made by many hands, and that his greatest moment, the proclamation of 17 August 1945, depended on Hatta, the pemuda and the end of the war as much as on him."

*Commentary.* A clear judgement that separates three roles (symbol, creator, organiser) and gives a ranking that the paragraphs have earned. Table A Level 5; Table B depends on the detail in the paragraphs before it.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the effects of Western education on the growth of Indonesian nationalism." [30]

A student wrote:

"Western education was important for Indonesian nationalism. The Dutch set up schools under the Ethical Policy, and some Indonesians went to the Netherlands. Sukarno studied engineering at Bandung and Hatta studied in Rotterdam. They learned about nationalism and democracy in Europe. Then they came back and set up parties. So Western education caused Indonesian nationalism."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): one claim, supported by two examples, with no criteria, no balance and no test of "caused". It does not say **how** education led to nationalism, and it ignores other causes (Islam, the Chinese, repression, the Japanese). Table B Level 2 (4–6): accurate but vague: no numbers, no dates, and "learned about nationalism" is asserted.

**The fix.** Choose a criterion and test it. "If we ask what education **made possible**, it created a new class with a common language: by 1928, about 75,000 Indonesians had completed Western primary school and nearly 6,500 had completed secondary school, from a population of about 60 million. Dutch let a Javanese doctor and a Minangkabau lawyer talk to each other, and gave them access to the ideas of the French Revolution, Marxism and the Chinese and Indian movements. It also gave them a grievance, because an educated Indonesian was still paid less than a European and shut out of the highest posts. But education alone does not explain the movement. Its numbers were small, and the mass movement, Sarekat Islam, came from traders and Islam, not from the Dutch schools; and the nationalists' choice of Malay, not Dutch, as the national language in 1928 shows that they turned the colonial tool into something of their own." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement, and the paragraph is on its way to Level 4 or 5.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was the Ethical Policy, and what were its three aims?
A: A Dutch policy announced by Queen Wilhelmina on 17 September 1901, accepting a moral duty to raise the welfare of the people of the Indies. Its aims were irrigation, migration (transmigration from Java) and education.
Q: Why did the Volksraad (1918) not satisfy nationalists?
A: It began with advisory powers only, with 39 members of whom 15 were Indonesians; in 1927 it gained limited law-making powers, but the Governor-General kept a veto and only a small part of the population could vote. Nationalists divided between cooperators who sat in it and non-cooperators who refused.
Q: Why did Chinese trade matter for nationalism?
A: The Chinese, about 2 per cent of the people, held a large part of the middle of the economy (shops, moneylending, rice milling). Resentment of them helped to start the Sarekat Dagang Islam (1911) and Sarekat Islam (1912), and the separation of Chinese and Indonesian communities left the question of who belonged to the nation unresolved.
Q: What did the Youth Pledge of 28 October 1928 declare?
A: One country (Indonesia), one people (the Indonesian people) and one language (Indonesian, based on Malay).
Q: Why was the PNI founded in 1927 important, and what happened to it?
A: It was the first all-Indonesian secular party aiming at independence, led by Sukarno, with non-cooperation and mass meetings as its methods. Sukarno and other leaders were arrested on 29 December 1929, and the party dissolved itself on 25 April 1931.
Q: How did Hatta and Sjahrir's Club Pendidikan Nasional Indonesia differ from Sukarno's Partindo?
A: The Club (PNI-Baru) stressed training a cadre of educated leaders and organisation; Partindo stressed mass politics and Sukarno's leadership. The Dutch arrested Hatta and Sjahrir in February 1934 and had already moved against Sukarno.
Q: What was the Soetardjo Petition, and what happened to it?
A: A request by Soetardjo Kartohadikusumo, passed by the Volksraad in July 1936 by 26 votes to 20, for a conference to prepare autonomy within the Dutch Kingdom. The Dutch government rejected it on 16 November 1938.
Q: What were Gerindo and Gapi?
A: Gerindo (24 May 1937) was a left-wing, anti-fascist nationalist party led by Amir Sjarifuddin and others. Gapi (21 May 1939) was a federation of eight organisations, with Thamrin, Amir Sjarifuddin and Abikusno as chairmen, whose slogan was Indonesia Berparlemen.
Q: Give three effects of the Japanese occupation on the economy and population.
A: Trade collapsed and the economy was reorganised for war; rice was taken by compulsory delivery and prices rose, with famine in 1944–45; millions were forced to work as romusha (estimates vary widely, for example 4 to 10 million recruited on one set of figures).
Q: What were the Triple A movement, Putera and Peta?
A: The Triple A movement (1942) was a Japanese propaganda body that failed. Putera (March 1943) was a mass organisation led by Sukarno, Hatta, Ki Hajar Dewantara and Mas Mansur. Peta (3 October 1943) was an army of Indonesian volunteers under Indonesian officers.
Q: What did Koiso promise on 7 September 1944, and why?
A: That independence would be given to the East Indies "in the future". Japan was losing, after the fall of Saipan, and wanted to keep Indonesians loyal.
Q: Name three forms of resistance to the Japanese.
A: The underground led by Sjahrir and the network of Amir Sjarifuddin; revolts such as Aceh (November 1942), Singaparna (February 1944) and the Peta revolt at Blitar (14 February 1945); and the discussions and protests of the pemuda in Jakarta.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the effects of the Ethical Policy on the growth of nationalism in the Dutch East Indies.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: how far the policy created the means of nationalism, how far it was a cause and how far it failed. For: education created a new class (about 1,500 Indonesians in European schools in 1900, about 75,000 completed Western primary school by 1928); a common language; contact with ideas; the Volksraad as a platform. Against: the numbers were small; the policy was under-funded and ran down by about 1930; the mass movement, Sarekat Islam, came from traders and Islam; Dutch repression after 1927 was the policy's opposite. Level 5 judges that the policy created the leaders but not the movement, and that its failure to deliver was as important as what it did.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the Volksraad did more to hold back than to help Indonesian nationalism.
A: Indicative content. Help: a platform for nationalists (Thamrin, Soetardjo); the 1936 petition; publicity. Hold back: advisory only until 1927, then limited; only a small electorate; the Governor-General's veto; it divided cooperators and non-cooperators; the Dutch used it as proof of progress. Level 5 uses a criterion (did it give Indonesians power or only a voice?) and judges that it gave a voice but no power, so that its main effect was to show the limits of cooperation.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the PKI in the growth of nationalism before 1942.
A: Indicative content. For: the first party to demand revolution (1920); trade unions and mass organisation; the risings of 1926–27. Against: the risings were crushed, the party banned, and its leaders exiled or killed; it split Sarekat Islam in 1921; the Dutch used it to justify repression; the nationalists distanced themselves. Level 5 judges that its importance was mostly indirect: it showed the danger of revolution and left the field to non-communist nationalists.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Dutch repression in the 1930s was the main reason why Indonesian nationalism made little progress before 1942.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: arrests of Sukarno (1929, 1933), Hatta and Sjahrir (1934); the dissolution of the PNI and Partindo; "rest and order" under de Jonge; the rejection of the Soetardjo Petition. Challenge: divisions among nationalists (Sukarno against Hatta and Sjahrir; Islam against secular); the Depression; small membership; cooperation inside the Volksraad also achieved little; the leaders' own strategies. Level 5 judges that repression was the main external cause but the divisions inside the movement were as important.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons why Japan encouraged Indonesian nationalism in 1942–45.
H: Separate the reasons for 1942–43 from the reasons for 1944–45.
H: How far did Japan have a real choice?
A: Indicative content. War effort (labour, rice, soldiers); anti-Western propaganda; failure of the Triple A movement and the need for Sukarno and Hatta (Putera, March 1943); the Army/Navy difference; the defeat after 1943, Saipan, the Koiso promise (7 September 1944), the BPUPK (1945). Level 5 ranks the reasons by timing: in 1942–43 the main reason was to mobilise, from 1944 it was to keep Indonesians loyal in defeat, and the promise was a means, not an aim.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the economic effects of the Japanese occupation did more harm than good to the nationalist cause.
H: Divide the effects into those that built nationalism and those that harmed it.
H: Think about who suffered and how they reacted.
A: Indicative content. Harm: romusha (estimates 4 to 10 million on one set of figures), famine in 1944–45, inflation, the loss of exports; hatred of the Japanese. Good: the Dutch economic dominance and the Chinese position were weakened; Indonesians took over posts; suffering turned villagers against the Japanese and to the idea of independence. Level 5 judges that the economic effects were mainly harmful to people, but that they helped nationalism by destroying the colonial order and by turning opinion against the Japanese, so the nationalist cause gained from the damage.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the importance of Hatta and Sjahrir, as against Sukarno, in the development of Indonesian nationalism up to 1945.
H: What did each offer: mass politics or cadre politics?
H: Who was more useful to the movement in the Japanese period?
A: Indicative content. Sukarno: symbols, speeches, mass appeal, Putera. Hatta: organisation, economics, the cooperation with Sukarno; Sjahrir: the cadre idea and the underground; their criticism of Sukarno's leader cult. Level 5 weighs them against criteria (public influence, organisation, survival under repression) and judges that Sukarno's role was the most visible but that the movement needed the combination of all three.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Indonesian nationalism was more united in 1945 than in 1928.
H: What united nationalists in 1928, and what in 1945?
H: Think of Islam, the left, the army and the pemuda.
A: Indicative content. United: the Youth Pledge in 1928 and the Congress of 1939; Putera and the Japanese bodies and the flag and language, accepted by 1945; the proclamation of 17 August 1945. Divided: Islam against secular nationalists; the left (Sjahrir, Amir Sjarifuddin, the PKI); the generation gap between the leaders and the pemuda; regional differences between the zones. Level 5 judges that nationalism was wider and better organised in 1945 but not more united in belief: it was united in aim and divided on what kind of state to make.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Indonesian nationalism was made by Dutch policy more than by Indonesians.' Evaluate this view.
H: What did the Dutch create, intentionally or not, and what did Indonesians add?
H: Consider the Dutch as makers (the territory, the education, the language of administration) and as opponents (repression).
A: Support: the Dutch created the territory called the Dutch East Indies, the schools and STOVIA, the Volksraad as a platform, a common Malay for administration; their repression radicalised the movement; the Ethical Policy produced the leaders. Challenge: Indonesians chose Malay, not Dutch; Islam and trade networks created the first mass movement, Sarekat Islam; leaders such as Sukarno and Hatta shaped the ideas (Marhaenism, cadre politics); the Japanese created the organisation and army. A* judgement: the Dutch made the **conditions** (a state, a class, a lingua franca, a grievance) but Indonesians made the **movement**; the Japanese made the **opportunity**. The question treats the Dutch and Indonesians as opposed when the movement used the colonial tools to attack colonialism.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the effects of Japanese forced labour on the growth of nationalism in Indonesia. (Use the disagreement over the number of romusha as part of your argument.)
H: Why do the estimates range from tens of thousands to millions?
H: What matters more for the argument, the exact number or the experience?
A: Support: figures of 4 to 10 million forced workers on one set of estimates, 200,000 to 500,000 sent from Java overseas and about 270,000 on another; the burden was heavy whatever the figure. Challenge: the word romusha was never exactly defined, records are poor, and the larger numbers include unpaid local labour; the effect on nationalism depended on where people lived and on what they were told. A* judgement: the exact number matters less than the fact that the figure is a range of millions, which shows the scale of the demand on villages; the argument about the effects on nationalism (hatred of the Japanese, a sense of a common suffering) rests on the experience, not on a single statistic, and a student who says "x million died" without a source is making an error.
