## Attempts at détente, 1972–79

:::def Détente
A French word meaning "relaxation". In Cold War history, the easing of tension between the United States and the Soviet Union from about 1969 to 1979, shown in arms control agreements, trade, summit meetings and agreements on Europe.
:::

### US motives for détente

Détente was the policy of Richard Nixon and his National Security Adviser, Henry Kissinger, and it continued under Gerald Ford and, in a changed form, under Carter. Their motives were practical, not idealistic.

- **Vietnam.** By 1969 the war was costing lives and money and splitting the country (see [A time of challenges, 1961–74 (2)](#hist.d.usa61.2)). Nixon wanted Moscow and Beijing to press Hanoi to settle, and wanted the United States to leave Vietnam without looking defeated.
- **The economy.** The war and the Great Society had produced a growing deficit, and in 1971 the United States had its first trade deficit of the twentieth century. Trade with a huge new market, and a lower arms bill, were attractive.
- **The Sino-Soviet split.** China and the Soviet Union had been enemies since the 1960s and fought on their border in 1969. Nixon's opening to China (his visit to Beijing in February 1972) put pressure on the Soviet Union, which had to worry about an American–Chinese understanding. Kissinger called this "triangular diplomacy".
- **Nuclear parity.** By about 1970 the Soviet Union had caught up with the United States in strategic missiles, so the United States could no longer count on superiority. A race that neither side could win was costly and dangerous, so limits were in the interest of both.
- **Realism.** Kissinger thought that great powers should deal with each other as powers, whatever their ideologies, and he believed in **linkage**: offering trade and recognition in return for restraint elsewhere, such as in Vietnam and the Middle East.

The Soviet Union had its own reasons. Brezhnev wanted recognition as an equal, Western grain and technology to help an economy that was growing more slowly, security in Europe, and a way to deal with China. Because each side wanted something different from détente, trouble came when it failed to deliver it.

:::key Why the United States chose détente
1. To get out of Vietnam and avoid another such war.
2. To save money and open trade.
3. To exploit the split between China and the USSR.
4. To manage a world in which the Soviet Union had reached nuclear parity.
5. To make the world safer by limiting a nuclear arms race.
:::

### SALT I, 1972: origins and nature

The **Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT)** began in Helsinki on 17 November 1969. They were possible because both sides had reasons to talk: parity had been reached, and the American plan for an anti-ballistic missile (ABM) system, meant to shoot down incoming warheads, worried both capitals. An ABM defence would tempt each side to build more offensive missiles to overwhelm it, so it made the arms race worse. After two and a half years of talks, Nixon and Brezhnev signed two agreements at a summit in Moscow on **26 May 1972**.

1. **The ABM Treaty.** Each country could have only two ABM sites, one to defend its capital and one to defend a field of missiles, and no more than 200 interceptors each. (A protocol of 1974 cut this to one site each.) The Senate ratified the treaty in the summer of 1972 by a large majority. It rested on the idea of **mutual assured destruction**: if neither side could defend itself, neither would dare to attack.
2. **The Interim Agreement on offensive weapons**, to last five years. It froze the number of land-based ICBM launchers at the existing levels (1,054 for the United States, 1,618 for the Soviet Union) and set limits on missile submarines and their launchers (the United States 710, the Soviet Union 950, up from 656 and 740).

The numbers look uneven. The Soviet Union was allowed more launchers than the United States, which critics such as Senator Henry Jackson attacked. Kissinger's answer was that the United States had a lead in other things: American missiles carried several warheads each (**MIRVs**, multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles, which the agreement did not restrict), and the United States had more strategic bombers, which the agreement did not cover. SALT I did not end the arms race: it capped launchers and left the warheads, and each side went on adding them. Its importance was different: it was the first time the two powers had agreed limits on strategic weapons, it accepted that each side could verify the other by satellite ("national technical means"), and it created the habit of regular summits. At the 1972 summit the two leaders also signed a statement of Basic Principles of Relations that spoke of peaceful coexistence and of restraint in crises. In 1973 they signed an Agreement on the Prevention of Nuclear War. At Vladivostok in November 1974 Ford and Brezhnev outlined a limit for a second treaty, SALT II: 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles on each side, of which 1,320 could have MIRVs.

### The Helsinki Accords, 1975

The Soviet Union had wanted a European security conference for twenty years, in order to have the postwar frontiers, and its control of Eastern Europe, accepted by the West. The West agreed to talks, which lasted two years, in return for discussion of human rights and of freer movement of people and ideas. The **Final Act** of the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe was signed at Helsinki on **1 August 1975** by thirty-five states, including the United States, Canada, the Soviet Union and every European country except Albania, which stayed away. It was not a treaty and had no legal force, but the leaders signed it, and it set out their commitments in three parts, called "baskets" (a later part dealt with the follow-up meetings).

| Basket | Subject | What it said |
|---|---|---|
| I | Security | Ten principles for relations between states, including respect for the frontiers of Europe (they could be changed only by peaceful means) and respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms (Principle VII) |
| II | Economics, science, technology and environment | Cooperation in trade, industry, science and transport |
| III | Humanitarian and cultural questions | Family reunification, travel, marriage between nationals of different states, working conditions for journalists, cultural exchange |

:::note Evidence: Principle VII of the Helsinki Final Act
*From the Final Act of the Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe, Helsinki, 1 August 1975. Heading of Principle VII only.*

"Respect for human rights and fundamental freedoms, including the freedom of thought, conscience, religion or belief."

*How to use it.* Nature: the title of one of ten principles in a document signed by the heads of thirty-five governments, including Brezhnev. Value: it shows that the Soviet Union formally accepted that rights were a legitimate subject for international discussion, which gave dissidents a document to cite. Limit: a political declaration, not a treaty, with no way of being enforced, and Moscow read it in a way that put its own control first. The same Final Act also accepted the postwar frontiers, which is why the Soviet government welcomed it.
:::

**Why Helsinki mattered.** The document worked in two directions.

- **For the Soviet Union**, it seemed a diplomatic victory: the West had accepted the frontiers of 1945 and the division of Germany, and the Soviet press published the whole text, as a triumph for Brezhnev.
- **For the West and for dissidents**, the human rights principle was a lever. Citizens' groups formed in the Soviet Union and Eastern Europe to monitor whether their governments kept the promises: the Moscow Helsinki Group was founded in 1976, and in Czechoslovakia Charter 77 appeared in 1977. In the West, "Helsinki Watch" committees followed, which later became part of Human Rights Watch. The Soviet authorities arrested many of the monitors. Carter's emphasis on human rights (see [The USA, 1974–93 (3)](#hist.d.usa74.3)) made Helsinki a point of friction with Moscow in 1977–79.
- **In the United States**, the Accords were attacked from the right as an acceptance of Soviet domination of Eastern Europe, and Ford was embarrassed in the 1976 campaign by this charge. Ronald Reagan, who was challenging Ford for the Republican nomination, used it.

The historian John Lewis Gaddis has argued that the Helsinki Final Act gave people in Communist states a standard to hold their governments to. Others point out that its effects were slow and limited, because the Soviet government jailed the monitors and kept control, and the West had accepted the frontiers in return. For an essay: the Accords were not a success for détente in the short term, since they brought a crackdown and quarrels, but they were important in the longer run, as one of the factors that weakened the legitimacy of Communist governments.

### Technical and economic cooperation, 1975–79

The first detente agreements (1972–74) set up a wide range of cooperation: joint commissions on trade, science and technology, health, the environment, space and transport. The best-known result was the **Apollo–Soyuz Test Project** on 17 July 1975, when an American and a Soviet spacecraft docked in orbit and their crews shook hands. Trade is a better measure of the practical results.

- **Grain.** After a poor Soviet harvest in 1972, Moscow bought very large amounts of American grain (an agreement of July 1972 committed it to buy at least $750 million in three years, with American credit available). Because the buying was done quietly, in many small orders, the Soviet Union took much of the American surplus, and food prices in the United States rose. It was a lesson in how the deals could cause political anger. In October 1975 a Long-Term Grain Agreement committed the Soviet Union to buy at least six million tonnes of American grain a year for five years.
- **Trade and credit.** The United States and the Soviet Union signed a trade agreement in October 1972, and American firms (such as Pepsi and Occidental Petroleum) set up deals in the Soviet Union. But in 1974 Congress passed the **Jackson–Vanik amendment**, which denied "most favoured nation" trading terms to countries that restricted emigration, and set a limit on Export–Import Bank credit. Brezhnev's government would not accept the conditions, and in January 1975 it cancelled the 1972 trade agreement. The benefits the Soviet Union had hoped for never came.
- **Technology.** Western firms supplied machinery and plants, but the American government restricted exports that had military uses.

**How important was the cooperation?** It was real but narrow. It helped to build a habit of dealing and gave both sides an interest in keeping things calm; grain trade continued through the 1970s. But trade was small compared with America's trade with Western Europe and Japan, most of it was American grain, and the Soviet Union did not get the technology and credit it had expected. The Jackson–Vanik case also showed that détente had critics in the United States: the left and the right both opposed a policy that, they said, accepted Soviet repression in return for little. Cooperation was too thin to survive a crisis.

### Détente under Ford and Carter

Ford kept the policy but under pressure from Reagan's challenge in 1976 he stopped using the word "détente". Carter began with a different emphasis: arms control with deeper cuts (his proposals of March 1977 were rejected by Moscow as an attempt to change the Vladivostok framework), human rights, and a sceptical view of Soviet behaviour in Africa. By 1979 the compromises were reached and the two leaders signed SALT II in June. But détente had already lost most of its support in the United States, and the way it ended is the subject of the next section.

:::key Détente, 1972–79: what it achieved and where it was weak
- **Achieved:** the first limits on strategic weapons (SALT I and the ABM Treaty); the Helsinki Final Act; regular summits; trade, grain and cooperation in space; a calmer Europe.
- **Weak:** the two sides understood it differently (the United States as a way of restraining Soviet behaviour everywhere; the Soviet Union as compatible with support for "national liberation" movements such as Angola); arms control did not stop the building of warheads; trade was small and Jackson–Vanik blocked what the Soviets wanted; critics on the right called it appeasement and critics on the left said it ignored human rights.
- **For your essay:** détente was a policy of limited aims that worked while both sides gained from it, and it had few defenders in America when Soviet actions seemed to break its rules.
:::

## The failure of détente, 1975–83

By 1980 détente was dead, and in 1983 relations were worse than at any time since the Cuban Missile Crisis. Historians list five causes, and your job in an essay is to rank them.

### The importance of ideology: the Second Cold War

The Cold War was a conflict between two systems, and détente never ended the conflict of ideas. In Moscow, the leadership accepted "peaceful coexistence" with the capitalist West but held that it was compatible with supporting revolutions in the poorer world, which it called "national liberation" movements. In Washington, American leaders thought that coexistence meant restraint everywhere. This difference of understanding was the heart of the quarrel, as Raymond Garthoff argued in *Détente and Confrontation* (1985): neither side had agreed what détente allowed, so each could say that the other was cheating.

The evidence for the Soviet view that it was acting within détente, and for the American view that it was not, built up in the late 1970s.
- **Angola, 1975–76.** Soviet arms and Cuban troops helped the MPLA to win the civil war after Portugal left.
- **Ethiopia, 1977–78.** The Soviet Union and Cuba armed Ethiopia against Somalia in the Ogaden war, which Brzezinski saw as a challenge.
- **Afghanistan, April 1978.** A Communist coup in Kabul brought a Marxist government to power next to the Soviet Union.
- **Soviet missiles in Europe.** From 1977 the Soviet Union began to deploy the SS-20, a mobile intermediate-range missile, which NATO answered in December 1979 with a "dual-track" decision: to deploy American Pershing II and cruise missiles in Western Europe unless the USSR agreed to remove its own.
- **Human rights.** The trial of Anatoly Shcharansky in 1978 and the exile of the physicist Andrei Sakharov to Gorky in January 1980 made it hard for American leaders to deal with Moscow without criticism.

In the United States a body of opinion grew that was sure that détente favoured the Soviet Union. The **Committee on the Present Danger**, formed in 1976, and the group of outside experts known as "Team B", which reviewed the CIA's estimates of Soviet strength in the same year, argued that Moscow was seeking superiority, not parity. Many of their members, including Richard Pipes, later joined Reagan's administration. The phrase **"Second Cold War"** is used by historians for the period from about 1979 to 1985, when the language, the arms race and the rivalry in the Third World were all at a Cold War pitch.

:::def Second Cold War
The period of renewed hostility between the United States and the Soviet Union from about 1979 to 1985, after the collapse of détente, marked by the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, an arms build-up, hostile rhetoric and the end of arms control talks.
:::

### The impact of the invasion of Afghanistan

Afghanistan, a poor mountainous country on the Soviet Union's southern border, had a Communist government after the coup of April 1978. Its attempts to change the country by force caused revolts, and in September 1979 one party leader, Hafizullah Amin, overthrew and killed another, Nur Muhammad Taraki. The Soviet leaders, led by Brezhnev with his Defence Minister Dmitri Ustinov, KGB chief Yuri Andropov and Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko, feared that the regime would collapse into Islamist control, or that Amin would turn to the West. On **24–27 December 1979** Soviet forces flew into Kabul and entered by road, killed Amin on 27 December and installed Babrak Karmal. They expected a short operation. It lasted nine years.

Carter, who had until then hoped to save SALT II, reacted with the strongest measures of his presidency. He told the American people that the invasion was a grave threat to peace, and he:
- asked the Senate on **3 January 1980** to stop considering SALT II;
- placed an **embargo on grain sales** to the USSR on 4 January 1980, withholding about 17 million tonnes that Moscow had hoped to buy;
- organised a **boycott of the Moscow Olympic Games** of July 1980, which about sixty countries joined;
- announced the **Carter Doctrine** in his State of the Union address on 23 January 1980: an attempt by an outside power to gain control of the Persian Gulf would be "regarded as an assault on the vital interests of the United States of America", and would be repelled "by any means necessary, including military force";
- restarted draft registration, increased defence spending and expanded the covert supply of weapons to the Afghan resistance (the **mujahideen**), which had begun at a low level in 1979.

:::note Evidence: Carter on the Persian Gulf, 23 January 1980
*Adapted from President Carter's State of the Union address to Congress, 23 January 1980. Shortened; the phrases are the speech's own.*

"An attempt by any outside force to gain control of the Persian Gulf region will be regarded as an assault on the vital interests of the United States of America, and such an assault will be repelled by any means necessary, including military force."

*How to use it.* Nature: a formal statement of policy by the President to Congress, soon called the Carter Doctrine. Purpose: to warn Moscow and reassure Gulf states, and to show a domestic audience that Carter, under attack for weakness after Iran, was acting. Value: it marks the point at which Carter's policy moved from détente to containment, and he used the language of earlier Presidents (Truman 1947). Limit: it was a statement, not an army: the Rapid Deployment Force that was to carry it out was still small.
:::

The effect on détente was decisive: it had no defenders left in the Senate, and the Soviet invasion made the case made by the Committee on the Present Danger look right. But for an essay it matters that the sanctions did not make the Soviet Union change course. The grain embargo hurt American farmers more than it hurt the Soviets, who bought grain elsewhere, and Reagan lifted it in April 1981. The Soviet Union did not leave Afghanistan until 1989.

### The change of leadership

**Ronald Reagan**, a former film actor and Governor of California, won the 1980 election and took office on 20 January 1981. He had spent a political lifetime attacking communism and he opposed détente, which he described as one-sided, and SALT II, which he called "fatally flawed". He appointed to senior posts people from the Committee on the Present Danger. His outlook mixed three things: moral conviction that the Soviet system was wrong; a belief that it was weak because its economy could not support its military; and a belief in "peace through strength", meaning that the United States should rebuild its forces before negotiating. He did not meet a Soviet leader until November 1985. Part of the reason was that the old men of the Politburo kept dying: Brezhnev in November 1982, Yuri Andropov in February 1984 and Konstantin Chernenko in March 1985. That was the Soviet side of the "change of leadership": the Soviet Union, with leaders who were old and ill, was unable to respond with new ideas until Gorbachev came.

### The new defence policy

Rearmament began under Carter, whose Presidential Directive 59 of July 1980 set out a plan for limited nuclear war and whose budgets grew, but Reagan took it much further.
- **A military build-up.** Defence spending rose sharply in real terms in his first term. He revived the B-1 bomber, ordered the MX missile and Trident submarines, and planned a much bigger navy.
- **Missiles in Europe.** The first Pershing II and cruise missiles arrived in Western Europe in November 1983, after Soviet refusal to withdraw the SS-20s.
- **A policy of pressure.** National Security Decision Directive 75, signed on 17 January 1983, set out the goal of containing and, over time, reversing Soviet expansionism, and of encouraging change inside the Soviet system. It said that the United States should compete with the Soviet Union in every part of the world.
- **Rhetoric.** On **8 March 1983** Reagan told the National Association of Evangelicals that the Soviet leaders were "the focus of evil in the modern world", and warned Americans against ignoring "the aggressive impulses of an evil empire".
- **The Strategic Defense Initiative.** On **23 March 1983** Reagan asked scientists to give the United States the means of rendering nuclear weapons "impotent and obsolete". His critics called it "Star Wars". SDI was to be a system of defences against missiles, based partly in space. In 1983 it was a hope, not a design, but Soviet leaders feared that it would destroy the balance of terror and that they could not afford to match it.

The consequences came quickly. On **1 September 1983** a Soviet fighter shot down a South Korean airliner, KAL 007, which had strayed over Soviet territory, killing all 269 people on board, and Reagan denounced it as a crime. In November 1983 the Soviet Union walked out of the Geneva talks on intermediate-range missiles when the Pershings arrived, and a NATO exercise called Able Archer 83 alarmed some Soviet leaders, who feared it might be cover for a real attack (historians disagree on how serious the danger was). By the end of the year contacts were almost cut off.

### The failure of ratification of SALT II

SALT II was signed in Vienna on **18 June 1979**. It set a ceiling of 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles for each side, to fall to 2,250 by 1 January 1981, and limits on missiles with multiple warheads (no more than 1,320 MIRVed missiles and bombers armed with cruise missiles, of which no more than 820 could be land-based missiles with MIRVs). A treaty needs a two-thirds majority in the Senate (67 of 100 votes), and from the start this was a doubtful prospect.
- **Opposition.** Hawks such as Senator Henry Jackson and the Committee on the Present Danger said the treaty let the Soviet Union keep a large number of heavy missiles and left the United States open to attack on its land-based missiles; they said the Soviet Backfire bomber was left uncounted; and many doubted that the United States could check Soviet compliance, especially after the Shah's fall had closed American listening posts in Iran.
- **The Soviet brigade in Cuba.** In the summer of 1979 American officials announced that a Soviet combat brigade was in Cuba. It had been there for years, but the news turned senators against the treaty, and the Chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, Frank Church, said it could not be ratified while the troops remained.
- **A weakened President.** Carter's standing was falling because of the economy and then the Iranian hostage crisis.
- **Afghanistan.** The invasion ended any chance. Carter withdrew the treaty from the Senate on 3 January 1980.

Neither side ratified SALT II, but both agreed to keep within its limits. Reagan, who had opposed the treaty, said he would do nothing to undercut it so long as the Soviets did the same, and kept to the limits until 1986, when he said the United States would no longer do so, citing alleged Soviet violations.

:::key Reasons for the failure of détente, 1975–83
1. **Different understandings of détente**: a "relaxation" the United States expected to limit Soviet behaviour everywhere, and a limit the Soviet Union accepted only in Europe and on arms. This made every Soviet action in Africa and Asia a "violation".
2. **Ideology and domestic opposition** in the United States: the Committee on the Present Danger, Team B, Jackson–Vanik, and Reagan's own campaign. Détente was a policy that few Americans loved.
3. **Afghanistan** (December 1979): the final blow, which took away what support was left.
4. **Reagan's election** (1980) and the **new defence policy**: the shift from limiting arms to out-building them, from coexistence to pressure on the Soviet system itself.
5. **The failure to ratify SALT II**: a symptom as much as a cause, since the treaty failed because opinion had already turned.
**A judgement to test:** détente had failed before Reagan arrived, because it rested on different expectations. Afghanistan ended it. Reagan changed it from a collapsed policy into an open competition.
:::

## The Reagan Doctrine and rollback

### Reasons for and aims of the Reagan Doctrine

:::def Reagan Doctrine
The policy, set out in Reagan's State of the Union address of 6 February 1985 and named by the columnist Charles Krauthammer, of giving open and covert help to anti-communist forces fighting Soviet-backed governments in the Third World, with the aim of rolling back Soviet gains.
:::

:::def Rollback
The policy of not just containing communism where it already existed but pushing it back, by supporting forces that were fighting communist governments. It had been discussed in the early 1950s and rejected by Eisenhower as too dangerous; the Reagan administration revived it in the 1980s.
:::

In the 1970s Marxist governments had taken power in Angola, Mozambique, Ethiopia, South Yemen, Afghanistan and Nicaragua, often with Soviet or Cuban help. Reagan's advisers drew three conclusions.
1. **Reverse the 1970s.** The Soviet Union had gained at America's expense in the Third World since Vietnam, and the United States should take the initiative.
2. **Make the Soviet Union pay.** Its economy was already weak; support for distant allies and for the war in Afghanistan was an extra cost. Raising that cost would stretch the Soviet system.
3. **Avoid another Vietnam.** After Vietnam, American troops could not be used, and Congress and the public would not accept it. Local forces armed and paid by the United States, with American weapons, training and money, could do the fighting.

On 6 February 1985, Reagan said in his State of the Union address that the United States must not "break faith with those who are risking their lives" in places "from Afghanistan to Nicaragua" to defy Soviet-backed aggression. Conservative think tanks, such as the Heritage Foundation, published lists of countries where change was possible. The doctrine was never a single plan: it was a set of separate covert wars, each with its own history, and each rested on a mixture of aims (security, ideology, domestic politics).

### Nicaragua: causes, nature and consequences

**Causes.** The Sandinista government that took power in July 1979 had friendly relations with Cuba and the Soviet bloc, accepted Cuban advisers and Soviet arms, and, the Reagan administration said, helped to send weapons to the guerrillas in El Salvador. Reagan, who thought that Central America was a Soviet target, said that another Cuba was being built close to the American mainland. He suspended in 1981 the aid that Carter had approved.

**Nature.** In late 1981 Reagan signed a secret order authorising the CIA to support anti-Sandinista forces with about $20 million, at first on the pretext of stopping the arms flow to El Salvador. These forces, called the **Contras** (from *contrarrevolucionarios*), were led at first by former officers of Somoza's National Guard and based in Honduras and Costa Rica. They grew to many thousands of fighters. Reagan called them "freedom fighters" and compared them to the American Founding Fathers. The war was fought in the countryside, where the Contras attacked schools, clinics and farms, and its human rights record was criticised by human rights organisations such as Americas Watch.

The United States did not rely on the Contras alone:
- In early 1984 the CIA helped to **mine Nicaragua's harbours**, which led to a storm in Congress and a ruling by the International Court of Justice in June 1986 that the United States had broken international law (the US had rejected the court's authority).
- On 1 May 1985 Reagan declared a **trade embargo** on Nicaragua.
- Congress, which was divided on the issue, passed the **Boland Amendments**. The first, in December 1982, banned funds used to overthrow the Sandinista government. The second, in October 1984, banned all aid to the Contras by American intelligence agencies, until Congress, in 1986, voted $100 million of new aid.

**The Iran–Contra affair.** When Congress cut off aid, officials on the staff of the National Security Council, led by a Marine officer, Oliver North, looked for other ways to fund the Contras. At the same time the administration was selling missiles to Iran, in the hope of freeing American hostages held in Lebanon and of improving relations with Iranian "moderates". Money from the Iranian sales was diverted to the Contras, in breach of the Boland Amendment. The scheme became public when a Lebanese magazine, *Ash-Shiraa*, revealed the arms sales on **3 November 1986**. The Tower Commission, set up by Reagan, reported on 26 February 1987, criticising his management style, and on 4 March he admitted in a television address that what had begun as a strategic opening to Iran had deteriorated into trading arms for hostages. Congressional hearings in 1987 and a special prosecutor followed: eleven people were convicted of crimes, though some convictions were later overturned, and in December 1992 President Bush pardoned six, including the former Defense Secretary Caspar Weinberger. Reagan himself was not charged, and historians still debate how much he knew about the diversion.

**Consequences.**
- **For Nicaragua.** The war killed tens of thousands, wrecked the economy and made the Sandinistas more dependent on Soviet aid. It also kept the Sandinistas on the defensive, which some scholars see as one reason they agreed to talk.
- **Peace.** Central American presidents, led by Costa Rica's Óscar Arias, signed a peace plan in August 1987; the Sandinistas and the Contras agreed a ceasefire at Sapoa in March 1988. The Sandinistas lost the election of February 1990 to Violeta Chamorro, and handed over power.
- **For the Reagan Doctrine.** Nicaragua is the least successful case: the Contras never won territory, Congress limited the policy, and the scandal damaged Reagan's reputation. Whether the Contras or the peace plan and the end of Soviet support caused the Sandinistas to accept elections is the central question.

### Afghanistan: causes, nature and consequences

**Causes.** The Soviet invasion in December 1979 (see above). The resistance, a loose set of Islamic parties and local commanders called the mujahideen ("those who fight a jihad"), had begun to fight before the invasion. Carter's administration, led on this by Brzezinski, began a covert programme, and Reagan's administration enlarged it as the Soviet Union's "Vietnam".

**Nature.** The programme was run by the CIA but operated through **Pakistan**, whose military ruler, General Zia ul-Haq, and its intelligence service, the ISI, chose which groups to arm. Saudi Arabia matched American funds, and China and Egypt supplied weapons. The scale grew each year: from tens of millions of dollars at the start to several hundred million by the middle of the decade (about $630 million in 1987, on a commonly cited estimate). In March 1985 Reagan signed a directive (NSDD-166) that changed the goal from raising the cost of the Soviet occupation to forcing the Soviets out. Congress pushed the policy as well, led by a Texas Democrat, Charlie Wilson, who helped to raise funds. In 1986 the United States began to supply **Stinger** shoulder-fired anti-aircraft missiles, which made Soviet helicopters and ground-attack aircraft much more vulnerable and forced Soviet pilots to fly higher.

**Consequences.**
- **For the Soviet Union.** The war cost the Soviet Union about 15,000 dead, and Gorbachev, in 1986, called it a "bleeding wound". He agreed to leave: the Geneva Accords were signed on 14 April 1988 and the last Soviet troops left on **15 February 1989**. The Soviet-backed government of Najibullah survived until 1992.
- **For Afghanistan.** Hundreds of thousands, perhaps more than a million, Afghans died and millions fled as refugees. After the Soviet withdrawal, the United States lost interest. The country fell into civil war, and the Taliban took Kabul in 1996.
- **Blowback.** Critics argue that the aid built up the Islamist networks that later turned against the United States. Defenders reply that the United States did not fund Arab volunteers such as Osama bin Laden, whose connections were separate, and that the war's end helped to free Eastern Europe. Historians disagree, and you should present the debate as one rather than choose a side without evidence.

### Angola: causes, nature and consequences

**Causes.** After Portugal left in 1975, three movements fought for power in Angola. The MPLA took the capital with Soviet and Cuban support; the rival UNITA, led by **Jonas Savimbi**, was backed by South Africa and, in 1975, by the CIA. Congress, still shaken by Vietnam, passed the **Clark Amendment** in 1976, which banned American covert aid to the Angolan groups. By the early 1980s some 25,000 Cuban troops were in Angola, and the number grew later (about 50,000 at the peak), with Soviet advisers and equipment. For the Reagan administration Angola was both a Cold War target and part of its policy towards South Africa, which it called "constructive engagement": it wanted a deal in which Cuban troops left Angola and South Africa gave up Namibia.

**Nature.** Reagan supported repeal of the Clark Amendment. Congress agreed in the summer of 1985 (signed on 8 August). In January 1986 Savimbi visited Washington and met Reagan, and the United States began to give UNITA covert aid, including Stinger and anti-tank missiles. South Africa went on supporting UNITA and fought directly against Cuban and Angolan forces.

**Consequences.**
- **Escalation.** The fighting at Cuito Cuanavale in 1987–88, between Angolan and Cuban forces on one side and UNITA and South African forces on the other, ended in a stalemate that both sides claimed as a victory, and it drove both to the negotiating table.
- **A settlement of one problem.** The Tripartite Accord, signed in New York on 22 December 1988 by Angola, Cuba and South Africa, provided for Namibia's independence (which came in March 1990) and for Cuban troops to leave Angola by July 1991. American mediation, led by Chester Crocker, brought it about.
- **No end to the civil war.** The war between the MPLA and UNITA continued until Savimbi was killed in 2002, with huge loss of life. Critics say American aid to a leader allied with apartheid South Africa prolonged a war and gave little in return.

:::key How effective was the Reagan Doctrine?
- **Afghanistan:** the clearest success: the Soviet army left. But Gorbachev's decision to leave was his own, and the cost to Afghanistan was heavy.
- **Angola:** a mixed case: Cuban troops left and Namibia became independent, but the civil war went on.
- **Nicaragua:** the least successful: the Contras did not win, Congress limited the policy and Iran–Contra damaged Reagan.
- **Overall:** it raised the cost of Soviet commitments in the Third World and became one of the pressures on the Soviet leadership, but it depended on local fighters with their own aims, it was expensive in lives for those countries, and its connection to the end of the Cold War is a matter of argument, taken up in the next section.
:::

## Reagan and Gorbachev

### The significance of Gorbachev's policies for relations with the United States

**Mikhail Gorbachev**, aged 54, became General Secretary of the Soviet Communist Party on **11 March 1985**, the day after Chernenko's death. He was the youngest Soviet leader since Stalin and he inherited an economy that had almost stopped growing, a technology gap with the West, a costly war in Afghanistan and a military burden that was far heavier, in proportion to the economy, than the American one. He believed the system could be reformed and, to reform it, needed a calmer world. His programme had two parts at home and one abroad.

:::def Perestroika
"Restructuring": Gorbachev's programme of reforming the Soviet economy and the Communist Party from 1985–86, by allowing some private and cooperative enterprise and loosening central planning.
:::

:::def Glasnost
"Openness": Gorbachev's policy of allowing public discussion and criticism of the Soviet past and present, with a freer press, from 1986.
:::

:::def New thinking
Gorbachev's approach to foreign policy from 1985–86: nuclear war could not be won, security had to be shared with opponents, class conflict was no longer the basis of foreign relations, and the Soviet Union should reduce its military burden and withdraw from costly foreign commitments.
:::

New thinking changed what the Soviet Union offered and what the United States could ask of it.
- **Arms control.** Gorbachev stopped Soviet nuclear tests unilaterally in August 1985 (he kept the moratorium until February 1987). In January 1986 he proposed to abolish nuclear weapons altogether by 2000. In 1987 he accepted the American "zero option" for intermediate-range missiles, agreed to unequal cuts (the Soviet Union had more missiles to give up), and allowed on-site inspection, which earlier Soviet leaders had refused.
- **The Third World.** He decided to withdraw from Afghanistan and encouraged the allies to settle disputes (including Cuba's troops in Angola).
- **Europe.** In a speech to the United Nations on **7 December 1988** he announced a cut of 500,000 in Soviet forces and said that every people had the right to choose its own path. In 1989 the Soviet Union did not use force when the Communist governments of Eastern Europe fell (see [The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (4): the collapse of Soviet control](#hist.d.soviet.4)).

**Why it mattered for the United States.** Gorbachev's policies removed the Soviet behaviour which American hawks had used as proof of hostility, and gave the American side a leader with whom agreements could be made. Not all Americans believed it. Reagan's Defense Secretary, Caspar Weinberger, and the CIA's director, William Casey, doubted that anything had changed; many in the press and Congress thought it a trick. British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, after meeting him in December 1984, had said that he was a man with whom one could do business, which helped. The key was that Gorbachev kept offering and then doing: when he agreed to inspection, withdrew missiles and left Afghanistan, doubters had to explain why.

### The summits

| Summit | Date | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Geneva | 19–21 November 1985 | First meeting of Reagan and Gorbachev. A joint statement said a nuclear war "cannot be won and must never be fought"; agreement on more summits and on exchanges. Dispute over SDI |
| Reykjavik | 11–12 October 1986 | Both leaders discussed deep cuts, and even abolishing all nuclear weapons. It broke down when Gorbachev demanded that SDI stay in the laboratory and Reagan refused |
| Washington | 7–10 December 1987 | **INF Treaty** signed on 8 December |
| Moscow | 29 May–2 June 1988 | Exchange of the INF ratifications; Reagan walks in Red Square and says that the "evil empire" was "another time, another era" |
| New York (Governors Island) | 7 December 1988 | Gorbachev, Reagan and President-elect Bush meet after Gorbachev's UN speech |
| Malta | 2–3 December 1989 | Bush and Gorbachev, meeting on ships in a storm, agree that the Cold War competition was ending |

The **INF Treaty** was the great practical result. It abolished all American and Soviet ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres, and allowed each side to inspect the other's sites. By the deadline of 1 June 1991 the two sides had destroyed 2,692 missiles (846 American, 1,846 Soviet). It was the first treaty to remove a whole class of nuclear weapons, not just cap their numbers, and the Senate approved it by 93 votes to 5 in May 1988. Reykjavik is often called a failure, but it shaped what followed: the leaders had seen that deep cuts were possible and that they trusted each other more than their advisers did.

### Reagan's influence in bringing about change in the Soviet regime

Why did the Soviet Union change? This is the main debate in the topic, and it divides historians. There are three answers.

:::cols
:::col Reagan's pressure made the change
- NSDD-75 (January 1983) aimed to contain and reverse Soviet expansion and to encourage change inside the USSR.
- The arms build-up, the missiles in Europe and SDI raised the cost of competition; Soviet leaders feared they could not match SDI.
- The Reagan Doctrine raised the cost of the Soviet empire: Afghanistan, Angola, Nicaragua.
- The Soviet economy, already weak, was hit by the fall in oil prices in 1986.
- Gorbachev's concessions (the INF zero option, withdrawal from Afghanistan) came when the pressure was greatest.
:::
:::col The Soviet system changed itself
- The economy had been stagnating since the 1970s; the system was failing before 1981.
- Gorbachev's reform ideas came from the Soviet reformers around him and from his own experience; he would have tried reform whoever was in the White House.
- The reformers wanted to cut the military burden for domestic reasons.
- Reagan's early hard line may have strengthened Soviet hardliners and delayed change.
- Gorbachev's decisions (glasnost, no force in Eastern Europe) were his own.
:::
:::col Reagan's change of course mattered
- From 1983–84 Reagan, helped by his Secretary of State George Shultz, moved to talks (his speech of 16 January 1984 spoke of Ivan and Anya, an imagined Soviet couple, wanting peace).
- He disliked nuclear weapons and was ready to bargain over them (Reykjavik).
- He trusted Gorbachev and overruled hardliners in his own camp.
- By being willing to deal, he gave Gorbachev a partner who could deliver, which made the reform of Soviet foreign policy worthwhile.
:::
:::

The historians fit these answers in different ways.
- **Raymond Garthoff**, in *The Great Transition* (1994), argued that the Cold War ended because of what Gorbachev did, driven by Soviet internal needs and by his "new thinking", and not because Reagan's military build-up forced the Soviet Union to give in. Garthoff, who was a diplomat and analyst as well as a historian, thought that real progress came when the administration turned to negotiation in Reagan's second term.
- **Archie Brown**, in *The Gorbachev Factor* (1996), a study by an Oxford political scientist, argued that Gorbachev was the most important single cause: his ideas and decisions changed Soviet policy and in the end the system. Brown does not deny that Reagan helped, but he rejects the view that Western strength left the Soviet Union with no choice.
- **James Mann**, in *The Rebellion of Ronald Reagan* (2009), argued that Reagan himself shifted from confrontation to engagement, against the advice of hardliners in his own party, and that this helped to bring the Cold War to an end; but he also did not accept that Reagan "won" it.
- **John Lewis Gaddis**, in *The Cold War: A New History* (2005), gave weight to the leaders: he credited Reagan with understanding that the Soviet system was vulnerable and was ready to negotiate with Gorbachev, and Gorbachev with refusing to use force to hold it together.

**A way to judge.** Ask what Reagan's policy did that would not have happened without him, and what happened that Soviet changes would have produced anyway. The Soviet economy was failing, and Gorbachev's reforms would have gone ahead. But the Reagan arms build-up, SDI and aid to the mujahideen made Gorbachev's task of reducing the arms burden more urgent, and Reagan's willingness to negotiate made agreements possible. The strongest answers say that Reagan's influence was real but secondary to Soviet causes, and that his effect lay as much in his readiness to deal with Gorbachev as in his pressure.

### Changing US policy towards the Soviet regime

American policy changed in three steps.
1. **From confrontation to engagement (1983–88).** After the war scare of 1983, Reagan, whose advisers were divided, gave more weight to Shultz and to the diplomats and negotiated with Gorbachev. At Geneva, Reykjavik and Washington he moved far beyond the position of 1981. At the Moscow summit he said the "evil empire" speech belonged to another time.
2. **Caution under Bush (1989).** George H. W. Bush, who became President in January 1989, paused to review policy, fearing that Gorbachev might fall, and critics said that he was too slow. In May 1989 he said that the United States wanted to move beyond containment and to bring the Soviet Union into the community of nations. When the Berlin Wall opened on 9 November 1989 he was careful not to gloat, so as not to make trouble for Gorbachev. At Malta in December 1989 he offered economic help, and accepted that reform in Eastern Europe could go on.
3. **Partnership and the end (1990–91).** Bush and his Secretary of State, James Baker, persuaded Gorbachev to accept German unification within NATO (3 October 1990), signed the Conventional Forces in Europe Treaty with him and the other members of NATO and the Warsaw Pact in November 1990, worked with Moscow against Iraq after its invasion of Kuwait in August 1990, and signed **START I** in Moscow on **31 July 1991**, the first treaty to cut strategic nuclear weapons (to 1,600 delivery vehicles and 6,000 warheads each). Bush supported Gorbachev against the hardliners' coup of 19–21 August 1991. The Soviet Union ended on 25–26 December 1991 and Bush then recognised the new states.

:::key Why relations changed, 1972–91: a five-stage summary
| Stage | Years | Relations | Main reason for the change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limited détente | 1972–79 | Arms limits, summits, trade | Parity, Vietnam, China, the economy |
| Collapse | 1979–80 | Détente ends | Afghanistan, Cuba brigade, SALT II fails |
| Second Cold War | 1981–84 | Hostility, arms race | Reagan, ideology, rearmament, SDI |
| Thaw and treaties | 1985–88 | Summits, INF Treaty | Gorbachev's new thinking; Reagan's turn to talks |

## Arms control, 1972–91, in one table

| Agreement | Date | Parties | Main terms | Ratified or in force? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ABM Treaty | 26 May 1972 | USA, USSR | Two ABM sites each (one from 1974); no more than 200 interceptors each at first | Ratified by the Senate in 1972; the US withdrew in 2002 |
| SALT I Interim Agreement | 26 May 1972 | USA, USSR | Froze ICBM launchers (1,054 and 1,618) and set submarine-launcher limits (710 and 950); five years | Approved by Congress; expired in 1977, both sides observed it |
| Vladivostok accord | November 1974 | Ford, Brezhnev | Outline for SALT II: 2,400 delivery vehicles; 1,320 with MIRVs | A framework, not a treaty |
| Helsinki Final Act | 1 August 1975 | 35 states | Frontiers, human rights, cooperation | Political commitment, not a treaty |
| SALT II | 18 June 1979 | Carter, Brezhnev | 2,400 falling to 2,250 delivery vehicles; MIRV limits | Signed; **not ratified**; both sides kept to it until 1986 |
| INF Treaty | 8 December 1987 | Reagan, Gorbachev | Abolished ground-launched missiles of 500–5,500 km; inspection; 2,692 destroyed | Senate 93–5 (May 1988); in force 1 June 1988 |
| START I | 31 July 1991 | Bush, Gorbachev | 1,600 delivery vehicles, 6,000 warheads each | Senate approved in 1992 (93–6); in force 5 December 1994 |

The pattern is that treaties grew from limiting (SALT I) to cutting (START I) as the political relationship improved. Notice also that the two treaties that failed or lapsed (SALT II and the Vladivostok framework) came in the years of weakening support for détente.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'Reagan's policies were the main reason for the end of the Cold War.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** Criteria: (1) *timing*: did the policy come before the change it is said to have caused? (2) *necessity*: would the change have happened without it? (3) *the actors' own evidence*: what did Soviet leaders say and do? For Reagan: NSDD-75 (17 January 1983), the build-up and SDI (23 March 1983), the Reagan Doctrine (Afghanistan, 15 February 1989), his turn to talks (Geneva 1985, INF 8 December 1987). Against: the Soviet economy's stagnation, Gorbachev's reforms (11 March 1985 onward), the UN speech (7 December 1988), no force in 1989, Bush's role. Judgement: Reagan was a significant cause of the change in US–Soviet relations, but not the main reason for the end of the Cold War, which depended on Soviet decisions.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Reagan's policies were an important reason for the end of the Cold War. He built up American weapons and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative in 1983, which the Soviet Union could not afford to match. He also helped the Afghan fighters, and the Soviet Union left Afghanistan in 1989. He then signed the INF Treaty with Gorbachev. So Reagan was the main reason the Cold War ended. However, Gorbachev was also important because he brought in reforms such as glasnost and perestroika."

*Commentary.* Accurate, and it acknowledges Gorbachev, but the claim "so Reagan was the main reason" is asserted from a list, and Gorbachev is added at the end as an afterthought. There are no criteria and no test of the claim. Table A Level 3 at best (7–9); Table B Level 3 (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a test.** "If Reagan's policies were the main reason for the end of the Cold War, the Soviet changes should follow them and not have started without them. But the Soviet economy was already stagnating in the 1970s, and the reforms that mattered most, glasnost and the new thinking, were Gorbachev's decisions after March 1985. In the same way the arms build-up of 1981–84 was followed by the hardest years of the Cold War, not by Soviet concessions: the Soviet Union walked out of the Geneva talks in November 1983. The concessions came after Gorbachev arrived and after Reagan himself had turned towards negotiation. So the pressure may have mattered, but the main change came from inside the Soviet Union."

*Commentary.* A test of timing is applied with accurate evidence, and the conclusion follows. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs a second and third criterion, a fair statement of the case for Reagan and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view that Reagan's policies were the main reason for the end of the Cold War treats a long, two-sided process as the work of one side. I shall test it against three criteria: whether the Soviet changes came after the pressure and not before it, whether they would have happened without it, and what Soviet leaders themselves did. My argument is that Reagan was an important cause of the change in superpower relations after 1985, but that the end of the Cold War in 1989–91 was mainly the result of decisions inside the Soviet Union, and of Gorbachev's in particular.

The case for Reagan is a serious one. His National Security Decision Directive 75, of 17 January 1983, set the aim of containing and over time reversing Soviet expansionism and of encouraging change inside the Soviet system. He backed it with a major military build-up, the deployment of Pershing II and cruise missiles in Western Europe from November 1983, and, on 23 March 1983, the Strategic Defense Initiative, which Soviet leaders feared they could not match. The Reagan Doctrine raised the cost of the Soviet empire: American support for the mujahideen, including Stinger missiles from 1986, contributed to the Soviet decision to leave Afghanistan, which Gorbachev had called a 'bleeding wound', and the last Soviet troops left on 15 February 1989. If timing is the test, there is something here: the concessions of 1987, the INF Treaty and the move to on-site inspection, came after four years of pressure. And Reagan's readiness to meet Gorbachev at Geneva in 1985 and Reykjavik in 1986 gave the new Soviet leader a partner who could deliver.

But on the criterion of necessity the case weakens. The pressure of 1981–84 did not produce concessions: the Soviet Union walked out of the Geneva talks in November 1983, and the years of greatest American pressure were the years of greatest Soviet hostility. The change began with Gorbachev, who became General Secretary on 11 March 1985. His diagnosis, that an economy that had barely grown since the late 1970s could not support the arms race, the war in Afghanistan and the empire in Eastern Europe, came from Soviet experience and from the reformers around him, and Archie Brown has argued that Gorbachev's own ideas were the most important single cause. The decisions that actually ended the Cold War were his: glasnost, the 7 December 1988 offer of a cut of 500,000 troops, and the decision not to use force when the Communist governments of Eastern Europe fell in 1989. Reagan's policies could not have caused these, and Gorbachev refused to defend the Soviet position with force, which no American policy compelled him to do. Even on Afghanistan the decision to leave was Gorbachev's, taken in 1986–87.

On the third criterion, what the actors did, the evidence points to a combination. Raymond Garthoff argued that the American military pressure was not the cause of Soviet change; James Mann argues that Reagan's own shift from confrontation to engagement, against hardliners in his party, helped to make an agreement possible. Both can be right, because the policy that worked was not the first Reagan policy of confrontation but the second, of negotiation. And Reagan's successor, George H. W. Bush, played a part in the final years: by his caution over the Berlin Wall in November 1989, by agreeing that a united Germany could stay in NATO on 3 October 1990 and by signing START I on 31 July 1991, he made it easier for Gorbachev to give way without disaster.

In conclusion, the view is only partly valid. Reagan's policies deserve credit for raising the stakes, and above all for his turn to negotiation, which turned Gorbachev's offers into treaties. But they were not the main reason. The cause that made the Cold War end, rather than merely change its form, was the Soviet leadership's decision that it could no longer sustain the competition, and that was a Soviet decision. The best judgement is that Reagan was a necessary part of the way the Cold War ended, as the partner and the pressure, but Gorbachev's choices were what ended it."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (13–14): three explicit criteria; a fair statement of the case for Reagan; a test of that case; a distinction between the cause of change in relations and the cause of the end of the Cold War; a clear, supported judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates and figures chosen as evidence (17 January 1983; 23 March 1983; 15 February 1989; 11 March 1985; 7 December 1988; 3 October 1990; 31 July 1991), and historians used to support rather than decorate the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 850 words, 35 to 40 minutes.

:::eg Assess the reasons for the failure of détente. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the reasons" asks you to identify several reasons, weigh their importance against a criterion and judge which mattered most or how they connect.

**Plan.** Reasons: (1) different understandings of détente (ideology); (2) the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (24–27 December 1979); (3) domestic opposition in the United States (Jackson–Vanik 1974, the Committee on the Present Danger, the failure of SALT II); (4) the change of leadership and new defence policy under Reagan (1981–83). Criteria: how far each was a cause of the collapse, as against a symptom or a final blow; whether détente would have survived without it. Judgement: underlying weakness (different understandings) made détente fragile; Afghanistan ended it; Reagan turned the collapse into open confrontation.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "Détente failed for several reasons. The Soviet Union invaded Afghanistan in 1979. Carter was angry and stopped selling grain to the USSR and the USA did not go to the Moscow Olympics. Reagan was elected in 1980 and he did not like communism. He called the Soviet Union an evil empire and started the Star Wars programme. The SALT II treaty was never ratified. All these things made détente fail."

*Commentary.* Accurate events, in order, but no argument: each sentence is a fact, and the last sentence only says that they "made détente fail". There is no ranking and no explanation of how each fact caused failure. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 to 3.

**Moving to Level 4: explain and link.** "The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan was the immediate cause of the failure of détente, because it removed what remained of American support for it. Carter had already asked for SALT II to be signed in June 1979, but after the invasion he asked the Senate on 3 January 1980 not to consider it. But the invasion fell on ground that had been prepared. The Senate had been moving against the treaty since the summer of 1979, when the discovery of a Soviet brigade in Cuba led Senator Church to say that ratification was unlikely, and Jackson–Vanik in 1974 had already shown that Congress would not give the Soviet Union the trade benefits it expected. Afghanistan was therefore the final blow to a policy that was already weakening."

*Commentary.* Two reasons are explained and connected, and the distinction between a trigger and an underlying weakness is used. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs the role of different understandings of détente, the effects of Reagan's change, and a sustained judgement.

**A Level 5 conclusion (the final paragraphs).**

"The reasons form a sequence, not a list. The underlying cause was that the two sides had never agreed what détente meant: for the United States it was a means of limiting Soviet behaviour in every region, and for the Soviet Union a means of securing parity and European stability while it went on supporting 'national liberation'. Angola in 1975, Ethiopia in 1977–78 and Afghanistan in 1978 each looked to Americans like a breach and to Moscow like a normal part of the world. This made détente fragile from the start, and it gave critics in the United States, such as the Committee on the Present Danger, an argument that was impossible to refute. Raymond Garthoff's view that the misunderstanding was central is, on this evidence, well founded.

Afghanistan was the decisive event, though not the root cause: it turned a weakening policy into a dead one, since after December 1979 the ratification of SALT II became impossible and Carter himself moved to containment, with the grain embargo, the Olympic boycott and the Carter Doctrine. Reagan's election and his new defence policy then determined what came next: not a return to détente under a different name, but a determined policy of pressure, as NSDD-75 of January 1983 shows. This made the failure of détente a period of open hostility, as in the autumn of 1983.

I therefore judge that the most important reason for the failure of détente was the misunderstanding that lay beneath it, because the other reasons only worked on a policy that already lacked a shared meaning. Afghanistan was the most important cause of the timing of the collapse, and Reagan of what replaced it. Had the two sides agreed on the rules, a Soviet move in Afghanistan might have been survivable; and without the misunderstanding, Reagan's arrival would not have meant the end of cooperation."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (13–14): the reasons are ranked against two criteria (cause of the collapse; cause of the timing), connected into a sequence, and a clear judgement is given. Table B, Level 4–5 (12–14): precise evidence in the paragraph and plan; more figures (the SALT II ceilings; 17 million tonnes of grain) would secure the top of Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the success of the Reagan Doctrine." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Reagan Doctrine was a policy of helping anti-communist fighters around the world. In Afghanistan the CIA gave weapons to the mujahideen, including Stinger missiles from 1986, and the Soviet army left in 1989. In Nicaragua the United States supported the Contras and Congress passed the Boland Amendments. There was a scandal called Iran–Contra where money from selling weapons to Iran went to the Contras. In Angola the United States helped UNITA after the Clark Amendment was repealed in 1985. Savimbi visited Washington in 1986. The Reagan Doctrine was very successful because the Soviet Union lost the Cold War."

**The level.** Table B: relevant and accurate detail on all three countries (Level 3), though without the dates and figures that would lift it. Table A: it is **narrative**, one country after another, with no criterion for "success" and no comparison; the final sentence is an assertion that does not follow, because the Soviet Union's defeat in the Cold War was not caused only by the Reagan Doctrine and the paragraph does not show that it was. Table A Level 2 (4–6).

**The fix.** Set a criterion and compare. "A policy of aid to anti-communist forces is successful if it achieves its aims at an acceptable cost. By that test the results were uneven. In Afghanistan the aim, a Soviet withdrawal, was achieved on 15 February 1989 and the Stingers, introduced in 1986, made the war more costly for Moscow; but Gorbachev had already decided to withdraw, and the cost to Afghanistan was heavy. In Nicaragua the aim, to remove the Sandinistas by force, failed: the Contras never held territory, Congress limited the aid and Iran–Contra, revealed on 3 November 1986, damaged the President. In Angola the Tripartite Accord of 22 December 1988 brought Namibian independence and a Cuban withdrawal, but the civil war continued until 2002. The doctrine therefore worked best where it complemented Soviet decisions to retreat, and worst where it depended on forces that could not win." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give three reasons why the United States wanted détente in the early 1970s.
A: To end the war in Vietnam without defeat; to save money, open trade and reduce the arms race after the Soviet Union reached nuclear parity; and to exploit the split between China and the USSR (Nixon visited Beijing in February 1972).
Q: What did SALT I (26 May 1972) agree, and what did it not cover?
A: The ABM Treaty (two defence sites each, later one) and an Interim Agreement freezing ICBM launchers (1,054 US, 1,618 USSR) and limiting submarine launchers. It did not limit warheads (MIRVs) or bombers.
Q: What were the three "baskets" of the Helsinki Final Act, and why did it matter for human rights?
A: I: security and principles (frontiers, human rights); II: economic, scientific and technical cooperation; III: humanitarian questions (family reunion, travel, information). Its human rights principle gave dissidents and monitoring groups (Moscow Helsinki Group 1976, Charter 77) a document to cite.
Q: Name the five causes of the failure of détente in this chapter.
A: The importance of ideology (different understandings of détente, the Second Cold War); the invasion of Afghanistan; the change of leadership (Reagan); the new defence policy (the build-up, SDI); and the failure to ratify SALT II.
Q: What was the Reagan Doctrine, and where was it applied?
A: Open and covert aid to anti-communist forces fighting Soviet-backed governments, to roll back Soviet gains. Applied in Nicaragua (the Contras), Afghanistan (the mujahideen, with Stingers from 1986) and Angola (UNITA, after the repeal of the Clark Amendment in 1985), among others.
Q: What was Iran–Contra?
A: The secret sale of American weapons to Iran in 1985–86, in the hope of freeing hostages in Lebanon, with money from the sales diverted to the Contras in breach of the Boland Amendment. It became public on 3 November 1986.
Q: What did the INF Treaty do?
A: It abolished all American and Soviet ground-launched missiles with ranges of 500–5,500 km, with on-site inspection (signed 8 December 1987; 2,692 missiles destroyed by 1 June 1991).
Q: What are three explanations of why the Cold War ended, and which historian is linked to each?
A: Reagan's pressure (the "Reagan victory" view); Soviet internal change and Gorbachev's decisions (Brown, Garthoff); and Reagan's own turn to engagement (Mann), with Gaddis giving weight to both leaders.
:::

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★ Assess the importance of the Helsinki Accords of 1975 in the history of détente.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: effect at the time and effect in the long run; effect on each side. Importance: the Soviet Union gained recognition of the postwar frontiers and a diplomatic success; the human rights principle (Principle VII) and Basket III gave dissidents a standard, and monitoring groups were created (Moscow Helsinki Group 1976, Charter 77); Carter used human rights as a theme. Limits: not a treaty; the monitors were arrested; critics in the United States said the West had conceded too much (Reagan's 1976 attack); it did not stop the decline of détente. Level 5 distinguishes short-term effects (friction) from long-term ones (a weakening of the Communist governments' claim to legitimacy) and judges which was more important.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in ending détente.
A: Indicative content. Importance: Carter's measures (SALT II withdrawn on 3 January 1980; grain embargo; Olympic boycott; Carter Doctrine); the invasion confirmed critics' warnings; it ended the Senate's chance of ratifying. Limits: détente was already weak (Cuba brigade, Angola, Ethiopia, Jackson–Vanik, the opposition to SALT II); the sanctions failed to change Soviet policy; Reagan's election mattered as much for what followed. Level 5 sets a criterion, e.g. whether détente could have survived without it, and judges that it was the decisive event but not the root cause.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that SDI was the most important part of Reagan's defence policy in bringing about change in the Soviet Union.
A: Indicative content. For: Soviet leaders feared SDI and its cost (Gorbachev made its limitation central at Reykjavik); it could not be matched; it may have pushed Moscow to bargain. Against: SDI was an idea in the 1980s, not a working system; the missiles in Europe, the build-up and the Reagan Doctrine also pressed the Soviet Union; Soviet economic decline and Gorbachev's own ideas explain more; Gorbachev dropped his demand on SDI in 1987 and the INF Treaty did not depend on it. Level 5 compares the pressures against a criterion (effect on Soviet decisions) and judges that SDI mattered mainly as a fear in Soviet minds and a bargaining point.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The Reagan Doctrine in Afghanistan was more successful than the Reagan Doctrine in Nicaragua.' Evaluate this view.
H: Decide what "success" means: an aim achieved, a lasting result, or a cost kept low.
H: Compare the roles of Congress in the two cases.
A: Indicative content. Afghanistan: Soviet withdrawal (14 April 1988 Accords; 15 February 1989); Stingers; strong support in Congress; but Gorbachev's own decision, a devastated Afghanistan, civil war and later Islamist consequences. Nicaragua: Contras did not win; Boland Amendments; Iran–Contra; ICJ ruling; but the peace plan and the 1990 elections, which the Sandinistas lost, and pressure that may have helped. Level 5 uses criteria, e.g. aims achieved and cost, and judges that Afghanistan was clearly more successful on the first test and not necessarily on the second.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Détente failed because of the actions of the Soviet Union rather than those of the United States.' Evaluate this view.
H: List Soviet actions (Angola, Ethiopia, Afghanistan, SS-20s, the treatment of dissidents) and American actions (Jackson–Vanik, the failure of SALT II, Reagan's policies).
H: Whose understanding of "détente" was more reasonable?
H: Use Garthoff's argument about different understandings.
A: Indicative content. Soviet: support for revolutions in Angola (1975–76) and Ethiopia (1977–78), SS-20 deployment, repression of dissidents, the invasion of Afghanistan. American: Jackson–Vanik, the Senate's rejection of SALT II, hawkish critics, Carter's inconsistency, Reagan's rhetoric and defence policy. Level 5 sets a criterion (who broke the terms as each side understood them?) and judges that each side acted in ways the other saw as violations, so the failure was shared, with the invasion of Afghanistan the largest single act.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Gorbachev, not Reagan, was the main reason for the improvement in relations between the United States and the USSR after 1985.' Evaluate this view.
H: What changed in the Soviet position between 1985 and 1988, and who made the changes?
H: What did Reagan do differently after 1983?
H: Think about what Bush added.
A: Indicative content. For Gorbachev: new thinking; moratorium on tests; acceptance of the zero option and inspection; Afghanistan; the UN speech; no force in 1989. For Reagan: his turn to negotiation (Shultz; Geneva; Reykjavik); trust; his readiness to defy hardliners; the INF Treaty; Moscow in 1988. For Bush: restraint in 1989, German unification, START I. Level 5 judges that Gorbachev made the larger changes and Reagan made agreements possible, and relates each to timing.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the effects of the Reagan Doctrine on the United States as well as on the countries it was applied to.
H: Think of Congress, the presidency and the American reputation.
H: Include Iran–Contra.
A: Indicative content. Effects abroad: the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan; Namibia's independence; the Contra war; Angola's civil war; refugees and deaths. Effects at home: the conflict between President and Congress (Boland, Clark); Iran–Contra, the Tower Commission, the prosecutions and pardons; a revival of confidence after Vietnam; the later debate on "blowback". Level 5 uses criteria, e.g. gains and costs for each, and judges the doctrine as effective as a way of raising Soviet costs and costly in its legacy.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'American policy towards the Soviet Union changed less between 1977 and 1989 than the Soviet Union itself changed.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare Carter, Reagan and Bush on arms control and on the Third World.
H: Identify continuities: containment, arms control, human rights.
H: Contrast with the Soviet changes: Brezhnev, Andropov, Gorbachev.
A: Support: American continuities (containment and the Cold War framework; arms control as a goal under every President; human rights from Carter to Reagan; covert aid in Afghanistan from 1979); the biggest change was Soviet (new thinking, the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine, withdrawal from Afghanistan, acceptance of inspection and asymmetric cuts, 1989). Challenge: American policy changed twice (from détente to pressure in 1979–81, from pressure to negotiation in 1983–88) and again under Bush; Reagan's Doctrine and SDI were new. Level 5 compares the scale and cause of the changes and judges that the Soviet change was larger and the American change was a response to it.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the historical debate about who ended the Cold War cannot be settled because the evidence for each side is incomplete.
H: What kinds of evidence are used for each side (Soviet archives, memoirs, American documents)?
H: What would count as proof that Reagan's pressure caused Soviet change?
H: Consider the different questions: the end of the Cold War, or the change in superpower relations?
A: Support: leaders' memoirs are self-serving; Soviet archives were opened only partly after 1991; causation is hard to prove because many factors acted at once; historians' positions reflect their politics. Challenge: much has been settled (the Soviet economy's condition; Gorbachev's decisions; the timeline of concessions); debate can be narrowed by separating questions (why Gorbachev changed course; why the West responded as it did; why the system collapsed). Level 5 identifies what the evidence can settle and what depends on a judgement, and gives an argued position.
