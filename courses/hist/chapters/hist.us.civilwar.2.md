## The growing strength of abolitionism

By the 1850s a small movement had become a political force. This section asks how and why, and what Southerners made of it. The first step is to see that abolitionism was never one thing.

:::def Abolitionism
The movement to end slavery. Abolitionists ranged from William Lloyd Garrison, who demanded immediate emancipation and rejected the Constitution as a pro-slavery document, to political abolitionists who wanted to use votes and the law. It is not the same as **Free Soil** or Republican opposition to the *extension* of slavery, which most Northerners could accept without wanting abolition.
:::

### Who the abolitionists were

William Lloyd Garrison began *The Liberator* in Boston in 1831 with the promise "I will be heard", and in 1833 helped found the American Anti-Slavery Society. Frederick Douglass, who had escaped from slavery in Maryland in 1838, became its greatest orator and in 1845 published his *Narrative*. In Rochester on 5 July 1852 he asked an audience of white Americans, "What, to the American slave, is your 4th of July?" Black abolitionists, men and women, free and escaped, were at the heart of the movement from the start. Black Americans ran vigilance committees and the networks of the Underground Railroad, and Harriet Tubman, who escaped from slavery in Maryland in 1849, made repeated journeys back to guide others to freedom.

The movement was a minority. Mobs attacked its meetings, and most Northern voters before the 1850s disliked slavery less than they disliked abolitionists. What changed in the 1850s was not that more people became abolitionists, but that more people came to share parts of the abolitionist case: that slavery was a moral evil, that the Slave Power was a danger to free government, and that slavery must not spread. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 (see [Civil War and Reconstruction (1)](#hist.us.civilwar.1)) and Bleeding Kansas did more to spread this feeling than any speech.

Southerners saw the matter very differently. The memory of Nat Turner's rising in Virginia in August 1831, in which enslaved men killed about sixty white people before being put down, and in reprisal many Black people were killed, made the fear of a slave revolt real. Planters believed that abolitionist writing could inspire another. So they defended their system more fiercely, restricted free speech, and treated even moderate Northern criticism as a threat.

### Uncle Tom's Cabin

The best-known book of the movement was a novel. Harriet Beecher Stowe, a Connecticut-born writer living in Ohio and then Maine, was angered by the Fugitive Slave Act and wrote *Uncle Tom's Cabin; or, Life Among the Lowly*. It appeared in weekly parts in the abolitionist newspaper *The National Era* from June 1851 and as a two-volume book on 20 March 1852. The book sold about 3,000 copies on its first day and about 300,000 in the United States in its first year, and the first British edition sold some 200,000 within months. It was translated into many languages. Plays based on it reached an audience far larger than the book's readers, though many of those plays (the "Tom shows") later spread racial stereotypes, and the name "Uncle Tom" later became an insult.

The novel's power was that it made the human cost of slavery vivid to readers who had never seen it. It showed families torn apart by sale, the cruelty of an owner (Simon Legree), and the courage of enslaved people, including a mother, Eliza, who crosses the frozen Ohio River with her child to escape. It argued that slavery corrupted everyone, even kind owners, because it could break up families whenever a debt or a death required a sale. It also made the Fugitive Slave Act personal: readers were asked what they would do if a fugitive came to their door.

:::def Uncle Tom's Cabin
The 1852 anti-slavery novel by Harriet Beecher Stowe. It turned Northern opinion against slavery by showing its effects on enslaved families, and in the South it was banned and denounced. It was the best-selling American book of the nineteenth century after the Bible.
:::

The South answered with a wave of "anti-Tom" novels, perhaps twenty or thirty in the decade before the war (among them Mary Eastman's *Aunt Phillis's Cabin*, 1852), which portrayed kindly masters and contented enslaved workers. Stowe replied with *A Key to Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1853), collecting real cases, newspaper reports and court records as evidence for her scenes. Southerners saw her book as a slander on their whole society, and the quarrel over it was one more thing on which North and South could not agree.

How much did it matter? The story that President Lincoln greeted Stowe as "the little lady who started this great war" is probably not true; historians have found no reliable record that he said it. A better judgement is that the novel did not cause the war but it changed what many Northerners felt about slavery, and in particular made the Fugitive Slave Act hard to enforce and made the Republican case easier to hear. Its effect cannot be measured exactly: we cannot count votes it changed. That is itself a lesson for source work: a famous book is evidence of what people read and talked about, not proof of what they did.

### Southern secessionism grows

On the other side, a minority in the South moved from defending slavery inside the Union to arguing that the South should leave it. They were called **fire-eaters**: William Yancey of Alabama, Robert Barnwell Rhett of South Carolina and Edmund Ruffin of Virginia were the best known. A Southern Rights convention at Nashville in June 1850 had talked of secession and then fizzled out, as most Southerners still hoped for fair treatment inside the Union. But each Northern success made the argument stronger: the Republican rise in 1856, the Northern defiance of the Fugitive Slave Act, and above all the idea that a party hostile to slavery would soon control the government. South Carolina's Senator James Hammond told his Northern colleagues in 1858 that cotton was king, and no one dared make war on it. Many Southerners believed their cotton gave them the power to survive outside the Union.

:::key Two movements, each growing by fear of the other
1. **Northern anti-slavery feeling** grew in the 1850s (the Fugitive Slave Act, *Uncle Tom's Cabin*, Kansas, Brooks–Sumner), but mostly as opposition to the *extension* of slavery and the Slave Power, not as a demand to abolish it.
2. **Southern secessionism** grew from a minority view to a majority one in the Deep South, fed by the fear that slavery would be hemmed in, that the federal government would soon be in hostile hands, and that Northerners wished to see a slave rising.
3. Each side's propaganda fed the other: abolitionist writers described the South as brutal and wicked; Southern politicians described Republicans as "Black Republicans" who wanted a race war.
:::

## John Brown and Harpers Ferry, 1859

### The raid

The abolitionist John Brown (see Pottawatomie, 1856, in [Civil War and Reconstruction (1)](#hist.us.civilwar.1)) believed that slavery was a war against the enslaved, and that it could be ended only by a war in return. In 1859 he planned to seize the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia (now West Virginia), arm enslaved people and begin a rising in the mountains. He raised money from a group of Northern supporters, some of whom knew his plan, and rented a farm in Maryland. On the night of Sunday 16 October 1859 he and 21 followers (16 white and 5 Black men) seized the arsenal and held some local people hostage. No rising followed. Local militia and townspeople pinned the raiders in the fire-engine house. On 18 October a force of US Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee stormed it in about three minutes and captured Brown, who was wounded. Ten of his men and one marine died, along with several local people.

Brown was tried at Charles Town, Virginia, convicted of treason, murder and inciting slaves to rebel, and sentenced to death on 2 November 1859. In his speech to the court he said that if he had acted as he had for the rich and powerful, "every man in this court would have deemed it an act worthy of reward", and that he believed he had done right. He was hanged on 2 December 1859. On the day he gave his jailer a note: "I John Brown am now quite certain that the crimes of this guilty, land: will never be purged away; but with Blood." Six of his followers were also hanged; five escaped.

:::def Harpers Ferry raid (1859)
John Brown's attempt, on 16–18 October 1859, to seize a federal arsenal in Virginia and start a rising of enslaved people. It failed within two days, but Brown's trial and execution made him a martyr in much of the North and a symbol of Northern hostility in the South.
:::

### Reactions

North and South read the same events in opposite ways.

- **In the North**, most Republican leaders condemned the raid as a crime. Even William Lloyd Garrison, a pacifist, first called it "misguided, wild, and apparently insane". But as Brown's trial went on and he spoke with great dignity, many people's views changed. On the day of his execution church bells were tolled, flags were flown at half-mast and meetings were held in many towns, and writers such as Henry David Thoreau and Ralph Waldo Emerson praised him. Lincoln, who was not an abolitionist, said in 1859–60 that Brown's violence and treason could not be excused even though Brown, like the Republicans, thought slavery wrong.
- **In the South**, the raid confirmed the fear of a slave rising and of Northern support for it. The discovery that Brown had been helped by wealthy Northern backers was taken as proof of a conspiracy. Southern states formed new militia companies. Senator James Mason of Virginia chaired a Senate committee that investigated whether Northerners had taken part. Above all, Southerners were shocked that so many Northerners treated Brown as a hero: it seemed to prove that the North's respect for the Constitution was a pretence.

Neither side was fully right. Republican leaders did condemn Brown (their 1860 platform denounced armed invasion of any state as among the gravest of crimes), and they had not known his plan. Yet the Northern admiration for him was real, and the Democrats made use of it in the 1860 campaign. For Southern fire-eaters, Harpers Ferry became the best argument that no Southern state was safe inside a Union that contained Republicans.

:::key Why Harpers Ferry mattered
1. It **hardened Southern opinion**: fear of a rising, and anger at Northern admiration for Brown, made the case for secession more convincing.
2. It **embarrassed the Republicans**, who had to say they were not abolitionists or John Brown's allies, while Southern speakers said they were.
3. It was an **event, not a cause on its own**: the raid fed the existing fear of the North, and it came a year before the election that triggered secession.
:::

## States' rights and the "Slave Power"

Two ideas of the 1850s explain how each side thought it was defending the Constitution.

### The South's argument: states' rights

:::def States' rights
The doctrine that the states, not the federal government, hold the main power in the Union, because the Union was formed by a compact between sovereign states that could, if necessary, be dissolved. In Southern hands it meant the right to protect slavery from Northern or federal interference, and in the end the right to secede.
:::

The idea had a long history: Jefferson and Madison's Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (1798–99), Calhoun's doctrine of nullification (see [Civil War and Reconstruction (1)](#hist.us.civilwar.1)). In the crisis of 1860–61 Southern leaders claimed that the states had made the Union and could leave it when it no longer served them, as South Carolina's convention of December 1860 declared.

But "states' rights" is a more complicated cause than it seems. The South did not always want weak federal government: it wanted a strong federal government when that protected slavery. In the 1850s the South demanded the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 (a federal law forcing Northern states to help), and demanded a federal slave code to protect slavery in the territories. South Carolina's own declaration of secession complains that Northern states had refused to carry out their duty under the Constitution to return fugitives, and says the laws of the general government "have ceased to effect the objects of the Constitution". In effect it blames Northern states for using *their* state rights against a federal law that protected slavery. The cause that came first, in the South's own words, was slavery.

:::warn Do not say the war was "about states' rights" without saying "the right to do what?"
"States' rights" was the language of the argument, but the secession documents themselves name slavery. Mississippi's declaration begins: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery". Georgia's says the question of slavery was "the great difficulty in the way of the formation of the Constitution" and that "the prohibition of slavery in the Territories is the cardinal principle" of the Republican Party. The strongest answers use these documents to show that states' rights was the method and slavery was the purpose.
:::

### The North's argument: the Slave Power

:::def Slave Power
In the language of Northern Republicans, a conspiracy of Southern slaveholders and their Northern allies (the "doughfaces") to control the federal government, spread slavery across the nation and silence its opponents.
:::

In the North, many feared that the "Slave Power" had taken over the government. They pointed to the Fugitive Slave Act (the federal government hunting people on Northern soil), the Kansas–Nebraska Act (repeal of the Missouri Compromise), the Gag Rule against petitions, Pierce's and Buchanan's support for the pro-slavery side in Kansas, and the attack on Sumner. Lincoln gave the fear a famous form in the House Divided speech of 1858: he described a conspiracy by "Stephen, Franklin, Roger and James" (Douglas, Pierce, Taney and Buchanan) to build a frame in which slavery could be made lawful in every state. The Dred Scott judgement, the next event, seemed to confirm the picture.

Southerners had their own fantasy in return: of the "Black Republican" who would abolish slavery and bring on a rising. Both pictures were exaggerated. Most Republicans did not want to abolish slavery where it existed; most Southern leaders were not plotting to make slavery lawful in every state. But the fact that each believed the worst of the other is itself a reason why compromise failed.

## The Dred Scott judgement, 1857

### The case

Dred Scott was an enslaved man who had been taken by his owner, an army surgeon named John Emerson, from Missouri to Illinois, a free state, and then to Fort Snelling in what is now Minnesota, where the Missouri Compromise banned slavery. After Emerson died, Scott sued for his freedom in a St Louis court in 1846, arguing that living on free soil had made him free. After a long series of trials the case reached the US Supreme Court, which in 1857 was led by Chief Justice Roger B. Taney of Maryland, himself a former slaveholder, with five of the nine justices from slave states.

### The judgement

On 6 March 1857 the Court decided against Scott by seven votes to two (the dissenters were John McLean and Benjamin Curtis). Each justice wrote an opinion, but Taney's was the opinion of the Court, and it made three main points:
1. **Black Americans were not citizens**, and so could not sue in a federal court. Taney said that people of African descent "are not included, and were not intended to be included" among the "citizens" of the Constitution, and he described them as having been regarded when it was written as "beings of an inferior order" who "had no rights which the white man was bound to respect".
2. **The Missouri Compromise was unconstitutional**: Congress had no power to ban slavery in the territories, so Scott's stay at Fort Snelling had not made him free.
3. Taney also said that the Fifth Amendment, which protects property from being taken without due process of law, protected the right to hold enslaved people in the territories.

The result stripped the Republican platform of its constitutional base: if Congress could not ban slavery in the territories, then the party's central aim (no extension of slavery) was against the Constitution as the Court read it. It also damaged popular sovereignty, because if Congress could not ban slavery, could a territorial legislature? That was the question Lincoln put to Douglas at Freeport. Scott himself was freed by his new owners, the Blow family, within three months, and died of tuberculosis in 1858.

:::def Dred Scott v. Sandford (1857)
The Supreme Court judgement of 6 March 1857, by seven votes to two, that Black Americans could not be citizens and that Congress had no power to ban slavery in the territories, which made the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional.
:::

### The reaction

In the South the judgement was welcomed: it declared that slavery was protected by the Constitution in all the territories. President James Buchanan had hoped that the Court would settle the question, and had said in his inaugural address, two days before the decision, that it would be "speedily and finally settled". In the North it did the opposite. Republicans said the decision was a political act by a Court packed with Southern judges, and that Buchanan had pressed one of the Northern justices to join the majority (historians believe he did, so it is not a myth). Lincoln said, in a speech at Springfield on 26 June 1857, "We think the Dred Scott decision is erroneous", and that the Republicans would work to have the Court overturn it. The case also gave weight to Lincoln's warning in the House Divided speech that the next step might be a ruling that no state could ban slavery.

:::key Why Dred Scott mattered
1. It **closed the legal route to compromise**: the Court had said Congress could not do what the Missouri Compromise and the Wilmot Proviso had tried to do.
2. It **strengthened the Slave Power story** in the North and gave the Republicans their main argument in 1858 and 1860.
3. It **split the Democrats**: Douglas's popular sovereignty was now hard to square with the Court's ruling, which led to the Freeport Doctrine and the Southern demand for a slave code.
4. But it was **not an immediate cause of war**: few enslaved people lived in the western territories, so the practical effect was small. Its importance was in what both sides believed it meant.
:::

## The election campaigns of 1860

### Four candidates, two contests

By the spring of 1860 the parties had changed completely (see [Civil War and Reconstruction (1)](#hist.us.civilwar.1) for the Democratic split at Charleston and Baltimore). Four candidates ran:

- **Abraham Lincoln** (Republican), nominated at Chicago on 16–18 May 1860 after three ballots, beating the front-runner William Seward of New York. His running mate was Hannibal Hamlin of Maine. The party's platform opposed the extension of slavery but promised not to interfere with it in the states where it existed. It also called for a protective tariff, free homesteads for settlers in the West and a railway to the Pacific, which appealed to Northern farmers and manufacturers. Lincoln had made his name as a national figure through the Lincoln–Douglas debates and his speech at the Cooper Union in New York on 27 February 1860, in which he argued that the Founders had meant Congress to control slavery in the territories.
- **Stephen Douglas** (Northern Democrat), who held that popular sovereignty was the only fair answer and who toured the South to defend the Union, an unusual step at a time when candidates seldom campaigned in person.
- **John C. Breckinridge** (Southern Democrat), the sitting Vice-President, nominated by the Southern wing of the party, which wanted federal protection for slavery in the territories.
- **John Bell** (Constitutional Union), a former Whig from Tennessee. His party, formed in May 1860 at Baltimore by moderate ex-Whigs, took no stand on slavery at all and stood simply for the Constitution, the Union and the enforcement of the laws.

The contest was really two separate elections. In the North it was Lincoln against Douglas. In the South it was Breckinridge against Bell, with Douglas trailing. Lincoln was not even on the ballot in ten Southern states, so he received no votes at all there. His supporters campaigned with torchlight marches by "Wide Awake" clubs; Lincoln himself, following the custom of the time, stayed quietly in Springfield, Illinois, and made no speeches.

The numbers show four things:
1. **Lincoln won with less than 40 per cent** of the popular vote, but with a clear majority in the Electoral College (180 of 303; 152 were needed), because he carried every free state except New Jersey, where he took four of seven electoral votes. The electoral system rewards winning states, not total votes.
2. **The result was entirely sectional.** Lincoln carried the North; Breckinridge won eleven states (the Deep South, plus Delaware, Maryland, North Carolina and Arkansas); Bell won Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee; Douglas won only Missouri and three of New Jersey's seven electoral votes, though he came second in the popular vote.
3. **Lincoln would very likely have won anyway.** Historians who have added up the opposition's votes state by state conclude that even if the three other candidates had joined forces everywhere, Lincoln would still have won the Electoral College.
4. **For the first time, a president was elected with no support from the South.** This was the point that mattered most to Southerners.

:::key Why the election of 1860 mattered
1. It was the first time a purely **sectional party** won the presidency.
2. It showed that the **North had the numbers**: the free states had enough electoral votes to elect a president without the South, and that would be so in every future election.
3. It brought to power a party whose platform denied the Southern claim that slavery had a right to expand, and whose leader had said the nation could not remain "half slave and half free".
:::

### Reactions to the result

In the North, Republicans celebrated and Lincoln said little. In the South the reaction was alarm, and in the Deep South something close to panic. The leaders who wanted secession said it was the moment they had warned of: "Black Republicans" now held the government. In South Carolina the legislature had already arranged to remain in session to see the result, and when it came, called a convention. In Charleston there were rallies, flags and the firing of guns. The Georgia declaration of January 1861 put the grievance plainly: the party of Lincoln, it said, was "an anti-slavery party", with the prohibition of slavery in the Territories as its "cardinal principle".

Not all Southerners agreed. There were two groups: **immediate secessionists**, who thought each state should leave at once; and **cooperationists**, who wanted to wait for a united Southern response or for some overt act by Lincoln. Many slaveholders in the Upper South (Virginia, Tennessee, North Carolina, Arkansas) and most small farmers in the mountains were unionist: they saw no reason for a state to leave because a president they disliked had been legally elected. The Deep South, where cotton planters had most to lose and held most power, did not wait.

President James Buchanan, in his last months, was no help. In his message to Congress on 3 December 1860 he said that no state had a right to secede, but also that the federal government had no power to stop a state by force. That left the Deep South free to act and Northern leaders without a policy until Lincoln took office.

## The failure of compromise

### The Crittenden Compromise

Senator John J. Crittenden of Kentucky, the heir to Henry Clay's role, proposed on 18 December 1860 a package of constitutional amendments. The central one would have restored the 36°30′ line and extended it to all territory "now held or hereafter acquired": slavery would be banned north of it and protected by federal law south of it. Other clauses would have protected slavery in the District of Columbia and compensated owners of fugitives who were rescued. Another would have barred any future amendment allowing Congress to interfere with slavery in the states.

It had support from moderates in both sections and might have won over much of the Upper South. But Lincoln, from Springfield, wrote to Republican leaders in Congress. To Congressman William Kellogg he wrote on 11 December 1860: "Entertain no proposition for a compromise in regard to the extension of slavery." He feared that a promise to protect slavery in any future territory, including land won from Mexico or Cuba, would reopen the question of expansion and destroy the whole purpose of the Republican Party. Republicans in Congress killed the plan in committee. The Deep South was by then leaving and was not interested in a compromise it had not asked for.

### Other attempts

A Peace Conference in Washington (4–27 February 1861), chaired by the former president John Tyler, brought together delegates from 21 of the 34 states. It recommended a plan similar to Crittenden's, which Congress did not accept. The Republicans did offer one concession on slavery where it existed: the **Corwin Amendment**, passed by Congress in the first days of March 1861, would have forbidden any future amendment to let Congress abolish or interfere with slavery in the states. Lincoln said in his inaugural address that he had no objection to making that guarantee express and irrevocable. It was never ratified by enough states, and the Confederacy ignored it.

:::key Why compromise failed in 1860–61
1. **The central issue was now extension, and neither side would move.** Southerners wanted slavery protected in the territories; Republicans had been elected on a promise to stop its spread. Any bargain on the territories meant that one side must give up its programme.
2. **The Deep South was already leaving** and did not need a compromise; by February 1861 it had formed a government.
3. **Lincoln was not yet president** and Buchanan would not lead, so no one had the power or the will to act.
4. **Trust had gone**: after Kansas–Nebraska, Dred Scott and John Brown, each side believed the other would break any agreement.
:::

## Secession: the stages before and after Lincoln's inauguration

:::def Secession
The formal withdrawal of a state from the Union. In 1860–61 eleven Southern states passed ordinances of secession through state conventions, claiming that the states had made the Union and could leave it. Lincoln and the North held that the Union was perpetual and that secession was illegal.
:::

### Stage 1: South Carolina and the Lower South (December 1860–February 1861)

South Carolina's convention met on 17 December and on **20 December 1860** voted unanimously, 169 to 0, to dissolve the Union between the state and the other states. On 24 December it adopted a *Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina from the Federal Union*. It states that the Northern states had broken the Constitution by refusing to return fugitives and by electing a president "whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery". "A geographical line has been drawn across the Union," it says, and the election has put into power "a sectional party" against the South's institution.

Six states followed in six weeks: Mississippi (9 January 1861), Florida (10 January), Alabama (11 January), Georgia (19 January), Louisiana (26 January) and Texas (1 February, confirmed by a vote of the people on 23 February). In each a state convention decided, and in each there was a vocal minority against it. Georgia and Alabama had large minorities who wanted to wait. The Mississippi declaration said: "Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery—the greatest material interest of the world."

The seceding states took over federal forts, arsenals, mints and customs houses within their borders. Buchanan did nothing. In Charleston harbour, Major Robert Anderson, commanding the federal garrison, moved his men on the night of 26 December 1860 from Fort Moultrie, which could not be defended, to **Fort Sumter** on an island in the harbour. When Buchanan sent a merchant ship, the *Star of the West*, with supplies, Confederate batteries fired on it on 9 January 1861 and turned it back.

Delegates of six states met at Montgomery, Alabama, on 4 February 1861 and formed the **Confederate States of America**. They adopted a provisional constitution on 8 February, which, unlike the US Constitution, expressly protected the right of property in enslaved people. They elected Jefferson Davis of Mississippi, a former US senator and Secretary of War, as president, and Alexander Stephens of Georgia as vice-president. Davis was inaugurated on 18 February. Texas joined in March.

### Stage 2: the Upper South and the border states wait

The Upper South did not follow. Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee and Arkansas voted against leaving the Union in early 1861, as did the four border states Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri (the Missouri convention rejected secession by 89 to 1 on 19 March). They had fewer enslaved people, more small farmers and stronger trade links with the North. Their position was: stay in, but do not coerce the South. This is why what Lincoln did next mattered so much.

### Stage 3: Lincoln's inauguration, 4 March 1861

Lincoln took the oath on 4 March in a Washington full of troops, in fear of an attack. His **First Inaugural Address** was written to reassure the Upper South while denying the right to secede.

- On slavery: "I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. I believe I have no lawful right to do so, and I have no inclination to do so."
- On the Union: "the Union of these States is perpetual", and "Physically speaking, we can not separate." So the ordinances of secession were, he said, legally void.
- On federal property: "The power confided to me will be used to hold, occupy, and possess the property and places belonging to the Government and to collect the duties and imposts", but beyond that, he said, there would be no invasion and no use of force against the people anywhere.
- To the South: "In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war. The Government will not assail you. You can have no conflict without being yourselves the aggressors."
- He ended with an appeal: "The mystic chords of memory, stretching from every battlefield and patriot grave to every living heart and hearthstone … will yet swell the chorus of the Union, when again touched, as surely they will be, by the better angels of our nature."

### Stage 4: Fort Sumter, 12–13 April 1861

By March, the question was Fort Sumter. Anderson's garrison of about 85 men was running out of food, and would have to surrender or leave by mid-April unless supplied. Lincoln was told by his military advisers that relieving it by force was unlikely to work. Most of his cabinet at first advised withdrawal; Secretary of State William Seward worked behind the scenes to evacuate it. But Lincoln knew that giving up Sumter would look like recognising the Confederacy, and so after some weeks he chose a middle course. On 4 April he ordered an expedition under Gustavus Fox to carry supplies, and on 6 April he told South Carolina's governor, Francis Pickens, that "an attempt will be made to supply Fort Sumter with provisions only", and that if it was not resisted, there would be no attempt to put in men, arms or ammunition.

Now the decision passed to Jefferson Davis. The Confederate cabinet, which met on 9 April, decided to demand the fort's surrender and, if refused, to reduce it before the relief ships arrived. Only the Secretary of State, Robert Toombs, is recorded as opposing it. General P. G. T. Beauregard demanded the surrender on 11 April; Anderson refused. At 4.30 a.m. on **12 April 1861** Confederate batteries around Charleston harbour opened fire. After 34 hours, with the fort burning and his supplies exhausted, Anderson surrendered on 13 April. No one was killed in the bombardment itself; the first deaths came in an accident during the surrender salute.

On 15 April Lincoln proclaimed that an insurrection existed and called on the states for 75,000 militia for 90 days. The effect was immediate: to the Upper South this was coercion. Virginia's convention voted to secede on **17 April** (confirmed by the people on 23 May), Arkansas followed on 6 May, Tennessee on 7 May (confirmed on 8 June) and North Carolina on 20 May. The four border states stayed in the Union, although Kentucky declared itself neutral and Missouri and Maryland were divided. The Confederacy now had eleven states and a capital, which moved to Richmond, Virginia, in May.

:::def Fort Sumter
A federal fort on an island in Charleston harbour, South Carolina. The Confederate bombardment of 12–13 April 1861, which forced Major Anderson to surrender, began the Civil War.
:::

:::key Why Fort Sumter led to war (and why it did not cause it)
1. **A stand-off had to end.** A Union fort in the harbour of the first state to secede could not remain for long without one side giving way: Lincoln could not give it up without recognising secession, and the Confederacy could not leave it without admitting that its independence was incomplete.
2. **Both sides chose.** Lincoln chose to resupply it (a peaceful but firm step); Davis and the Confederate cabinet chose to attack before the supplies arrived.
3. **The call for volunteers turned a clash into a general war** by driving the Upper South to join the Confederacy.
4. But Sumter was the **trigger**, not the cause. If the Union had not already been split by slavery, secession and the election of 1860, there would have been no Sumter.
:::

## Why did the war break out in April 1861? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | Slavery and the different societies built on it; the Slave Power and "Black Republican" fears; states' rights as a doctrine | Made the sections see each other as threats |
| Medium-term | Abolitionism and *Uncle Tom's Cabin*; the Fugitive Slave Act; Kansas–Nebraska; Dred Scott; John Brown | Destroyed trust and the means of compromise |
| Short-term | The Democratic split; the election of 1860; the failure of the Crittenden plan and the Peace Conference | Gave the Deep South both the occasion and the opportunity to secede |
| Triggers | South Carolina's secession (20 December 1860); Sumter (12–13 April 1861) | Turned secession into war |

:::key A judgement you can defend
**Slavery** was the underlying cause, and the secession documents say so in plain words. **Lincoln's election** was the occasion for secession: it showed the Deep South that it could no longer control the federal government, and the secessionists feared the Republicans would confine slavery to its present area, where they believed it would be doomed. **Secession was not war**: the Union stayed whole in the Upper South until Fort Sumter. What made it war was a clash of two positions that could not both be true. The Union was perpetual (Lincoln) and a state could leave it (Davis). When Lincoln refused to give up Sumter and Davis chose to take it, those positions met. The **decisions** of two men at Sumter and the call for troops decided the date and the shape of the war, but not why it came.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **David Potter**, *Lincoln and His Party in the Secession Crisis* (1942), argued that the Republicans misjudged how strong Unionist feeling in the South was, and that Lincoln's approach rested on a hope the Union men would win control. His later book, *The Impending Crisis, 1848–1861* (1976), stressed that the differences between North and South were made decisive by the way each came to see the other as a threat to its way of life.
- **Kenneth Stampp**, *And the War Came: The North and the Secession Crisis, 1860–1861* (1950), traced how Northern leaders, and especially Republicans, handled the crisis and why the effort to compromise failed.
- **James McPherson**, *Battle Cry of Freedom* (1988), argued that the election of Lincoln convinced the Deep South that slavery was no longer safe in the Union, and that the Confederate constitution's protections for slavery show what it was founded to defend.
- Older and newer arguments differ on responsibility for Sumter: some historians say Lincoln's resupply put the Confederates in the position of having to fire first; others say that Davis could have waited, so the decision to shoot was his.
You can use this at AS to show why causes are weighed differently, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the election of Lincoln in 1860 led to secession.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Lincoln was elected in November 1860 and he was a Republican. The Republicans did not like slavery. South Carolina left the Union in December 1860 and other states followed. Georgia said the Republicans were anti-slavery. So Lincoln's election led to secession."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes. The word "so" asserts a link but nothing explains why a Republican victory meant secession. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The election led to secession first because it showed the South it had lost power. Lincoln won 180 electoral votes without a single Southern electoral vote: his name was not even on the ballot in ten Southern states. For the first time the North could elect a president without the South and would be able to do so every time. Southern leaders concluded that they could no longer protect slavery through national politics. A second reason was fear for slavery itself. The Republican platform opposed the extension of slavery, and Lincoln had said in 1858 that the nation could not remain half slave and half free. Southern secessionists took this to mean that the party would confine slavery until it died. Georgia's declaration said that the prohibition of slavery in the territories was the 'cardinal principle' of the Republicans."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was that the secessionists were already prepared. Fire-eaters such as Yancey and Rhett had warned for years that the election of a Republican would justify leaving, and John Brown's raid in October 1859 had made many Southerners fear that Republicans would tolerate a slave rising. South Carolina had arranged to have its convention ready, and met within six weeks. In the Deep South, where slavery was most important and the fear of Northern power was greatest, the argument won quickly.

These factors were linked. Loss of political power made slavery's future look insecure; fear of Republican policy and Harpers Ferry made the insecurity feel urgent; and the preparations of the secessionists gave them the means to act at once. Lincoln's election was the occasion, not the sole cause: his actual promise was not to interfere with slavery in the states, and the Upper South stayed in the Union until Fort Sumter. The most important reason was the loss of political power to the North, because it made every other threat permanent. Without a voice in the federal government, the Deep South saw no secure future for slavery inside the Union."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (power loss made fear permanent, preparation made action quick), and a supported conclusion about the main factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

An increasing hostility on the part of the non-slaveholding States to the institution of slavery has led to a disregard of their obligations, and the laws of the General Government have ceased to effect the objects of the Constitution. A geographical line has been drawn across the Union, and all the States north of that line have united in the election of a man to the high office of President of the United States, whose opinions and purposes are hostile to slavery. On the 4th day of March next, this party will take possession of the Government.

*Adapted from the Declaration of the Immediate Causes Which Induce and Justify the Secession of South Carolina from the Federal Union, adopted by the state convention, 24 December 1860. Shortened; the phrases in the text are the declaration's own.*

**Source B**

I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. I believe I have no lawful right to do so, and I have no inclination to do so. I hold that the Union of these States is perpetual. In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war.

*Adapted from Abraham Lincoln's First Inaugural Address, Washington, 4 March 1861. Shortened.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the causes of secession.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is from South Carolina and says that the North is hostile to slavery and has elected a president who is against it. Source B is from Lincoln and says he will not interfere with slavery. So they disagree about whether slavery was under threat. Source A is biased because it is from South Carolina and Source B is biased because it is from Lincoln."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comments on bias are general assertions without context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources agree that slavery lay at the centre of the dispute. Source A names it as the cause of the Southern grievance: the 'hostility' of the North to 'the institution of slavery' and a president 'hostile to slavery'. Source B, though written to calm the South, is also about slavery: Lincoln's opening promise not to interfere with it 'where it exists' shows that he too understands the fear of its security as what the quarrel is about. Both also accept the language of law and the Constitution. South Carolina says the Northern states had shown 'a disregard of their obligations' under 'the Constitution', and Lincoln says he has no 'lawful right' to act against slavery in the states.

They differ over what the election means and over what should follow. For Source A, Lincoln's election is a threat that justifies leaving: the 'States north of that line' have elected him, and 'this party will take possession of the Government'. For Source B, it is no threat: Lincoln promises not to touch slavery in the states, and he denies that secession is a lawful response, because the Union is 'perpetual'. Source A speaks of a 'geographical line' and the North's hostility; Source B speaks of civil war as an outcome that lies in the 'hands' of the South.

The differences arise from context. Source A was adopted by a convention that had already voted 169 to 0 to secede on 20 December and wanted to justify the step to its own people and the world. Its purpose is to make a legal case, so it selects grievances to prove that the Northern states, not South Carolina, had broken the compact: it must therefore stress the Northern states' broken obligations (in the full text, above all their refusal to return fugitives) and the election. It was drafted by a state that had long threatened secession and was eager for it, so it is strong evidence of what the Deep South's leaders said was their reason, but it was written to persuade, and the grievance it chose was the one that justified leaving. Source B was spoken by a president who needed to hold the Upper South: Virginia, Tennessee, North Carolina and Arkansas were still in the Union. It is therefore designed to reassure, and Lincoln promises not to interfere with slavery in the states, which he had said throughout the campaign, while holding that the Union could not be broken. That Lincoln made these promises, and yet seven states had left already, suggests that the cause of secession was not what Lincoln actually intended to do but what the Republicans' victory meant for slavery's future and for Southern power. Source A is thus better evidence of what the secessionists said motivated them; Source B is better evidence of the Union's position, not of Southern motives."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the convention, the Upper South) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The attack on Fort Sumter was the main reason the Civil War broke out in April 1861.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: Sumter was the first shots; before 12 April seven states had left but no fighting; the attack, and Lincoln's call for 75,000 volunteers three days later, moved four more states into the Confederacy (Virginia 17 April ...); Davis chose to fire before the relief ships arrived.
- Against 1, the stand-off: secession and the Confederate government (4–18 February) had already created two governments with rival claims, Anderson's fort sat in the way; war was likely whatever happened.
- Against 2, deeper causes: slavery, the Slave Power and "Black Republican" fears; Kansas–Nebraska, Dred Scott, John Brown, the 1860 election.
- Against 3, the failure of compromise: Crittenden, Peace Conference, Lincoln's refusal on extension.
- Judgement: Sumter was the trigger; the criterion is what turned secession into war and then why the two sides were in a position to fight.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The attack on Fort Sumter was very important. Before April 1861 there had been no fighting, although seven states had left the Union. On 12 April the Confederates fired on the fort for 34 hours until Anderson surrendered. Lincoln responded by calling for 75,000 volunteers, and then four more states seceded and the Civil War began. This shows that Fort Sumter was the main reason for the outbreak of war."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet Sumter could happen only because the Union was already split. Seven Southern states had left between 20 December and 1 February, the Confederate States had formed a government at Montgomery on 4 February, and Jefferson Davis had been inaugurated on 18 February. By then, every compromise had failed. Crittenden's plan fell when Lincoln told Republicans to 'entertain no proposition' on extending slavery, and the Peace Conference's plan was ignored. A stand-off between a federal fort and a Confederate state could end only in surrender or fighting. Behind this lay a clash of positions: Lincoln held that the Union was 'perpetual' and Davis that a state could leave it. These positions came from deeper causes: in Mississippi's words, the South's position was 'thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery', and the election of 1860 had convinced the Deep South that it could no longer protect it.

Sumter was nevertheless the point at which a political dispute became a war, and that is its importance. Until 12 April, Virginia, Tennessee, Arkansas and North Carolina had voted to stay in the Union, hoping for a settlement; the border states stayed neutral. It was the firing on a fort flying the national flag, and Lincoln's call for volunteers to put down an 'insurrection', that made the Upper South choose. Virginia voted to secede on 17 April, only two days later. Lincoln and Davis each made a choice: Lincoln to supply the fort 'with provisions only', which put the next step in Davis's hands, and Davis to open fire before the supplies came. A different decision by either might have delayed war, as the Confederate cabinet's one dissenter, Toombs, had warned.

Overall, the attack on Fort Sumter was the occasion for the outbreak of war and the reason it began in April, but it was not the main reason. The war broke out because the Union had already split over slavery, because the election of Lincoln convinced the Deep South that slavery was unsafe inside it, and because neither side would give up its position that the Union was either perpetual or dissolved. Sumter turned these conflicts into fighting, and made it a war of eleven states rather than seven."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (what turned a political dispute into war, and what made one possible) and distinguishes the trigger from the causes. With an opening paragraph on the case for Sumter, this is Level 5 (19–20). Note how "the main reason for the outbreak" is tested: the best answers separate why secession happened, why war followed and why it began in April.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: To what extent was the Dred Scott judgement a turning point in the road to the Civil War?
**What the levels want.** The same levels as above: Level 3 explains one side, Level 4 explains both, Level 5 reaches a comparative judgement.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Turning point: it closed the legal road to compromise (Congress cannot ban slavery in territories); it proved the Slave Power to Northern Republicans; it split the Democrats (Freeport); it made the Republican platform unconstitutional in the Court's eyes.
- Not a turning point: tension was already high after 1854 (Kansas–Nebraska, Bleeding Kansas, Brooks–Sumner); few enslaved people lived in the territories so the practical effect was small; it was reversed in practice by Northern defiance; later events (John Brown's raid, the 1860 election) had a more direct effect on secession.
- Criterion: did it change the direction of events or only confirm them?

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Dred Scott judgement of 1857 was a turning point because it said that Black Americans were not citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories. This made the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional. Many Northerners were angry because it seemed to prove that the Supreme Court was on the side of slaveholders, and Lincoln said that it was 'erroneous'. The South was pleased because it meant slavery could spread. This made the North and South even more divided and was an important turning point."

*Commentary.* Accurate and explains one side, but it never tests the claim "turning point" against other events. Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs.**

"The case for a turning point is strong in one respect: it changed the constitutional ground. Until 6 March 1857 Congress's power to bar slavery from the territories, used in 1820 and claimed by the Wilmot Proviso, was an open question; after it, the Republican Party's central aim was, in the Court's view, unconstitutional. A party that wanted to stop the spread of slavery now had to defy the highest court or change it, and Lincoln chose to say, at Springfield in June 1857, that the party would work to reverse it. The decision also exposed the weakness in Douglas's popular sovereignty. If Congress could not ban slavery, could settlers? Lincoln's question at Freeport in 1858 forced Douglas to give the answer that cost him the South, and so helped to split the Democrats in 1860, which in turn helped Lincoln to win.

However, it is easy to overstate. Tension was already extreme by 1857: the Kansas–Nebraska Act had broken the Missouri Compromise itself in 1854, Kansas was in a state of civil war, and Sumner had been beaten unconscious in the Senate in 1856. The judgement removed a law that Congress had already repealed, and its practical effect was small, since few enslaved people lived in the western territories. Its importance was in what both sides *believed* it meant: for Northerners, confirmation of the Slave Power; for Southerners, a vindication. It also did not change the actions of governments directly: no territory was opened to slavery as a result. The events that led straight to secession were later ones: John Brown's raid in 1859, which convinced many Southerners the North would tolerate a rising, and the election of 1860.

Overall, Dred Scott was a turning point in the way the quarrel was argued and in the politics of the Democrats and Republicans, but not in the sense of changing direction. It confirmed a path already taken after 1854 and added fuel, and it removed the last legal basis for compromise on the territories. The decisive turning points came in 1859–60, when fear in the South of an anti-slavery government became real."

*Commentary.* Both sides analysed with precise evidence, a criterion (did it change direction or confirm it) and a clear comparative judgement. Level 5 (18–19).

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

For the last ten years we have had numerous and serious causes of complaint against our non-slaveholding confederate States with reference to the subject of African slavery. The prohibition of slavery in the Territories is the cardinal principle of the Republican party, and the majority of the people of the North demand that we shall receive them as our rulers.

*Adapted from the Declaration of Causes of Georgia's secession, approved by the state convention at Milledgeville, 29 January 1861. Shortened; the phrases in the text are the declaration's own.*

**Source B**

Our position is thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery, the greatest material interest of the world. Its labor supplies the product which constitutes by far the largest and most important portions of commerce of the earth.

*From the Declaration of the Immediate Causes which Induce and Justify the Secession of the State of Mississippi from the Federal Union, January 1861. Spelling modernised.*

**Source C**

I have no purpose, directly or indirectly, to interfere with the institution of slavery in the States where it exists. In your hands, my dissatisfied fellow-countrymen, and not in mine, is the momentous issue of civil war. The Government will not assail you. You can have no conflict without being yourselves the aggressors.

*Adapted from Abraham Lincoln's First Inaugural Address, Washington, 4 March 1861. Shortened.*

**Source D**

The new constitution has put at rest, forever, all the agitating questions relating to our peculiar institution, African slavery as it exists amongst us. This was the immediate cause of the late rupture and present revolution. Our new government rests upon the great truth that the negro is not equal to the white man; that slavery, subordination to the superior race, is his natural and normal condition.

*Adapted from a speech by Alexander H. Stephens, Vice-President of the Confederate States, at Savannah, Georgia, 21 March 1861. Stephens spoke without a prepared text; the speech was taken down by a reporter and printed in newspapers, and Stephens later said that the report was imperfect.*

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that the defence of slavery was the main reason for the secession of the Southern states?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (slavery in the Territories and the Republicans), B (the state's position "thoroughly identified" with slavery), D (slavery as the "immediate cause" and the base of the new government). Challenge or qualify: C (Lincoln says slavery in the states is not under threat, so the *defence* of slavery in the sense of protecting it where it existed was not needed; this suggests the cause was fear of the future, power or the Union's break); A (the real grievance is the *Territories* and Republican rule, not existing slavery); D (Stephens speaks of a settled constitution, after the event, not of the decision to leave).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: an official statement adopted by a convention to explain secession; reliable as evidence of what the convention said was its reason, but its purpose was to justify the step to its people and the world, so it picks the strongest legal and moral points. It is notable that it is so frank about slavery. Its reference to ten years of complaints shows a long grievance, as the Compromise of 1850 and Kansas–Nebraska would suggest.
- **B**: a similar official statement, opening bluntly. Its purpose is to justify secession; its claim that slavery was 'the greatest material interest of the world' reflects the importance of cotton (almost 60 per cent of exports), but it also uses economic terms to hide the human cost. It shows how a Deep South planter state saw its position.
- **C**: spoken by Lincoln to reassure the Upper South: its purpose is to prevent secession spreading. It tells us what the Union leader promised, not why the South left. That the promise did not prevent secession is itself evidence: the South did not leave because of a threat to existing slavery but because of what the Republican victory meant for slavery's future.
- **D**: a political speech to a Georgian audience, an off-the-cuff statement from the Confederate vice-president that the Confederacy was founded on the principle of racial hierarchy. It is vivid, from the man who held the second office, and the nearest we have to the founders' own view. But it was improvised and recorded imperfectly, and Stephens later disputed parts of the report; it is also a speech celebrating a new government, so it may exaggerate.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A, B and D agree that slavery was central, and they support the view in different ways. Georgia and Mississippi name slavery in their own declarations of cause: for Georgia the issue is the Republicans' aim to stop its spread, for Mississippi it is the state's whole position. Stephens goes further, saying that the Confederacy rests on slavery. The three sources, written by official bodies and by the Confederate vice-president within three months, are strong evidence because they come from the leaders who decided on secession, and because they were not trying to hide the issue. Source C qualifies this. If Lincoln's pledge not to touch slavery in the states was sincere, as his record in 1858–60 suggests, then the 'defence' of slavery in the narrow sense, protecting it where it existed, was not why states left. They left because the Republicans would control the government and prevent the spread of slavery, which Southern leaders believed would doom it in the long run. That reading is consistent with Georgia's 'cardinal principle' and with the fact that the Upper South, which had fewer enslaved people and was less sure of the danger, stayed until Fort Sumter. The sources therefore agree to a large extent: the defence of slavery, understood as its security and its growth, was the main reason for secession. The sources do not say that it was the only reason, since Source C shows that some matters were about power and union rather than slavery as such, and the Upper South's secession in April, which came from Lincoln's call for volunteers, was not mainly about slavery at all."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and context; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "'States' rights were the main reason the Southern states seceded.' How far do you agree?" [20]

A student wrote:

"States' rights were the main reason the Southern states seceded. The South believed that the states had made the Union and could leave it. They thought the federal government had too much power. South Carolina was the first to secede, on 20 December 1860, and then Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana and Texas followed. In February 1861 they made the Confederate States of America with Jefferson Davis as president. This shows that the states' rights were the most important reason the South left the Union."

**The level.** Level 2 (5–8). Accurate dates and a clear opening claim, but the paragraph never *tests* it: it asserts states' rights, then lists the order of secession, which does not show why the states left. It uses no evidence about what the states said, and no alternative perspective.

**The fix.** Start with the claim, then use the secession documents as evidence for it and against it. "States' rights was the doctrine the South used to justify secession, but the secession documents name slavery as the reason. South Carolina's declaration complains of Northern states that failed to return fugitives and of a president 'hostile to slavery', and Mississippi's states that its position is 'thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery'. Even the doctrine of states' rights was applied unevenly: the South demanded a federal Fugitive Slave Act in 1850 that overrode Northern states, and in 1860 a federal slave code. So states' rights was the method that the Deep South used, and slavery was the reason." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Why was *Uncle Tom's Cabin* important?
A: Published in March 1852 after the Fugitive Slave Act, it sold about 300,000 copies in the United States in its first year, made Northerners feel the human cost of slavery and turned moderate opinion against it. Southerners denounced it and wrote "anti-Tom" novels.
Q: What did John Brown try to do at Harpers Ferry, and how did each section react?
A: On 16–18 October 1859 he seized the federal arsenal hoping to start a slave rising; he failed and was hanged on 2 December. Many in the North saw him as a martyr; in the South his raid and Northern praise for him confirmed the fear that the North would back a slave revolt.
Q: What did the Dred Scott judgement (1857) decide?
A: By seven votes to two, that Black Americans could not be citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories, which made the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional.
Q: What do the terms "states' rights" and "Slave Power" mean, and which section used each?
A: States' rights: the doctrine that the states, not the federal government, hold the main power, used by Southerners to defend slavery and secession. Slave Power: the Northern belief that a group of Southern slaveholders controlled the federal government.
Q: Who were the four candidates in 1860 and which won each part of the country?
A: Lincoln (Republican) won the free states; Douglas (Northern Democrat) won only Missouri and part of New Jersey; Breckinridge (Southern Democrat) won the Deep South and four other slave states; Bell (Constitutional Union) won Virginia, Kentucky and Tennessee.
Q: Why did the Crittenden Compromise fail?
A: It would have extended the 36°30′ line and protected slavery south of it in all present and future territory. Lincoln told Republicans not to accept any compromise on the extension of slavery, so they killed it in Congress, and the Deep South was already leaving.
Q: Which states seceded before Lincoln's inauguration, and which after Fort Sumter?
A: Before: South Carolina, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana and Texas. After Sumter and the call for volunteers: Virginia, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina.
Q: What happened at Fort Sumter, and why did it lead to war?
A: On 12 April 1861 Confederate batteries bombarded the federal fort in Charleston harbour and Anderson surrendered on 13 April. Lincoln then called for 75,000 volunteers, which led the Upper South to join the Confederacy.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry in 1859 increased tension between North and South.
A: Indicative content: Brown's attempt to start a slave rising confirmed the Southern fear of slave revolt, which had been present since Nat Turner (1831); the discovery that wealthy Northerners had funded him seemed proof of a Northern conspiracy; Northern admiration for Brown after his trial and execution (bells, meetings, writers' praise) shocked Southerners and seemed to show Northern contempt for the Constitution; Republican leaders had to disown him, but Democrats used the raid against them; fire-eaters used it to argue for secession. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: the raid played on existing fears, Northern praise made the fear seem justified, and so each side's reaction made the other more hostile. It ends with a judgement on the main reason, e.g. Northern praise for Brown mattered more than the raid itself.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) had such an impact on opinion in the North and the South.
A: Indicative content: the novel was published in the year after the Fugitive Slave Act, which had made slavery a Northern concern; it was written as a story, which made abstract arguments human and readable (about 300,000 copies in the first year, plays reaching more people still); it showed families separated, a cruel owner, a brave escape; the South saw it as a slander on a whole society and answered with "anti-Tom" novels, so the argument hardened. Level 4 connects the timing, the form and the Southern reaction and judges which was most important, e.g. its form (a novel) explains the reach, while its timing explains the effect.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far did the Dred Scott judgement of 1857 strengthen the belief in a "Slave Power" in the North?
A: For: the Court's majority included five Southern justices; it said Congress could not ban slavery in the territories and made the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional; Buchanan was believed to have pressed a justice to join the majority; Lincoln's House Divided speech presented the decision as part of a plan by Douglas, Pierce, Taney and Buchanan; Republicans used it in 1858 and 1860. Against: belief in a Slave Power already existed from the Fugitive Slave Act and Kansas–Nebraska; the case did not lead to slavery in any new territory; some Northern Democrats supported the Court. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that Dred Scott confirmed and gave a legal form to a belief already strongly held, rather than creating it.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the election of Lincoln the main reason for the secession of the Deep South?
A: For: South Carolina seceded within six weeks; seven states left before he took office; Georgia's and South Carolina's declarations cite the election of a president "hostile to slavery"; no Southern electoral votes; fire-eaters had promised secession in this case. Against: the long-term role of slavery; the fear of Republican policy rather than Lincoln's promises (he said he would not touch slavery in the states); Harpers Ferry, Dred Scott and Kansas; the Democratic split that made his victory possible; the failure of the Crittenden plan. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "what gave the Deep South both a reason and the means to leave in 1860–61?", and judges, e.g., that the election was the occasion, while slavery and the loss of political power were the cause.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the Dred Scott decision.
A: Similarities: both concern the Dred Scott judgement and the limits of what Congress and the Court could do about slavery; both speak with confidence and in a legal register. Differences: A (Taney) justifies the decision and claims it describes the framers' view that Black people had no rights; B (Lincoln) rejects the decision and says the Republicans will work to overturn it. Explanation: A is the Chief Justice, a former slaveholder, writing the opinion of the Court (the nature is judicial, the purpose to justify the ruling and settle the question); B is a politician out of office, speaking in Illinois to build the Republican case against the Slave Power, so the purpose is political. Level 4 explains the differences through nature and purpose and judges which is better evidence of what the decision meant for the nation, e.g. A of what the majority believed, B of how the North would respond.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources support the view that by 1860 compromise between North and South had become impossible?
H: Which sources show a hard line (A, C, D) and which a willingness to challenge a law without breaking it (B)?
H: Use your own knowledge of the Crittenden plan and the 1860 election to judge.
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: A shows the highest court declaring Black people without rights and the Missouri Compromise unconstitutional, closing the legal route to compromise; C shows a man willing to use violence, with a belief that only "blood" could end slavery; D shows even an abolitionist editor condemning the raid, but only as "insane", which shows how divisive it was. Challenge: B shows Lincoln, a leader of the Republicans, planning to change the law by lawful means, not by force; D shows the leading Northern abolitionist condemning the raid, so the North was not simply behind Brown; C is the act of one man, not a party. Evaluation with knowledge: A is an official judgement, but Taney was describing past opinion to make a legal point; B was a campaign speech and a summary; C is a private note by a man about to die; D was written on 21 October, before the North's opinion of Brown changed after his trial. Level 5 judges how far: the sources show that the middle ground was shrinking, but they also show that Northern leaders sought to change the law by lawful means and that Brown was condemned at first, so compromise was not yet impossible in 1859; the decisive step was the 1860 election and the Deep South's response.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was the failure of the Crittenden Compromise due to Lincoln and the Republicans?
H: Set a criterion: what would have been needed for a compromise to work?
H: Consider both the Republicans' position and the Deep South's already moving to secession.
A: For: Lincoln told Republicans (11 December 1860) to "entertain no proposition" on extension; Republicans killed it in committee; the plan would have needed Republican votes; the Republicans were a minority who refused to abandon the principle on which they had been elected. Against: the Deep South was already leaving and had not asked for the compromise; the plan, which extended the line to future territory, would have encouraged filibustering for Cuba or Mexico; Southern leaders had stated that they wanted federal protection of slavery in all territories; Buchanan did nothing; the Upper South mattered but the plan did not reach it in time. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "who had the power to make it work?", and judges that Republican refusal was necessary for failure but the Deep South's movement made success unlikely anyway.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was Abraham Lincoln responsible for the outbreak of war at Fort Sumter?
H: Separate what Lincoln did (the decision to resupply "with provisions only") from what Davis did (the decision to fire first).
H: Ask what alternatives Lincoln had: could he have evacuated the fort, and what would that have meant for the Union?
A: Lincoln's responsibility: he refused to recognise secession and to give up Sumter; his notice to Pickens put the next step in Confederate hands; some historians argue he expected the Confederates to fire first, which would unite the North. Against: Lincoln had no lawful way to recognise secession; his inaugural promised to hold federal property but not to attack; the supply mission carried no troops; Davis and the Confederate cabinet voted to fire, with only Toombs dissenting; the Confederacy had demanded Sumter since December; Seward and others had offered to give up the fort in return for Virginia's loyalty, which Lincoln refused. A* judgement: Lincoln chose the policy that made a clash possible, but he chose a policy that offered peace if the Confederates did nothing; Davis chose to start the war, so responsibility for the shooting belongs to Davis while responsibility for the stand-off was shared.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How reliable is Alexander Stephens's Savannah speech of 21 March 1861 as evidence of why the Southern states seceded?
H: Who was Stephens, and what did he say that the secession documents do not?
H: What do we know about how the speech was recorded?
A: Strengths: Stephens was the Confederate vice-president, so it was a leader speaking for the new government; it agrees with the secession declarations (Mississippi: "thoroughly identified with the institution of slavery"; Georgia: slavery in the Territories); it states plainly what other documents wrap in legal language. Weaknesses: he was speaking without a text, to a friendly crowd, to celebrate the new constitution and to persuade; the text comes from newspaper reports and he later said the report was imperfect; he speaks for the government's founding principles, not for ordinary voters or for the Upper South, which seceded for different reasons after Sumter; and he had himself opposed secession in Georgia's convention. Judgement: reliable evidence that Confederate leaders saw slavery as the foundation of their new state, less reliable for the motives of all Southerners or for the exact wording.

:::recap
- **Abolitionism and secessionism**: Garrison, Douglass; *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (20 March 1852; about 300,000 copies in a year); Southern "anti-Tom" replies; fire-eaters.
- **John Brown**: Harpers Ferry, 16–18 October 1859, hanged 2 December 1859; Northern praise and Southern fear.
- **States' rights and Slave Power**: the South's doctrine, but the secession declarations name slavery; the North's fear of a Slave Power; Lincoln's House Divided.
- **Dred Scott (6 March 1857)**: 7–2; Black Americans not citizens; Congress cannot ban slavery in the territories; closed the legal road to compromise.
- **1860 election (6 November)**: Lincoln 39.8 per cent and 180 of 303 electoral votes, no Southern states; Douglas 29.4, Breckinridge 18.2, Bell 12.6.
- **Failure of compromise**: Crittenden (18 December 1860), Lincoln's refusal on extension, the Peace Conference (4–27 February 1861), the Corwin Amendment.
- **Secession**: South Carolina 20 December 1860, six more states by 1 February, Confederacy at Montgomery (Davis inaugurated 18 February); Lincoln's inaugural 4 March; Sumter 12–13 April; call for 75,000 volunteers 15 April; Virginia 17 April, Arkansas, Tennessee and North Carolina by 20 May; four border states stay.
- **Judgement**: slavery was the underlying cause; Lincoln's election the occasion for secession; Sumter and the call for volunteers the trigger that made it a war of eleven states.
