## What A Level History is, and who it suits

Cambridge International **AS & A Level History (9489)** is the history qualification used by schools and private candidates around the world. You study real periods in depth, from the French Revolution to the end of the Cold War, and you are examined in four ways: reading and comparing **sources** (Paper 1), writing **essays** on a whole topic (Papers 2 and 4), and analysing a **historian's interpretation** (Paper 3). There are no practicals, no coursework and no data to learn; you are marked on what you know, how you explain, and how you judge.

It suits students who like reading, who enjoy arguing a case, and who can write clearly in English under time pressure. It does not need any special mathematics or science. It is a respected qualification for university subjects such as history, law, politics, international relations, journalism and many others, because it trains the habit of weighing evidence and writing a balanced argument. You do not need IGCSE or O Level history to begin (Cambridge recommends it as prior study, but this course starts from zero in [History from zero: evidence, sources and arguments](#hist.f.basics)).

## The four papers

Everything in this table is from the Cambridge syllabus for 2027, 2028 and 2029.

| Paper | Title | Time | Marks | What you answer | Weight (AS) | Weight (A Level) | AOs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Historical Sources | 1 h 15 min | 40 | one two-part sources question, from one section (option) | 40% | 20% | AO1 25%, AO3 75% |
| 2 | Outline Study | 1 h 45 min | 60 | two two-part questions, from one section (option) | 60% | 30% | AO1 50%, AO2 50% |
| 3 | Historical Interpretations | 1 h 15 min | 40 | one question, on one extract, from one section (topic) | none | 20% | AO1 50%, AO4 50% |
| 4 | Depth Study | 1 h 45 min | 60 | two questions from a choice of three, on one section (option), 30 marks each | none | 30% | AO1 50%, AO2 50% |

:::key What each paper asks of you
1. **Paper 1** (sources): four sources on one topic. **(a)** [15] "Compare these two sources as evidence about …". **(b)** [25] "How far do these sources agree that …?", using all four sources and your own knowledge.
2. **Paper 2** (essays on an outline study): two questions, each with **(a)** [10] "Explain why …" and **(b)** [20] "To what extent …?" or "How far do you agree …?". You answer both parts of both questions.
3. **Paper 3** (interpretations): one extract from a historian, author not named: "What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it?" [40].
4. **Paper 4** (depth study): three questions per option, one on each topic, each [30], with the command words **Assess** and **Evaluate**. You answer two.
:::

What is on the papers, exactly. Every paper has the same front page: you write in an answer booklet, you are told how many sections to answer from (one section for Papers 1 and 3, one section for Papers 2 and 4), and the number of marks for each part is printed in brackets. Nothing else is supplied: no notes, texts or maps. Paper 1 prints the sources, each with a provenance line in italics; Paper 3 prints the extract. Sections for Papers 1, 2 and 4 are **A: European**, **B: American** and **C: International**; the sections of Paper 3 are the three topics.

## Routes: AS, staged A Level, or all at once

There are three ways through the qualification.

1. **AS only.** Papers 1 and 2, in one exam series. AS is graded a–e.
2. **Staged A Level.** Papers 1 and 2 (AS) in the first year, Papers 3 and 4 in the second. The AS marks carry forward to the A Level under the rules in the Cambridge Handbook.
3. **A Level all at once.** All four papers in one series. A Level is graded A*–E.

The exams are held in **June and November** each year. Cambridge recommends about **180 guided learning hours** for AS and about **360** for the A Level (these include teaching, practice and revision). In the USA new syllabuses replace 9489 from 2027: there, only students who took the AS in 2026 can finish the A Level (Papers 3 and 4) in June 2027. Check your school or exam centre for which routes and series they offer.

## The AS options and the 2027–29 rotation

At AS you choose **one option** (one section of Papers 1 and 2), and each option has **three topics**. You study **all three** topics. In any one year, one topic is on Paper 1 (sources) and the other two are on Paper 2 (essays). The topic on Paper 1 changes each year in a fixed rotation, so every topic is a source topic in one year and an essay topic in the other two. Learn all three topics for both kinds of question.

| Year | European: Paper 1 / Paper 2 | American: Paper 1 / Paper 2 | International: Paper 1 / Paper 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2027 | France / Germany, Russia | Gilded Age and Progressive Era / Civil War, Great Crash | Imperialism / 1919–29, 1929–39 |
| 2028 | Germany / France, Russia | Great Crash, Depression and New Deal / Civil War, Gilded Age | 1919–29 / Imperialism, 1929–39 |
| 2029 | Russia / France, Germany | Civil War and Reconstruction / Gilded Age, Great Crash | 1929–39 / Imperialism, 1919–29 |

The topics and their ids:

- **A European option: Modern Europe, 1774–1924.** France, 1774–1814. Liberalism and nationalism in Germany, 1815–71. Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924.
- **B American option: the USA, 1820–1941.** The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77. The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920. The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41.
- **C International option: international history, 1870–1939.** Imperialism and the emergence of world powers, c.1870–1918. International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation. International history, 1929–39: the rise of extremism and the road to war.

Each topic has **four key questions**, and this course has **one chapter per key question**. Each AS chapter teaches both the source question and the essay question for that content.

## Paper 3 and Paper 4

**Paper 3** has three topics (one section each). You study **one**. Each is two chapters: a **context** chapter (the events) and a **debate** chapter (the historians).

1. **The origins of the First World War**: "Who or what was to blame?" ([context](#hist.interp.ww1.1), [debate](#hist.interp.ww1.2))
2. **The Holocaust**: "Who or what was to blame?" ([context](#hist.interp.holocaust.1), [debate](#hist.interp.holocaust.2))
3. **The origins and development of the Cold War**: "Who or what was to blame?" ([context](#hist.interp.coldwar.1), [debate](#hist.interp.coldwar.2))

**Paper 4** has three options, each with three topics, and three questions per option (one on each topic). You answer two, so you need at least two topics of your option; studying all three gives you a real choice on the day.

- **A European option, Europe 1919–41:** Mussolini's Italy; Stalin's Russia; Hitler's Germany.
- **B American option, the USA 1945–93:** Truman, Eisenhower and post-war USA; a time of challenges, 1961–74; the USA, 1974–93.
- **C International option, 1909–94:** the Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953–91; end of minority rule in South Africa and Zimbabwe, 1948–94; the route to independence: Malaysia and Indonesia, c.1909–67.

## Why the syllabus is split by topic, and how this course joins it up

The syllabus is divided by country and topic for a simple reason: every exam question is set on **one** topic, so that you can prepare for it and the board can set a fair paper. The price is that the topics can feel like separate boxes. The same year can appear in several of them. In 1917, for instance, Russia's Bolsheviks seized power, the United States entered the First World War, and the French army mutinied.

The course joins the topics up in three ways. First, [History from zero (2): the big picture](#hist.f.eras) puts every topic on one time axis, in five eras, with the ideas and forces that crossed borders. Secondly, each topic and debate chapter ends with a **Connections** box that points to the other topics it links to: a cause, a consequence, a comparison. Thirdly, [Dictatorships compared](#hist.d.compare) lines up the three European dictators on the same questions. None of these is examined directly, but a Paper 2 or Paper 4 answer that says "the same hunger crisis that shook France in 1789 hit Russia in 1917" shows range and earns credit for analysis.

## Assessment objectives

Four **assessment objectives** (AOs) describe what is marked. Each paper tests a mix of them.

| AO | What it asks for | Where it is tested |
|---|---|---|
| AO1 | Historical knowledge: recall, select and use appropriate knowledge accurately | All four papers |
| AO2 | Historical explanation, analysis and judgement: cause and consequence, change and continuity, significance, connections, and reaching a judgement | Papers 2 and 4 (50% each) |
| AO3 | Historical sources: understand, analyse, evaluate and interpret sources in context | Paper 1 (75%) |
| AO4 | Historical interpretations: understand, explain and analyse how historians have interpreted and approached the past | Paper 3 (50%) |

Across the whole qualification the weightings are: **AS** AO1 40%, AO2 30%, AO3 30%, AO4 0%; **A Level** AO1 45%, AO2 30%, AO3 15%, AO4 10%.

## How the marks work: levels, not points

History is marked by **levels**, not by points for each fact. An examiner reads your answer and decides which level descriptor fits best, then chooses a mark within the level: the top mark if the answer meets the level convincingly, the middle if adequately, the bottom if it just meets it. Marks are positive; nothing is deducted. The descriptors differ by paper, and each paper guide lists them in full. In outline:

- **Paper 1 (a) [15]** has four levels: describing each source (Level 1, 1–3), a comparison with similarities **or** differences (Level 2, 4–7), similarities **and** differences supported from the sources (Level 3, 8–11), and a **developed comparison** that explains why they agree and differ, using context and source evaluation (Level 4, 12–15).
- **Paper 1 (b) [25]** has five levels. Level 3 (11–15) uses sources on both sides, with generic comments on bias; Level 4 (16–20) uses all four accurately and in context; Level 5 (21–25) **evaluates** the sources as stronger or weaker evidence and reaches a judgement on "how far".
- **Paper 2 (a) [10]** has four levels: describing a topic, describing factors, explaining factors (Level 3, 6–8), and **connecting** factors to reach a reasoned conclusion (Level 4, 9–10).
- **Paper 2 (b) [20]** has five levels. A single perspective explained reaches Level 3 (9–12); alternative perspectives explained reach Level 4 (13–16); Level 5 (17–20) adds a **clear comparative judgement**.
- **Paper 3 [40]** has two tables of 20 marks each: AO4 (the extract: its interpretation and approach, Levels 1–6) and AO1 (your knowledge). Level 6 requires complete understanding of the interpretation as a whole.
- **Paper 4 [30]** has two tables of 15 marks: analysis and judgement (AO2) and supporting knowledge (AO1), over five levels. Level 5 needs wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained argument and a supported judgement.

The sentence to take from this: in every paper, the step up from the middle levels to the top is **weighing** (comparing the strength of arguments, or of sources), not more facts.

:::exam Marks come from level descriptors, not from counting
There are no marks for "points" in History. A 12-point list of facts with no explanation stays in Level 2 or 3. Five carefully explained points, linked to the question and weighed against each other, can reach Level 5. The worked answers in every topic chapter show this level by level.
:::

## Study order and pace

A sensible order for one student:

1. **Foundations** (about a week). Read [History from zero: evidence, sources and arguments](#hist.f.basics) and [History from zero (2): the big picture](#hist.f.eras). Then [Skills: reading and comparing sources](#hist.skills.sources) and [Skills: writing the analytical essay](#hist.skills.essays).
2. **Your AS option**, topic by topic, **in order** (1, 2, 3, 4 within a topic). Each key-question chapter is planned at about 8–11 hours. Do a recall test at the start of each session on the previous chapter.
3. **After each topic**, write one Paper 2 (a) and one (b) answer on it, and one source question if it is the topic for Paper 1 in your exam year (see the rotation table). Mark your work against the levels.
4. **Revision for AS**: the paper guides, the mixed practice and mock papers for Papers 1 and 2.
5. **A Level only**: Paper 3 (the context chapter, then the debate chapter, and [How historians disagree](#hist.interp.skills)), and Paper 4 (two or three topics), then the Paper 3 and 4 guides and mocks.

How long? Cambridge's figure is about 180 hours for AS and about 360 for A Level. As a guide from this course: twelve AS chapters at 8–11 hours each is about 100–130 hours of reading, recall and written practice for an AS option, and the rest of the 180 is practice papers and revision. A Level adds a Paper 3 topic (the two chapters are about 18–22 hours) and a Paper 4 option (two or three topics of about four chapters each; the Paper 4 chapters run to about ten hours each).

:::tip A weekly rhythm that works
Five to seven hours a week for AS: two sessions of reading a chapter section by section (with the recall box at the start), one session for a written answer under time, and one short session testing yourself on the timeline and key people. Always write one timed answer a week. History is learned by writing.
:::

## How to reach A*

A* at A Level is not about knowing more than everybody; it is about doing a small number of things consistently on every answer.

:::key Ten habits of a top-level History student
1. **Answer the question set.** Read the command word and the focus words (Assess, Evaluate, Explain, Compare) before you plan. Put the focus in your first sentence.
2. **Know the detail.** Dates, names, figures, causes: relevant, precise and accurate. Detail is what makes an argument convincing.
3. **Explain; do not tell.** Every paragraph makes a point and explains how its evidence supports it.
4. **Weigh.** Say which cause, source or view is stronger, and **why**, using a stated criterion (necessity, scale, duration, links).
5. **Give both sides** on a Paper 2 (b), a Paper 4 essay or a Paper 1 (b), then a conclusion that **compares** them.
6. **Use the sources in context** on Paper 1. Evaluate nature, origin and purpose against what you know; do not write "biased" and stop.
7. **Read the extract as a whole** on Paper 3. Find the **overall** interpretation and the historian's approach; do not argue with the historian.
8. **Plan for two to three minutes** before each essay: a few words for each paragraph and the judgement.
9. **Practise under time**, and **mark yourself** against the level descriptors in the paper guides.
10. **Review errors.** For each timed answer, write one sentence on the level you reached and the single change that would take it up a level.
:::

:::warn Mistakes that cost the most
1. **A story, not an argument.** Narrative stays in Level 2.
2. **Only one side** on an "How far" question: the cap is Level 3.
3. **No judgement.** A conclusion that says "both are important" without comparing them stops at Level 4.
4. **Knowing every topic a little.** You answer on your own options only, so depth in them beats a thin cover of all of the course.
5. **Treating a source's date or author as the whole evaluation.** The question is what the source is evidence of.
:::

How to divide your time on the day is in each paper guide: for example, Paper 1 is 75 minutes for 40 marks, so about 25 minutes on part (a) [15] and about 45 minutes on part (b) [25] leaves a few minutes for reading the sources; Paper 2 is 105 minutes for two questions worth 30 marks each, so about 50 minutes per question. These are suggestions, not rules; practise until the pace feels natural.
