:::why
Appeasement is the most argued-over policy in British history. For Winston Churchill and the writers of *Guilty Men* it was a failure of nerve that let a dictator grow strong; for later historians it was a hard choice made by leaders who were weak, afraid of another war and not sure the public would follow them. Both sides agree on one fact: in September 1938 Britain and France, with Italy, agreed that a democratic state should hand over a third of its territory to Germany, and in March 1939 Hitler took the rest. To judge that decision you have to understand the pressures on the men who made it, and that is the skill the exam rewards: explaining *why* people acted as they did, and then weighing *how well* it worked.
:::

## What was appeasement?

In everyday English "to appease" means to calm someone down by giving way to them, and since the Second World War the word has meant weakness. In the 1930s it did not. To most people it meant something more respectable: settling disputes by negotiation and by removing the causes of complaint before they led to war.

:::def Appeasement
The policy, followed above all by Britain and France between about 1935 and March 1939, of avoiding war with Germany (and Italy) by negotiating and by giving way to some of their demands, in the hope that satisfying their grievances would make them peaceful. In the 1930s the word did not carry its modern meaning of weakness.
:::

Appeasement was not a single decision. It grew over several years and it was **British and French** policy, not British alone. Britain led, because she was the stronger partner and France was reluctant to act without her, but France had her own reasons, which are set out below.

Three features help you to describe it:
- **A mood before it was a policy.** Many British politicians, including the Prime Minister Stanley Baldwin (1935–37), believed that the Treaty of Versailles had been too harsh and that Germany had real grievances. Giving way on those grievances was thought fair as well as safe.
- **Chamberlain made it active.** When Neville Chamberlain became Prime Minister on 28 May 1937 he did not wait for Hitler to make demands. He wanted to go out and settle Europe's problems by personal diplomacy, with Germany and with Italy. This is why historians speak of Chamberlain's appeasement as a policy and Baldwin's as a drift.
- **It was meant to be conditional.** Appeasers did not plan to give Hitler whatever he wanted. They hoped to satisfy "reasonable" claims (Germans living in Germany's neighbours, the Rhineland, Austria) and to hold the line against unreasonable ones (domination of Europe). The tragedy is that Hitler's aims were not limited.

### Economic considerations: the Depression and the cost of rearming

The Great Depression hit Britain hard. Unemployment reached nearly 3 million in January 1933, about 22 per cent of insured workers, and the government that took office in the crisis of August 1931 (the National Government) came to power to balance the budget and defend the pound. In September 1931 Britain left the gold standard. The Chancellor of the Exchequer from November 1931 to May 1937 was Neville Chamberlain, and the Treasury under him saw spending on arms as a danger: it would raise taxes, add to the national debt and could wreck the recovery that was slowly beginning. The Treasury argued that a sound economy was itself a "fourth arm of defence", alongside the navy, army and air force, because a country that went bankrupt could not fight a long war.

:::def Ten-Year Rule
A rule first laid down by the British Cabinet in August 1919, under which the armed forces were told to plan on the assumption that Britain would not be involved in a major war for ten years. It was made a rolling rule in 1928 and was ended by the Cabinet on 23 March 1932.
:::

The Ten-Year Rule shows how the money went. For almost thirteen years the forces had been starved on the basis that there would be no war. Its end in March 1932 did not bring much new money: the Cabinet said at the same time that this must not be taken as a reason for increased spending, because of the poor financial and economic situation. Defence spending was about £102 million in 1932. It rose only slowly after Hitler became Chancellor: the Defence Requirements Committee of 1933–34 asked for about £71 million of new spending over five years and the Treasury cut that to £50 million. By the later 1930s the numbers were much larger, as the figures below show, but they came late and always had to be set against what Chamberlain's Treasury thought the country could afford.

In the chart, spending in 1938–39 is more than three times that of 1934–35. Two points follow. First, the build-up was real: the policy of appeasing Hitler did not mean doing nothing. Second, it happened at the speed the Treasury allowed, and critics such as Churchill said it was too slow to deter Germany in time. The economic reason for appeasement is therefore **not only** that Britain was poor. It is that Britain's leaders believed that a policy of rapid rearmament would hurt the economy and unsettle the public, and that if war could be avoided, avoiding it was cheaper than fighting.

### Military considerations: three enemies, not enough ships and the bomber

The military case was the one Chamberlain used most often. Three facts mattered.

1. **The empire was overstretched.** In 1937 the Chiefs of Staff warned the Cabinet that Britain could not fight Germany in Europe, Italy in the Mediterranean and Japan in the Far East at the same time. Japan had been at war with China since July 1937, and the fleet that would defend Singapore could not also defend the Mediterranean. The Chiefs of Staff therefore wanted diplomacy to reduce the number of Britain's enemies. This was a strong reason to try to keep Italy friendly (the talks that led Eden to resign) and to keep Germany from becoming an enemy at all.
2. **Britain was vulnerable to air attack.** In November 1932 Baldwin had told the Commons that "the bomber will always get through". Planners expected that a war would begin with a "knock-out blow" from the air on London, and the Air Ministry's own estimate in 1937 was of roughly 600,000 dead in the first two months of a war. The memory of the Spanish Civil War, where Guernica was bombed in April 1937, strengthened the fear. Britain's defences against the bomber (radar, modern fighters, anti-aircraft guns, shelters) were not ready until about 1938–39.
3. **The army was the smallest of the three services.** After 1918 Britain had not planned to send a large army to the Continent again; the "limited liability" policy kept spending on the army low until early 1939. Britain's help to France in 1939 would have been small compared with France's own forces.

The effect was to make the Chiefs of Staff cautious. In the Czech crisis of 1938 they told ministers that Britain could not save Czechoslovakia by force; they thought the best that could be done was to defeat Germany after a long war. A policy that bought time looked, from this angle, like prudence. The Dominions added to the caution. At the Imperial Conference of 1937 leaders such as Mackenzie King of Canada and Hertzog of South Africa made clear that their countries would not easily follow Britain into a war over central Europe. A British government that went to war without the Dominions risked splitting the empire.

### Social considerations: public opinion and the memory of 1914–18

Over 700,000 men from the United Kingdom had been killed in the First World War. In the 1920s and 1930s many books, plays and films described the war as a waste, and the strongest feeling in Britain was that it must never happen again. Two events show how that feeling appeared in politics.

The **Oxford Union debate** of 9 February 1933 considered the motion "That this House will in no circumstances fight for its King and Country". It was carried by 275 votes to 153. It was a student debate on a theme chosen for its shock value, and it was not a national referendum, but it shocked the newspapers and became a symbol: Churchill called the vote an "abject, squalid, shameless avowal". In October 1933 a Labour candidate who ran on a peace platform won a by-election in Fulham East, which many took as a sign that voters wanted peace more than rearmament.

The **Peace Ballot** was a more serious test. Between November 1934 and June 1935 the League of Nations Union, a pressure group that backed the League, organised a house-to-house vote, which about half a million volunteers canvassed. About 11.5 million people took part (the result was announced on 27 June 1935). Roughly 10 million backed Britain's staying in the League and roughly 10 million backed economic sanctions against an aggressor, but support for military measures was lower: about 6.8 million said yes (around 59 per cent of those voting) and about 2.4 million said no. The Ballot was not a vote for pacifism: it was a vote for collective security by all means short of war, with a hesitant willingness to use force. But governments read it two ways. It showed that the public would back firm words and sanctions, and that it would not easily back a war that was not clearly about defending Britain.

:::key What public opinion did, and did not, do
1. It made it politically hard for any government to rearm fast in 1933–35: Baldwin said later that a government that asked for rearmament in 1933 would have lost the election (he was defending his record after the event, so treat the claim with care).
2. It made it hard to threaten war over places most people had never heard of (Chamberlain's broadcast of 27 September 1938 speaks of "a quarrel in a far-away country").
3. It did not stop the government rearming from 1936, nor did it oppose resistance once Hitler broke his word in March 1939. Opinion was a constraint, not a cause that explains everything.
:::

### Ideas: fascism as a bulwark against Bolshevism, and sympathy for Nazi policies

The fourth pressure is the hardest to measure. Many Conservatives feared communism more than fascism. The Russian Revolution of 1917 and the Soviet seizure of power were not far in the past, there was a Communist Party in Britain, and the Spanish Civil War seemed to show a Communist threat on Europe's edge. To such people Germany looked like a barrier: a strong, anti-communist Germany would stand between the USSR and the West.

:::def Bulwark against Bolshevism
The idea, held by some British and French conservatives in the 1930s, that a strong fascist Germany (and Italy) would stop the spread of communism from the USSR into western Europe, and so should be tolerated or even helped.
:::

How far did this shape policy? The evidence is mixed and you should say so.
- **For:** Lord Londonderry (Secretary of State for Air until 1935) and Lord Lothian were among those who wanted friendship with Germany; an Anglo-German Fellowship was founded in 1935 by businessmen and peers; the *Daily Mail* owner Lord Rothermere praised the British Union of Fascists in January 1934 and admired Hitler. Chamberlain distrusted the Soviet Union deeply, and in 1938 the USSR was kept out of the Czech negotiations and not invited to Munich. When Halifax visited Hitler in November 1937 he said that Britain might accept peaceful changes in Danzig, Austria and Czechoslovakia (this is why the visit is often cited).
- **Against:** Chamberlain and his advisers did not like Nazi methods: Chamberlain condemned in private the persecution of the Jews after the pogrom of 9–10 November 1938 (*Kristallnacht*). Their main motive for appeasing Hitler was to avoid war, not to help him. Fear of communism explains why they preferred not to ally with the USSR; it does not by itself explain why they negotiated with Germany, since a policy of resisting both was possible.
- **Sympathy for Nazi policies.** A smaller group (some of them in the press and in high society) admired Nazi order and thought Hitler's anti-Semitism a German affair. They were a minority, and not a decisive voice in government.

:::warn Avoid two easy mistakes
Do not write that Britain appeased Hitler "because the British government was pro-Nazi". Chamberlain's government wanted peace; the evidence for sympathy is for a minority. And do not write "Britain appeased because she was weak" without explaining: she was weak in particular ways (air defence, Singapore, the Treasury's view of the economy) and not weaker than Germany in every respect. A precise reason scores higher than a general one.
:::

:::key Why Britain appeased: four pressures
1. **Economic**: the Depression, the Treasury's priority of a sound economy (the "fourth arm of defence") and slow rearmament.
2. **Military**: three potential enemies, fear of bombing, a small army and doubt about the Dominions.
3. **Social**: the memory of 1914–18, the Oxford debate (1933) and the Peace Ballot (1934–35).
4. **Ideological**: fear of communism, the idea of Germany as a bulwark, a feeling that Versailles was unfair, and a (small) sympathy for Nazi order.
Chamberlain's own character and judgement (his confidence that he could deal with Hitler) turned these pressures into a policy.
:::

## Why France pursued appeasement

France was Germany's neighbour and had lost about 1.4 million dead in the First World War, so she had more to fear than Britain from a revived Germany. Yet she did not stop Hitler either. Her reasons were of a different kind from Britain's.

### Economic problems

The Depression reached France later than Britain and Germany and lasted longer: while Britain recovered from 1933, French production was still weak in 1935 and 1936. France stayed on the gold standard until 1936, which kept the franc overvalued and French goods dear on world markets, and governments tried to cut spending to defend the currency, which lowered incomes and caused more discontent. Money for rearmament was short, and the arguments over how to find it were bitter. When the Popular Front took power in 1936 (below) it raised wages and cut the working week to 40 hours; many employers and army officers said this reduced output at the moment when France needed more weapons, and Blum devalued the franc in September 1936. The effect was that France was neither rich enough to rearm fast nor sure that rearmament was fair.

### Political divisions

The Third Republic had weak governments: ministries rose and fell every few months and parliament was split among many parties. On 6 February 1934 right-wing leagues rioted outside the Chamber of Deputies after a financial scandal (the Stavisky affair); about fifteen people were killed, and the government fell. The left answered by uniting. For the next four years French politics was a quarrel between left and right so fierce that foreign policy was often judged by which side it helped.

:::def Popular Front
The alliance of Socialists, Radicals and (in support) Communists that won the French general election of April–May 1936. Its leader, the Socialist Léon Blum, became Prime Minister in June 1936, the first Socialist and the first Jew to hold the post.
:::

The **elections of 1936** matter in two ways. Between the German march into the Rhineland (7 March) and the election (26 April and 3 May), France had a caretaker government that would not take a big risk before voters went to the polls. And the Popular Front's victory frightened the right: some conservatives said that the real danger was not Hitler but communism, so that a war would end in a revolution. Blum's government was also divided about Spain: it first planned to help the Spanish Republic and then, fearing civil war in France and the loss of British support, proposed the Non-Intervention Agreement (see [chapter 2 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.2)). Blum's government fell in June 1937; the governments that followed moved to the centre and then to the right.

### The threat of communism

France had a large Communist Party that supported the Popular Front after 1935. A Franco-Soviet Pact was signed on 2 May 1935, but the French military doubted that the Red Army would be useful, and many politicians feared that an alliance would bring Communist influence into France. French leaders also did not trust the USSR to fight, since Poland and Romania, which lay between, would not let Soviet troops cross. So fear of communism pushed France in two directions at once: to keep the Soviet pact on paper, and to avoid doing anything that would make France dependent on Moscow.

### French Grand Strategy and the Maginot Line

After 1918 the French army was built to protect France from invasion with as little loss of life as possible. France had a smaller population than Germany (about 42 million, against about 66 million in Germany in 1933) and had lost a generation of young men. Her answer was the **Maginot Line**.

:::def Maginot Line
A chain of concrete forts, tunnels and gun positions built along France's frontier with Germany from about 1929–30, named after André Maginot, the Minister of War who won funding for it. It did not cover the Belgian frontier, where Belgium's neutrality (declared in October 1936) was meant to give protection.
:::

The Maginot Line shaped French thinking in three ways. It encouraged a **defensive** habit of mind: the army prepared to fight a defensive war behind concrete, and had few forces ready to march into Germany and few that could take the offensive quickly, as would have been needed to help Poland or Czechoslovakia. It made France **depend on Britain**: the French general staff, led by General Maurice Gamelin, said in March 1936 that France could not act against the Rhineland without mobilising, and the government would not mobilise without a promise of British support. And it gave a false sense of security: if the frontier was safe, some argued, there was less need to fight for allies in eastern Europe such as Czechoslovakia, where France had treaty obligations (the Franco-Czechoslovak treaty of 1924). In 1938 Bonnet and others in the cabinet looked for ways to avoid keeping this promise.

:::key Why France appeased: four reasons
1. **Economic**: the late and long slump, the franc, and quarrels over who should pay for rearmament.
2. **Political**: weak governments, the violent left–right split after February 1934, and elections in 1936 that divided opinion further.
3. **Fear of communism**: the Popular Front and a large Communist Party made many on the right more afraid of revolution than of Hitler.
4. **Strategic**: a defensive army, the Maginot Line, a smaller population and a heavy reliance on Britain, so that France would not move without her.
:::

## Actions taken to appease Hitler, 1935–39

### The response to German rearmament and the Naval Agreement, 1935

From the day he took power in January 1933, Hitler rearmed in secret, and the League's Disarmament Conference failed (see [chapter 1 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.1)). On 16 March 1935 he threw off the pretence: he announced conscription, with a peacetime army of 36 divisions (about 550,000 men), and revealed that Germany had an air force, the *Luftwaffe*. Each of these broke the Treaty of Versailles. The British government's reaction was to send Sir John Simon, the Foreign Secretary, to Berlin on 25–26 March, where Hitler insisted on the right to a large air force. At Stresa in April, Britain, France and Italy condemned the breach, but only in words: they said they would "oppose" any treaty repudiation, but they made no plan for what to do.

Then, on **18 June 1935**, Britain signed the **Anglo-German Naval Agreement**. The German navy would be limited to 35 per cent of the Royal Navy's total tonnage, and Germany could build submarines up to 45 per cent of British submarine tonnage. The British navy wanted it. It fixed a limit on German naval building, at a time when Britain feared that a naval race, like the one before 1914, would again lead to war. British officials also hoped that Hitler's agreement would show that he would stick to treaties.

From France and Italy's point of view it was a betrayal. Britain had signed a treaty that **accepted Germany's right to rearm** two months after joining a protest against rearmament, and without telling her Stresa partners. France said she had been treated as a junior partner; Mussolini saw that Britain valued Hitler's goodwill more than the Stresa Front. That autumn he invaded Abyssinia. In terms of appeasement the lesson for Hitler was the same as the lesson of March: a breach of the treaty could be turned into a bargain.

### The Rhineland, 7 March 1936

The Treaty of Versailles had made the left bank of the Rhine, and a strip 50 kilometres wide on the right bank, a **demilitarised zone** where Germany could keep no troops, and at Locarno (1925) Germany had freely accepted this, with Britain and Italy as guarantors. On Saturday 7 March 1936, Hitler sent German troops into the zone. About 22,000 men entered (estimates vary), though only three battalions crossed to the west bank of the Rhine. He had picked his moment with care: Italy was at war in Abyssinia and the League was quarrelling over sanctions; France was in an election campaign; and he offered, in the same speech, a 25-year non-aggression pact and a return to the League. It was a gamble: Hitler's orders were reportedly that the troops should withdraw if the French moved, and he later said that the 48 hours after the march were the most nerve-racking of his life.

**Why nothing was done:**
- **Britain.** Ministers did not think the Rhineland was worth a war. Many agreed that Germany had the right to garrison her own territory, and Britain had no army to send. The public mood, shown by the Peace Ballot eight months earlier, was against a war on a technicality. Foreign Secretary Eden called the march a breach of the treaties but argued that it was not a threat to peace, and Britain asked for negotiations. The British government did hold staff talks with France and Belgium, but it would not promise military action.
- **France.** The government of Albert Sarraut and the Foreign Minister Pierre-Étienne Flandin said that France would not leave Strasbourg under German guns, but the general staff said that the army was designed for defence and that any move needed general mobilisation. With a general election weeks away and no promise from Britain, the government did nothing. It asked the League Council, which met in London and condemned the breach, but Germany ignored it.

:::def Remilitarisation
The return of armed forces to a zone where a treaty had forbidden them. In March 1936 Germany remilitarised the Rhineland, which the Treaty of Versailles and the Locarno agreements had demilitarised.
:::

The effect was large. Belgium, doubting that France or Britain would defend her, left her alliance with France and declared neutrality in October 1936, which left a gap in France's defences. Germany began building the Westwall (the "Siegfried Line") and made it harder for France to attack Germany if she helped her eastern allies. Hitler's prestige in Germany rose, and the generals who had counselled caution had been proved wrong. The countries of eastern Europe, such as Czechoslovakia and Poland, saw that France would not act against Germany without Britain, and that Britain would not act at all. Historians often call March 1936 the last real chance to stop Hitler cheaply, although others say it came too early for Britain and France to be ready for war, and that the French army was not in a position to do it fast.

### The Anschluss, March 1938

Hitler was born in Austria and wanted to unite Austria and Germany, an *Anschluss* ("union"). The treaties of 1919 forbade it. In 1934 Austrian Nazis killed the Austrian Chancellor, Dollfuss, and Mussolini moved troops to the Brenner Pass to warn Hitler off; by 1938 Mussolini had drawn close to Hitler and no longer protected Austria. On 12 February 1938 Hitler summoned the new Austrian Chancellor, Kurt von Schuschnigg, to Berchtesgaden and demanded that Nazis be given posts in the Austrian government. Schuschnigg gave way, then on 9 March announced a plebiscite for 13 March on Austrian independence. Hitler decided he could not risk a vote he might lose. On 11 March, under German pressure, Schuschnigg resigned, and the Nazi Arthur Seyss-Inquart became Chancellor and invited German troops to enter. They crossed the frontier on 12 March and Hitler drove in triumph through Linz and then Vienna. On 13 March Austria was declared part of the Reich and in a plebiscite on 10 April, held under Nazi control, the official vote for union was over 99 per cent.

**The response.** Britain and France sent protests. There was no military response, for four reasons:
- Britain had said, in effect, that she would not fight for Austria. In February 1938 Chamberlain told the Commons that small states must not be led to think that the League would protect them when it could not, and Halifax had told Hitler in November 1937 that Britain might accept changes in Austria if they came about peacefully.
- France had no government for the key days: the ministry of Chautemps resigned on 10 March and Blum formed a new one on 13 March, after the troops had crossed the frontier.
- Mussolini, the former protector, accepted the change, and Hitler thanked him.
- There was a feeling that the Austrians were Germans and many welcomed the union. Many crowds in Vienna did, in fact, cheer the arrival of German troops (although Nazi opponents, socialists and Jews were arrested at once, and persecution of Jews in Vienna began within days).

The USSR proposed on 17 March a conference of the great powers to discuss how to resist further aggression. Chamberlain refused on 24 March, saying that it would divide Europe into blocs and that it was premature. He also told the Commons that Britain would not make new commitments to Czechoslovakia, though France and perhaps others might be drawn in if war began. The effect of the Anschluss was to surround Czechoslovakia on three sides and to give Germany a population of about 7 million, an army to absorb, Austrian gold and foreign currency, and iron ore.

### The Sudetenland crisis, 1938

Czechoslovakia was a democracy created in 1918 with its frontier in the mountains along the German border, whose fortifications were among the strongest in Europe. Its army of about 35 divisions was well equipped, with weapons made in the Škoda works, one of the biggest arms factories in Europe. It had treaties with France (1924) and the USSR (1935); the Soviet treaty promised help only if France acted first. About 3.2 million of its 14.7 million citizens (22 per cent, in the 1930 census) were Germans, living mostly in the border regions known as the **Sudetenland**. The Depression had hit their industries hard, and many felt like second-class citizens. A Nazi-leaning party, the Sudeten German Party under Konrad Henlein, won most of their votes in 1935 and in April 1938 demanded self-government at Karlsbad. Henlein's task, as Hitler told him, was to ask for more than Prague could give.

:::def Sudetenland
The border regions of Bohemia and Moravia, inside Czechoslovakia, where most of the country's roughly 3.2 million Germans lived. It had never been part of Germany; the name was used for it after 1918.
:::

**The story, step by step.**
1. **May 1938.** On 20–22 May reports of German troop movements led Czechoslovakia to mobilise part of her army. Britain and France warned Germany, and Hitler backed down. He was furious at being seen to retreat, and on 30 May he issued a secret directive saying that he meant to smash Czechoslovakia by military action in the near future, with the army to be ready by 1 October.
2. **Summer 1938.** Chamberlain sent a businessman and former minister, Lord Runciman, to mediate. Runciman reported that the Sudeten Germans had real grievances and that the border areas should pass to Germany. Britain pressed Czechoslovakia to accept a deal, and kept urging France to do the same.
3. **12 September.** At the Nuremberg rally Hitler attacked the Czechs. Riots in the Sudetenland followed and the Czech government declared martial law.
4. **Berchtesgaden, 15 September.** Chamberlain, aged 69, flew to Munich and then travelled on to Hitler's mountain home. Hitler demanded that areas with a German majority be transferred to Germany. Chamberlain said he would take this back to his Cabinet and the French. On 18–19 September Britain and France told Czechoslovakia to hand over the areas with more than 50 per cent Germans, and on 21 September the Czech government, left without allies, gave way.
5. **Godesberg, 22–23 September.** Chamberlain flew to Bad Godesberg, on the Rhine, with the agreement. Hitler now said it was not enough: the area was to be occupied by the German army by 1 October, and the Poles' and Hungarians' claims were to be met as well. Chamberlain said he would pass the terms to the Czechs but could not recommend them, and the Czechs mobilised on 23 September. In London the Cabinet divided, and the First Lord of the Admiralty, Duff Cooper, urged firm action. Trenches were dug in parks, gas masks were handed out and the fleet was mobilised on 27 September.
6. **26–28 September.** In a speech at the Berlin Sportpalast on 26 September Hitler said that the Sudetenland was his last territorial demand in Europe. Chamberlain went on the radio on 27 September ("a quarrel in a far-away country between people of whom we know nothing"; see Source A below). On 28 September Mussolini, asked by Chamberlain to intervene, persuaded Hitler to hold a four-power conference. Chamberlain told the Commons, mid-speech, that Hitler had invited him to Munich, and MPs cheered.
7. **Munich, 29–30 September.** Chamberlain, Daladier, Hitler and Mussolini met without Czechoslovakia or the USSR. Mussolini presented a plan that had in fact been largely drafted by German officials. The agreement was signed in the early hours of 30 September.

:::def Munich Agreement
The agreement signed in the early hours of 30 September 1938 by Germany, Britain, France and Italy, under which the Sudetenland was transferred to Germany in stages between 1 and 10 October. Czechoslovakia was not a party to it, and the Czech government accepted it on 30 September.
:::

### The Munich conference and its outcome

The terms were these. The evacuation of the Sudetenland was to begin on 1 October and be finished by 10 October, with the German army occupying four zones in turn. An international commission of Germany, Britain, France, Italy and Czechoslovakia would fix the details, decide where plebiscites should be held (on the model of the Saar plebiscite) and make the final frontier. Britain and France promised to join an international guarantee of what remained of Czechoslovakia; Germany and Italy would give theirs once the claims of the Polish and Hungarian minorities had been settled. Hitler and Chamberlain also signed a joint declaration the same morning (see Source B in the worked examples).

Czechoslovakia lost about a fifth of its territory, most of its border fortifications and a large share of its industry. Poland took the Teschen district on 2 October and Hungary received part of southern Slovakia in November. The Czech government, left with neither land defences nor allies, was reorganised into a weaker federal state, and President Beneš resigned on 5 October. The international guarantee never came into force.

**The reception in Britain and France.** Chamberlain returned to Heston airport on 30 September waving the paper he and Hitler had signed. At Downing Street he spoke of "peace with honour", and the phrase recorded for his next words is "peace for our time". Crowds cheered him in London, and in Paris Daladier was cheered too (privately, many accounts say, he did not expect it to last). Most newspapers and most of the public were relieved. The Commons voted for the agreement on 6 October by 366 votes to 144, though about thirty Conservatives abstained, among them Churchill, Eden and Duff Cooper, and Duff Cooper resigned as First Lord of the Admiralty.

:::warn "Peace for our time"
Chamberlain's phrase on 30 September 1938 was **"peace for our time"**. Do not write "peace in our time", which is a line from the Book of Common Prayer that he may have been echoing; some reports of the day print it that way, but the usual record is "for". Whichever you use, remember that it was said to a crowd outside Downing Street, not in the Munich Agreement itself.
:::

### Responses to the occupation of Prague, March 1939

The settlement lasted less than six months. After Munich, Germany increased pressure on what was left of Czechoslovakia. Slovak nationalists wanted autonomy and, encouraged by Berlin, on 14 March 1939 Slovakia declared its independence. Hitler summoned the Czech President, Emil Hácha, to Berlin that night and, with threats of an air attack on Prague, forced him to sign a request for German "protection". German troops entered Prague on 15 March and on the next day Bohemia and Moravia became a German **Protectorate**. This was the first time that Hitler had taken over a non-German people: the pretence of "self-determination" had gone.

**The British response.** On 15 March Chamberlain told the Commons that Britain's guarantee no longer applied, because Slovakia's independence had ended the state whose frontiers she had promised to guarantee; he said he regretted it but did not propose to act. Two days later, in a speech at Birmingham on 17 March, he was much stronger. He reminded his audience of the assurances Hitler had given at Munich, and he asked whether this was the end of one adventure or the beginning of a new one and whether it was an attempt to dominate the world by force. Britain, he said, would resist such an attempt. The reasons for the change were that public opinion and many of his own party had turned against appeasement, and that Prague had shown that Hitler's claims were not limited to Germans. The following two weeks brought a rush of signals:
- 22–23 March: Germany took the Memel territory from Lithuania, and Poland was pressed to give up Danzig.
- 31 March: Britain and France guaranteed Poland (see [chapter 4 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.4)); on 13 April the guarantee was extended to Romania and Greece, after Italy had invaded Albania on 7 April.
- April–May: Britain introduced a form of conscription in peacetime, the Military Training Act (May 1939), which called up men aged 20 and 21 for six months' training.

**France's response** was to follow Britain. Daladier had already used emergency powers in late 1938 to speed up armament production, and the French government accepted the guarantee to Poland on the same day as Britain's.

:::key Was Prague the end of appeasement?
Yes in two ways: Britain and France gave up the idea that Hitler's claims were limited, and they guaranteed an eastern state for the first time. No in one: Chamberlain still hoped for a negotiated settlement over Danzig, and until August 1939 he and Halifax tried to keep contact with Hitler. The guarantee was meant as a deterrent to stop a war, not as a promise to fight for every frontier of Poland. The change in policy was real, but the change in hope was slower.
:::

## The successes of appeasement

The syllabus asks for three: war postponed, time bought for rearmament, and popular consent. Each is an argument that appeasers made at the time and that some historians still make. To use it in an essay, state the claim, give the evidence, and then say how far it holds.

### War postponed

Munich meant that Britain did not go to war in September 1938. The war came a year later, when Britain was in a stronger position in some ways. That is the simplest meaning of "success". Chamberlain also said that he could not see what Britain could do to save the Czechs: the Chiefs of Staff told him that Britain could not defend Czechoslovakia directly, and a war in 1938 would have been fought with a defence against the bomber that was not ready, a small army and uncertain Dominion support.

*The limits of the claim.* A postponement is only a success if the delay helps you more than your enemy. And in September 1938 Britain would not have been alone: France had treaty obligations, the USSR had a treaty with Czechoslovakia, and the Czechs had about 35 divisions and the strongest frontier fortifications in central Europe. Some German generals, among them the Chief of the General Staff, Ludwig Beck (who resigned in August 1938), believed that Germany was not ready for a general war and plotted to remove Hitler if war came. Munich spoiled that plan. The question "could Britain have fought in 1938?" cannot be answered with certainty; it is a good example of why historians argue.

### Buying time for rearmament

This is the strongest of the three. Before Munich, much of Britain's rearmament was still on paper; after it, the programmes began to deliver.
- **Aircraft.** The first Hawker Hurricane squadron received its aircraft in December 1937 and the first Spitfire squadron in 1938. The Air Ministry's plans from 1936 aimed at larger forces, and from late 1937, under Sir Thomas Inskip, the Minister for the Co-ordination of Defence, the priority moved from bombers to fighters. By September 1939 about 27 of Fighter Command's squadrons flew Hurricanes or Spitfires (roughly 18 with Hurricanes and 9 with Spitfires), against a handful in 1938.
- **Radar.** The Chain Home system, built round the east and south coasts from 1936, had about 20 stations by the outbreak of war, where there had been none in 1935, and the control system that linked the stations, the fighter bases and the anti-aircraft guns grew with it.
- **Civil defence.** The Air Raid Precautions Act (December 1937, in force from January 1938) made local authorities plan for air raids. The Munich crisis exposed gaps: trenches were dug in the parks, gas masks were issued and shelters were short. After it, many more volunteers joined as wardens, shelters were provided, and in September 1939 evacuation plans for children from the cities were carried out.
- **Spending and the army.** As the chart shows, defence spending more than tripled between 1934–35 and 1938–39. In April–May 1939 conscription was introduced, and the Territorial Army was doubled.

*The limits of the claim.* Germany used the time too. Her rearmament was faster than Britain's, and the Munich year gave her the Sudetenland, the Czech arms and the second half of Czechoslovakia. The honest question is "who gained more from the year?" and historians disagree. A good answer sets Britain's gains (modern fighters, radar, shelters) against Germany's (resources, a better strategic position) and says which weighed more. It should also note that Chamberlain did not say at the time that he was playing for time: he said he had saved the peace. "Buying time" is partly an argument made after the event.

### Gaining popular consent

When the war came in September 1939 Britain went to it united. Parliament and the press supported it, and the Dominions followed: Australia and New Zealand declared war on 3 September, South Africa on 6 September (after a vote in which the Prime Minister, Hertzog, was defeated) and Canada on 10 September. Supporters of Chamberlain argued that this unity could be won only by showing, through Munich and its aftermath, that the government had tried everything to keep the peace. By March 1939 most people in Britain accepted that Hitler could not be trusted, and the moral position was clear. A war in 1938 over "a far-away country" might have divided the nation and the empire.

*The limits of the claim.* This is an argument about legitimacy, and it is hard to test. Some public opinion was ahead of the government: the Bridgwater by-election of November 1938, in which an independent opponent of appeasement, Vernon Bartlett, won a seat the Conservatives had held, showed the mood. And a government can build a united people by leading, as well as by waiting.

:::key The case that appeasement succeeded
1. **War postponed**: no war in 1938, when Britain was weakest in the air, and a war in 1939 with Britain better prepared.
2. **Time to rearm**: Hurricanes and Spitfires, radar, civil defence and conscription all came in 1938–39.
3. **Popular consent**: the public and the Dominions went to war convinced that Britain had tried everything.
Each claim has a limit: Germany also gained from the delay, and the Czech army and Czech arms were lost.
:::

## The failures of appeasement

### The increased resources of the dictators

Every concession added to Germany's strength.
- **Austria (1938)**: nearly 7 million people, an army to absorb, iron ore and Austria's gold and foreign currency reserves, which helped to pay for imports of raw materials for German rearmament.
- **The Sudetenland (October 1938)**: a large share of Czechoslovakia's industry and, above all, the border fortifications, which meant that the rest of the country could not be defended.
- **Bohemia and Moravia (March 1939)**: the Škoda works and the Czech army's weapons: about 1,500 aircraft, about 500 tanks and over a million rifles (estimates vary). Czech tanks were used by German armoured divisions in 1939–41, and Czech factories went on producing for Germany.
- **Allies lost.** Germany faced a fight in the east with the Czech army not among her enemies. Britain and France lost a possible ally with about 35 divisions.

### The increased morale of the dictators

Hitler concluded that Britain and France would not fight for the territory of other states. A reported remark of his, to his generals on 22 August 1939, was that his enemies were "little worms" whom he had seen at Munich (the exact words differ between the surviving versions of the speech, so use it with care). In Germany, the plotters who might have removed him in September 1938 lost their moment. Mussolini's prestige rose too: at Munich he was hailed as the man who saved the peace. Japan also watched the democracies give way.

### Growing opposition to the policy

Appeasement was never universally popular and its critics grew in number.
- **Churchill**, the most persistent critic, warned from 1933 that Germany was rearming; after Munich he called it "a total and unmitigated defeat".
- **Anthony Eden** resigned on 20 February 1938, because Chamberlain wished to open talks with Mussolini although Italy had not withdrawn from Spain.
- **Duff Cooper** resigned from the Cabinet on 1 October 1938, saying that the Munich settlement was a mistake.
- A group of Conservatives around Eden and another around Leo Amery criticised the policy in the Commons; Labour voted against Munich; and the *Guilty Men* of 1940, written under the name "Cato" by three journalists, made the criticism a bestseller.
- **Abroad**, the USSR was shut out of Munich and of the Czech talks, and drew the conclusion that the West would not fight Hitler, and might hope to turn him eastwards. Many in Eastern Europe lost faith in France, the guarantor of the Little Entente.

:::key The case that appeasement failed
1. **Resources**: Germany took Austria, the Sudetenland and then the rest of Czechoslovakia, with their industry, gold, arms and manpower.
2. **Morale**: Hitler's belief that the democracies would not fight was strengthened, and the German opposition lost its chance.
3. **Allies**: Czechoslovakia, a well-armed ally, was lost; the USSR was alienated; eastern European states lost faith in France.
4. **Opposition**: at home, Churchill, Eden and Duff Cooper led a growing body of critics; abroad, the policy lost credit.
5. **The aim was not met**: the policy was meant to prevent a war and the war came in 1939.
:::

## Weighing appeasement: was it a sensible policy?

The words "sensible" and "successful" are not the same. A policy may be sensible when it is chosen and still fail, or may succeed by luck. To judge it you need a **criterion**. Three are worth using.
- **Did it meet its own aim?** The aim was to avoid war by satisfying Germany. It failed, since Hitler's aims were not limited; and it failed in the way critics predicted.
- **Was it reasonable given what leaders knew?** In 1935–37, when Britain's forces were weak and Hitler's demands could be seen as revising Versailles, it was a reasonable policy with limited risks. By 1938 evidence was growing that his aims were larger, and by March 1939 it was clear. The policy of pressing the Czechs to accept the loss of their frontier and excluding the USSR is harder to defend.
- **Were there better alternatives?** Alternatives were a firmer line with France and the USSR (a "Grand Alliance"), faster rearmament or a stand in 1936 or 1938. Each had costs and uncertainties: the Soviet army had just been weakened by Stalin's purges, Poland and Romania would not allow Soviet troops to cross, and Britain in 1936 could not have moved an army to the Rhine.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **"Cato"** (three journalists, writing in *Guilty Men*, 1940) blamed Chamberlain and his colleagues for the failure to prepare. Churchill's account, *The Gathering Storm* (1948), is the same story told by the leading critic.
- **R.A.C. Parker**, *Chamberlain and Appeasement* (1993), **Robert Self**, *Neville Chamberlain* (2006), and **Frank McDonough**, *Neville Chamberlain, Appeasement and the British Road to War* (1998), are among the historians who explain appeasement mainly through the pressures on Chamberlain (weakness, the Treasury's view, public opinion and his own beliefs) rather than through stupidity or cowardice, and who differ about how far he had other choices.
Use this at AS to show that "sensible" is a judgement, not a fact. Paper 2 rewards your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

:::key A judgement you can defend
Appeasement was a **reasonable policy in the early and middle 1930s** (when Britain and France were weak and Hitler's aims were unclear), and it did buy time. It became a **mistake in 1938**, when it meant forcing a democracy with a strong army and a strong frontier to give in, and it was not given up until after Prague. The policy's best defence is that it made the war a united one; its worst failure is that Germany got stronger in the delay. Which side you stress depends on your criterion: if it is "what the leaders could have known", it was sensible until 1938; if it is "what it achieved", it failed.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Britain did not oppose the remilitarisation of the Rhineland in March 1936.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Hitler sent troops into the Rhineland on 7 March 1936. This broke the Treaty of Versailles and Locarno. Britain did not oppose it because Britain was in the Depression and the people did not want a war. France did not act either. Hitler said he wanted peace and offered a 25-year pact. So Britain did nothing."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but every point is stated, not explained: "the people did not want a war" is not linked to any evidence. The word "so" again stands in for the reasoning. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was that Britain's leaders did not think the Rhineland was worth a war. Many thought that Germany had a right to put troops in her own territory, and some, like Lord Lothian, thought that Versailles had been too harsh. A second reason was that Britain was not ready to fight. The Chiefs of Staff were worried about bombing and about the Mediterranean, where Britain's fleet was busy with the Abyssinian crisis, and Britain had no army that could be sent to the Rhine quickly. A third reason was public opinion: only eight months earlier the Peace Ballot had drawn about 11.5 million voters, most of whom backed sanctions, while only about 6.8 million backed military measures, so a government that threatened war over the Rhineland risked its support."

*Commentary.* Three factors, each explained with accurate support. The answer shows why each mattered. It is Level 3 because the factors are listed side by side and nothing says which was the most important or how they linked.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add France's position and then show how the factors link:

"France also mattered. She had the larger army, but the general staff said it was built for defence and that acting against the Rhineland needed mobilisation. The government of Sarraut was a caretaker one, a general election was seven weeks away, and it would not mobilise without British support. Britain therefore held the key: if she had backed France, France might have moved.

These factors were linked. Britain's military weakness and the mood of the public both made it unlikely that she would promise support; because Britain would not promise it, France would not move; and because Germany had offered talks and a 25-year pact, Britain's leaders could say that the right response was negotiation. The most important reason was the belief that Germany had a right to garrison her own territory, because it removed the sense that this was an act of aggression: without that belief, weakness and public opinion would have been reasons to prepare, not reasons to do nothing. The result was that the League condemned the breach and Britain asked for talks, but no one acted."

*Commentary.* Three factors plus France's position, **connected** (British refusal made French action unlikely) and a supported conclusion that ranks the factors. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Appeasement was a sensible policy.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Criterion: "sensible" means reasonable given what leaders knew, and likely to meet its aim.
- For: weakness (Treasury, Chiefs of Staff, three enemies, bomber); public opinion (Oxford, Peace Ballot); the Dominions; time bought (Hurricane and Spitfire squadrons, radar, ARP); Hitler's early demands looked like revising Versailles.
- Against: Hitler's aims were wide (Hossbach, 1937: see the next chapter) and each concession gave him resources (Austria, Czech arms); morale; USSR alienated; alternatives existed (an alliance with France and the USSR, a stand in 1938 with 35 Czech divisions); the war came anyway.
- Judgement: sensible to 1937, much less so in 1938; at best a delay.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Appeasement was a sensible policy because Britain was not strong enough to fight. The Treasury said that rearmament would harm the economy and the Chiefs of Staff said that Britain could not fight Germany, Italy and Japan together. Public opinion was against war: the Oxford Union voted in 1933 not to fight for King and Country and the Peace Ballot got 11.5 million votes. Chamberlain gave Britain time to build Hurricanes, Spitfires and radar. So appeasement was sensible."

*Commentary.* Accurate and well supported, but one-sided, and the final sentence asserts: it does not say what "sensible" means or whether the policy worked. An answer made of paragraphs like this, with the other view merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet it is hard to call appeasement sensible once the evidence about Hitler's aims is considered. A sensible policy is one that is likely to meet its aim, and the aim of appeasement was to satisfy Germany so as to avoid war. That depended on Hitler's demands being limited. By November 1937, when he told his service chiefs at the meeting recorded in the Hossbach Memorandum that Germany's problem was space and that Austria and Czechoslovakia should be overthrown, the evidence was there, and the Anschluss in March 1938 and Prague in March 1939 confirmed it. Each concession also fed the dictator: Austria gave Germany gold, men and ore, and the Munich Agreement took from Czechoslovakia a frontier of fortifications and then, in March 1939, the equipment of an army of about 35 divisions and the Škoda works. The alternative was not necessarily war in 1938 alone: an alliance with the USSR and France, and a stand over Czechoslovakia, had a real chance of deterring Hitler, and some German generals were prepared to remove him if war began. Munich removed that chance and it alienated the USSR, which signed the Nazi–Soviet Pact in 1939.

The case for the policy is strongest for the period up to 1937 and weakest for 1938. Until the Hossbach meeting, Hitler's moves (conscription, the Rhineland) could be seen as the revision of an unfair treaty, Britain was weak and the alternative was a war for which she was not ready. The Treasury's priority on economic strength and the Chiefs of Staff's warning that Britain could not fight three enemies were reasonable views at the time. And the time bought did matter: by September 1939 Fighter Command had about 27 squadrons of Hurricanes and Spitfires and a radar chain along the coast, which a year earlier it did not.

Overall, appeasement was a sensible response to Britain's weakness in the mid-1930s, but not a sensible policy in 1938. The test is the criterion: if 'sensible' means what a reasonable leader could do with what he knew, it was sensible until about 1937; if it means a policy that was likely to achieve its aim, it was not, because the gains in time were smaller than the gains in resources that Germany took, and because Britain gave up the one moment when she would have had allies. It was a defensible response in the early years, but it was extended too far."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph analyses an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a stated criterion, and distinguishes periods. With a Level 3 opening on the case for the policy, this is Level 5 (18–20).

Use **Source A** and **Source B** in the box above.

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about British attitudes to the Czechoslovakia crisis of 1938.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 5 marks).** "Source A is Chamberlain, who says it is horrible that people are digging trenches because of a quarrel in a far-away country. Source B is Churchill, who says that Britain has had a defeat. They disagree about Munich. Chamberlain is the Prime Minister so he is reliable, but Churchill was against him so he is biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, with a little support; no similarity, and the comments on reliability and bias are assertions that do not use the context of 1938. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources are opposed on the main question but share some ground. Both accept that the crisis had put Britain in a dangerous position: Chamberlain speaks of 'digging trenches and trying on gas-masks', and Churchill of a 'defeat' whose reckoning is yet to come. Both also treat the outcome as something done to Czechoslovakia by a strong neighbour, since Chamberlain says that a small nation is 'confronted by a big and powerful neighbour'. Neither source says that Britain should have gone to war to save Czechoslovakia by force alone.

On the real issue they differ sharply. For Chamberlain, a war over the Sudeten question would be a war for 'a quarrel in a far-away country between people of whom we know nothing', and he says that Britain cannot in all circumstances involve the whole Empire for a small nation's sake. This presents the crisis as a question of whether Britain's interests were at stake, and concludes that the answer is no. For Churchill the same crisis is 'a total and unmitigated defeat', 'a defeat without a war' and only 'the beginning of the reckoning'. He treats it as a question of Britain's long-term security, so that the loss of Czechoslovakia would weaken Britain whether or not she fought.

The context explains the difference. Chamberlain spoke on the radio on the evening of 27 September, the day the fleet was mobilised, with trenches being dug in London parks and the Godesberg demands unanswered. His purpose was to prepare the public for a possible war and to justify his search for a settlement, so he speaks to ordinary listeners in simple, emotional language and stresses the nation's reluctance. Churchill spoke on 5 October, after the agreement was signed, in the Commons debate on Munich; his purpose was to oppose the government and he was the leader of those who thought the policy wrong. He spoke as a critic who had been out of office for years and had little to lose, so he uses language that is deliberately strong. Both sources are therefore partial. Source A is good evidence of the official view and of the appeal that made the policy popular, since the Commons voted for the agreement by 366 votes to 144; Source B is good evidence of the opposition, but a minority one in October 1938. Churchill's warning about what would follow was proved right in March 1939, but the source cannot show that at the time."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the fleet mobilised, the trenches, the debate after Munich) and through each source's purpose; a final comparison of the value of each for the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

Use **Sources A to D** in the box above.

**Question.** 'By the spring of 1939 the policy of appeasement had failed.' How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: C (the "first sip" of a "bitter cup" predicts more to come, and it was borne out); D (a guarantee to Poland is a reversal of the policy: Britain now promises to fight for a state's independence). Challenge or qualify: A and B show appeasement at its height and succeeding in its own terms in September 1938: war was avoided and "consultation" was promised; D also shows hope that war can be avoided, since it is a conditional promise, aimed at deterring Hitler.

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: an official legal text agreed by four governments, so very reliable as evidence of what was agreed. It shows the terms that Hitler wanted: occupation in stages by 10 October. But it says nothing of the Czech view, though Czechoslovakia sits on the commission, and it was imposed on her. As evidence of success it shows that war was avoided, not that peace was secured.
- **B**: a joint statement signed by Hitler and Chamberlain, so it records what Britain thought it had achieved. It was drafted by Chamberlain, who wanted something to show the public. Its value is as evidence of Chamberlain's hopes and purpose; it is weak evidence about Hitler's intentions, which the occupation of Prague later contradicted.
- **C**: spoken by a critic in a debate in which he opposed the government, so he exaggerates for effect. But it is a prediction that events bore out, and the speaker had long warned of German rearmament, so it is a strong piece of evidence on the failure side once placed against March 1939.
- **D**: a formal statement to Parliament by the Prime Minister, so it is accurate evidence of British policy on 31 March 1939. It was given after the occupation of Prague, which tells us what caused the change. But it promises support only against action that "clearly threatened Polish independence" that Poland would resist, not for Poland's frontiers, and so it leaves room for another negotiation over Danzig.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources C and D support the view that appeasement had failed by the spring of 1939, though for different reasons. In Source C, Churchill, who had opposed the policy, predicted in October 1938 that Munich was only the first of many bitter cups; Source D shows the policy being reversed after Hitler's occupation of Prague on 15 March, since Britain now committed itself to fight for a state in eastern Europe, which Chamberlain had refused to do for Czechoslovakia six months before. Sources A and B show what the policy had seemed to achieve at its height: A shows that war was avoided and that the Czech frontier was changed by agreement, and B that Hitler had promised consultation. But Source B's promise was broken in March 1939, when Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia, and A's guarantee of the new frontiers was never given. Churchill's speech is a one-sided view, but events supported it, and Source D is an official statement and so strong evidence that the policy had changed. On balance the sources support the view to a large extent: appeasement as a way of making Hitler peaceful had failed. But they also show why it was not dropped altogether: D is a conditional promise intended to deter, not an announcement of war, and in 1939 Britain still hoped to avoid war. It had failed as a policy of satisfying Hitler, but not yet as a policy of avoiding war, and that last question was still open in the spring of 1939."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1938–39; the answer separates two meanings of "failed" and judges "how far". Level 5.

Question: "How far was fear of communism the main reason for appeasement?" [20]

A student wrote:

"Fear of communism was the main reason for appeasement. Chamberlain hated the Soviet Union and he did not invite it to the Munich conference. **The level.** Some accurate material, but the paragraph **asserts** its judgement and does not argue it: the evidence shows that Chamberlain distrusted the USSR, which explains why he did not seek a Soviet alliance, not why he made concessions to Germany. The other reasons are dismissed in one line with no comparison. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "valid assertions with limited support".

**The fix.** Say what the evidence shows, and then compare. "Fear of communism explains why Britain did not look to the USSR, but not why she gave way to Germany, and it was probably not the main reason. Chamberlain distrusted Stalin, and the USSR was excluded from Munich, but his own letters and speeches stress the danger of war and the weakness of Britain's forces. The Chiefs of Staff's warning that Britain could not fight three enemies, the Treasury's view of the economy and the memory of 1914–18 all pointed towards negotiation whatever the view of the USSR. Fear of communism strengthened the case for appeasement rather than creating it." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

Q: What was the Ten-Year Rule, and when did it end?
A: A rule of 1919 that the armed forces should plan on no major war for ten years (made a rolling rule in 1928). The Cabinet ended it on 23 March 1932, but without promising more money.
Q: What did the Oxford Union debate of 9 February 1933 and the Peace Ballot of 1934–35 show about British opinion?
A: The Oxford motion not to fight "for King and Country" passed by 275 to 153 and became a symbol of anti-war feeling. The Peace Ballot drew about 11.5 million voters: most backed the League and economic sanctions, but only about 6.8 million backed military measures. Opinion wanted collective security without war.
Q: Give two reasons why France did not oppose the remilitarisation of the Rhineland.
A: The army was built for defence and needed mobilisation to act; the caretaker government faced an election seven weeks later; and it would not move without a promise of British support, which Britain would not give.
Q: What was the Maginot Line, and how did it affect French policy?
A: A chain of fortifications on the German frontier, begun about 1929–30. It encouraged a defensive strategy, made France depend on Britain, and gave a false sense of security that weakened France's commitment to allies in eastern Europe.
Q: What happened at Berchtesgaden (15 September 1938), Godesberg (22–23 September) and Munich (29–30 September)?
A: At Berchtesgaden Hitler demanded the transfer of the German-majority areas and Chamberlain agreed to put this to the Czechs. At Godesberg Hitler demanded occupation by 1 October and more besides. At Munich Britain, France, Germany and Italy agreed that German troops would occupy the Sudetenland between 1 and 10 October; Czechoslovakia was not present.
Q: Name three successes and three failures of appeasement.
A: Successes: war postponed; time for rearmament (Hurricanes, Spitfires, radar, civil defence, conscription); popular and Dominion consent in 1939. Failures: Germany gained resources (Austria, Sudetenland, Czech arms); the dictators' morale rose; the USSR was alienated and opposition at home grew.
Q: Why was the occupation of Prague on 15 March 1939 a turning point?
A: It showed that Hitler's aims were not limited to Germans or to the revision of Versailles, since he took over a Slav people and broke the promises of Munich. Public opinion and Chamberlain changed, and Britain and France guaranteed Poland on 31 March.
Q: Why is "peace for our time" a good phrase to use carefully?
A: Chamberlain said it on 30 September 1938 to a crowd outside Downing Street and not in the Munich Agreement; it is "for" our time, not "in" our time; and it describes what he hoped the Anglo-German Declaration meant, not what the declaration said.

:::recap
- **Appeasement**: British and French policy of avoiding war by negotiating and giving way to German demands; Chamberlain made it active from May 1937.
- **Britain's reasons**: economic (Depression, Treasury, "fourth arm of defence"), military (Ten-Year Rule ended 1932, three enemies, bomber fear, Dominions), social (Oxford debate 275 to 153 in February 1933; Peace Ballot about 11.5 million voters, 1934–35), ideological (fear of communism, Versailles seen as unfair, a minority with sympathy for Nazi order).
- **France's reasons**: late slump and the franc; weak governments and the left–right split after 6 February 1934; the Popular Front (elections April–May 1936, Blum in June); fear of communism; a defensive army and the Maginot Line; dependence on Britain.
- **Actions**: conscription (16 March 1935); Naval Agreement (18 June 1935, 35 per cent); Rhineland (7 March 1936); Anschluss (12–13 March 1938); Berchtesgaden (15 September), Godesberg (22–23 September), Munich (29–30 September 1938); Prague (15 March 1939); guarantee to Poland (31 March 1939).
- **Munich**: Sudetenland occupied 1–10 October 1938; four powers, no Czechoslovakia; Chamberlain's "peace for our time" (30 September); Commons vote 366 to 144; Churchill's "total and unmitigated defeat" (5 October).
- **Successes**: war postponed; time to rearm (Hurricanes, Spitfires, radar, civil defence, conscription); popular and Dominion consent.
- **Failures**: Germany gained Austria, the Sudetenland and Czech arms; dictators' morale rose; the USSR was alienated; opposition (Churchill, Eden, Duff Cooper) grew.
- **Judgement**: reasonable in 1935–37, a mistake in 1938; it failed in its aim but made the war a united one.
