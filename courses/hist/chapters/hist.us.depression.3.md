## March 1933: the crisis Roosevelt inherited

### The winter of 1932–33

Roosevelt won the election on 8 November 1932, but under the Constitution as it then stood the new president did not take office until 4 March. The four months in between, nicknamed the "lame-duck" period, were the worst of the whole Depression. Hoover was rejected and without authority; Roosevelt had no power. (The 20th Amendment, ratified on 23 January 1933, moved future inaugurations to 20 January, a date first used in 1937.)

Two men, two attitudes. Hoover wanted Roosevelt to join him in public statements on the debts and the banks and to promise not to change the gold standard or the budget. Roosevelt declined to commit himself to policies before he held office. Each man suspected the other of playing politics, and the conversations led nowhere. The cost of the stand-off fell on the banks.

The banking system was already failing (9,096 suspensions in 1930–33, as the previous chapter showed). In February 1933 the final panic began. On 14 February the governor of Michigan declared an eight-day bank holiday after the collapse of one of Detroit's big banking groups; depositors elsewhere took this as a signal to take out their savings while they still could, and state after state closed its banks or limited withdrawals. People hoarded gold and currency, and gold flowed out of the Federal Reserve. By the morning of 4 March, banks in 37 states had closed completely or restricted withdrawals, and the governors of New York and Illinois, the two great financial states, had closed theirs in the early hours; all twelve Federal Reserve Banks kept their doors locked that day.

A further shock came on 15 February, when Giuseppe Zangara, an unemployed bricklayer, fired at Roosevelt in Miami. He missed; the shots mortally wounded Anton Cermak, the mayor of Chicago, who died on 6 March. Roosevelt's calm that night impressed those who saw it.

:::key What Roosevelt faced on 4 March 1933
1. **A banking system that had stopped**: no way to cash cheques, pay wages or trade in most of the country.
2. **Mass unemployment**: about one worker in four (24.9 per cent in 1933 on Lebergott's figures, 20.6 per cent on Darby's: see the previous chapter).
3. **Falling prices and falling farm incomes**: farm debts and foreclosures, with farmers resisting sheriffs' sales in the Midwest.
4. **Fear**: no one believed that government had an answer. Any plan that restored confidence would count as a success.
5. **No single plan of his own**: Roosevelt had campaigned for "bold, persistent experimentation", a balanced budget and a "new deal", but not for any one programme.
:::

### The new administration and the Brains Trust

Roosevelt chose a team that mixed politicians, professors and reformers.

| Person | Role | Why they matter |
|---|---|---|
| Raymond Moley, Rexford Tugwell, Adolf Berle | The **Brains Trust**: Columbia University professors who advised Roosevelt during the 1932 campaign | Supplied ideas and drafts. Tugwell believed in national economic planning; Berle (co-author of *The Modern Corporation and Private Property*, 1932) wanted the power of big corporations controlled; Moley helped write speeches and the early legislation |
| Louis Howe | Roosevelt's oldest political adviser | Managed his political career and press |
| Frances Perkins | Secretary of Labor | The first woman in a cabinet; a social reformer who later chaired the committee that drafted the Social Security Act |
| Harold Ickes | Secretary of the Interior and head of the Public Works Administration | Honest and cautious with money; slow to spend the PWA's billions |
| Henry Wallace | Secretary of Agriculture | Ran the AAA; an Iowa farm editor who believed in planning |
| Harry Hopkins | Head of the Federal Emergency Relief Administration | A social worker who had run relief in New York; put jobs ahead of caution |
| Lewis Douglas | Director of the Budget | A budget-balancer who opposed much of the New Deal and resigned in 1934 |
| William Woodin | Secretary of the Treasury | A businessman and composer who ran the Treasury through the bank holiday |

The advisers did not agree. Historians describe at least four strands of thinking inside the early New Deal. **Planners** (Tugwell, Hugh Johnson of the NRA) wanted government to coordinate industry. **Anti-monopolists** (the followers of the Supreme Court justice Louis Brandeis, such as the Harvard law professor Felix Frankfurter) wanted to break up concentrated economic power and regulate finance. **Budget-balancers** (Douglas) wanted government spending cut. **Inflationists** (many senators from farming states) wanted higher prices and easier money. Roosevelt listened to all of them, tried what had support in Congress and the country, and changed course when it failed. That is why the First New Deal contains policies that pull in different directions, and why historians argue about its coherence.

:::def The Brains Trust
The small group of Columbia University academics (chiefly Raymond Moley, Rexford Tugwell and Adolf Berle) who advised Roosevelt in the 1932 campaign and helped draft early New Deal policy. The name was a journalist's.
:::

Roosevelt's own approach was the thing that most contemporaries remembered. He believed in action, in being seen to act, and in trying one remedy after another. In a speech at Oglethorpe University in May 1932 he had said the country needed "bold, persistent experimentation": "take a method and try it. If it fails, admit it frankly and try another."

### The Democratic majority in Congress

The elections of 1932 gave the Democrats large majorities in both houses of the 73rd Congress, which met in a special session from 9 March 1933. Roosevelt could also count on a group of progressive Republicans, such as Senators George Norris of Nebraska and Robert La Follette Jr. of Wisconsin, who often voted with him. Many members were new, frightened by the crisis and anxious to be seen doing something.

Two warnings. First, Congress was not a rubber stamp. It amended or even began some of the most important measures: Senator Carter Glass and Representative Henry Steagall wrote the Banking Act, and Steagall insisted on deposit insurance against Roosevelt's own doubts. Norris had fought for years for public power on the Tennessee River; the Senate passed a thirty-hour-week bill that pushed the administration to produce its own industrial plan, which became the NIRA. Second, the Democratic majority included many conservative Southerners. They chaired most of the important committees through the seniority system, and they were willing to support emergency action in 1933 but not always a permanent expansion of federal power. This is the root of several later conflicts: see the section on critics below.

## The First Hundred Days, 9 March to 16 June 1933

The special session of Congress lasted from 9 March to 16 June (99 days, which journalists rounded to "the Hundred Days"). During it Congress passed fifteen major laws and many smaller ones. Roosevelt kept control of the agenda by sending a stream of messages and by talking directly to the public.

:::def The First Hundred Days
The special session of Congress, 9 March to 16 June 1933, during which Roosevelt's administration passed about fifteen major laws to deal with banking, relief, agriculture and industry. The phrase became the measure by which later presidents' first months are judged.
:::

### The inauguration and the bank holiday

Roosevelt's **Inaugural Address** on 4 March 1933 was short and direct. "So, first of all, let me assert my firm belief that the only thing we have to fear is fear itself," he said, and he attacked the "money changers" who had "fled from their high seats in the temple of our civilization". He said the country wanted "action, and action now": the first task was to put people to work. And he warned that if Congress did not act he would ask for "broad Executive power to wage a war against the emergency, as great as the power that would be given to me if we were in fact invaded by a foreign foe." The crowd cheered. The speech promised no particular programme, but it showed leadership.

On Monday 6 March at 1 a.m. he used an executive power, a wartime law of 1917, the Trading with the Enemy Act, to proclaim a **national bank holiday**: every bank closed, no gold or currency was to be exported or hoarded, and the Treasury and the Federal Reserve were to work out how to reopen. He called Congress for 9 March.

On 9 March the **Emergency Banking Act** went through in a single day. The bill was introduced that afternoon, and was read to the House as it voted, with few copies printed; the Senate passed it that evening (73 to 7). It gave the president control over banking and gold, allowed the Federal Reserve to issue extra currency, and set up a system under which Treasury inspectors sorted banks into those sound enough to reopen at once, those that needed help, and those that must stay closed. The Republican minority, and Hoover's Treasury officials, helped write it: it was a conservative solution, which saved the existing banking system rather than nationalising it.

On Sunday 12 March Roosevelt gave the first **fireside chat**, a radio talk that explained in plain words what had been done and why. He described how banks work, and said: "I can assure you that it is safer to keep your money in a reopened bank than under the mattress." He ended: "Together we cannot fail." The next day the first banks reopened; by 15 March banks holding about 90 per cent of the country's banking resources were open, and deposits exceeded withdrawals. About 4,000 banks never reopened, but the panic had stopped.

:::def The fireside chat
One of about thirty evening radio talks (the first on 12 March 1933) in which Roosevelt explained policy to the public in plain language, as if talking in the family sitting room. They gave him a direct line to millions of voters that bypassed the newspapers, most of which were hostile.
:::

:::def The bank holiday
The closure of all American banks proclaimed by Roosevelt on 6 March 1933 so that the banking system could be examined and reopened on a sound basis. Only banks licensed by the Treasury reopened, which is why the public trusted them.
:::

Why did it work? Not because the banks suddenly became sound; many had not been. It worked because Roosevelt's actions and words changed what people expected. The government now stood behind the banks that reopened; the president had explained the position in terms people understood; and the Treasury's careful inspection meant the banks that reopened were, on the whole, ones that were solvent. The economist William Silber argues that the Act's guarantee of Federal Reserve support worked like an implicit deposit insurance. The stock market rose sharply on its reopening, and in the following weeks people put back more than half the currency they had hoarded.

### Executive actions and congressional legislation

The syllabus distinguishes between what Roosevelt did on his own authority and what Congress legislated. Keep the two lists apart in an answer, because they show different things about his leadership.

:::cols
:::col Executive actions (the president alone)
- Proclaiming the **bank holiday** (6 March 1933) under the 1917 Trading with the Enemy Act
- **Executive Order 6102** (5 April 1933): citizens must hand in gold coin and bullion; in April the government embargoed gold exports, which meant leaving the gold standard
- Fireside chats and regular **press conferences**, which let him set the news agenda
- **Creating agencies and programmes by executive order**: for example the Civil Works Administration in November 1933, paid for out of PWA money
- Appointing the people who ran the new bodies, which gave him patronage
:::
:::col Congressional legislation (laws passed by Congress)
- The Emergency Banking Act (9 March), Economy Act (20 March), CCC (31 March)
- FERA and the AAA (12 May), the TVA (18 May)
- The Securities Act (27 May), the Home Owners' Loan Act (13 June)
- The Banking Act or Glass–Steagall Act and the NIRA (16 June)
- Repeal of the Prohibition law, through the Cullen–Harrison Act (22 March) legalising 3.2 per cent beer and wine, and the 21st Amendment in December

The two together explain the speed. The president made the emergency decisions and set the mood; Congress, in a mood to cooperate, turned the plans into lasting institutions. Executive action, though, was also what critics later attacked as a danger to constitutional government, and the Supreme Court would strike down some of it.

### Money, gold and the budget

Two early decisions pointed in opposite directions.

The **Economy Act** of 20 March 1933 was a conventional measure. Roosevelt had promised a balanced budget in the campaign, and Lewis Douglas persuaded him to try to cut $500 million from a federal budget of about $3.6 billion (about 14 per cent). It cut the salaries of federal employees and reduced veterans' benefits. It was a deflationary law passed in a depression, and many Democrats opposed it. It was soon overtaken by events: from May Roosevelt began to spend on relief.

The **gold standard** decision was a break with orthodoxy. By requiring the surrender of gold in April 1933 and halting its export, Roosevelt took the dollar off gold. In June Congress cancelled the "gold clauses" in contracts. After some experiments in raising the gold price, the **Gold Reserve Act** (30 January 1934) fixed the price at $35 an ounce instead of $20.67, which cut the dollar's gold content to about 59 per cent of its former value. The effect was to make the dollar cheaper, stop the fall in prices, and allow the money supply to grow. Several economists, notably Christina Romer in her article "What Ended the Great Depression?" (1992), argue that this monetary change, together with the gold that flowed into the United States from 1934, did more for recovery than any of the agencies. That is a standard point in the debate: the policies that mattered most for recovery may not have been the ones that got most publicity.

### Jobs and relief: the CCC, FERA and the CWA

The **Civilian Conservation Corps** (Emergency Conservation Work Act, 31 March 1933) was Roosevelt's favourite. Unmarried young men from relief families were sent to camps run on military lines to plant trees, fight erosion and fires, and build trails and dams. They received $30 a month, of which $25 had to be sent home to their families. About 250,000 were enrolled by the summer of 1933; around 3 million men served before it closed in 1942. It was popular, cheap and visible; but it was for young men only (women were excluded, apart from a few small camps), and its camps were segregated by race.

The **Federal Emergency Relief Administration** (12 May 1933), under Harry Hopkins, gave $500 million to the states, half of it to match state and local spending and half to be sent where need was greatest. It marked the end of the idea that relief was a local matter, as Hoover had held. Hopkins spent the money quickly. FERA paid for both direct relief (cash and food) and work-relief projects.

Hopkins then persuaded Roosevelt to use the **Civil Works Administration** (8 November 1933 to 31 March 1934), an emergency scheme for the winter of 1933–34. The CWA paid wages for ordinary work such as repairing roads and schools; at its peak about four million people were on its payroll. It was expensive (about $200 million a month), which worried Roosevelt, and it was closed in the spring. But it had shown that the government could create millions of jobs within weeks.

### Farms: the Agricultural Adjustment Act

The farm crisis was as severe as the banking crisis (see the previous chapter). Farm prices had fallen so far that it cost more to harvest some crops than to sell them. The **Agricultural Adjustment Act** (12 May 1933) set up the **Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA)** with one aim: to raise farm prices to "parity", the level at which a crop would buy as much as it had in 1909–14, which farmers remembered as a golden age. The method was to cut supply. The government paid farmers to produce less, with the money raised by a **processing tax** on the firms that turned crops into food and clothing (millers, meat packers, cotton mills).

Since 1933's crops were already planted, the AAA's first act was shocking. Cotton farmers were paid to plough up about ten million acres of growing crop, and some six million piglets and sows were slaughtered. Newspapers, and the hungry, noticed the waste. Wallace defended it as a harsh necessity.

Two more laws supported farmers: the Farm Credit Act and the Emergency Farm Mortgage Act helped to refinance farm debts and ease foreclosures.

### The Tennessee Valley Authority

The **TVA** (18 May 1933) built dams on the Tennessee River in seven poor Southern states to control floods, produce electricity and cheap fertiliser, and promote regional planning. It was the first time the federal government had sold power in competition with private utilities. George Norris, who had spent a decade trying to get the Muscle Shoals dam in Alabama into public hands, was the Senate sponsor.

### Regulating banking and finance

The Hundred Days also began the regulation of finance. The **Securities Act** (27 May 1933) required companies selling shares to the public to publish honest information ("truth in securities"). The **Banking Act** (16 June 1933), usually called **Glass–Steagall**, did two things: it separated commercial banking (taking deposits and lending) from investment banking (dealing in shares), because the Pecora hearings in the Senate had shown how banks had mixed the two in the 1920s; and it set up the **Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC)**, which from 1 January 1934 insured deposits up to $2,500 at member banks. Roosevelt had doubted deposit insurance; he feared it would put a premium on bad banking. Congress and public opinion overruled him. The **Home Owners' Loan Corporation** (13 June 1933) bought mortgages that borrowers could not pay and replaced them with long-term, cheaper loans; in three years it refinanced about a million homes.

### Industry: the NIRA, the NRA and the PWA

The biggest and most controversial law of the session was the **National Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA)** of 16 June 1933. When he signed it, Roosevelt said: "History probably will record the National Industrial Recovery Act as the most important and far-reaching legislation ever enacted by the American Congress." It had two parts:

- **Title I** set up the **National Recovery Administration (NRA)** under General Hugh Johnson. Each industry would draw up a **code of fair competition** setting minimum wages, maximum hours and sometimes prices and output, with government approval. The antitrust laws were suspended for firms obeying a code. **Section 7(a)** gave workers "the right to organize and bargain collectively through representatives of their own choosing". Child labour was banned in most codes. Firms that followed the codes could display the **Blue Eagle** emblem with the slogan "We Do Our Part", and consumers were urged to buy only from them.
- **Title II** set up the **Public Works Administration (PWA)** under Harold Ickes with $3.3 billion to spend on roads, dams, schools and other large public works.

The idea behind the NRA was that cut-throat competition had driven wages and prices down in a spiral; if all firms in an industry agreed to pay decent wages and not undercut each other, purchasing power would recover and jobs would return. The idea behind the PWA was the older one of spending public money to create demand.

:::def The NRA
The National Recovery Administration, created by the NIRA in June 1933, which tried to end the price-and-wage spiral by getting each industry to agree a code of fair competition on wages, hours and prices, backed by the Blue Eagle emblem and by government authority.
:::

### The alphabet agencies at a glance

Newspapers called the new bodies the "alphabet agencies" because they were known by their initials. Do not learn them as a list: for each, know its aim, how well it worked and what happened to it. The table gives the essentials.

|---|---|---|---|---|

| CCC | March 1933 | Jobs for young men on conservation | Popular; about 3 million men; segregated; no women | Closed 1942 |
| FERA | May 1933 | Federal grants for state relief ($500m to start) | First national relief; gave poor states money; mostly direct relief | Replaced by the WPA in 1935 |

:::warn "First" and "Second" New Deal are historians' labels
Roosevelt never used the terms. Historians divide the New Deal into a **First** (1933–34, emergency recovery and relief, with cooperation between government and business) and a **Second** (1935–38, welfare, labour rights and a heavier attack on wealth). The dates are blurred: the SEC and the Gold Reserve Act (1934) belong to the First; the Wagner Act and Social Security (1935) belong to the Second; the FHA (1934) and the WPA (1935) show how one set of ideas ran into the next. Do not write as if the New Deal changed overnight on 1 January 1935; write "the early New Deal" and "from 1935".
:::

## How successful was the First New Deal? The impact of the alphabet agencies

"Successful" needs a criterion. Four are useful: **recovery** (output and jobs), **relief** (help for the destitute), **reform** (lasting changes that make another crash less likely) and **confidence** (whether people believed the government could act). An agency can score well on one and badly on another.

### Agriculture

**Case for success.** Farm prices and incomes rose. Cotton, wheat and corn prices were far higher in 1935 than in 1932, and total farm income in 1935 was about half as high again as in 1932 (a figure often quoted from official statistics). The AAA's payments reached millions of farms. Cheap credit saved many farmers from foreclosure. Farmers did not forget: the Midwest and the West voted for Roosevelt in 1934 and 1936.

**Case against.** The AAA paid for scarcity at a time when people went hungry, and it was not the only reason for higher prices: the severe drought of 1934 cut crops more than any payment. It helped large and middle-sized commercial farmers most. In the South, landlords took the payments for the cotton acres they took out of production, and many then evicted the tenants and sharecroppers who had worked that land, or paid them little or nothing, although the contracts said the landlord should share the money with them. Wallace's dismissal of the AAA's liberal lawyers in February 1935 for protesting at this showed whose interest the programme served. In July 1934 sharecroppers in Arkansas, black and white, founded the **Southern Tenant Farmers' Union** to demand a share of the money and an end to evictions; landlords answered with violence. The AAA also did nothing for farm labourers, who had no land at all.

**Verdict.** A success for farm prices and for commercial farmers; a failure for the poorest rural Americans, and for consumers. The Court's decision against it in 1936 did not end farm support, because Congress replaced it, but it showed the limits of the method.

### Business and industry

**Case for success.** The NRA gave the country a sense that something was being done. Over two years it brought agreement from almost every major industry. The codes abolished child labour in most industries, set minimum wages and maximum hours, and may have helped to stop the spiral of falling wages and prices. About 23 million people worked under NRA codes. Section 7(a) mattered more than its authors intended: the United Mine Workers' organisers toured the coalfields saying that the president wanted workers to join the union, and union membership began to climb. The PWA built schools, hospitals, bridges, tunnels and dams that are still in use, such as the Triborough Bridge in New York and the Bonneville Dam in Oregon.

**Case against.** The codes were mostly written by the large firms in each industry, which used them to hold up prices and to limit competition from small firms, so consumers paid more and small businesses felt squeezed. Wages were set at different levels for different regions, for men and women, and for Black and white workers, which left many of the poorest workers on the lowest rates. Many employers broke the codes and set up company unions to avoid bargaining with real ones. The NRA's administrative burden was heavy: it produced thousands of regulations. A review board chaired by the lawyer Clarence Darrow, set up in 1934, reported that the codes helped monopolies at the expense of small business. The strikes of 1934, including the Teamsters' strike in Minneapolis, the Auto-Lite strike in Toledo and the San Francisco general strike, showed that Section 7(a) had raised workers' hopes faster than it protected them. Hugh Johnson, tired and drinking too much, resigned in September 1934. The PWA was too slow: Ickes, determined to avoid waste or scandal, spent the money carefully, and it did not create jobs fast enough in 1933–34.

**Verdict.** The historian William Leuchtenburg, in *Franklin D. Roosevelt and the New Deal* (1963), judged that the NRA gave some two million people jobs, helped stop the deflationary spiral, set national standards on hours, wages and child labour, and civilised competition; but that it did little to speed recovery, and probably hindered it by supporting restricted output and higher prices. (This is a paraphrase of his argument, not a quotation.) By the time the Supreme Court struck it down in May 1935, few of its original supporters were sorry.

### Banking, finance and the stock market

This was the most lasting success of the First New Deal.

- **Banks.** The bank holiday and the Emergency Banking Act ended the panic. The FDIC stopped bank runs: in 1933 about 4,000 banks suspended business, but in each year after 1933 fewer than a hundred did. Glass–Steagall separated banking from share-dealing.
- **The stock market.** After the Securities Act, the **Securities Exchange Act** of 6 June 1934 created the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** to police the stock exchanges, ban the worst forms of manipulation and insider dealing, and regulate how shares are bought on credit. Roosevelt appointed Joseph P. Kennedy, who had made money as a speculator in the 1920s, as the first chairman. His appointment was a clever choice: it reassured the business community that the SEC would not be hostile, and Kennedy proved an effective regulator.
- **Mortgages.** HOLC and the FHA stabilised house prices and the home-lending market.

Business leaders protested at the SEC and the Securities Act, but the stock market did not collapse, as they had predicted; the SEC is still active. Reform, not recovery, was the achievement.

### Jobs, families and the destitute

Roosevelt believed that the government should give work, not "the dole". The First New Deal therefore built a patchwork of work relief: the CCC, the CWA, the PWA and FERA's work projects. This was a huge change from Hoover's reliance on voluntary giving, because the federal government now accepted responsibility for the unemployed. The numbers are large: about 4 million jobs through the CWA, 250,000 and then many more in the CCC, and relief for millions through FERA. The HOLC saved homes. By the end of 1934, the federal government was paying for or running relief for several million families.

The limits were also clear. The programmes reached only a fraction of the unemployed, and hardly any of the long-term poor. Most were for men; women's work projects such as sewing rooms were poorly paid. Black Americans were hit hardest by unemployment and received less of the benefits, because local officials ran many relief programmes and Southern officials often favoured whites. The CCC camps were segregated. The Native American "New Deal" of John Collier, the Commissioner of Indian Affairs, ended the policy of breaking up tribal land (the Indian Reorganization Act, 1934), but it was not part of the main recovery effort.

1. **Confidence and the banks**: a clear success. The panic ended in March 1933 and deposit insurance meant it did not return.
2. **Relief**: a major change. Federal money and federal jobs reached millions, though unequally and not enough.
3. **Reform**: lasting success in finance (FDIC, SEC), power (TVA) and housing (HOLC, FHA); a reform of labour law began with Section 7(a).
4. **Recovery**: partial. Falling unemployment and rising output, but the NRA and AAA were weak or harmful as recovery devices; the policies that did most (leaving gold, the end of the banking panic) were quieter ones.
5. **Fairness**: the benefits went disproportionately to white, male, commercial and industrial Americans.
**Judgement:** the First New Deal stabilised the economy and reformed the financial system more than it revived it.

Roosevelt's popularity in 1933 hid a growing opposition. Critics came from both sides, and the left and right attacked the New Deal for opposite reasons.

### Critics from the Left

**Socialists and Communists.** The Socialist Party under **Norman Thomas** argued that the New Deal was an attempt to save capitalism, not replace it, and wanted public ownership of the main industries. The Communist Party under Earl Browder was small, but it was influential in some unions and in organisations of the unemployed. Until 1935 it called the New Deal a step towards fascism; then, when the Soviet Union and the international Communist movement told Communists to cooperate with other anti-fascists (the "Popular Front"), it softened its line. Both parties were weak at the polls; their importance is that they pushed debate to the left and showed what the discontented might do.

**Dr Francis Townsend.** A retired California doctor, Townsend proposed in 1933–34 that every American over 60 who had retired from work should receive $200 a month, on condition that they spent it within the month. The scheme was to be paid for by a 2 per cent tax on all business transactions. Economists pointed out that it would have cost far more than the whole federal budget and would have given pensions to a small minority of the population. But "Townsend Clubs" sprang up across the country, and by 1936 the movement claimed about two million members. It showed how great the demand for pensions was. Roosevelt later told his Secretary of Labor, Frances Perkins, that Congress could not resist the pressure of the Townsend Plan unless it passed a real system of old-age insurance. That is how Social Security came about (see the next chapter).

**Father Charles Coughlin.** A Roman Catholic priest at Royal Oak, Michigan, Coughlin used a weekly radio show to reach a huge audience: estimates run to tens of millions. He began by backing Roosevelt ("Roosevelt or ruin") and attacking bankers, and called for the nationalisation of banks and key industries and for silver-based inflation. In 1934 he founded the National Union for Social Justice. By 1935 he had broken with Roosevelt, accusing him of serving the bankers, and in 1936 his movement joined with Townsend's followers and Long's to back a third-party candidate, William Lemke of North Dakota, for the Union Party. Lemke got about 2 per cent of the vote. Coughlin's later radio talks became openly anti-Semitic, and his career collapsed in 1939–42. In 1935 he was a serious worry for Roosevelt because he spoke to the same working-class Catholic voters in the cities whom the Democrats needed.

**Senator Huey Long.** Long was the most dangerous. As Governor of Louisiana (1928–32) he had built roads, hospitals and schools and ruled the state almost as a dictator. As a Senator from 1932 he broke with Roosevelt, who he said had not done enough. In February 1934 he launched the **Share Our Wealth** movement in a national radio broadcast, "Every Man a King". In its 1935 form, as he set it out in a speech, the plan would:
- limit fortunes by a capital levy, so that no one would own more than a few million dollars;
- limit the amount anyone could earn or inherit in a year to $1 million;
- give every family a home with a radio and a car, free of debt;
- shorten the working week to thirty hours and the year to eleven months;
- pay old-age pensions, give free education to every child through university, and a bonus for veterans.

Long's organisation claimed millions of members in "Share Our Wealth clubs". Economists said the sums did not add up: there was not enough wealth in the rich to give everyone what he promised. But politicians feared him. Democratic managers worried in 1935 that he might run as a third-party candidate and take several million votes from Roosevelt in 1936, and the president told advisers that he must take the wind out of Long's sails. Long was shot in the State Capitol at Baton Rouge on 8 September 1935 by Dr Carl Weiss, the son of a man he had attacked, and died two days later.

:::note A source: Huey Long, January 1935
It is not Roosevelt or ruin; it is Roosevelt's ruin. … We ran Mr. Roosevelt for the presidency of the United States because he promised to us by word of mouth and in writing … that the size of the big man's fortune would be reduced so as to give the masses at the bottom enough to wipe out all poverty … There are thousands of share-our-wealth societies organized in the United States now. We want 100,000 such societies formed for every nook and corner of this country …

*Shortened from a radio address by Senator Huey P. Long, January 1935, printed in The Annals of America, vol. 15 (Encyclopaedia Britannica, 1968). The phrases are Long's own, in the order he spoke them.*
:::

**The effect of the Left.** The critics from the Left never won an election, and Long's death and the weak showing of the Union Party in 1936 ended their political threat. But they pushed Roosevelt to move left in 1935 (the Wealth Tax, Social Security and the Wagner Act) and they kept the pressure on the question of poverty in old age.

### Critics from the Right

**Republicans.** In 1933 the Republicans, having lost the election by a landslide, did not oppose emergency action. By 1934 they had begun to argue that Roosevelt was spending too much, increasing the power of the federal government at the expense of the states, and threatening liberty. Former president Hoover led the attack with his book *The Challenge to Liberty* (1934). Republicans were also weak in Congress, holding about a quarter of the seats after 1934. They were less a danger in votes than as a voice.

**Southern Democrats.** These were potentially the more serious opposition because they were in Roosevelt's own party and chaired its committees. Men such as Senator Carter Glass and Senator Harry Byrd of Virginia believed in balanced budgets and states' rights, and distrusted federal power. As the New Deal grew they objected to its cost, to the NRA's wage codes (which would raise the wages of Southern workers, a threat to cheap labour), to federal relief payments that gave farm labourers and domestic servants an alternative to low wages, and to any hint that Washington might interfere with segregation. They supported the New Deal when it helped the South (the AAA, the TVA, rural credit), and turned against it when it helped labour or Black Americans. They were the core of what became a conservative coalition with the Republicans in the late 1930s.

**Industry and business.** Business leaders at first welcomed the NRA, in which they had much influence, and the Chamber of Commerce supported it. But they came to resent Section 7(a), the growth of unions, new taxes, the SEC and the feeling that Roosevelt had blamed them for the Depression. By 1934–35 many were hostile. Their view was that government interference damaged "business confidence" and delayed recovery. In August 1934 conservative Democrats such as Al Smith (the 1928 presidential candidate) and John W. Davis (the 1924 candidate) joined with business leaders, including members of the Du Pont family, to found the **American Liberty League**. It said it defended private property and the Constitution against a drift to socialism. It spent heavily on pamphlets and radio, and grew to about 125,000 members by mid-1936. Its main effect was to help Roosevelt: its list of wealthy sponsors made the president's claim that he was fighting "economic royalists" look true. Roosevelt remarked early on that the League seemed to uphold two of the Ten Commandments, the ones that protect property, and to ignore the commandment to love your neighbour.

**The press.** Most newspaper owners disliked Roosevelt. William Randolph Hearst, who had backed him in 1932, turned against him; Colonel Robert McCormick's *Chicago Tribune* attacked him daily. They called the New Deal socialist, "the Raw Deal", a threat to liberty and an attempt at dictatorship. In 1936 most newspapers supported Landon and Roosevelt won by a landslide. Part of the explanation is that the radio let him go over the editors' heads, and that most voters were far more concerned with jobs than with the press's complaints.

:::key Left or Right: which was the greater threat to the New Deal?
1. **The Left** was a threat to Roosevelt's *votes* in 1935–36: Long, Coughlin and Townsend reached millions, and a third party might have split the Democratic vote. It collapsed with Long's death and the Union Party's poor showing (about 2 per cent in 1936).
2. **The Right** was a threat to the New Deal's *powers*: the Supreme Court, the conservative Southern Democrats in Congress, and a business community that could slow recovery by not investing. Its strength grew after 1936.
3. **The interaction**: attacks from the Left pushed Roosevelt towards the Second New Deal of 1935; attacks from the Right then made it harder to carry through.
**Judgement:** the Left was the greater short-term threat to Roosevelt, the Right the greater long-term threat to what he built.
:::

## The Supreme Court and the New Deal

### The Court and its problem

In 1933 the Supreme Court had nine justices, most of them appointed by Republican presidents, and Roosevelt had no vacancy to fill in his first term. Commentators divided the Court into three groups.

The four conservatives, **Willis Van Devanter, James McReynolds, George Sutherland and Pierce Butler**, were nicknamed the "Four Horsemen" (of the Apocalypse). The three liberals were **Louis Brandeis, Harlan Stone and Benjamin Cardozo**. **Chief Justice Charles Evans Hughes** and **Justice Owen Roberts** held the balance. The Court was not simply hostile: it upheld the Minnesota mortgage moratorium (1934), Roosevelt's gold policy in the gold-clause cases (February 1935) and the TVA (1936). But on several central laws it ruled against him.

### The key cases

| Case and date | Law | Result | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan*, Jan 1935 | NIRA oil ("hot oil") provision | Struck down, 8–1 | Congress had given the president lawmaking power with no guiding standard |
| *Railroad Retirement Board v. Alton*, May 1935 | Railroad pension law | Struck down, 5–4 | Beyond Congress's commerce power |
| *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States*, 27 May 1935 | NIRA codes | Struck down, 9–0 | Unlawful delegation of law-making power; poultry in Brooklyn was not in interstate commerce |
| *United States v. Butler*, 6 Jan 1936 | AAA processing tax | Struck down, 6–3 | Regulating farm production was a power of the states, and the tax was a means to that end |
| *Carter v. Carter Coal Co.*, May 1936 | Guffey Coal Act | Struck down | Mining was production, not interstate commerce |
| *Morehead v. Tipaldo*, June 1936 | New York minimum wage for women (a state law) | Struck down, 5–4 | Interfered with liberty of contract |

:::def Delegation of power
Giving another body the authority to make binding rules. In *Schechter* the Supreme Court held that Congress could not hand the president or industry bodies unlimited power to write the "codes of fair competition" without setting clear standards.
:::

**Schechter** was the shock. On 27 May 1935, a day that the press called "Black Monday" (three decisions went against the administration the same day), the Court unanimously decided that the NIRA was unconstitutional. The Schechter brothers ran a kosher poultry business in Brooklyn and had been convicted of breaking the NRA's Live Poultry Code (among other offences, for selling unfit chickens). Chief Justice Hughes wrote the opinion. It held first that Congress had given the president and the code authorities far too much legislative power without a standard to guide them. Second, it held that the Schechters' business was local, and that the poultry had come to rest in New York, so the commerce power did not reach it: "Extraordinary conditions do not create or enlarge constitutional power." The liberal Justice Cardozo, who agreed with the result, called the delegation "delegation running riot". The result was that the codes ended. The NRA was already unpopular, and Title I of the NIRA was due to lapse within weeks if Congress did not renew it, so some officials privately admitted relief at being freed from a failing scheme. Congress then extended a reduced NRA only until April 1936, and passed the Wagner Act (July 1935) to protect union rights in its place.

**Butler** (January 1936) struck down the AAA. The justices ruled that the processing tax was a way of regulating agricultural production, which the Constitution left to the states. Justice Stone's dissent said that courts are not the only part of government that must be assumed to have the capacity to govern, and that the power to tax and spend includes the power to relieve a national economic crisis. The decision shocked the farm lobby, but Congress quickly passed the Soil Conservation and Domestic Allotment Act (1936), paying farmers to move land from surplus crops to soil-protecting ones, and the second AAA in 1938.

:::key Why the Supreme Court struck down parts of the New Deal
1. **The Constitution's limits on federal power.** The Court read the commerce clause narrowly: Congress could regulate trade between states but not what it called local activity (a chicken sold in Brooklyn, coal mined in Kentucky).
2. **Delegation of law-making.** Congress could not give the president or industry bodies broad power without clear standards.
3. **States' rights and the Tenth Amendment.** Agriculture and working conditions were, the majority held, for the states.
4. **The judges' own views.** The Four Horsemen believed in "liberty of contract" and limited government, and disliked the New Deal; there was no Roosevelt appointee on the Court before 1937.
5. **Poor drafting.** Hasty emergency laws, some argued (Hughes himself said so later), gave the Court easy targets.
**Judgement:** the Court's decisions rested mainly on a view of the Constitution that most of the New Deal's supporters thought out of date; whether the justices were being principled or political is the central debate.
:::

### Roosevelt's reaction and the court plan

After Schechter Roosevelt held a long press conference on 31 May 1935, in which he said the decision would force the country back to a "horse-and-buggy" definition of interstate commerce, and asked whether the national government had the power to deal with a national economy. But he did nothing about the Court for nearly two years. After the 1936 landslide, on **5 February 1937**, he sent Congress a bill to "reorganize the judiciary". For every federal judge who stayed on the bench six months after reaching seventy, the president could appoint an extra one, up to six new Supreme Court justices (a maximum of fifteen). Six of the nine justices were seventy or older. Opponents called it "court packing". The bill failed in the Senate, but by then the Court had changed its course: in March and April 1937 it upheld a state minimum-wage law (*West Coast Hotel v. Parrish*) and the Wagner Act (*NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel*), and from the summer of 1937 retirements gave Roosevelt the chance to appoint justices. By 1940 he had named five. The next chapter, [Crash, Depression and New Deal (4)](#hist.us.depression.4), analyses the plan, why it failed and what it cost him.

:::exam Linking the Court to the success of the New Deal
A strong answer on the Court does not stop at "the Court struck down the NRA and the AAA". It asks what difference that made. The NRA was already unpopular and the AAA was replaced by other farm laws, so the immediate damage to recovery was limited. The greater effects were political: the decisions forced Roosevelt to rethink his strategy in 1935, and they led to the court plan of 1937.
:::

## Weighing the evidence: the main questions

**Was the First New Deal "successful"?** Break the question down by criterion. On the banks, yes; on relief, a lot but not enough; on reform, lasting and large; on recovery, partial; on equality, no. The most useful sentence in an essay is a comparison: *which of the New Deal's aims did it achieve better than the others, and why?*

**Was the First New Deal a coherent plan?** Not really, and Roosevelt did not claim it was. It combined planning (NRA), reform (SEC, FDIC), relief (FERA, CCC) and orthodoxy (the Economy Act). The coherence was in the style, not the programme: a willingness to act and to try again.

**Were the critics from the Left or the Right more dangerous?** See the key box. The point to reach is that the answer depends on the time frame and the kind of danger: votes in 1935, or power in 1937.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add links and a judgement:

"These reasons were connected. The decisive reason was the narrow view of the Constitution. Even the liberal Cardozo agreed with the unanimous Schechter ruling on delegation, which shows that the NIRA had real legal flaws, but only the conservatives struck down the AAA, which shows that the Court's constitutional philosophy mattered most in its decisions on federal power. *Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the constitutional view, delegation and the make-up of the Court), with a supported conclusion about the decisive one. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is Roosevelt saying that people should trust the banks. Source B is Huey Long saying that Roosevelt has broken his promises. So they disagree. Source B is biased because Long was an enemy of Roosevelt."

*Commentary.* One difference, loosely supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share more than their authors would admit. Both rest on the idea of trust between Roosevelt and the people. Source A makes confidence the key to recovery: 'the confidence of the people' is 'more important than currency, more important than gold'. Source B is about betrayed confidence: people 'ran' Roosevelt because of his promises and were 'fooled'. Both also use the language of rescue, one in which 'together we cannot fail', the other in which people grabbed at Roosevelt 'like a drowning man grabbing at a straw'.

They differ over whether the trust was justified. Source A asks the public to trust Roosevelt and the banks and presents the relationship as a partnership ('together'). Source B says the trust was misplaced, accuses Roosevelt of siding with the rich, and says that a promise to share wealth had been broken. Source A is about a technical matter, banking, and the speaker is calm and reassuring; Source B is angry and personal.

The context explains the difference. Source A was delivered on 12 March 1933, in the middle of the bank holiday, on the Sunday before the banks reopened. Roosevelt had to persuade millions to put their money back, and a fireside chat in plain words was his tool: its purpose is to build confidence, so it presents the government as honest and the future as safe. It is excellent evidence of what Roosevelt wanted the public to believe, and the real response (people returned more than half their hoarded currency) shows it worked. Source B was spoken in January 1935 by a Senator who had broken with Roosevelt and was building his own Share Our Wealth movement to challenge him from the left; his purpose is to recruit followers by presenting himself as a disillusioned convert and Roosevelt as the friend of the rich. His claim that Roosevelt promised to reduce big fortunes is his own reading of the 1932 campaign, not Roosevelt's words, and Long was himself a possible candidate against him in 1936. It is therefore weak evidence of what Roosevelt promised but good evidence of the disappointment felt by people who wanted far more radical action, and of the political pressure on Roosevelt in 1935. Taken together, the two show that the New Deal started as a relationship of confidence and that by 1935 some of the confidence had turned into disappointment, but the sources come from the two ends of the argument, so neither is a measure of how most Americans felt."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported by short quotations; the differences explained through context (the bank holiday, Long's campaign) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: How successful was the First New Deal in reviving the economy?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Success: banks (Emergency Banking Act, FDIC; 4,000 suspensions in 1933, fewer than a hundred a year after); unemployment fell from 24.9 per cent (1933) to 16.9 per cent (1936) on Lebergott's figures, 20.6 to 9.9 on Darby's; farm income up about half 1932–35; CCC, CWA, PWA jobs; gold policy ended deflation.
- Limits: unemployment still 14.3 per cent in 1937; the NRA held up prices and probably slowed recovery (Leuchtenburg); the AAA cut output and hurt tenants; the PWA was slow; the recovery had other causes (gold inflows, the end of the panic); Supreme Court struck down the NRA and AAA.
- Judgement: criterion = "reviving", meaning getting output and jobs moving again; the First New Deal restored the conditions for recovery (banks, confidence, prices) more than it created recovery through its agencies.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The First New Deal revived the economy a great deal. Roosevelt closed the banks and reopened them under the Emergency Banking Act, which ended the panic. The Civilian Conservation Corps and the Civil Works Administration gave jobs to millions, and the PWA built bridges and schools. The AAA raised farm prices. By 1936 unemployment had fallen from 24.9 per cent to 16.9 per cent. So the New Deal revived the economy."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with some figures, but one-sided. The last sentence asserts the link; it does not show that the fall in unemployment was caused by the New Deal. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with the limits merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the agencies themselves contributed less to recovery than this suggests, and some held it back. The NRA was supposed to raise wages and prices together, but its codes were written largely by big firms and tended to restrict output and raise prices, which historians such as Leuchtenburg judge probably delayed recovery, even though it helped to end child labour and cut hours. The AAA raised farm prices by cutting production and evicted tenants in the South. The PWA, under Ickes, spent its $3.3 billion so cautiously that it did not provide jobs when they were most needed. And the unemployment figures depend on how work-relief is counted: Lebergott counts CCC and CWA workers as unemployed and shows 20.1 per cent in 1935, while Darby counts them as employed and shows 14.2 per cent. That the debate exists shows that much of the improvement was jobs created directly by the government rather than jobs in private industry.

The more important causes of recovery lay elsewhere. Ending the banking panic in March 1933 was the necessary first step, because no economy can grow when its payments system has stopped. Then the break from gold in 1933–34 stopped the deflation and, with gold flowing in from Europe, let the money supply grow. These are policies of the First New Deal, but they were quiet, technical decisions rather than the alphabet agencies that dominated the headlines. Unemployment was still 14.3 per cent in 1937, which shows how incomplete recovery remained.

Overall, the First New Deal was successful in reviving the economy only in a limited sense. It was highly successful in stopping the collapse and in restoring confidence and the conditions for growth, notably through the bank holiday, deposit insurance and leaving gold. It was much less successful in the area where it concentrated its effort, the NRA and the AAA, and the jobs it created directly were temporary and left unemployment high. The statement 'revived' is therefore true of output and prices from 1933, but the policies that did it were not the ones for which the New Deal was famous."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (which measures actually produced recovery) and distinguishes the visible agencies from the quieter policies. With an opening paragraph on the case for success, this is Level 5 (18–20). Note that "revive" is not the same as "relieve": separating recovery from relief is itself a mark of a strong answer.

Q: Why did the banks fail so badly in the winter of 1932–33, and how did Roosevelt end the crisis in March 1933?
A: Depositors with no insurance feared losses and withdrew their savings, which closed even sound banks; Michigan's bank holiday on 14 February triggered a chain of closures in 37 states by 4 March. Roosevelt proclaimed a national bank holiday (6 March), Congress passed the Emergency Banking Act (9 March), Treasury inspectors licensed only sound banks to reopen, and the fireside chat of 12 March restored confidence.
Q: Name the Brains Trust and three of Roosevelt's advisers or cabinet members, saying what each did.
A: Moley, Tugwell and Berle (Columbia academics who advised in 1932). Perkins (Labor Secretary, first woman in a cabinet), Ickes (Interior and the PWA), Wallace (Agriculture and the AAA), Hopkins (relief), Douglas (the Budget; resigned 1934).
Q: What is the difference between an executive action and a law? Give an example of each from March 1933.
A: An executive action is taken by the president alone (the bank holiday proclamation, 6 March, or the gold order of 5 April); a law is passed by Congress (the Emergency Banking Act, 9 March).
Q: What was the AAA, how did it work, and what were its weaknesses?
A: It aimed to raise farm prices to parity by paying farmers to produce less, financed by a processing tax. Prices and incomes rose, but it helped large farmers more, led to evictions of Southern tenants and sharecroppers, and was struck down in *Butler* (January 1936).
Q: What were the NRA's codes, Section 7(a) and the Blue Eagle?
A: Codes of fair competition set wages, hours and sometimes prices for each industry; Section 7(a) gave workers the right to organise and bargain collectively; the Blue Eagle was the emblem of firms that obeyed the codes ("We Do Our Part").
Q: What did the Glass–Steagall Act do?
A: It created the FDIC (deposits insured from 1 January 1934, up to $2,500) and separated commercial banking from investment banking.
Q: Name two critics from the Left and two from the Right, and say what each wanted.
A: Left: Long (Share Our Wealth, limits on fortunes), Townsend ($200 a month for the over-60s), Coughlin (nationalise banks, change the currency). Right: the Liberty League (defend property and the Constitution), Southern Democrats such as Glass and Byrd (balanced budgets, states' rights), Hoover and Republicans, and most newspaper owners.
Q: Give two reasons the Supreme Court struck down the NIRA in *Schechter*.
A: Congress had delegated law-making power to the president and industry with no clear standard; and the Schechters' local poultry business was not in interstate commerce, so the commerce power did not reach it.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Roosevelt declared a bank holiday on 6 March 1933.
A: Indicative content: the banking panic had spread from Michigan (14 February) until 37 states had closed or restricted banks by 4 March, and the Federal Reserve was losing gold; no payments system was working and the Treasury and the Fed needed time to decide which banks were sound; he had the legal tool in the Trading with the Enemy Act; and a general holiday removed the stigma of any single bank closing. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: the panic made action urgent; the holiday gave time; legal power and political will made it possible; and it prepared the Emergency Banking Act of 9 March, which licensed sound banks to reopen. It ends with a judgement on the main reason (the collapse of confidence).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Agricultural Adjustment Act was passed in 1933.
A: Indicative content: farm prices had collapsed (it cost more to harvest some crops than to sell them); debts and foreclosures were widespread and farmers were protesting; the idea of "parity" with 1909–14; surplus production could not be cleared under market conditions; Wallace and the farm lobby wanted government to cut supply; processing taxes could pay for it. Level 4 connects the background (surplus, debt) to the method (paying farmers to reduce output) and judges the main reason (the price collapse).
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The most important achievement of the First Hundred Days was ending the banking crisis.' How far do you agree?
A: For: the bank holiday and Emergency Banking Act ended the panic within days; banks holding about 90 per cent of resources were open by 15 March; the FDIC (Glass–Steagall) stopped runs for good; every other reform depended on restored confidence. Against: the CCC and FERA introduced national responsibility for relief; the AAA, NRA and PWA tried to start recovery; the TVA and SEC were lasting reforms; the gold decisions were more important for recovery. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "which achievement most changed the situation of the country, or lasted longest", and judges, e.g., that the banking measures were the most important because the others rested on them, though the creation of federal responsibility for relief had the longest effect.

:::recap
- **March 1933**: bank runs spread from Michigan (14 February); 37 states had closed or restricted banks by 4 March; Roosevelt, inaugurated 4 March ("the only thing we have to fear is fear itself"), proclaimed a bank holiday (6 March); the Emergency Banking Act (9 March) and the first fireside chat (12 March) restored confidence.
- **Team**: the Brains Trust (Moley, Tugwell, Berle); Perkins, Ickes, Wallace, Hopkins, Douglas; mixed strands (planners, anti-monopolists, budget-balancers, inflationists); big Democratic majorities in Congress.
- **First Hundred Days** (9 March–16 June 1933): about fifteen major laws. Executive actions: the bank holiday, gold orders, fireside chats. Laws: Economy Act, CCC, FERA, AAA, TVA, Securities Act, HOLC, Banking Act (FDIC), NIRA (NRA and PWA).
- **Agriculture**: AAA raised prices by cutting output; income up about half 1932–35; landlords gained, tenants and sharecroppers often lost; Butler struck down the tax (January 1936).
- **Industry**: NRA codes, Section 7(a), Blue Eagle; some gains in hours, wages and child labour; big-business control; strikes in 1934; Schechter ended it (27 May 1935).
- **Finance**: FDIC (from 1 January 1934), Glass–Steagall, SEC (June 1934): the lasting successes.
- **Jobs and relief**: CCC ($30 a month), FERA ($500 million), CWA (4 million), HOLC; federal responsibility for relief, but unequal.
- **Record**: unemployment (Lebergott) 24.9 per cent (1933) to 16.9 (1936); still 14.3 per cent in 1937; leaving gold and ending the panic mattered more than the headline agencies.
- **Critics**: Left (Long's Share Our Wealth, Coughlin, Townsend, Socialists, Communists) a threat to Roosevelt's votes; Right (Republicans, Southern Democrats, business, the Liberty League of August 1934, the press) a threat to the New Deal's powers.
- **The Court**: the Four Horsemen and two swing votes; Schechter (9–0), Butler (6–3); the court plan of 5 February 1937 is analysed in the next chapter.
- **Judgement**: the First New Deal stabilised the economy and reformed finance more than it revived it.
:::
