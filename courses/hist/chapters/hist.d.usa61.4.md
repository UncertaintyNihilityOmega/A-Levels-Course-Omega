:::why
In 1960 most Americans trusted their government, their police, their universities and their families to know best. By 1970 millions did not. Women demanded equal pay and control of their own bodies, Black Americans spoke of power and pride, gay men and women fought back against a police raid, Native Americans occupied an island and a village, students marched against a war, and cities burned. At Kent State, National Guardsmen shot dead four students. ## Why were there so many challenges? Four tests

The question asks *why*, so this chapter compares the movements using four tests:
1. **Whose authority was challenged?** The law, the government, the police, the family, the university, the Church, or the idea of who counts as a full citizen.
2. **What did the challengers want?** Reform within the system (equal pay, a vote on the war) or a change of the system itself (revolution, separation).
3. **How effective were they?** Did they change laws, attitudes or lives, and did the change last?
4. **How did the authorities answer?** With concession, with repression, or with both.

A final question links the whole chapter: *why then?* Many movements grew at once in the same years, and the common causes are as important as the particular ones. They are drawn together at the end.

## The aims, methods and achievements of the feminist movement

### The background: the "problem that has no name"

In 1960 American women had the vote, and 37.7 per cent of those aged 16 and over were in the labour force (in paid work or looking for it), but the law and custom still treated them as secondary. Employers advertised jobs under "male" and "female" headings. Most women who worked held clerical, teaching, nursing or factory jobs, and women's median pay was about 60 per cent of men's. Banks could refuse a woman credit without her husband's signature. In many states abortion was a crime. Yet more women were going to college and going out to work, and the birth-control pill, approved for sale in 1960, gave them control over childbearing for the first time. The gap between what women were and what the culture expected them to be was growing.

Three events in the early 1960s opened the door. Kennedy set up the President's Commission on the Status of Women in 1961 (see [Post-war USA (3)](#hist.d.usa45.3)), which reported in 1963 that women suffered widespread discrimination. The **Equal Pay Act (10 June 1963)** required equal pay for equal work in the same workplace. And **Betty Friedan**, a writer and a former student of psychology, published *The Feminine Mystique* in February 1963. It argued that American women had been sold a "mystique" of fulfilment through housework, marriage and motherhood, and that it left many of them unhappy. It sold in very large numbers and persuaded many women that their private discontent had a public cause.

Then came a legal accident. When the Civil Rights Bill of 1964 was in the House, Howard W. Smith of Virginia, an opponent of the bill, proposed adding "sex" to the list of banned grounds of discrimination in employment in Title VII, and it was kept in (see [A time of challenges (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3)). The Act now banned sex discrimination at work, but the new Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC) did little at first to enforce the ban, and it did not at once stop newspapers printing separate "male" and "female" job advertisements. Women who had expected a federal agency to enforce the law found that they would have to organise to make it work.

### Betty Friedan, NOW and the liberal feminists

:::def NOW
The National Organization for Women, founded in June 1966 by Betty Friedan and other women at a conference in Washington, and formally launched in October 1966 with Friedan as its first president. It aimed to bring women into full participation in American society in equal partnership with men, using the methods of the civil rights movement: lobbying, lawsuits and public pressure.
:::

Friedan and others, among them the lawyer **Pauli Murray**, who had written about "Jane Crow", the sex-based counterpart of racial segregation, founded NOW because the EEOC would not act. They called it "an NAACP for women". Its aims were to enforce Title VII, to win equal pay and equal access to jobs and education, to secure maternity leave, child care and tax allowances for child care, and, from 1967, the **Equal Rights Amendment (ERA)** and the repeal of abortion laws. Its leaders were mostly educated, professional and middle-class women, and its methods were **legal and political**: lawsuits against airlines and employers that dismissed women for marrying or becoming pregnant, protests against the sex-segregated job advertisements, lobbying of Congress and presidents, and the organisation of the **Women's Strike for Equality** on 26 August 1970, the fiftieth anniversary of votes for women, when tens of thousands marched in New York and in other cities.

NOW's decision to demand the repeal of abortion laws split it: some members left in 1968 to found the Women's Equity Action League, which concentrated on jobs and education. This shows an early problem of the movement, that women disagreed about priorities.

### The women's liberation movement

:::def Women's liberation
The name given to the radical wing of the women's movement of the late 1960s, made up mostly of younger women, many of whom had been in the civil rights movement or the New Left (the radical student movement of the 1960s, which wanted more than the older left's trade-union politics; see [A time of challenges (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3)). It argued that women's oppression was found in the family, in sexuality and in culture as well as in law, and that it required a change in society and not only in the law.
:::

Younger women had learned organising skills in the civil rights movement and the student New Left, and many were angry that male leaders gave women the typing and the coffee while men made the speeches. They formed their own groups from 1967–68, such as the New York Radical Women and, later, the Redstockings. Their characteristic method was **consciousness-raising**: small groups of women met to talk about their own lives and found that experiences they thought of as private, such as unequal housework, sexual pressure, violence and unwanted pregnancy, were shared. Their slogan was that "the personal is political". They also used dramatic, symbolic protest. On **7 September 1968** about 400 women demonstrated at the Miss America pageant in Atlantic City, crowning a sheep and throwing items such as girdles, bras and curlers into a "Freedom Trash Can". No bras were burned, though the press later used the "bra-burner" image to mock the movement. Other strands included health (the Boston Women's Health Book Collective's *Our Bodies, Ourselves*, first published under that title in 1971), writing (Kate Millett's *Sexual Politics* and the anthology *Sisterhood Is Powerful*, both 1970), the first rape crisis centres and refuges for women who had been beaten, and women's studies courses on campuses. *Ms.* magazine, edited by Gloria Steinem, began publishing in 1972 as a mainstream voice for the movement.

The two wings differed in style and aim, but they overlapped, and the difference should not be exaggerated.

### Achievements and impact

Between 1963 and 1974 the movement won important changes, in law above all.
- **Employment and pay.** Title VII (1964); Executive Order 11375 (1967), which added sex to the ban on discrimination by federal contractors; and the Equal Employment Opportunity Act of 1972, which gave the EEOC power to sue.
- **Education.** **Title IX** of the Education Amendments of 1972 (23 June 1972) barred sex discrimination in any education programme that received federal money, which opened college admissions, scholarships and, over time, sport to women.
- **The courts.** In *Reed v. Reed* (22 November 1971) the Supreme Court struck down a state law for discriminating on grounds of sex for the first time.
- **The ERA.** Congress passed the Equal Rights Amendment by large majorities in 1971–72 and sent it to the states on **22 March 1972**. It would have said that equality of rights "shall not be denied or abridged" because of sex. Thirty states ratified it by the end of 1973 and 35 by 1977, three short of the 38 (three-quarters of the states) that it needed.
- **Credit.** The Equal Credit Opportunity Act (28 October 1974) banned discrimination in credit on grounds of sex or marital status.
- ***Roe v. Wade*** (**22 January 1973**). Norma McCorvey ("Jane Roe") challenged Texas's ban on abortion. By seven votes to two, in an opinion written by Justice Harry Blackmun, the Supreme Court held that the constitutional right to privacy covers a woman's decision to end a pregnancy. The Court's rule was that states could not ban abortion in the first three months, could regulate it in the second three months to protect the woman's health, and could limit it in the last three months except where her life or health was at risk. The companion case, *Doe v. Bolton*, was decided the same day. Some states had already reformed their laws, and *Roe* made the change national. (The Supreme Court overruled *Roe* in 2022, which belongs to a later history.)

Life changed less than the law. Women's labour-force participation rose from 37.7 per cent in 1960 to 43.3 per cent in 1970 and 45.7 per cent in 1974 (aged 16 and over, the same base throughout), but women's median pay was about 60.7 per cent of men's in 1960 and about 59.4 per cent in 1970, so it did not improve. The chart shows only four years and says nothing about the kinds of jobs women held, which were mainly low-paid, or about the unpaid work at home that most women still did. Attitudes shifted fast: the idea that a woman could have a career and a family became normal in the 1970s, and a generation of women entered law, medicine and business whose mothers had not. The achievements were therefore greatest in **law, education and attitudes**, and smallest in **pay and the sharing of housework**.

### Opposition to the women's movement

The movement met strong opposition, and most of it came from other women as well as men.
- **Phyllis Schlafly**, a conservative Republican activist from Illinois, organised **STOP ERA** in 1972 (the letters stood for "Stop Taking Our Privileges"). She argued that the ERA would take away women's legal protections, such as the claim to financial support from a husband, and would make women liable for the draft and for combat, and that feminism was hostile to the family. She mobilised conservative and religious women, in churches and local groups, in the states that had not yet ratified.
- **Religious opposition.** The Catholic Church and conservative Protestant churches opposed abortion, and after *Roe* the right-to-life movement organised against it.
- **Social and economic conservatism.** Many men and women disliked the idea of changing the roles of the sexes, and many employers resisted equal pay.
- **Ridicule and hostility in the media**, with the "bra-burning" image, which made the movement seem extreme.
- **Divisions within the movement.** Black women, working-class women and lesbians felt that the leadership, mostly white and middle-class, did not speak for them.

The opposition did not reverse the changes in law, but it stopped the ERA, and it created a political division, over abortion and the family, that was to shape American politics in the years after 1974 (see [The USA, 1974–93 (2)](#hist.d.usa74.2)).

:::key Judging the women's movement
**Aims and methods:** NOW (1966): equal rights within the system by law, lobbying and lawsuits; women's liberation (1967–): change in family, sexuality and culture, by consciousness-raising and direct action.
**Achievements:** Equal Pay Act (1963); Title VII (1964); Title IX and the Equal Employment Opportunity Act (1972); the ERA passed by Congress (1972); *Roe v. Wade* (1973); changed attitudes; women in education and the professions.
**Limits:** pay ratio about 60 per cent in 1960 and about 59 per cent in 1970; the ERA short of three states; divisions of race, class and sexuality.
**Opposition:** Schlafly and STOP ERA (1972), religious groups, media ridicule.
**Judgement to defend:** the movement won **legal equality quickly and a change of attitudes**, but **less change in pay, work and the home**, and its victories provoked a conservative opposition.
:::

## Alternative approaches by African Americans

By the mid-1960s some Black Americans doubted that nonviolence, integration and appeals to white conscience were enough. Southern law had changed, but Northern ghettos had not, police violence went on, and civil rights workers were murdered with little punishment (see [A time of challenges (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3)). Three alternatives gave a different answer: the Nation of Islam and Malcolm X, Black Power, and the Black Panther Party.

### Malcolm X and the Nation of Islam

:::def Nation of Islam
A Black religious and political organisation founded in Detroit in 1930 and led from 1934 by Elijah Muhammad. It combined elements of Islam with a message of Black self-reliance, strict personal discipline and separation from white society, and rejected integration as a goal and nonviolence as a strategy.
:::

The Nation of Islam (NOI) taught Black pride, hard work, education and self-help. Its members avoided alcohol, drugs and crime, ran their own schools, shops and newspaper, and were known for their discipline. It taught that white society was an enemy and that Black people should live separately, and it did not take part in the civil rights campaigns. Estimates of its membership in the 1960s vary widely, but it was small compared with the NAACP and the Black churches, and it was influential beyond its numbers. The boxer Cassius Clay joined it in 1964 and took the name Muhammad Ali.

**Malcolm X** was born Malcolm Little in Omaha, Nebraska, on 19 May 1925. He joined the NOI in prison, where he served six years in the 1940s and 1950s, took the name Malcolm X to replace what he called his "slave name" and became its best-known minister in Harlem. He was a magnetic speaker, and he attacked King's nonviolence and the idea of integration. In 1963 he called the March on Washington a "farce".

In 1964 he changed. He left the NOI in March after a quarrel with Elijah Muhammad, and in April made the pilgrimage to Mecca, where he saw Muslims of every race together and said that he now rejected the idea that all white people were enemies. He founded the Organization of Afro-American Unity in June 1964, modelled on the Organization of African Unity, and wanted to link the American struggle to the anticolonial movements of Africa and Asia, and to take the case of Black Americans to the United Nations as a matter of human rights. His speech "The Ballot or the Bullet" (3 April 1964) told Black Americans to use their vote and warned that if they were denied it they might turn to armed self-defence, and at the founding rally of the new organisation he said that the struggle must be pursued "by any means necessary". On **21 February 1965** he was shot dead while speaking at the Audubon Ballroom in Harlem, aged 39; three men were convicted of the murder in 1966, but two of them were cleared in 2021 after a new investigation, and who exactly planned and ordered it remains debated.

His importance was greater after his death. *The Autobiography of Malcolm X*, written with the journalist Alex Haley and published in 1965, was widely read and inspired a generation of young Black activists, among them Stokely Carmichael and Huey Newton, who took from him the ideas of pride, self-defence, and the link between Black struggle and the Third World (the poor, newly independent countries of Africa and Asia). He was not a leader of a mass movement, but he changed the terms of debate.

### Stokely Carmichael and the growth of Black Power

**Stokely Carmichael**, born in Trinidad in 1941 and raised in New York, studied at Howard University and joined the Freedom Rides and Freedom Summer. He helped to organise Black voters in Lowndes County, Alabama, in 1965, where the new party, the Lowndes County Freedom Organization (a local Black political party), took a black panther as its emblem. By 1966 he, like many in SNCC, was disillusioned by the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party's rejection at Atlantic City in 1964 and by the continuing violence (see [A time of challenges (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3)). He replaced John Lewis as chairman of SNCC in May 1966.

In June 1966 James Meredith began a "March Against Fear" through Mississippi and was shot and wounded early in the march. Other leaders, including King, Carmichael and Floyd McKissick of CORE, took up the march. On **16 June 1966**, after Carmichael had been arrested in Greenwood, he told a rally there that Black people should demand "Black Power". The slogan spread at once.

:::def Black Power
A slogan and a movement of the later 1960s calling for Black pride, self-determination and political and economic power controlled by Black communities themselves. It rejected the idea that Black Americans must win acceptance from white society by moral persuasion alone, and stressed unity, community control and the right to self-defence.
:::

Carmichael and the political scientist Charles V. Hamilton set out the idea in the book *Black Power: The Politics of Liberation* (1967). The movement meant different things to different people:
- **Political:** Black organisation and voting power, Black candidates for office, and community control of schools and police. In 1967 Carl Stokes in Cleveland and Richard Hatcher in Gary, Indiana, became the first Black mayors of large American cities, and Kenneth Gibson won in Newark in 1970.
- **Cultural:** pride in Black history and appearance ("Black is beautiful", the Afro hairstyle, African dress), the Black Arts Movement, and music such as James Brown's "Say It Loud, I'm Black and I'm Proud" (1968). At the Mexico City Olympics on 16 October 1968 two American sprinters, Tommie Smith and John Carlos, raised black-gloved fists on the medal stand.
- **Educational:** Black Studies programmes, won by student protest, such as the strike at San Francisco State College in 1968–69, which led to one of the first Black Studies departments.
- **Economic:** Black-owned business and "Black capitalism", which President Nixon himself supported, creating an Office of Minority Business Enterprise in 1969.
- **Separatist and revolutionary** strands, which called for a separate Black nation or for armed struggle, were a minority.

SNCC under Carmichael and then H. Rap Brown moved away from nonviolence and, in 1966–67, away from white members, and it fell apart in the following years. Carmichael himself left the United States in 1969 for Guinea.

### Bobby Seale, Huey Newton and the Black Panther Party

The **Black Panther Party for Self-Defense** was founded in **October 1966** in Oakland, California, by two college students, **Huey Newton** and **Bobby Seale**. The name came from the Lowndes County organisation. Oakland's Black residents faced a mostly white police force with a reputation for violence, and the Panthers' first act was to patrol the police: they followed patrol cars with law books and, as California law then allowed, loaded guns openly carried, and watched arrests to make sure the police obeyed the law. On 2 May 1967 about thirty Panthers walked armed into the state capitol in Sacramento to protest a bill to ban loaded weapons in public, which became law as the Mulford Act. The pictures made them known across the country.

:::note Evidence: the Black Panther Party's Ten-Point Program
"We want freedom. We want power to determine the destiny of our Black Community."

*From point 1 of the Ten-Point Program, "What We Want, What We Believe", of the Black Panther Party for Self-Defense, Oakland, California, October 1966, as reproduced by the US National Archives. Quoted exactly. It was written by Newton and Seale to set out the party's aims for Black communities and for the authorities who feared it; the other points asked for jobs, decent housing, education, an end to police brutality, release of Black prisoners and fair trials, and exemption of Black men from the military draft.*
:::

The Panthers' **political activities** were radical and wide in aim: the Ten-Point Program, which ended with a call for "land, bread, housing, education, clothing, justice and peace", a socialist outlook influenced by Marx, Mao and the African revolutionaries, and alliances with white radicals, such as the Peace and Freedom Party, for which the Panther Eldridge Cleaver stood for President in 1968. Their language was often violent ("off the pigs"), and there were fatal shootouts with the police, including the one in October 1967 in which Newton was wounded and a police officer was killed and for which he was convicted of manslaughter (the conviction was overturned on appeal).

The **social programmes**, which the Panthers called "survival programs", were their most lasting work and were in several respects the most remarkable. The **Free Breakfast for Children Program**, started in Oakland in January 1969, spread to chapters across the country and fed many thousands of children a week. Others followed: free medical clinics, testing for sickle cell anaemia, free clothing and shoes, legal aid, "liberation schools" and a bus service for relatives of prisoners. By 1969 the party had several thousand members and chapters in dozens of cities.

The authorities answered with force. FBI Director J. Edgar Hoover publicly called the Panthers a grave danger to the country's internal security, and the FBI's secret **COINTELPRO** programme (counterintelligence), extended to "Black nationalist" groups in 1967, set out to disrupt the party with informers, false rumours and forged letters. Local police raided Panther offices. On 4 December 1969 Chicago police raided the apartment of the 21-year-old Chicago leader **Fred Hampton** and killed him and another Panther, Mark Clark; the raid used information supplied by an FBI informant, and later investigations found that the police had fired almost all the shots. By the early 1970s many leaders were dead, in prison or in exile, and the party also split, partly because of the FBI's efforts and partly because of quarrels between Newton and Eldridge Cleaver, and it faded by the mid-1970s.

### Opposition to Black Power

Opposition came from many directions.
- **Other civil rights leaders.** Roy Wilkins of the NAACP and Whitney Young of the Urban League criticised the slogan as divisive, and King, who shared the call for pride and for political organisation, disliked the slogan, which he said could be heard as a call for violence or separation. The movement's coalition with white supporters broke up.
- **White backlash.** Many white Americans, shocked by the riots and by the language of Black Power, turned towards "law and order". George Wallace won 13.5 per cent of the vote in 1968 and Richard Nixon won on a law-and-order campaign.
- **The government.** The Anti-Riot provisions of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, the FBI's COINTELPRO, and local police repression.
- **The media**, which showed the Panthers' guns and the militant words more than their breakfasts.
- **Many Black Americans**, who were religious, middle class or cautious and did not support the militants, even if they shared some of their anger.

:::key Judging Black Power and the Panthers
**Aims:** Black pride, self-determination, community control; the Panthers' Ten-Point Program (freedom, jobs, housing, education, an end to police brutality).
**Methods:** political organisation (Lowndes County, Newark); cultural change; Black Studies; armed patrols and rhetoric (Panthers); survival programmes (free breakfasts, clinics).
**Achievements:** cultural pride and identity; the first Black mayors of big cities (1967, 1970); Black Studies; community programmes; a changed debate on race.
**Limits and opposition:** divisions among Black leaders; white backlash; the FBI's COINTELPRO; police violence (Hampton, 4 December 1969); no large change in jobs or housing; the Panthers' own violence and splits.
**Judgement to defend:** Black Power **changed attitudes and politics** more than it changed material conditions, and the Panthers' community work was more lasting than their armed protest.
:::

## The emergence of identity politics

:::def Identity politics
A style of politics in which people organise around a shared identity, such as race, sex, sexuality or ethnicity, which has been the basis of their disadvantage, in order to claim rights, respect and power as a group. In the 1960s it grew from the civil rights movement, whose methods and language other groups borrowed.
:::

### Discrimination and the struggle for lesbian, gay and transgender rights

In 1961 homosexual acts were crimes in every state except Illinois, which had decriminalised them that year. Under an executive order of 1953 homosexuals were barred from federal employment, as supposed security risks, and thousands lost their jobs. The armed forces dismissed them, the medical profession classed homosexuality as a mental illness, police raided gay bars and sometimes published the names of those arrested, and most gay people hid their identity. A few small "homophile" groups (the name the cautious early gay rights groups used for themselves), among them the **Mattachine Society** (1950) and the **Daughters of Bilitis** (1955), campaigned for acceptance in a cautious and respectable way, and in the 1960s activists such as Frank Kameny held small, formally dressed pickets in Washington and Philadelphia. Transgender people had no legal recognition and faced especially severe harassment; in August 1966 transgender women and others fought back against a police raid at Compton's Cafeteria in San Francisco.

**Stonewall.** In the early hours of **Saturday 28 June 1969** police raided the Stonewall Inn, a gay bar on Christopher Street in Greenwich Village, New York. Raids on such bars were routine, and customers usually left quietly. This time a crowd gathered outside and refused to disperse. It threw objects at the police, who barricaded themselves in the bar, and there was fighting in the streets for several nights. Those who took part included gay men, lesbians, drag queens and transgender people, many of them young and poor, and accounts differ about who did what on the first night. The riots were not the beginning of the movement, since the homophile groups were more than a decade old, but they changed its mood: after Stonewall, many activists argued that gay people should stop hiding and begin to fight.

**The Gay Liberation Front and the Gay Activists Alliance.** The **Gay Liberation Front (GLF)** was formed in New York in July 1969. It was radical, borrowed the language of Black Power and of the New Left, called on people to "come out", and linked gay rights to a wider revolution against racism, war and sexism. Within months, some activists who wanted a more focused and disciplined organisation broke away to form the **Gay Activists Alliance (GAA)** in December 1969. It concentrated on gay rights, had formal rules, and used public confrontations called "zaps" to embarrass politicians. On 28 June 1970, the first anniversary, the first Pride marches, called Christopher Street Liberation Day, took place in New York, and that weekend in Chicago, Los Angeles and San Francisco as well.

**Impact by 1974.** Hundreds of gay organisations were formed in the following years, in colleges and cities, and the movement began to win some changes. The American Psychiatric Association's board voted in December 1973 to remove homosexuality from its list of mental disorders. A handful of states decriminalised homosexual acts in the early 1970s, and gay candidates began to stand for office, but most states did not decriminalise until much later (the Supreme Court did so for the whole country only in 2003). Discrimination in employment, housing and the military continued, and the movement faced strong opposition from many churches, police forces and politicians. There were tensions inside it, too: transgender activists such as **Sylvia Rivera** and **Marsha P. Johnson**, who in 1970 founded a group to shelter homeless young transgender people, felt pushed to the edge of the movement they had helped to start, and Rivera was shouted down at the New York Pride rally in 1973.

### Problems facing Native Americans

By the 1960s Native Americans were among the poorest and least healthy people in the United States. Reservation unemployment was many times the national rate, housing and schools were poor, and life expectancy was well below the national average. Much of the land guaranteed by treaties had been lost. In 1953 Congress, in House Concurrent Resolution 108, adopted a policy of **termination**: ending the federal government's special relationship with Native nations, and with it their status and their federal services, at the earliest possible time. Between 1953 and the mid-1960s it terminated 109 tribes and bands, among them the Menominee of Wisconsin and the Klamath of Oregon, whose poverty grew. A parallel **relocation** programme encouraged Native people to leave reservations for cities such as Los Angeles, Chicago and Minneapolis, where many found low-paid work, poor housing and discrimination and not the promised opportunities.

New Native organisations appeared in response. The National Indian Youth Council was formed in 1961, and in June 1961 a conference of about 450 Native people from about ninety tribes issued a "Declaration of Indian Purpose", calling for a say in policy. Johnson's message "The Forgotten American" (6 March 1968) called for an end to termination, and the **Indian Civil Rights Act of 1968**, passed as part of that year's Civil Rights Act, applied much of the Bill of Rights to tribal governments. In July 1970 President Nixon rejected termination and called for self-determination, the Taos Pueblo regained its sacred Blue Lake in December 1970, and the **Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act** (December 1971) gave Alaska Natives title to land and a payment of money. But the main push came from Native activists themselves.

### The American Indian Movement

The **American Indian Movement (AIM)** was founded in **Minneapolis in July 1968** by Dennis Banks, Clyde Bellecourt, George Mitchell and others, to protect urban Native people from police harassment, by patrolling the streets and monitoring arrests, in the manner of the Black Panthers. It soon addressed wider issues of treaties, land and tribal rights, and became the best-known organisation of "Red Power".
- **Alcatraz, 1969–71.** On **20 November 1969** about 80 Native people, calling themselves Indians of All Tribes, landed on Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay, an abandoned federal prison, and claimed it under an old treaty that returned unused federal land to Native people. They offered to buy it for $24 in glass beads and cloth, and demanded a Native university and cultural centre. The occupation lasted until **11 June 1971**, nearly nineteen months, when the government removed the last few occupants. The island was not given to them, but the occupation won wide sympathy and helped to change Nixon's policy.
- **The Trail of Broken Treaties, 1972.** In November 1972 a convoy of Native activists drove to Washington to present demands and occupied the headquarters of the Bureau of Indian Affairs for several days.
- **Wounded Knee, 1973.** On **27 February 1973** about 200 AIM members and Oglala Lakota from the Pine Ridge Reservation in South Dakota seized the village of Wounded Knee, the site of the massacre of 1890. They were protesting against the tribal chairman, Richard Wilson, whom they accused of corruption and violence, and against the federal government's broken treaties. FBI agents and US Marshals surrounded the village with armoured vehicles, and there were exchanges of fire. Two Native men were killed. The standoff lasted **71 days**, until 8 May 1973, and the world watched it on television. On 27 March 1973 the actor Marlon Brando refused his Academy Award and sent the actress Sacheen Littlefeather to explain that he was protesting at the treatment of Native Americans by the film industry and at Wounded Knee. In 1974 a federal judge dismissed the charges against the AIM leaders Dennis Banks and Russell Means, citing misconduct by the government.

The results were mixed. AIM's protests brought Native grievances into national view, helped to win the end of termination, and led to the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975, which gave tribes more control of their own services. But poverty on the reservations stayed severe, there was violence at Pine Ridge in the years after Wounded Knee, and AIM was divided and was harassed by the FBI.

**Why it emerged:** the example of civil rights; discrimination and exclusion; a new generation willing to protest openly; the New Left and Black Power as models.
**Gay rights:** Stonewall (28 June 1969), GLF (July 1969), GAA (December 1969), Pride marches (1970), the APA vote (December 1973); limits: most states still criminalised homosexual acts, tensions over transgender people and women.
**Native Americans:** termination (1953), AIM (July 1968), Alcatraz (20 November 1969 to 11 June 1971), Wounded Knee (27 February to 8 May 1973); Nixon's change of policy (1970); limits: poverty remained.
**Judgement to defend:** these movements **won visibility and an end to the worst policies** (termination, the psychiatric label) but little material change by 1974, and they showed that the language of civil rights could be used by many groups.

## Popular protests and the reaction of the authorities

### The anti-Vietnam War movement

American combat troops began to arrive in large numbers in March 1965, and by 1968 there were more than half a million in Vietnam (see [A time of challenges (2)](#hist.d.usa61.2)). Opposition began on the campuses and grew steadily.
- **Students and the New Left.** **Students for a Democratic Society (SDS)**, founded in 1960, issued the Port Huron Statement in 1962, which called for "participatory democracy", and by 1965 turned to the war. Teaching sessions called **teach-ins** began at the University of Michigan on 24–25 March 1965, and on 17 April 1965 SDS led about 20,000 people in a march on Washington. At Berkeley the Free Speech Movement (1964) had already shown that students would challenge the university's authority, and many of its leaders had been in Mississippi in Freedom Summer.
- **Draft resistance.** The draft made the war personal. Young men burned their draft cards, fled to Canada or went to prison, and in April 1967 the heavyweight champion Muhammad Ali refused induction and was stripped of his title. On 21 October 1967 tens of thousands marched on the Pentagon (the US defence headquarters, near Washington).
- **Wider opposition.** On 4 April 1967 King spoke against the war at Riverside Church in New York, linking it to poverty at home. After the **Tet Offensive** (January 1968) many ordinary Americans began to doubt that the war could be won, and on 31 March 1968 Johnson said that he would not seek re-election.
- **Mass protest.** On **15 October 1969**, the first national Moratorium, millions took part in demonstrations, vigils and meetings across the country, and on 15 November hundreds of thousands marched in Washington. Veterans joined in: in April 1971 members of Vietnam Veterans Against the War threw their medals on the steps of the Capitol.

Nixon's reply was to appeal over the heads of the protesters. In a television address on **3 November 1969** he spoke of a "silent majority" of Americans who supported him, and his Vice-President, Spiro Agnew, attacked protesters and the press. The authorities also used the law and secret surveillance: the FBI's COINTELPRO programme extended to the New Left in 1968, and the CIA's Operation CHAOS spied on anti-war groups from 1967; a Senate inquiry condemned both in 1975–76.

### The Democratic National Convention, Chicago, 1968, and the reaction of Mayor Daley

1968 was the worst year of the decade: the Tet Offensive, Johnson's withdrawal (31 March), King's murder (4 April), the murder of Senator Robert Kennedy (shot on 5 June, died on 6 June), and, from 26 to 29 August, the **Democratic National Convention** in Chicago, which was to choose Vice-President Hubert Humphrey as the candidate. Thousands of anti-war protesters, including SDS members, the National Mobilization Committee to End the War in Vietnam, and the **Yippies**, a group of theatrical radicals led by Abbie Hoffman and Jerry Rubin, came to protest. Fewer than 10,000 came, far fewer than the organisers had hoped.

**Richard J. Daley**, the Democratic Mayor of Chicago and a powerful party boss, was determined that there would be no disorder. He refused most permits for marches and for camping in the parks, and he put about 23,000 police, National Guardsmen and soldiers into the city. The week was full of clashes. On the night of **28 August** police attacked a crowd of protesters and bystanders outside the Conrad Hilton hotel, in front of television cameras, beating many with batons and using tear gas, and the protesters chanted "The whole world is watching". Hundreds were injured and hundreds were arrested.

An inquiry led by the Chicago lawyer **Daniel Walker** for a presidential commission published its findings on 1 December 1968 as *Rights in Conflict*, the "Walker Report". It found that the protesters had provoked the police with obscenities and thrown objects, but that the response of many police was "a police riot": indiscriminate violence against protesters, bystanders, journalists and medical helpers. Daley rejected such criticism. He defended the police, blamed outside agitators and the news media, and said that the city had been protected from "terrorists". He did not lose the support of many Chicagoans: opinion polls and letters showed that many Americans, especially older and white ones, approved of what the police had done, which is one sign that the protests had divided the country.

The effects were lasting. The Democratic Party was split before the election; Humphrey was nominated with the war unresolved, and Nixon won in November on a promise of "law and order". In 1969 eight protest leaders (the "Chicago Eight") were tried for conspiracy and for crossing state lines to incite a riot, under the anti-riot provision of the Civil Rights Act of 1968, and the trial became a platform for the defendants' contempt of the court. The convictions were later overturned.

### Kent State, 1970

On **30 April 1970** Nixon announced that US troops had entered Cambodia, which seemed to many students to widen the war he had promised to wind down. At **Kent State University** in Ohio there were protests from 1 May, and on the night of 2 May the campus ROTC building (the university's Reserve Officers' Training Corps, which trained student officers for the army) was burned. Ohio's governor sent in the National Guard. On **4 May**, a Monday, about 2,000 people gathered at a rally on the campus, despite a ban, and Guardsmen, with bayonets, tear gas and loaded rifles, moved to disperse them. After a confrontation, a group of Guardsmen turned and fired. The shooting lasted about 13 seconds, and at least 61 shots were fired. **Allison Krause** (19), **Jeffrey Miller** (20), **Sandra Scheuer** (20) and **William Schroeder** (19) were killed, and nine other students were wounded. Some of the dead were not taking part in the protest but were walking between classes.

The impact was immediate and national. About four million students, by one estimate, joined a strike, and hundreds of colleges and universities closed or were disrupted. Tens of thousands marched in Washington on 9 May. The Scranton Commission, appointed by Nixon, concluded in September 1970 that the shootings were unjustified. Yet public opinion at the time was mixed, and polls showed that many Americans blamed the students and not the Guard. In 1974 a federal judge dismissed the charges against eight Guardsmen who had been tried for violating the students' rights. The killings made many Americans doubt the authorities' reaction to dissent. A second shooting went almost unnoticed: on the night of 14–15 May 1970, police fired on a women's dormitory at Jackson State College, a Black college in Mississippi, killing two people, Phillip Gibbs and James Earl Green, and wounding twelve more. The contrast in the attention they received was noted at the time.

### Inner-city riots

The riots of the 1960s were different from the protests of students: they were spontaneous outbursts, mostly in the poor Black neighbourhoods of Northern and Western cities, with looting and arson, often after an incident with the police.

- **Watts, Los Angeles, August 1965.** The Voting Rights Act had been signed five days earlier. On 11 August a police traffic stop of a young Black man, Marquette Frye, in the Watts district led to a crowd, a fight, and then six days of rioting. About 14,000 National Guardsmen were deployed. Thirty-four people died, more than 1,000 were injured and between about 3,400 and about 4,000 were arrested (sources differ). Watts had high unemployment, poor schools, a police force with a bad reputation among Black residents, and in 1964 Californian voters had approved a measure that overturned a fair housing law. A state commission under John McCone blamed unemployment, poor schooling and resentment of the police.
- **Newark and Detroit, July 1967.** In Newark, 12–17 July, after the arrest of a Black taxi driver, 26 people died. In Detroit, on 23 July, a police raid on an after-hours club began five days of violence in which 43 people died and more than 7,000 were arrested. Johnson sent in thousands of federal paratroopers, the first use of federal troops in a Northern city since 1943, and on 27 July set up the **National Advisory Commission on Civil Disorders**, chaired by Governor Otto Kerner of Illinois.
- **April 1968.** After King was shot dead in Memphis on the evening of 4 April, riots broke out in more than 100 cities. About 40 people died and thousands were arrested. In Washington, more than 13,000 federal troops were sent to the capital, and on 15 April Chicago's Mayor Daley told the police to "shoot to kill" arsonists and to "shoot to maim or cripple" looters, an order widely criticised.

The **Kerner Commission** reported on 29 February 1968. It rejected the idea that the riots were a conspiracy and said that their cause was white racism and its effects: segregated housing and schools, high unemployment, and hostility between Black communities and the police. It called for large programmes of jobs, housing and reform of the police. The words of its main conclusion are quoted in [A time of challenges (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3). Johnson, who thought that the report ignored what he had already done, gave it a cool reception, and Congress did not provide the money.

The authorities' answer to the riots was **force and then law and order**. Troops, curfews and mass arrests were used to end each riot, and after the April 1968 riots Congress passed the Omnibus Crime Control and Safe Streets Act (19 June 1968), giving money to local police, and the anti-riot provision of the Civil Rights Act of 1968 (see [A time of challenges (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3)). The riots swung white opinion, especially in the suburbs, towards "law and order", and helped Nixon win in 1968, even though the Kerner report called for reform.

:::key Judging protests and the authorities' reaction
**Anti-war movement:** SDS (1960), teach-ins (March 1965), draft resistance, Moratorium (15 October 1969); the authorities answered with Nixon's "silent majority" (3 November 1969), Agnew, and the FBI's COINTELPRO and the CIA's Operation CHAOS.
**Chicago, 1968:** about 23,000 police, Guardsmen and soldiers; the police attack on 28 August; the Walker Report (1 December 1968) called it a "police riot"; Daley defended the police; the party split and Nixon won.
**Kent State, 4 May 1970:** four students killed in about 13 seconds; a national student strike; Scranton Commission; Jackson State (14–15 May) received less attention.
**Riots:** Watts (11 August 1965; 34 dead), Newark (July 1967; 26 dead), Detroit (July 1967; 43 dead), April 1968 (over 100 cities); Kerner (29 February 1968); the answer was troops and then law and order.
**Judgement to defend:** the authorities' **use of force turned some protest into mass opposition** (Chicago, Kent State) but also produced a **backlash that benefited Nixon**, and the Kerner Commission's recommendations were not carried out.
:::

## Why did challenges to authority grow in the 1960s?

Many reasons contributed, and an "Assess the reasons" question asks you to compare them.

| Reason | How it worked | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **The civil rights example** | It showed that organised protest could win, and provided tactics (sit-ins, marches), language ("freedom", "liberation") and experienced organisers | Many leaders of SDS and of the women's liberation groups had worked in SNCC or other civil rights projects; AIM copied the Panthers' police patrols; Berkeley 1964 |
| **A young, educated and affluent generation** | The baby boomers (about 76 million born 1946–64) entered college in huge numbers, with the security to question authority | The number of college students more than doubled in the 1960s; SDS and the campus protests |
| **The Vietnam War and the draft** | It made politics personal, and the "credibility gap" between official claims and the television pictures destroyed trust | Teach-ins (1965), Moratorium (1969), Tet (1968), Kent State (1970) |
| **Rising expectations and unfulfilled promises** | The Civil Rights Acts and the Great Society raised hopes that the changes did not meet | Watts (1965, five days after the Voting Rights Act); Kerner (1968); Black Power |
| **Anger at discrimination and conformity** | Women, Black Americans, gay people and Native Americans met specific injustice, and the 1950s ideal of conformity made it harder to bear | *The Feminine Mystique*, Stonewall, termination |
| **Television and the media** | Pictures of Birmingham, Chicago and Kent State spread protest and showed the authorities' reaction | "The whole world is watching" |
| **Loss of trust in leaders** | Assassinations (Kennedy 1963, Malcolm X 1965, King and Robert Kennedy 1968), lies about Vietnam and then Watergate | The credibility gap; the Walker Report |
| **The authorities' own reactions** | Force against peaceful protest created sympathy and radicalised some protesters | Birmingham, Chicago, Kent State, Wounded Knee |

The most important causes were **the civil rights example**, which supplied the method and the language, and **the Vietnam War**, which turned scattered dissent into mass protest and brought the middle-class young into it. Without a young and affluent generation, neither would have had the same effect, and the failure of reforms to meet the hopes they raised explains why Black and urban protest became more radical after 1965. The authorities' reactions were not a cause of the first protests, but they turned many later protests into confrontations.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'The Black Power movement achieved more than it is given credit for.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Given credit" means the common view that Black Power was a violent and divisive failure. Criteria: (1) did it achieve its own aims (pride, self-determination, community control)? (2) did the change last? (3) what did it cost? Support for the view: pride and culture; the first Black big-city mayors; Black Studies; the Panthers' survival programmes; a changed debate. Challenge: little change in jobs and housing; divisions in the movement; white backlash; the Panthers' violence and destruction by the FBI; the legal gains came from civil rights. Judgement: it achieved more than the "failure" label allows, in culture, politics and community, but little in material terms.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Black Power achieved quite a lot. Stokely Carmichael said 'Black Power' in 1966 and it made Black people feel proud. The Black Panthers gave free breakfasts to children and held armed patrols. There were Black mayors for the first time. However, there was also violence, and many white people did not like it, and the FBI attacked the Panthers. So Black Power achieved some things but not everything."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with both sides, so Table B Level 3. But the paragraph is a **list of achievements and then a list of problems**, with a "some but not everything" conclusion: no criteria, no test of "more than it is given credit for". Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If success is judged by whether Black Power achieved its own aims, the claim is partly justified. Its aim was Black self-determination, and by 1970 Black Americans controlled the city governments of Cleveland, Gary and Newark, where Carl Stokes, Richard Hatcher and Kenneth Gibson were mayors, and the Panthers' breakfast programme, which began in Oakland in January 1969, fed children in cities across the country. But if success is judged by material equality, the picture is poorer: Black unemployment stayed about twice the white rate, and the Panthers' leaders were killed or jailed, as Fred Hampton was on 4 December 1969."

*Commentary.* Two criteria are applied and tested against evidence. This is Level 4. It still needs the contrast with the civil rights movement, a view of what was *lasting*, and a sustained judgement of the phrase "given credit".

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is partly valid. Black Power is often remembered as a violent, divisive phase that followed the real achievements of the civil rights movement and then collapsed, and on that picture it achieved little. But this underrates what it did in three areas, even though it failed in a fourth. I will judge it by three criteria: whether it achieved its own aims, whether its effects lasted, and what it cost.

On its own aims, which were pride, self-determination and community control, Black Power achieved more than its critics allow. After Stokely Carmichael's call in Greenwood on 16 June 1966, 'Black is beautiful' and the idea of Black history and culture spread through schools and colleges. Student strikes, such as that at San Francisco State in 1968–69, won Black Studies programmes. In politics, Carl Stokes in Cleveland and Richard Hatcher in Gary became mayors in 1967 and Kenneth Gibson won in Newark in 1970; the number of Black elected officials in the United States rose from about 1,500 in 1970 to about 3,500 in 1975, though much of that rise was due to the Voting Rights Act, as the 1965 Act made the new voters possible. The Panthers' Free Breakfast for Children Program, which began in January 1969, and their clinics showed that the movement could provide services to the poor that the government did not. Peniel Joseph argues that Black Power should be seen as part of the same struggle as civil rights, and the evidence for continuity, in the Lowndes County emblem, in the organisers of SNCC, supports him.

On durability, the picture is more mixed. The cultural change lasted, and so did Black political organisation in the cities. But the organisations did not. SNCC split in 1967–68, and the Panthers, which had several thousand members by 1969, were weakened by the FBI's COINTELPRO, by police raids such as that in Chicago on 4 December 1969, in which Fred Hampton was killed, and by their own quarrels, so that they had faded by the mid-1970s. On material conditions Black Power achieved little: Black poverty was still about 42 per cent in 1966, unemployment was about twice that of white Americans, and the Kerner Commission of 1968 found that the cities were still moving toward two societies. And it cost the movement support. The slogan and the Panthers' guns alarmed white opinion, helped George Wallace to take 13.5 per cent of the vote in 1968 and Nixon to win on law and order, and divided Black leaders such as Roy Wilkins from the militants.

Overall, then, the view is justified in culture, in local political power and in community work, where Black Power achieved more than its reputation suggests. It is not justified in material equality, where the gains were small, or in organisation, where the movement was broken. The fairest judgement is that Black Power was less a failed alternative to civil rights than its second phase: it did not win the laws, which came from the first phase, but it changed how Black Americans saw themselves, and that outlasted its organisations."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): three explicit criteria; balanced argument with the "given credit" claim tested directly; a historian used to support rather than decorate; a sustained, qualified judgement. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates and figures (16 June 1966; January 1969; 4 December 1969; 1,500 to 3,500; 42 per cent; 13.5 per cent), selected for the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 650 words, 35 to 40 minutes.

:::eg Assess the reasons why protest movements grew in the late 1960s. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. An "Assess the reasons" answer must weigh the reasons against each other and judge which mattered most, using criteria such as how necessary each was and how widely it acted.

**Plan.** Reasons: (1) the example of civil rights; (2) the young, educated generation; (3) the Vietnam War; (4) disappointed hopes; (5) the media; (6) loss of trust; (7) the authorities' own reaction. Criteria: was the reason *necessary* (would the protests have grown without it?) and did it affect *many* movements? Judgement: civil rights and Vietnam were the most important, with the young generation as the necessary condition.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "Protest movements grew in the late 1960s because there was the Vietnam War, which people did not like. There was also civil rights, which inspired women and gay people. There was also television so people could see what was happening. These reasons caused many protests."

*Commentary.* True, but the reasons are only named, not explained, and there is no weighing. No dates, no examples, no criteria. Table A Level 2 (4–6), Table B Level 1–2.

**Moving to Level 4: explain how each reason worked and compare.** "The most important reason was the civil rights movement, because it supplied the method and the language that other movements used. Students who had gone to Mississippi in Freedom Summer in 1964 brought their experience to the Free Speech Movement at Berkeley that autumn, and the women who formed the radical women's groups in 1967–68 had worked in SNCC and the New Left. Black Power and the American Indian Movement, founded in July 1968, copied the Panthers' patrols against the police. The Vietnam War was the second reason, because it turned scattered campus dissent into a mass movement: the draft made it personal for young men, and the Tet Offensive in 1968 made many Americans doubt the government's claims. But the war depended on the first reason, since the peace movement used the tactics of sit-ins and marches that the civil rights movement had made familiar."

*Commentary.* The reasons are explained and ranked, and the dependence between them is argued. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs a counter-weight (the young and affluent generation, the media), a clear criterion and a judgement that tests the ranking.

**A Level 5 conclusion.**

"Overall, the movements grew because of several causes working together, but they were not equal. The necessary condition was a generation of young Americans, the baby boomers, who were numerous, educated and secure enough to question authority; without them the protests of the 1960s would have been smaller, as the 1950s had been. The *method* came from civil rights, whose tactics and language every later movement borrowed, and which taught that organised protest could win. The *accelerant* was the Vietnam War, which turned scattered dissent into mass protest and brought the middle-class young into it. Disappointed hopes explain the *form* that Black and urban protest took after 1965: the riots at Watts, five days after the Voting Rights Act, and the Black Power movement came from the gap between the new laws and daily life. Television carried each protest to the whole country, and the authorities' own reactions at Chicago and Kent State turned dissent into confrontation. I judge the civil rights example and the Vietnam War to be the most important reasons, because between them they explain both how and why so many groups protested at once, and why it was the late 1960s and not the 1950s."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies a clear scheme (necessary condition, method, accelerant, form), makes a judgement that is argued and links the answer back to the dates. With paragraphs on each reason, this is Level 5.

Question: "Assess the importance of the Stonewall riots (1969) in the struggle for gay rights." [30]

A student wrote:

"The gay rights movement started with the Stonewall riots in 1969. Before then gay people had no organisation and did not protest. The police raided the bar and for the first time gay people fought back. After that they won equal rights across America. So Stonewall was the most important event in the history of gay rights."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the paragraph asserts a single cause and a single effect and ends with an unsupported claim that Stonewall was "the most important event". There are no criteria and no comparison with other causes. Table B Level 2 (4–6): the knowledge is partly wrong. Homophile groups such as the Mattachine Society (1950) and the Daughters of Bilitis (1955) had campaigned for years, and Compton's Cafeteria in San Francisco had seen resistance to police in 1966. The claim that gay people "won equal rights across America" is untrue for 1974, when most states still criminalised homosexual acts.

**The fix.** Use the same facts to prove an argument. "Stonewall did not begin the gay rights movement, which had existed since the Mattachine Society of 1950, but it changed its mood and its scale. Within weeks the Gay Liberation Front (July 1969) was calling on people to 'come out', and the Gay Activists Alliance (December 1969) was organising public 'zaps'. A year later the first Pride marches took place, on 28 June 1970. The American Psychiatric Association's decision of December 1973 to stop treating homosexuality as an illness showed the effect of this activism. Yet most states still treated homosexual acts as crimes in 1974, so Stonewall's importance lay in *mobilising* a movement, not in winning rights." Now every fact is evidence for a judgement, and the claim is limited and dated.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What did NOW aim to do, and how did it differ from the women's liberation movement?
A: NOW (founded 30 June 1966; Friedan its first president) aimed to bring women into full and equal participation in American society by law, lobbying and lawsuits, for example to enforce Title VII and win the Equal Rights Amendment. Women's liberation, made up mostly of younger women from the New Left and civil rights, wanted change in the family, sexuality and culture as well as law, and used consciousness-raising and direct action such as the Miss America protest of 7 September 1968.
Q: What did *Roe v. Wade* decide, and when?
A: On 22 January 1973, by seven votes to two, the Supreme Court held that the constitutional right to privacy covers a woman's decision to end a pregnancy: states could not ban abortion in the first three months, could regulate it in the second three and could limit it in the last three except to protect her life or health.
Q: Who led opposition to the Equal Rights Amendment, and why?
A: Phyllis Schlafly, whose STOP ERA campaign began in 1972. She argued that the ERA would take away women's legal protections (support from a husband), make women liable for the draft and undermine the family. By 1977 only 35 states had ratified, three short of the 38 needed.
Q: Why did Malcolm X change his views in 1964, and why was he influential after his death?
A: He left the Nation of Islam in March 1964 and, after the pilgrimage to Mecca in April, rejected the idea that all whites were enemies, and founded the Organization of Afro-American Unity. He was shot dead on 21 February 1965; *The Autobiography of Malcolm X* (1965) inspired later activists with its ideas of pride, self-defence and links with the Third World.
Q: What did "Black Power" mean, and who first used it as a slogan?
A: Black pride, self-determination and political and economic power controlled by Black communities. Stokely Carmichael, chairman of SNCC, called for it at a rally in Greenwood, Mississippi, on 16 June 1966.
Q: What were the Black Panthers' political activities and social programmes?
A: Politics: armed patrols against the police (from October 1966), the Ten-Point Program, alliances with white radicals. Social programmes: the Free Breakfast for Children Program (from January 1969), health clinics, sickle cell testing, free clothing and "liberation schools". The FBI's COINTELPRO and police raids (Fred Hampton, 4 December 1969) destroyed the party by the mid-1970s.
Q: What was Stonewall, and what followed?
A: On 28 June 1969 customers resisted a police raid on the Stonewall Inn in New York, and rioting lasted several nights. The Gay Liberation Front (July 1969) and Gay Activists Alliance (December 1969) were formed, the first Pride marches took place in June 1970, and the American Psychiatric Association stopped classifying homosexuality as a mental disorder in December 1973.
Q: What problems did Native Americans face, and what did AIM do?
A: Poverty, poor housing and health, lost land, termination (109 tribes and bands, 1953–mid-1960s) and relocation. AIM (July 1968) patrolled against police harassment, occupied Alcatraz (20 November 1969 to 11 June 1971) and Wounded Knee (27 February to 8 May 1973, 71 days).
Q: What happened at Chicago in August 1968 and at Kent State in May 1970?
A: In Chicago, police attacked protesters outside the Conrad Hilton on 28 August; the Walker Report (1 December 1968) called it a "police riot", and Mayor Daley defended the police. At Kent State on 4 May 1970 Ohio National Guardsmen fired for about 13 seconds and killed four students, Allison Krause, Jeffrey Miller, Sandra Scheuer and William Schroeder.
Q: Give the main causes and consequences of the Watts riots and of the riots of 1967–68.
A: Causes: unemployment, poor housing and schools, police hostility and discrimination in Northern and Western cities. Watts (11 August 1965): 34 dead; Newark (26 dead) and Detroit (43 dead) in July 1967; riots in over 100 cities after King's murder (4 April 1968). Consequences: the Kerner Commission (29 February 1968) blamed white racism, but the main response was troops and, in 1968, "law and order" and the Crime Control Act.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the impact of Betty Friedan and the National Organization for Women on the position of women in the 1960s.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: effect on law, on attitudes, on organisation. Friedan: *The Feminine Mystique* (February 1963); NOW founded June 1966, "an NAACP for women". Impact: pressure on the EEOC to enforce Title VII; lawsuits and campaigns against sex-segregated job advertisements; lobbying for the ERA and Title IX and later *Roe*; the Women's Strike for Equality (26 August 1970). Limits: the radical women's liberation movement, with different aims; splits over abortion (Women's Equity Action League, 1968); the movement's middle-class and white leadership; pay about 60 per cent of men's. Level 5 judges that NOW's impact was greatest on law and least on pay and the sharing of housework, and says what the movement would have lacked without it.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The women's movement had achieved most of its aims by 1974.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: Equal Pay Act (1963); Title VII (1964); Title IX (23 June 1972); Equal Employment Opportunity Act (1972); the ERA passed by Congress (22 March 1972); *Roe v. Wade* (22 January 1973); *Reed v. Reed* (1971); the Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974); changed attitudes. Challenge: the ERA 35 of 38 states; pay ratio about 60.7 per cent (1960) and 59.4 per cent (1970); participation 37.7 to 45.7 per cent (1960–74); opposition (Schlafly, religious groups); the liberationists' aims for the family and sexuality unachieved. Level 5 distinguishes legal from social aims and judges that legal aims were largely won and social ones not.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the impact of Malcolm X on the struggle for African American rights.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: effect during his life, effect after his death. During his life: criticism of nonviolence and integration; Nation of Islam; the split of March 1964 and the OAAU; the Ballot or the Bullet (3 April 1964). After his death: the Autobiography (1965); Black Power (Carmichael, Newton); pride and the link with Africa. Limits: he led no mass movement, was in the NOI only until 1964, and was killed on 21 February 1965 aged 39. Level 5 judges that his main impact was on ideas and on the next generation.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Black Panther Party's social programmes were more important than its armed protest.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare what each did for Black communities and what each cost the party.
H: Consider how the authorities and the media treated each.
A: Indicative content. Support: Free Breakfast for Children (from January 1969), clinics, sickle cell testing, schools; these won community support and were lasting; the FBI feared them. Challenge: the armed patrols (from October 1966; Sacramento, 2 May 1967) made the party's name and drew recruits; the Ten-Point Program; the violence also brought destruction (Fred Hampton, 4 December 1969; COINTELPRO). Level 5 judges that the social programmes were the more lasting contribution but that the armed protest created the party and its fame, and it brought the repression that destroyed it.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which Stonewall changed the struggle for gay rights.
H: What existed before Stonewall? What was new afterwards?
H: What had changed in law by 1974?
A: Indicative content. Before: Mattachine Society (1950), Daughters of Bilitis (1955), cautious pickets, Compton's (1966); a criminal and medical label. Change: mood and scale (GLF July 1969, GAA December 1969, Pride 1970, hundreds of groups); the APA decision (December 1973). Limits: most states still criminalised homosexual acts in 1974; discrimination in employment and the military; tensions over transgender people and women; much of the progress came later. Level 5 judges that Stonewall was a turning point in mobilisation more than in rights.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The reaction of the authorities did more to cause than to end protest in the years 1965–70.' Evaluate this view.
H: Take Chicago, Kent State and the riots in turn.
H: Remember that Nixon's 1968 win and the "silent majority" show another kind of effect.
A: Indicative content. Support: Chicago (the police attack of 28 August 1968; the Walker Report); Kent State (4 May 1970; the student strike); Wounded Knee; the Panthers; the Birmingham-style pattern of force producing sympathy. Challenge: the riots and protests ended partly through troops, curfews and arrests; the backlash for "law and order" (Nixon, Wallace 13.5 per cent) and the "silent majority" (3 November 1969) made some protest less effective; surveillance (COINTELPRO, CHAOS) weakened groups. Level 5 distinguishes protests caused by policy (the war, discrimination) from the effect of the authorities' reaction on their size and character.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons for the inner-city riots of the 1960s.
H: Separate the causes of the discontent from the causes of each outbreak.
H: What did the Kerner Commission conclude, and what did it leave out?
A: Indicative content. Underlying: segregation in Northern and Western cities, unemployment, bad housing and schools, police hostility, the gap between the Civil Rights Acts and daily life, television. Triggers: arrests (Watts, 11 August 1965; Newark; Detroit, 23 July 1967) and King's murder (4 April 1968). Kerner (29 February 1968): white racism. Level 5 weighs the long-term and the immediate causes, and uses figures (34, 26 and 43 dead) and the contrast with the Southern movement.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the American Indian Movement did little to improve the position of Native Americans.
A: Indicative content. Support: poverty on reservations stayed severe; violence at Pine Ridge after 1973; AIM was divided and harassed by the FBI; Wounded Knee ended without its demands met. Challenge: Alcatraz (20 November 1969 to 11 June 1971) and Wounded Knee (71 days) won national attention; termination was ended (Nixon, July 1970); the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (1971) and the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act (1975). Level 5 judges that AIM changed the political climate more than material conditions, and weighs its role against Native leaders' and others' actions.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The challenges to authority of the 1960s succeeded only when they worked with the federal government and not against it.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare the women's movement, the Panthers and the anti-war movement.
H: Where was federal power on the movement's side, and where was it the opponent?
A: Support: the women's movement won through Congress and the Supreme Court (Title IX, Equal Employment Opportunity Act, *Roe*) and the ERA failed in the states, where federal power was absent; Native Americans won termination's end through Nixon's policy; the Panthers were crushed by the FBI; SDS and the radical anti-war groups were infiltrated. Challenge: the anti-war movement succeeded against the government, by changing public opinion (Tet, Moratoria, Johnson's withdrawal, 31 March 1968); gay activism changed the APA, not Washington; the Panthers' survival programmes lasted without federal help; Kent State forced official inquiry. A* judgement separates change by law (which needed federal power) from change in attitudes (which did not), and explains why the first was more secure.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The protest movements of the 1960s and early 1970s had more in common than divided them.' Evaluate this view.
H: Take methods, language, aims and the response of the authorities in turn.
H: Where did the movements cooperate, and where did they quarrel?
A: Support: shared methods (marches, sit-ins, occupations) and language (liberation, power, pride); veterans of civil rights in the women's and anti-war movements; the same opponents (the FBI's COINTELPRO), and the same "silent majority" backlash. Challenge: different aims (reform or revolution); Black Power's separatism and women's liberation's criticism of male leaders; the wariness of NOW's leaders towards lesbian rights (Betty Friedan's remark of 1969 that lesbians were a "lavender menace", a danger to the movement's respectable image); the Panthers' and AIM's armed protest against Stonewall's and NOW's non-violent politics. A* judgement says that the movements shared a method and a cause (extending citizenship) but differed over how far the system could be reformed, and that the tensions were as important as the common ground.
