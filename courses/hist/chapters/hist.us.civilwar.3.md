:::why
In April 1861 many people in the North thought the rebellion would be over in a summer, and many in the South thought the North would not fight. Four years later more Americans had died than in any other war the country has fought. The North had far more people, factories and railways, so why did it take so long to win? The answer is a lesson in a central skill of this course: telling the things that gave one side an advantage on paper from the things that actually decided events. It also shows how a war changes its own aims. In 1861 the Union fought to keep the country together; by 1865 it was fighting, and the Constitution had been rewritten, to end slavery.
:::

## Strategy: the Anaconda Plan and the Southern response

### Two sides, two problems

The war started with each side facing a different problem. The Union had to **conquer**: to win it had to defeat Confederate armies and occupy or control a region of roughly three-quarters of a million square miles, with poor roads, few railways and a hostile population. The Confederacy only had to **survive**: if the Union gave up, the Confederacy would be an independent country, whatever the map looked like. That difference explains much of why the war was long. A country can lose battles and still not lose a war of this kind, while an invader that wins a battle may find that it has only moved the problem a few miles south.

Neither side expected four years. After Fort Sumter, Lincoln called for 75,000 volunteers for 90 days. The first great battle changed that view. At **Bull Run** (Manassas) in Virginia on 21 July 1861, a Union army under General Irvin McDowell attacked a Confederate force near the railway junction at Manassas. The attack almost worked, but Confederate reinforcements arrived by railway, and the Union troops, who had been in the army only a few weeks, broke and ran back to Washington. The battle showed that the war would not be quick, that the armies were raw, and that railways were already deciding where battles were fought.

### The Anaconda Plan of the North

The North's plan came in two stages. The popular demand, led by newspapers such as Horace Greeley's *New-York Tribune*, was "On to Richmond": a quick march to capture the new Confederate capital (the capital moved from Montgomery to Richmond, Virginia, in the summer of 1861, only about a hundred miles from Washington). The alternative came from the general-in-chief of the army, **Winfield Scott**, a veteran of the War of 1812 and the Mexican War. By 1861 he was 74, too old and unwell to command in the field. On 3 May 1861 he sent a plan to General George B. McClellan, and it was soon given a nickname by its critics: the **Anaconda Plan**. A printed map of 1861 showed the Confederacy squeezed by a coiled snake.

:::def Anaconda Plan
The strategy proposed by General Winfield Scott in May 1861: a naval blockade of the Southern coast, a drive down the Mississippi River to cut the Confederacy in two, and the build-up of a large, well-trained army, so that the South would be squeezed and starved rather than conquered by a single quick invasion.
:::

Its main parts were:
1. **A naval blockade** of the whole coast of the seceded states, about 3,500 miles of shoreline with nearly two hundred harbours and river mouths. Lincoln announced the blockade on 19 April 1861. The aim was to cut off the South's cotton exports, which earned the money to buy weapons and machinery, and its imports of manufactured goods.
2. **A river campaign** down the Mississippi to split the Confederacy, separating Texas, Arkansas and most of Louisiana from the states east of the river and denying the South the river as a trade route and a source of food and horses.
3. **A large, slowly built army** to avoid another Bull Run, with the volunteers properly trained, and a limited offensive at the right time.

Scott's critics said it was too slow and too cautious, and the North wanted battles. In practice, as the war went on, the Union did what the plan said, and added to it. The navy grew from about ninety ships in 1861 to hundreds by 1865. In early 1862 Flag Officer David Farragut took New Orleans from the sea, the South's largest city and port, in late April 1862. Union forces on land took the Tennessee and Cumberland rivers. The blockade did not stop all trade: fast **blockade runners**, many of them built in Britain, carried cotton out and arms in, especially through Wilmington, North Carolina, and the odds of capture were low at first. But each year the blockade closed more ports, the Union captured more of the coast, and the cost of everything rose in the South. The Anaconda Plan was not the whole of Union strategy, but it was the framework: pressure on the edges, a drive down the river, and a growing army in the centre. The final Union strategy under Grant and Sherman added the destruction of Confederate armies and resources.

### The South: defence, and the offensive-defensive

The Confederacy had a defensive strategy for good reasons. It had far fewer men, and its aim was independence, not the conquest of Washington. A defender also had a tactical advantage. The **rifled musket**, which was accurate at several hundred yards, replaced the old smooth-bore musket on both sides, and soldiers learned to dig in. A charging line of infantry was cut down far more easily than in the Napoleonic wars. That made decisive victories rare, armies hard to destroy, and casualties huge when commanders ordered attacks. It favoured the side that was defending.

Jefferson Davis and his generals did not, however, adopt a purely passive defence. They pursued what is called an **offensive-defensive** strategy: defend the territory of the Confederacy, but strike hard when a chance arose, to destroy enemy armies and, above all, to win victories that would change opinion. There were two big reasons to strike north.
- **To affect Northern will and foreign opinion.** A victory on Northern soil might persuade Northerners that the war could not be won, and persuade Britain and France to recognise the Confederacy.
- **To supply the army and relieve Virginia.** A Confederate army in Maryland or Pennsylvania could live off the land instead of devastated Virginia.

The two main offensives came under Robert E. Lee. In September 1862 he crossed the Potomac into Maryland and was stopped at **Antietam**. In June and July 1863 he marched into Pennsylvania and was defeated at **Gettysburg**. In the West, Braxton Bragg's invasion of Kentucky in autumn 1862 ended at Perryville (8 October). After these failures the Confederacy was on the defensive again, and its strategy became to hold out until the Northern election of 1864. Whether the offensives were wise or reckless is a major debate (see the historians' note below).

:::def Offensive-defensive
Confederate strategy of defending the South's territory while using offensives into the North to destroy Union armies, to threaten Northern cities and to influence politics at home and abroad.
:::

:::key Four campaigning seasons, four stories
1. **1861–early 1862: a stalemate in the East, success in the West.** Bull Run showed the war would be long. McClellan built a fine army and then would not use it. In the West, Grant took Forts Henry and Donelson and the Union gained Tennessee and New Orleans.
2. **1862–63: Lee's offensives.** In the East, Lee defeated a series of Union generals, but his invasions of Maryland and Pennsylvania failed.
3. **1863: the turning point.** Vicksburg and Gettysburg came within a day of each other (4 and 3 July). The Union held the Mississippi, the Confederacy was cut in two, and the chance of foreign help faded.
4. **1864–65: the long grind.** Grant fought a war of attrition in Virginia while Sherman took Atlanta and marched to the sea. The Confederacy lost the ability to feed and move its armies.

## Military leadership

Generals did not win the war on their own. But the **order in which each side found good commanders** is the most important single reason why the war lasted four years.

### The Union: slow to find its generals

The Union started with advantages, but its early commanders were not good enough to use them.

| Commander | Role | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winfield Scott | General-in-chief to November 1861 | Experience; the Anaconda Plan, which was the framework for victory | Too old to lead in the field |
| George B. McClellan | Commander of the Army of the Potomac (July 1861–November 1862); general-in-chief (November 1861–March 1862) | An excellent organiser who made an army of volunteers; the troops loved him | Overcautious; believed false reports that Lee had far more men; contemptuous of Lincoln; failed to follow up Antietam; removed 7 November 1862 |
| Ambrose Burnside | Army of the Potomac (November 1862–January 1863) | Loyal; modest | Fredericksburg (13 December 1862), where repeated frontal attacks failed with heavy losses |
| Joseph Hooker | Army of the Potomac (January–June 1863) | A good organiser; improved morale | Lost his nerve at Chancellorsville (1–4 May 1863) |
| George G. Meade | Army of the Potomac from 28 June 1863 | Won at Gettysburg, days after taking command | Did not pursue Lee's army after Gettysburg, which Lincoln thought a lost chance |
| Ulysses S. Grant | The West; then general-in-chief from March 1864 | Determined and flexible; understood that destroying armies and resources mattered more than taking cities; accepted heavy losses | Heavy casualties in 1864; his early reputation was damaged by Shiloh |
| William T. Sherman | Atlanta campaign, March to the Sea | Understood that the South's economy and will were targets | Controversial: "hard war" against property and morale |
| Philip Sheridan | Shenandoah Valley, 1864 | Aggressive; destroyed the farms that fed Lee's army | |

Lincoln himself was part of the story. He had no military training, but he read widely and learned fast. He was a **hands-on commander-in-chief**: he pressed his generals to act, changed commanders when they did not, and wanted the Union to attack Lee's army, not Richmond. For two years he changed the command of the Army of the Potomac again and again, and each new general failed. By 1863 he had learned from Grant's success at Vicksburg to give large powers to the one general who delivered. In March 1864 Congress revived the rank of lieutenant-general for Grant, and Lincoln made him general-in-chief on 9 March (he took command on 12 March).

What Grant did was a change of method as much as of man. Earlier Union offensives were separate and unconnected: the armies in Virginia, Tennessee and the Mississippi moved at different times, so the Confederates could shift reinforcements between them by railway. In 1864 Grant coordinated the armies so that all would advance **at once** and give the Confederacy no rest. He would travel with Meade's army in Virginia, with the aim of destroying Lee's army or pinning it down; Sherman would attack Johnston in Georgia; Sheridan would strip the Shenandoah Valley, which fed Lee's army. The price was terrible: in the six weeks from the Wilderness (5 May) to the first assaults on Petersburg in mid-June 1864, the Union army lost more than 50,000 men killed, wounded and missing. But Grant could replace his losses and Lee could not.

:::def Attrition
A strategy of wearing down an enemy by continuous fighting until it can no longer replace its losses in men and supplies. It suited the North in 1864–65 because of its larger population.
:::

### The Confederacy: brilliant at first, then short of men

The Confederacy had some of the best generals in the war, which is one reason it lasted so long.

| Commander | Role | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Robert E. Lee | Military adviser to Davis from March 1862; commander of the Army of Northern Virginia from 1 June 1862; general-in-chief only on 31 January 1865 | Daring, brilliant at using a smaller force; won the Seven Days, Second Bull Run, Fredericksburg and Chancellorsville; inspired his soldiers | His attacking style cost men the South could not replace; the failed offensives of Antietam and Gettysburg; concentrated on Virginia more than on the West |
| Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson | Corps commander; Shenandoah Valley campaign (spring 1862) | Fast, bold; a superb partner for Lee | Shot by his own men on 2 May 1863 at Chancellorsville; died 10 May. Lee never replaced him fully |
| Joseph E. Johnston | Army commander in Virginia (to May 1862) and the West | A careful defender | Constant quarrels with Davis; fell back rather than fight; removed from command at Atlanta on 17 July 1864 |
| P. G. T. Beauregard | Commander at Sumter and First Bull Run | Early victories | Disputes with Davis |
| Braxton Bragg | Army of Tennessee | Won Chickamauga | Failed to use the victory; lost Chattanooga; quarrelled with his own officers |
| James Longstreet | Lee's senior corps commander | Skilled defender | Doubted the order to attack on 3 July at Gettysburg |
| John Bell Hood | Replaced Johnston at Atlanta | Brave | Reckless attacks that lost Atlanta and ruined his army |

Lee is the most famous of these, and historians disagree about his generalship. Many praise his boldness, and without him the war would probably have been shorter. Others point out that his victories in Virginia did not win the war, and his aggressive style used up men the South needed. A third view, set out by Gary Gallagher in *The Confederate War* (1997), is that Lee's victories kept Confederate morale high and Northern morale low, and that this was exactly what the South needed to prolong the war.

The Confederacy also had **command problems**. Davis kept a strong personal grip on military matters, quarrelled with several generals (Johnston, Beauregard and Bragg's critics), and gave little attention to the Western theatre, where the Union gained most ground. There was no overall commander in 1861–64 who could coordinate the armies as Grant did. Lee became general-in-chief only on 31 January 1865.

:::key Why leadership lengthened the war
1. **Union generals were slow to learn**: McClellan, Burnside and Hooker failed to use the Army of the Potomac; Lincoln did not find his general until 1863–64.
2. **Confederate generals were bold at first**: Lee and Jackson won battles in Virginia against larger armies, and Johnston, Bragg and others made the Union fight for every mile in the West.
3. **Defence was stronger than attack** with rifled muskets and trenches, so even good generals could not destroy armies quickly.
4. **The Union found a method in 1864**: coordinated, relentless pressure by Grant, Sherman and Sheridan. Until then it was fighting a war of separate campaigns.
:::

### Population

The Union's lead in men was the key. It had about two and a half times the Confederacy's population and roughly four times as many white men of fighting age. Some two million men served in the Union army and navy during the war, and about 800,000 to a million (estimates vary) served the Confederacy. The Confederacy had a special problem: about 3.5 million of its 9 million people were enslaved. These people were a large share of the workforce on the farms and in the war industries, and the white South's willingness to arm them was nil until the last weeks of the war, when the Confederate Congress passed a law in March 1865 allowing Black men to be recruited, too late to matter. Slavery meant that white men could leave the farms for the army, since the enslaved people worked the fields, but it also meant that planters feared slave risings and that many enslaved people ran to Union lines when armies came near. Historians call this a **double-edged** resource: it fed the Confederate war effort and threatened it.

### Industry

The North's factories made nearly all the nation's firearms, iron, locomotives and textiles in 1860. The war added to this. The Union could equip armies with rifles, boots and blankets in huge numbers. The South had one major ironworks, the Tredegar Iron Works in Richmond. The Confederacy still built an arms industry from almost nothing: its Ordnance Bureau under Josiah Gorgas created powder mills, arsenals and foundries, and by 1863 the army was no longer short of weapons. But there was little spare capacity, and shoes, uniforms and iron were short by 1864–65.

The North also had a farming economy that did not collapse. The Midwest produced a grain surplus (the new reaping machines helped, with so many men away) and exported wheat to Britain. This is part of the reason "King Cotton" failed, as the section on foreign powers explains.

### Infrastructure: railways, rivers and the telegraph

**Infrastructure** is the physical network of an economy: its railways, roads, rivers, canals and communications.

The Union's railways were more numerous and better connected, and the government built or ran military lines. The telegraph and railway let Lincoln and his generals send armies hundreds of miles in days. The Southern network was smaller, built to carry cotton to ports and not to link the Confederacy together. Lines had different gauges, some cities had no through connection, and Southern railways could not replace worn track and engines because the iron and machinery were gone. In 1864–65 Sherman and Sheridan destroyed track, depots and bridges on a large scale. Rivers were just as important. The Union's gunboats, with ironclads like those of Grant's Henry-Donelson campaign, turned the Tennessee, Cumberland and Mississippi into highways for invasion.

:::key Why resources did not win the war quickly
1. **Geography**: the Confederacy was huge, with few railways and poor roads. The Union needed a vast army to conquer and hold it.
2. **Defence**: the rifled musket and the trench gave the defender an advantage over the attacker.
3. **Time**: it took the North two years to turn its resources into trained armies, ships and effective generals.
4. **Mobilisation**: the Confederacy put a far greater share of its men and wealth into the war than the Union did.
5. **The aim**: the Confederacy needed only to survive. If the Northern will to fight collapsed, the North's wealth would be irrelevant.
:::

:::note How many died?
The usual older figure for deaths in the Civil War is about 620,000: roughly 360,000 Union and 260,000 Confederate soldiers, a figure worked out from army records in the 1890s. In 2011 the demographer J. David Hacker used census data to argue that the total was nearer 750,000 (within a range of roughly 650,000 to 850,000). About two-thirds of the soldiers who died, on both sides, died of disease, not wounds. Civilian deaths and the deaths of enslaved people who fled to Union lines are not included in either figure. Use "about 620,000 to 750,000" and say that historians differ.
:::

## Political leadership: Lincoln and Davis

### The two presidents

| | Abraham Lincoln | Jefferson Davis |
|---|---|---|
| Background | Self-taught lawyer; one term in the House of Representatives; no military experience (apart from a few weeks in the militia in 1832) | West Point graduate; colonel in the Mexican War; Secretary of War 1853–57; US senator |
| Elected | President of the United States, 1860; re-elected 1864 | Provisional president, February 1861; elected for a six-year term in November 1861 (inaugurated 22 February 1862) |
| Style | Patient, humorous, willing to listen; held a divided party together; wrote and spoke with great skill | Dutiful, hard-working, proud; interfered in military details; often rigid with critics |
| Cabinet | Included his rivals for the nomination (Seward, Chase, Bates); managed their quarrels | Many changes; relations with Vice-President Stephens and several generals poor |
| Weaknesses | Slow to find generals; clashes with Radical Republicans; wartime restrictions on civil liberties | Often ill; quarrelled with generals; weak at persuading governors and the public |
| Strengths | Kept the Northern coalition together; picked Grant; used words to explain war aims | Created a government, army and treasury almost from nothing; held the Confederacy together to 1865 |

**Lincoln's strengths.** He understood what the war was for, and he said so clearly: the Union, and later the end of slavery. The **Gettysburg Address** (19 November 1863) is only a few hundred words and redefined the war as a test of whether a nation "conceived in Liberty, and dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal" could survive. He held together a coalition of Republicans, Unionist Democrats and Border State men that was always about to split; he made Radical Republicans (who wanted a harder war and emancipation) and conservative Democrats (who did not) each feel that he was on their side often enough to keep them in the coalition. He judged when the public would accept emancipation, Black soldiers and the draft.

**Lincoln's weaknesses.** He changed generals for two years without finding the right one, and some Radicals thought him too slow on slavery. The 1862 elections went badly for the Republicans, who lost heavily in the House (though they kept control), and early in 1863 morale was low. In August 1864 he privately wrote that it seemed "exceedingly probable that this Administration will not be re-elected" (the so-called Blind Memorandum).

**Davis's strengths.** Davis believed in the cause and worked day and night. He had real military experience, and the Confederacy created a war machine from almost nothing. Davis did his best to hold the Confederacy together long after others would have given up, and he did not agree to negotiate for anything less than independence.

**Davis's weaknesses.** He was an uninspiring leader, who often quarrelled with people. He interfered in military matters, was loyal to favourites (such as Bragg) and fell out with his best commanders (Johnston, Beauregard). The Confederacy's own founding principle, **states' rights**, worked against him: governors such as Joseph Brown of Georgia and Zebulon Vance of North Carolina resisted conscription and kept supplies for their own state troops, and Vice-President Alexander Stephens became one of his sharpest critics. The Confederacy never developed political parties that could discipline dissent, which sounds an advantage but meant that every disagreement was personal.

:::key The leaders compared
1. **War aims**: Lincoln could change the aims of the war (to include emancipation) and carry public opinion with him. Davis was tied to a cause (states' rights and slavery) that made a strong central government difficult.
2. **Managing the home front**: Lincoln kept a coalition together. Davis lost the support of governors, and the poorer white South's support for the war weakened.
3. **Managing the generals**: Lincoln made mistakes but found Grant. Davis kept some generals too long and quarrelled with others.
:::

### Conscription, taxation and the management of resources

Fighting a long war meant finding men and money. Both governments did things that no American government had done before, and both provoked opposition.

**Conscription in the Confederacy.** By the spring of 1862 the volunteers who joined in 1861 for twelve months were due to go home. On **16 April 1862** the Confederate Congress passed the first conscription law in American history: white men between 18 and 35 could be drafted for three years, and the men already in the army had to stay. The age limit was raised to 45 in September 1862, and in February 1864 to cover men from 17 to 50. The system was very unpopular because it allowed **exemptions and substitutes**. Men in some jobs (such as railwaymen and officials) were exempt, and until December 1863 a man could pay a substitute to serve for him. In October 1862 an exemption was added for the owner or overseer of a plantation with twenty or more enslaved people (the "Twenty Negro Law"), meant to keep order on plantations. It led to the complaint, heard across the South, that it was **"a rich man's war and a poor man's fight"**. Even so, conscription worked in the sense that it kept an army in the field.

**Conscription in the Union.** The North relied on volunteers for two years, with bounties paid by the federal government, states and towns. As enlistment slowed, Congress passed the **Enrollment Act** on **3 March 1863**: men aged 20 to 45 had to register, and names were drawn by lottery. A man who was drafted could avoid service by paying **$300** or finding a **substitute**. This was about a year's pay for a labourer, and the poor resented it. Fewer than one in ten Union soldiers was a draftee or a paid substitute, because the main effect was to push men to volunteer for the bounties and so avoid the draft in their district.

**The New York draft riots.** The first draft in New York City began on 11 July 1863, soon after Gettysburg. From **13 to 16 July**, mobs, many of them Irish working-class men and women who saw the draft and the war as unfair to the poor and feared competition from freed Black workers, attacked draft offices, the homes of wealthy Republicans, and above all Black New Yorkers. The Colored Orphan Asylum was burned (the children escaped) and Black men were murdered. At least about 120 people died (the official figure was 119 or 120; the real number was probably higher) and troops had to be brought from Gettysburg to stop the riot. Resistance to the draft in the North was real, but it was local and did not threaten the Union's ability to raise men.

**Taxation and money in the Union.** The North paid for the war in three ways: by borrowing (roughly two-thirds of the cost), by taxes (roughly a fifth) and by printing money (about an eighth), in rounded figures. 
- Taxes: the **Morrill Tariff** (March 1861) raised duties; the Revenue Act of 1862 set up the **Bureau of Internal Revenue** and an **income tax** (the first federal income tax that was actually collected), with excise taxes on many goods.
- Money: the **Legal Tender Act** (25 February 1862) authorised $150 million of paper money, nicknamed **greenbacks** from their colour, which could be used to pay any debt even though they could not be exchanged for gold. By the end of the war about $450 million had been issued. Prices in the North rose by about 80 per cent during the war, which hurt wage-earners but was manageable.
- Loans: financier Jay Cooke sold war bonds directly to ordinary people on a large scale, and the **National Banking Acts** of 1863 and 1864 created a system of national banks that had to hold government bonds.
- With Southern senators and representatives gone, the Republican majority also passed laws that Southerners had long blocked: the **Homestead Act** (May 1862), the **Pacific Railway Act** and the **Morrill Land-Grant College Act** (both July 1862). Historians note that the war strengthened the federal government and Northern industry.

**Taxation and money in the Confederacy.** The Confederacy had a harder problem. It had few banks and little ready money, the blockade cut its customs income, and planters opposed direct taxes. It borrowed, but mostly **printed paper money**, and kept printing. By the end of the war prices had risen by roughly **9,000 per cent**, and Confederate money was almost worthless. The government introduced a **tax-in-kind** in April 1863 (a tenth of farmers' produce) and the army took supplies by **impressment**, paying in worthless money or promises. These policies turned farmers against the government. On **2 April 1863** a crowd of women and boys, many demanding bread, rioted in Richmond; Davis himself came out to speak to them and threatened to order troops to fire. The shortages and inflation were part of the reason that desertion rose in 1864–65.

:::warn Do not call the Confederacy poor in every way
The Confederacy was not simply weak or badly led. It raised an army of well over 800,000 men over the war from a white population of about 5.5 million, built an arms industry and a railway system from scratch, and kept fighting for four years. What failed was the ability to renew men, food, iron and money as the war went on. Say what failed and when.
:::

### Retaining the support of the Border States

The four **border states**, Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky and Missouri, were slave states that stayed in the Union in 1861. They mattered for several reasons: their population and industry (Maryland surrounded Washington, DC), their position on the Ohio and Mississippi rivers and the railway lines, and the large number of men they supplied to the Union armies. Lincoln is said to have remarked that he hoped to have God on his side but must have Kentucky; the safer evidence is a letter of 22 September 1861 to Orville Browning, in which he wrote: "I think to lose Kentucky is nearly the same as to lose the whole game."

How he kept them:
- **Maryland.** Soldiers from Massachusetts were attacked by a mob in Baltimore on 19 April 1861. Lincoln suspended habeas corpus along the railway line to Washington on 27 April, and the army arrested pro-secession members of the Maryland legislature in September 1861, so the state could not secede.
- **Kentucky.** The state declared itself neutral in May 1861. Lincoln respected this and did not send in troops. When a Confederate force occupied Columbus, Kentucky, in September 1861, the legislature turned to the Union. Lincoln treated the state carefully for the rest of the war.
- **Missouri.** The state was torn by guerrilla war. When General John C. Frémont, commanding in Missouri, declared martial law on 30 August 1861 and freed the enslaved people of rebels, Lincoln ordered him to change it, and removed him in November, because the Border States would have been alienated. In May 1862 he also cancelled a similar order by General David Hunter.
- **Delaware** had few enslaved people and no problem.

Lincoln also appealed to the Border States to adopt **compensated emancipation** (the government would pay owners who freed their slaves), in a message of March 1862, but the Border State representatives rejected it in July 1862. This is one reason why the Emancipation Proclamation, when it came, did not cover the Border States. In 1863 West Virginia, formed from the western counties of Virginia that stayed loyal, became the 35th state (20 June).

:::key Why the Border States mattered to the length of the war
1. They kept the Union's geography workable: with Kentucky and Missouri lost, the Ohio and Mississippi rivers would have been Confederate lines.
2. They kept Washington from being surrounded and supplied the Union with men.
3. They limited what Lincoln could do about slavery until the Confederacy's resistance made it necessary.
:::

## The Emancipation Proclamation

### Why Lincoln issued it

In 1861 Lincoln insisted that the war was only about the Union. In his reply to Horace Greeley, who had attacked him in print for not acting against slavery, he wrote on **22 August 1862**: "My paramount object in this struggle is to save the Union, and is not either to save or to destroy slavery. If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves I would do it". He added that his "oft-expressed personal wish" was "that all men every where could be free". Just over four months later he issued the Proclamation. Why?

:::key Reasons for the Emancipation Proclamation
1. **Military necessity.** Slavery was a resource for the Confederacy: enslaved men and women dug trenches, grew food and worked in factories. Taking them away weakened the South. Enslaved people were also running to Union lines, and the army had to decide what to do with them. General Benjamin Butler had called them "contraband of war" in May 1861, and Congress passed two **Confiscation Acts** (August 1861; the Second Confiscation Act, 17 July 1862) freeing the enslaved people of rebels. Lincoln's power as commander-in-chief gave him a legal basis to act.
2. **Black soldiers.** Allowing Black men to serve would provide a new source of manpower at a time when volunteering was falling. The Militia Act of July 1862 already allowed it in a limited way.
3. **Political pressure and principle.** Radical Republicans, abolitionists such as Frederick Douglass, and many Northern voters wanted the war to end slavery. Lincoln himself hated slavery. He first wanted to bring the Border States and Northern Democrats along, and by mid-1862 he judged the balance of opinion had shifted.
4. **Foreign policy.** A war for freedom would make it very hard for Britain and France, where slavery was condemned, to recognise the Confederacy.
5. **Timing.** Lincoln told his cabinet of the plan on 22 July 1862. Secretary of State William Seward advised him to wait for a military victory, or it would look like a "last shriek on the retreat". The victory at **Antietam** (17 September) gave him the chance, and he issued the **preliminary proclamation on 22 September 1862**. It warned that enslaved people in any state still in rebellion on 1 January 1863 would be "then, thenceforward, and forever free".
:::

### What it said, and what it did not

The final **Emancipation Proclamation** was issued on **1 January 1863** (just over 100 days after the warning). It declared that "all persons held as slaves within said designated States, and parts of States, are, and henceforward shall be free", and stated that "such persons of suitable condition, will be received into the armed service" of the United States. It ended with Lincoln's claim that it was "an act of justice, warranted by the Constitution, upon military necessity", for which he invoked "the considerate judgment of mankind, and the gracious favor of Almighty God".

Its limits were important:
- It applied only to areas **in rebellion**, where the federal government could not enforce it. It did not free enslaved people in the **loyal Border States** (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri), nor in Tennessee, nor in parts of Louisiana and Virginia held by the Union and listed as exceptions.
- It was a **war measure**, based on the president's power as commander-in-chief, not a law. Lincoln feared that the courts might say it ended with the war. This is why he then pushed for the 13th Amendment, which Congress passed in January 1865.
- Its effect depended on Union armies moving south: it freed people as the army arrived.

:::def Emancipation Proclamation
The executive order issued by Abraham Lincoln on 1 January 1863, using his war powers as commander-in-chief, declaring free all enslaved people in the states and parts of states still in rebellion, and allowing Black men to join the Union armed forces. It did not apply to the loyal Border States.
:::

### Consequences

- **The war's aims changed.** From 1863 the Union was fighting both to restore the Union and to end slavery. This made compromise much harder: there could be no return to the old Union. Southerners saw it as a threat to their society. In his message to the Confederate Congress of 12 January 1863 Jefferson Davis called the proclamation "the most execrable measure recorded in the history of guilty man" and asked for authority to treat Union officers who carried it out as inciting slave insurrection.
- **Enslaved people left.** As Union armies advanced, hundreds of thousands of enslaved people left plantations and came to Union lines, weakening the Confederate economy. Historians such as Ira Berlin stress that enslaved people **freed themselves**, and Lincoln's order recognised what they were already doing.
- **Black soldiers.** About **180,000 Black men** served in the Union army (the United States Colored Troops, as they were called, from 1863) and around 20,000 in the navy. Their service helped the Union's numbers in 1864–65. They were paid less than white soldiers until Congress changed the law in June 1864, and many were killed after capture. The attack of the 54th Massachusetts on Fort Wagner (18 July 1863) became a symbol of their courage, and Black soldiers' service strengthened the case for citizenship after the war.
- **Foreign policy.** After 1 January 1863 British and French governments could not support the Confederacy openly, because to do so would be to support slavery. Workers' meetings in Britain, such as the one in Manchester at the end of December 1862, supported the Union cause.
- **Opposition in the North.** Many Northern Democrats opposed fighting to end slavery. The Democrats gained heavily in the autumn 1862 elections, and the draft riots of July 1863 included an anti-Black element. Some soldiers deserted over it. But the Republicans held, and by 1864 emancipation was part of the Union's cause.

:::warn Do not say the Proclamation freed all the slaves
It freed none at the stroke of a pen in places Union armies did not reach, and it left slavery legal in the loyal Border States and the exempted areas. It was the 13th Amendment, ratified in December 1865, that ended slavery everywhere in the United States. Write precisely: "it declared free the enslaved people in the states still in rebellion".
:::

## Limitations on civil liberties

War brought restrictions on rights on both sides.

**Lincoln and habeas corpus.** The Constitution says that the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus (the right to challenge imprisonment without trial) "shall not be suspended, unless when in cases of Rebellion or Invasion the public Safety may require it". It does not say who may suspend it. Lincoln suspended it on 27 April 1861 along the railway line from Philadelphia to Washington, and later more widely. In *Ex parte Merryman* (May 1861) Chief Justice Roger Taney, sitting alone as a circuit judge, ruled that only Congress could suspend it, but Lincoln ignored him. Congress passed the **Habeas Corpus Suspension Act** on 3 March 1863, authorising the president to suspend the writ. Over the war, at least 13,000 civilians (estimates vary) were arrested by the military, mainly in the Border States, for disloyalty. Newspapers were closed or threatened: in June 1863 General Burnside suspended the *Chicago Times*, and Lincoln cancelled the order three days later.

**The Vallandigham case.** The best-known case is that of **Clement Vallandigham**, a Democratic congressman from Ohio and a leading **Copperhead** (the Republican nickname for Peace Democrats, who wanted an end to the war and a negotiated peace). In May 1863 he made a speech attacking the war and the draft. General Burnside, who commanded in Ohio and had issued an order (General Order No. 38) threatening anyone who declared sympathy for the enemy, arrested him on 5 May, and a military court sentenced him to prison. Lincoln changed the sentence and sent him south to the Confederates. Vallandigham ended up in Canada and ran for governor of Ohio in absentia in 1863, losing heavily. The affair made Lincoln defend himself in the letter of 12 June 1863 to Erastus Corning and other Democrats: "Must I shoot a simple-minded soldier boy who deserts, while I must not touch a hair of a wiley agitator who induces him to desert?" His case was that in a rebellion, the government must be able to stop speech that was stopping the army from being raised.

**Why it matters.** Critics, such as Vallandigham, called Lincoln a tyrant. Supporters said that the Union would not have survived if it had waited for trials. After the war, in *Ex parte Milligan* (1866), the Supreme Court ruled that military trials of civilians were unconstitutional where the civil courts were open. The balance is for you to judge: the number of arrests was large, but the North remained a society with elections (in 1862, 1863 and 1864), a free press for most of the war, and an opposition party that could win.

**In the Confederacy.** Davis, too, asked Congress to suspend habeas corpus (first in February 1862, then in March 1863 and February 1864), declared martial law in Richmond and some areas, and required internal **passports** for travel. Conscription itself was a major restriction. The Confederate government, with its states' rights principles, had to work against its own constitution, and this was one source of governors' opposition.

:::key Civil liberties in the war
1. **Lincoln acted first, and asked Congress later**: habeas corpus was suspended by presidential order (27 April 1861) and authorised by Congress on 3 March 1863.
2. **The targets were opponents of the war**: Copperheads such as Vallandigham, Border State secessionists and some newspapers.
3. **The Confederacy did the same** and, like the Union, had to restrict rights it had been created to protect.
4. **Effect on the length of the war**: restrictions helped the Union hold the Border States and raise troops. They also gave opponents a grievance that Democrats used in 1864.
:::

## The role of foreign powers

The Confederacy hoped that Britain and France would recognise it, break the blockade or force a peace. The Union feared exactly that. Neither happened, and the reasons say a great deal about why the war was fought out to the end.

:::def Recognition
The formal act by which one government accepts another as the legitimate government of an independent state. Recognition of the Confederacy by Britain or France would have treated it as a nation, not a rebellion, and might have led to mediation or help against the blockade.
:::

### Great Britain

Britain was the key power. It was the world's largest naval power, and its cotton mills in Lancashire took most of their raw cotton (about three-quarters in 1860) from the American South. The Confederacy believed in "**King Cotton**": that a cotton shortage would force Britain to intervene. It also had sympathisers among the British upper classes, who admired the Southern gentry and disliked democracy, and in the press (*The Times* was often hostile to the North).

What happened:
- **Neutrality, May 1861.** On 13 May 1861 Queen Victoria's government proclaimed neutrality and recognised the Confederates as **belligerents** (a side in a war, which allowed them to buy ships and weapons and to be treated as soldiers rather than as pirates). That was not recognition of the Confederacy as a state, but the Union was angry.
- **The Trent affair, November 1861.** On 8 November Captain Charles Wilkes of the US warship *San Jacinto* stopped the British mail steamer *Trent* and took off two Confederate envoys, James Mason and John Slidell, who were going to Britain and France. Britain demanded their release and an apology, and sent troops to Canada. Lincoln's government, and Seward in particular, decided that the Union could not afford a second war, and on 26 December released the men. The crisis passed, but it showed how quickly the war could widen.
- **The cotton famine.** Lancashire mills stopped or went on short time as cotton imports collapsed in 1862–63, and hundreds of thousands of workers suffered. But Britain had large stocks from the bumper crops of 1859–60, found new cotton in Egypt and India, and imported Northern wheat when its own harvests failed. The Confederacy also kept cotton at home in 1861 to try to force the issue. "King Cotton" did not work, partly because the South had stopped exports itself.
- **Mediation, autumn 1862.** After the Confederate victories of the summer, the prime minister, Lord Palmerston, and the foreign secretary, Lord Russell, discussed a joint offer of mediation with France in September 1862. On 7 October the Chancellor of the Exchequer, William Gladstone, said at Newcastle that Jefferson Davis and the Southern leaders "have made a nation". Opposition within the cabinet, the Union victory at Antietam and then the Emancipation Proclamation killed the idea by November.
- **The Alabama and the Laird rams.** Confederate agents had warships built in British yards. The **CSS *Alabama***, built by Laird at Birkenhead, slipped out in July 1862 and sank or captured around sixty Union merchant ships before the USS *Kearsarge* sank her off Cherbourg on 19 June 1864. Charles Francis Adams, the US minister in London, then protested against two ironclad **rams** being built at Laird's in 1863. On 5 September 1863 he wrote to Lord Russell: "It would be superfluous in me to point out to your lordship that this is war." The British government stopped the ships: Russell had in fact decided to detain them on 3 September, before the note arrived, and they were seized in October and later bought for the Royal Navy. After the war, Britain agreed to arbitration and paid the United States $15.5 million for the damage done by the *Alabama* and other cruisers (1872).
- **Emancipation and public opinion.** From January 1863, British trade unionists and radicals held large meetings in support of the Union. In reply to the working-men of Manchester, on 19 January 1863, Lincoln wrote that he knew and deeply deplored their sufferings, and called their support for the Union "an instance of sublime Christian heroism which has not been surpassed in any age or in any country".

:::key Why Britain did not recognise the Confederacy
1. **Interests**: Britain had other sources of cotton and a profitable trade with the North, including grain, and its navy was strong enough to protect its trade.
2. **Fear of war with the Union**: recognition might lead to war, and Canada was exposed.
3. **Slavery**: British opinion, and especially the working class, opposed slavery, and the Emancipation Proclamation made it very hard for any government to support the South.
4. **Military events**: recognition depended on Confederate success. Antietam and then Gettysburg and Vicksburg in 1863 made the Confederacy look less likely to win.
:::

### France

The emperor **Napoleon III** was more open to the Confederacy than Britain's ministers. France needed cotton for its mills, and Napoleon wanted to extend French power in Mexico. In **1861** France, Britain and Spain sent forces to Mexico over unpaid debts; Britain and Spain withdrew, but France stayed and in June 1863 took Mexico City. In 1864 Napoleon installed the Austrian archduke **Maximilian** as emperor of Mexico. A Confederate victory would have given France a friendly neighbour to a weaker United States; a strong Union was a danger to the project. Napoleon received Confederate envoy John Slidell, and French shipyards were allowed to start building warships for the Confederacy. In **October 1862** he proposed to Britain and Russia a six-month armistice, which both refused.

But Napoleon would not act alone. He needed Britain's navy, and he did not want to be dragged into a war with the United States while his army was in Mexico. After Gettysburg and Vicksburg, the Confederates' chances of recognition faded, and the French government eventually stopped the warships. In 1865–67, after the Civil War, the United States pressed France to withdraw from Mexico and Maximilian was executed in June 1867. France's role is therefore partly the story of an opportunity that Napoleon wanted but could not use.

### Russia

Russia's role was smaller but famous. The tsar, **Alexander II**, had freed the serfs in 1861 and was more friendly to the Union than to a slave-owning Confederacy. Russia had good reasons to keep the United States strong as a balance against Britain and France. The Russian ministers in Washington and St Petersburg sympathised with the Union, and Russia refused Napoleon's proposal in 1862.

The best-known event is the visit of Russian fleets in autumn 1863. A squadron from the Baltic arrived in **New York in September 1863** and another in San Francisco in October, and the sailors were welcomed with banquets and speeches. Many Americans took it as a sign that Russia stood with the Union against Britain and France. Historians are more cautious. Russia was in a crisis with Britain and France over the Polish rising of 1863, and sending its ships to American ports kept them from being trapped in the Baltic if war broke out in Europe. The visit was a gesture of goodwill and a useful move for Russia, not an alliance, but it did encourage the Union at a good moment.

:::key How much did foreign powers affect the length of the war?
1. **They did not intervene**, so the Confederacy had to win the war by itself. This is the main reason the South lost.
2. **The chance of intervention mattered in 1862**: hope of recognition encouraged Davis and Lee to go north in September 1862, and fear of it pushed Lincoln to wait for a victory before announcing emancipation.
3. **The blockade was accepted** by Britain, which could have challenged it, because Britain itself used blockades and the Union's was effective. That helped the Union.
4. **After July 1863** the question was settled in practice. Gettysburg, Vicksburg and the Emancipation Proclamation ended the Confederacy's best chance of outside help.
:::

## The end of the war, 1864–65

Atlanta fell on **2 September 1864**, and by the autumn Union armies held the Mississippi, Tennessee and much of the Deep South. But in the summer of 1864, with Grant's armies stuck at Petersburg and casualty lists growing, Northern morale had sunk. Lincoln expected to lose the election. The Democrats nominated McClellan at Chicago on 31 August on a platform, written by Copperheads such as Vallandigham, that called the war a failure and asked for an armistice. McClellan said he would not accept the peace plank. Then news came of Atlanta, and Sheridan's victories in the Shenandoah Valley in September and October. On **8 November 1864** Lincoln won 212 of the 233 electoral votes and about 55 per cent of the popular vote. Soldiers voted overwhelmingly for him. The result told the South that the North would not give up and removed its last real hope.

From Atlanta, Sherman marched with about 60,000 men to Savannah between **15 November and 21 December 1864**. He cut loose from his supplies and destroyed railways, mills, and stores of food, intending to break the Confederacy's will and its ability to supply its armies. He took Savannah and then marched north through the Carolinas in 1865. He defended the policy in a letter of 12 September 1864 to the mayor of Atlanta, James Calhoun, who had asked him to cancel the evacuation of the city's civilians: "You cannot qualify war in harsher terms than I will. War is cruelty, and you cannot refine it".

Grant held Lee in the trenches at Petersburg from June 1864 to April 1865. On 2 April 1865 Lee's lines broke and Richmond was abandoned. On **9 April 1865** Lee surrendered to Grant at Appomattox Court House. Grant allowed the men to go home with their horses. Joseph Johnston surrendered to Sherman near Durham, North Carolina, on 26 April, Jefferson Davis was captured in Georgia on 10 May, and the last Confederate forces in the Trans-Mississippi surrendered on 2 June 1865. Five days after Appomattox, on 14 April, Lincoln was shot at Ford's Theatre in Washington; he died on 15 April.

## Why did the Civil War last four years? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the reasons by type and then by how they connect.

| Reason | Why it lengthened the war | Why it shortened the war, or limited its effect |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy and geography | The Confederacy was huge and could trade space for time; the Union had to conquer it. Defence was stronger than attack | The Anaconda Plan squeezed the coasts and rivers; Grant and Sherman showed how to use Union strength |
| Military leadership | Union generals failed in Virginia until 1864; Lee and Jackson won victories against larger armies | The Union found Grant and Sherman; the Confederacy's best generals were in one theatre; Davis's quarrels |
| Resources | The North needed two years to turn its lead in men and factories into trained armies and a strategy; the Confederacy put a bigger share of its men in uniform | The Union's resources made the final result almost certain if the Union kept fighting |
| Political leadership | Davis held the Confederacy together to 1865 | Lincoln held the Northern coalition together and found a war aim that carried the people; Davis lost the support of governors and farmers |
| Border States and civil liberties | Fear of losing the Border States slowed emancipation | Holding them kept the Union strong enough to win |
| Emancipation | Made compromise impossible, so the war had to be fought to a finish | Freed labour, added Black soldiers and ended the hope of foreign help |
| Foreign powers | The hope of recognition in 1862 kept the South fighting | No recognition came, and the blockade was never broken |
| Northern will | The 1864 election, the draft and war-weariness might have ended the war in a compromise peace | Atlanta and Lincoln's re-election confirmed that the North would continue |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The Union's **resources** made its eventual victory very likely, but they did not make it quick. The war lasted four years because **three things delayed** the use of those resources: a geography and technology of war that favoured the defence, a Confederacy that fought with great skill and unity at first, and Union leaders who needed two years to find a strategy and generals equal to the task. The war ended when Grant, Sherman and Lincoln's re-election together turned the North's lead into a continuous campaign that the Confederacy could not meet, and when the loss of Atlanta, Savannah, the Carolinas and Richmond removed the South's ability to supply its armies. The point for an essay: resources are the **necessary** cause of Northern victory, but leadership, strategy and will are what decided **when** and **whether** the resources were used.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **David Donald** edited *Why the North Won the Civil War* (1960), a collection of essays on the question. Several emphasised the North's material advantages and the weakness of the Confederate state, with the Confederacy struggling under the same strains as its enemy.
- **James McPherson**, *Battle Cry of Freedom* (1988), argued that the outcome was not inevitable: the war turned on **contingency**, a series of moments (the summer of 1862, the autumn of 1862, the summer of 1864) at which the result could have gone either way. He agrees that the North's resources mattered, but only because Northern leaders and soldiers used them.
- **Gary Gallagher**, *The Confederate War* (1997), argued that Confederates had strong national feeling and kept fighting for years, so that their defeat is explained by Union armies, not by a lack of Southern will.
You can use this at AS to show why causes are weighed differently, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Lincoln issued the Emancipation Proclamation on 1 January 1863. He had warned of it in September 1862 after the Battle of Antietam. The Radical Republicans wanted him to end slavery. The war was going on for a long time and Lincoln needed to find a way to win. It also freed the slaves in the South and let Black men join the army. Britain and France were thinking about helping the South."

*Commentary.* Accurate, relevant material, but it is a list. The sentences do not say how any of the facts made Lincoln act. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was military necessity. By 1862 the war was not being won: McClellan had failed on the Peninsula, and the Union needed men. Enslaved people were a source of labour for the Confederacy, growing its food and building its defences, and thousands were also running to Union lines. A proclamation, which Lincoln could issue as commander-in-chief, would take that labour away from the South, and allow Black men to be received into the armed forces, which it did on 1 January 1863. A second reason was foreign policy. In the autumn of 1862 Palmerston and Russell were discussing mediation. Lincoln knew that Britain, where opposition to slavery was strong, could not easily help a Confederacy fighting to keep slavery if the Union was openly fighting to end it."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add a third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was political pressure and principle. Radical Republicans in Congress had passed the Second Confiscation Act in July 1862, and abolitionists such as Frederick Douglass had been demanding action. Lincoln disliked slavery, and in his reply to Horace Greeley on 22 August 1862 he had stressed that his 'oft-expressed personal wish' was that all men could be free, but he put saving the Union first. He had to bring the Border States and Northern Democrats along, which is why he had held back. By the summer of 1862 the stalemate in the war had made it clear that the old policy of fighting only for the Union was not enough.

These factors were linked. Military necessity gave Lincoln the legal basis, because as commander-in-chief he could free enslaved people as a war measure, a point that mattered to a lawyer who knew the Constitution protected slavery in the states. Foreign policy and pressure from his own party gave him the reasons to use that power. The timing was decided by Antietam on 17 September 1862, as Seward had advised: a victory meant the proclamation would not look like a desperate act. The most important reason was military necessity, because it provided both the legal ground and the purpose. The other reasons explain why Lincoln was willing to use it in 1862 rather than later."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (military necessity as the legal basis; foreign and political pressure as the reasons to act; Antietam as the timing), with a supported conclusion about which reason mattered most. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

I would save the Union. I would save it the shortest way under the Constitution. My paramount object in this struggle is to save the Union, and is not either to save or to destroy slavery. If I could save the Union without freeing any slave I would do it, and if I could save it by freeing all the slaves I would do it; and if I could save it by freeing some and leaving others alone I would also do that. What I do about slavery, and the colored race, I do because I believe it helps to save the Union.

*Adapted from a letter by Abraham Lincoln to Horace Greeley, editor of the New-York Tribune, 22 August 1862. The letter was a public reply to Greeley's editorial of 20 August urging Lincoln to enforce the confiscation laws and act against slavery.*

**Source B**

All persons held as slaves within said designated States, and parts of States, are, and henceforward shall be free. The parishes of Louisiana named, the forty-eight counties designated as West Virginia, and certain counties of Virginia are for the present left precisely as if this proclamation were not issued. Such persons of suitable condition will be received into the armed service of the United States. And upon this act, sincerely believed to be an act of justice, warranted by the Constitution, upon military necessity, I invoke the considerate judgment of mankind.

*Adapted from the Emancipation Proclamation, issued by Abraham Lincoln as President and Commander-in-Chief, 1 January 1863. The extract joins and shortens passages; the list of excepted places is summarised.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about Lincoln's aims in the Civil War.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that Lincoln wants to save the Union and does not mind about slavery. Source B says that the slaves are free and can join the army. So the sources disagree, because in Source A he is not freeing the slaves and in Source B he is. Source A is a letter and Source B is an official order, so Source B is more reliable."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on reliability is a general assertion about the kind of document, with no reference to what the author was doing. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"At first sight the sources look opposed. In Source A Lincoln says his 'paramount object' is to save the Union and not 'either to save or to destroy slavery'; in Source B he declares that all persons held as slaves in the designated areas 'shall be free' and will be received into 'the armed service'. A reader might conclude that Lincoln's aims changed completely in five months.

But the sources also share an important idea. In Source A, what Lincoln does about slavery he does 'because I believe it helps to save the Union'; in Source B emancipation is 'warranted by the Constitution, upon military necessity'. In both he justifies action against slavery as a means to win the war, not as an end in itself. Both also show him acting with caution: in A he would free 'some' and leave 'others alone' if that saved the Union, and in B he leaves certain places 'precisely as if this proclamation were not issued'. The sources agree that the Union came first, but disagree on how far it was now necessary to free slaves to win.

The context explains both. Source A was a public reply to Greeley, a Republican editor who had attacked Lincoln in print for not enforcing the Confiscation Acts. Lincoln was writing for Border State and Northern Democratic readers as much as for Greeley, and he wanted to reassure them that he was not an abolitionist, so he stresses the Union. Yet by then he had already decided to issue a proclamation: he had told his cabinet on 22 July, and was waiting for a victory, so the letter hides as much as it shows. Source B comes after Antietam and the preliminary proclamation (22 September), when Lincoln had decided that military necessity now justified freeing the slaves of rebels. Its legal language, the exceptions for the loyal Border States and Union-held areas, and the appeal to 'military necessity' reflect a president who had to defend the order in court and to keep the Border States in the Union. So Source A is good evidence of what Lincoln wanted the public to believe in August 1862, but weak evidence of his private intentions; Source B is strong evidence of his policy as law, but does not tell us how far he had moral convictions as well. Taken together, they show that Lincoln's aim was constant, to save the Union, and that his methods changed as the war showed that the Union could not be saved without striking at slavery."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the Greeley editorial, the Border States, Antietam) and through each source's nature and purpose; a closing comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The North won the Civil War because of its greater resources.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: population (22 million to 9 million; about four times as many white men of fighting age); industry (about nine-tenths of manufactured goods) and railways (about 22,000 to 9,000 miles); finance (greenbacks, bonds, a 80 per cent price rise against about 9,000 per cent in the Confederacy); the navy and blockade; attrition in 1864.
- Against 1, leadership: McClellan's failures; Lee's victories; the war was won when Grant and Sherman used resources differently.
- Against 2, politics: Lincoln's skill and the 1864 election; Davis's weakness and the governors; emancipation and Black soldiers.
- Against 3, Confederate mistakes and foreign powers: the offensives of 1862 and 1863; "King Cotton"; no recognition.
- Judgement: resources made Northern victory likely but not inevitable, and leadership and political will converted them into victory. Criterion: what turned a lead on paper into a win.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The North's resources were very important in winning the war. In 1860 the North had 22 million people while the South had 9 million, and 3.5 million of them were enslaved. The North had about nine-tenths of the factories and more than twice as many miles of railway. This meant that it could make weapons and move troops, while the South was short of everything. By 1864 the South was running out of men and Grant could replace his losses and Lee could not. So the North won because of its greater resources."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: having resources is not the same as winning. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet resources alone do not explain the result, because for two years the Confederacy, with far fewer of them, held its ground. In the East the Union's larger army was beaten at Bull Run, in the Seven Days, at Second Bull Run, at Fredericksburg and at Chancellorsville, usually by Lee, who was often outnumbered. The explanation is leadership: McClellan, Burnside and Hooker could not use the advantage, and the rifled musket favoured the defender. The Union began to win when it found commanders who used its resources, and it did so in the West first, where Grant took Forts Henry and Donelson in February 1862 and Vicksburg in July 1863. In 1864 Grant coordinated the armies so that Lee could not be reinforced from elsewhere, and Sherman took Atlanta. Resources did not win the war until these generals made them count.

Political leadership and will were also decisive. Lincoln held together a coalition of Republicans and Unionist Democrats, kept the Border States, and changed the aims of the war with the Emancipation Proclamation, which brought about 180,000 Black soldiers into the Union army and ended the hope of British or French recognition. In 1864, when casualties made him expect defeat, it was the capture of Atlanta that helped him win 212 of 233 electoral votes, and it was the fact that Northern voters supported continuing the war that gave Grant the time to use his advantage. Davis, by contrast, faced a states' rights revolt from governors such as Brown of Georgia and Vance of North Carolina, and inflation and shortages that wore down support: Confederate prices rose roughly 9,000 per cent against about 80 per cent in the Union.

Overall, the North's greater resources were a necessary condition of victory, because without them the Union could not have raised, armed and supplied armies large enough to conquer so large a territory. But they were not sufficient. They explain why the Confederacy was always likely to lose a war of attrition, but not why the war lasted four years, nor how the Union won. That depended on the Union learning to apply its strength, on Lincoln's political skill, and on the Confederacy's failure to win the early offensives that might have brought recognition. The statement is therefore true of the long-term outcome, but the decisive factor was the way resources were used, not the resources themselves."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (what turned a lead on paper into a win) and distinguishes necessary from decisive causes. With an opening paragraph on the case for resources, this is Level 5 (19–20).

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (a cabinet minister expects the Confederacy to be a nation), C (a British yard built a Confederate warship), D (Punch's "mocking pencil" had drawn Lincoln for "the self-complacent British sneer"). Challenge or qualify: B (the Lancashire workers, who suffered most from the war, backed the Union), C (the government stopped the ship once Adams protested, and so the policy was not simply pro-Southern), D (the magazine turned to praise Lincoln).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: spoken by a senior minister at a public dinner, so it carries real weight, and the speech caused a sensation. But Gladstone was one voice in a divided cabinet; Palmerston and Russell were cautious, and Gladstone later admitted his mistake. It shows what some leaders believed in October 1862, when the Confederacy was at its high point, not British policy, which remained neutral.
- **B**: Lincoln is writing to flatter and encourage an audience whose support he needs, so he may exaggerate. But the Manchester meeting's address was real, and it shows that many British workers backed the Union, though they were short of work. It is evidence about the working-class view and about how Lincoln saw the cause, not about the government.
- **C**: a diplomat writing to protest, with a purpose of pressure, so he stresses the danger. But it shows that British ships were being built for the Confederacy; and Russell's response (the rams were detained) shows that the government ended up holding to neutrality.
- **D**: a poem written after Lincoln's death, in an unusual mood of regret; its purpose is to apologise to the American people. It shows that a leading magazine had been hostile in tone to Lincoln, but one magazine is not British opinion as a whole.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A, C and D agree that parts of British society and some British interests leaned towards the Confederacy, but they describe different things. Source A shows a minister in October 1862 expecting the South to be a separate nation; Source C shows that private British builders were making Confederate ships; Source D shows that the most popular satirical magazine had mocked Lincoln. Source B challenges this: the working-men of Manchester, whose jobs depended on Southern cotton, supported the Union, which is the opposite of what 'King Cotton' diplomacy expected. Context confirms both pictures. Palmerston and Russell did discuss mediation in September 1862, and the Alabama and the Laird rams show how British yards helped the South. But the cabinet refused to recognise the Confederacy, Britain accepted the blockade, Russell stopped the rams, and after Antietam and the Emancipation Proclamation the idea of intervention died. The sources therefore agree only to a limited extent: they show real sympathy for the Confederacy among the governing class and in some of the press, but not that British policy favoured the South. British opinion was divided, and the policy that mattered was neutrality."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1862–65; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the Union's greater population the main reason for its victory in the Civil War?" [20]

A student wrote:

"In 1860 the North had a population of about 22 million and the South had about 9 million, of whom 3.5 million were slaves. The North had many more factories and railways than the South, and a bigger navy. The North also had Abraham Lincoln as president, who was a good leader, while Jefferson Davis was less good. Grant became general-in-chief in 1864 and Sherman marched through Georgia. In April 1865 Lee surrendered at Appomattox, and the war was over."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative and a list**: it names population, factories, Lincoln and Grant without saying how any of them led to victory, and it never answers the question about population. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "Population was the most important single reason for the Union's victory, because it let the North win a war of attrition. The Union had about 22 million people to the Confederacy's 9 million (and 3.5 million of the Confederate total were enslaved), so by 1864 Grant could accept the heavy losses of the Wilderness and Cold Harbor and still replace them, while Lee, who could not, was pinned in the trenches at Petersburg. However, population did not by itself end the war: for two years the larger Union armies failed against Lee, and it took better generals and the Emancipation Proclamation, which added about 180,000 Black soldiers, to turn numbers into victory." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name the three parts of the Anaconda Plan.
A: A naval blockade of the Southern coast, a drive down the Mississippi to cut the Confederacy in two, and the building of a large, trained army rather than a quick invasion.
Q: Why did Lee invade the North in September 1862 and June 1863?
A: To win a victory on Northern soil that would weaken Northern will and win recognition from Britain and France, and to live off the land away from devastated Virginia. Both invasions failed, at Antietam and Gettysburg.
Q: Give two reasons why McClellan was removed in November 1862.
A: He was overcautious and exaggerated Lee's strength; he failed to follow up the victory at Antietam; he was also contemptuous of Lincoln. Lincoln lost patience and replaced him on 7 November 1862.
Q: What was the "Twenty Negro Law", and what did it show?
A: A Confederate exemption (October 1862) from the draft for the owner or overseer of a plantation with twenty or more enslaved people. It showed that conscription fell hardest on the poor and fed the complaint that it was "a rich man's war and a poor man's fight".
Q: How did the Union and the Confederacy each pay for the war?
A: The Union borrowed, taxed (the Revenue Act of 1862 and the income tax) and issued greenbacks (Legal Tender Act, February 1862), and prices rose about 80 per cent. The Confederacy printed money and used a tax-in-kind (April 1863) and impressment, and prices rose roughly 9,000 per cent.
Q: Why was Kentucky so important to Lincoln?
A: It was a border slave state on the Ohio River, which was a strategic route into the Confederacy; losing it would have made Missouri and Maryland harder to keep. Lincoln wrote in September 1861 that to lose Kentucky was nearly the same as to lose the whole game.
Q: Which enslaved people did the Emancipation Proclamation not free?
A: Those in the loyal Border States (Delaware, Maryland, Kentucky, Missouri), in Tennessee, and in the parts of Louisiana and Virginia occupied by the Union that were listed as exceptions. It applied only to areas still in rebellion.
Q: What was the Trent affair, and how was it settled?
A: In November 1861 a US warship stopped the British mail steamer Trent and took off two Confederate envoys, Mason and Slidell. Britain demanded their release and sent troops to Canada; Lincoln's government released the envoys on 26 December 1861.
Q: What did the visit of Russian fleets in 1863 show, and why should it be treated with caution?
A: It was taken as a sign of Russian friendship for the Union and was welcomed in New York and San Francisco. But Russia was facing a crisis with Britain and France over Poland and may have wanted its ships out of the Baltic, so it was not an alliance.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Confederacy introduced conscription in April 1862.
A: Indicative content: the one-year volunteers of 1861 were due to go home in the spring of 1862 and Union armies were advancing (Forts Henry and Donelson, Shiloh, New Orleans, McClellan's army approaching Richmond); the Confederacy had a smaller population and could not rely on volunteers; the war would be long, and Davis and Lee needed a reliable army; states' rights meant the central government had to take the power, which was controversial. It ends with a judgement on the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Britain did not recognise the Confederacy.
A: Indicative content: Britain had other sources of cotton and large stocks, and imported Northern wheat; the Trent affair showed war with the Union risked Canada; British opinion, especially after the Emancipation Proclamation, would not support slavery; Antietam in September 1862 and then Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863 made the Confederacy look weaker; the British government accepted the blockade and wanted neutrality. Q: [20 marks] ★★ How successful was Jefferson Davis as a war leader?

H: Compare the Proclamation with Antietam (September 1862), Gettysburg and Vicksburg (July 1863), and Atlanta (September 1864).
A: For: changed the aims and nature of the war; brought in about 180,000 Black soldiers; ended the chance of British and French recognition; freed labour from the Confederacy; made a compromise peace impossible. Against: its effect depended on military success (Antietam gave it its moment; Gettysburg and Vicksburg made it safe); the Border States were excluded; Atlanta and the 1864 election secured the Northern will to continue; the 13th Amendment, not the Proclamation, ended slavery. Level 5 judges using a criterion, e.g. "which event did most to make Union victory certain?", and may conclude that the military turning point was July 1863 but the Proclamation was the political and moral turning point.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent did foreign powers influence the course of the Civil War?
H: Decide what "influence" means: did they change events, or did the fear or hope of their action change decisions?
H: Compare the three powers: which came closest to acting, and what stopped them?
A: Support: the Trent affair, the cotton famine, the Alabama and the rams show Britain's importance; the hope of recognition shaped Confederate offensives and Lincoln's timing of emancipation; France's Mexican adventure; the Russian visit encouraged the Union. Challenge: no power recognised the Confederacy or broke the blockade; the war was decided by American armies and leaders; Britain's neutrality helped the Union by accepting the blockade; Russia's visit was not an alliance. Level 5 judges that foreign powers influenced decisions and hopes more than events: their non-intervention, rather than intervention, was what mattered.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ James McPherson argues that the outcome of the Civil War depended on contingency, a series of moments when the result could have gone either way. How far does the evidence of 1862–64 support this view?
H: Identify the moments: the summer of 1862 (McClellan on the Peninsula, Lee's Seven Days), the autumn of 1862 (Antietam and the Proclamation), the summer of 1864 (the Overland Campaign and Atlanta).
H: Then ask whether the North's resources and the Confederacy's weaknesses made the outcome likely even if those moments had gone the other way.
A: Support: McClellan could have taken Richmond in 1862; had Lee won at Antietam, Britain and France might have mediated; Lincoln expected to lose the election in August 1864, and without Atlanta the Democrats might have won and negotiated an armistice that left the Confederacy independent. Challenge: the Confederacy's population and industry made a long war unwinnable; the blockade and the loss of the Mississippi were wearing it down; Lincoln's coalition, though shaken, held; and Britain's reluctance to intervene had deeper causes. A* judgement: resources made Union victory probable, but the moments McPherson stresses decided whether it would come and in what form, so the evidence supports contingency about how and when, not about whether.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Gary Gallagher argues that Confederate will to fight stayed strong until military defeat, and that the Confederacy lost because of Union armies, not because the South gave up. How far do the sources in this chapter and your knowledge support this view?
H: Which evidence shows will? Think of Lee's victories, conscription, Davis's refusal to negotiate, and the conduct of the Richmond bread riot.
H: Which evidence shows will weakening? Think of desertion, inflation, the governors and Sherman's march.
A: Support: the Confederacy kept fighting for four years, with conscription and a strong army to the end; Davis rejected compromise; Lee's victories raised morale until 1863; the army surrendered only when it was surrounded at Appomattox, and Johnston's and Kirby Smith's armies held out until April–June 1865. Challenge: desertion rose sharply in 1864–65; inflation and impressment turned farmers and women against the government (Richmond, April 1863); governors resisted the central government; Sherman's march showed the interior could not be defended; Davis's own supporters lost heart after Atlanta. Judgement: Gallagher's view is strongly supported for 1861–64, as the army and civilians continued, but wartime strain is evident in the home front, so the evidence supports will as a reason the war lasted four years, and military defeat as the reason it ended.
:::

:::recap
- **Strategy**: Scott's Anaconda Plan (3 May 1861): blockade, Mississippi, a trained army. The Confederacy needed only to survive and used an offensive-defensive strategy; its invasions failed at Antietam (17 September 1862) and Gettysburg (1–3 July 1863).
- **Leadership**: the Union's generals failed in Virginia (McClellan, Burnside, Hooker) until Grant became general-in-chief (March 1864); Lee and Jackson won victories but Lee's attacks cost men the South could not replace.
- **Resources**: the Union had about 22 million people to 9 million (3.5 million enslaved), roughly nine-tenths of manufacturing and about 22,000 miles of railway to 9,000. They gave the North its eventual advantage but defence, geography and leadership delayed it.
- **Lincoln and Davis**: Lincoln held the coalition together and found Grant; Davis created a government but quarrelled with generals and governors.
- **Conscription, taxes, money**: Confederate conscription (16 April 1862); Union Enrollment Act (3 March 1863; $300 or a substitute); draft riots (13–16 July 1863); greenbacks (25 February 1862); Union prices up about 80 per cent, Confederate about 9,000 per cent.
- **Border States**: Kentucky, Missouri, Maryland, Delaware kept in the Union by care, force and the arrest of opponents.
- **Emancipation Proclamation**: preliminary 22 September 1862, effective 1 January 1863; military necessity, Black soldiers (about 180,000), foreign opinion, political pressure; it did not free enslaved people in the Border States.
- **Civil liberties**: habeas corpus suspended (27 April 1861; Congress 3 March 1863); Vallandigham (May 1863); the Confederacy also restricted rights.
- **Foreign powers**: Britain (Trent, cotton, Alabama; neutral), France (Napoleon III, Mexico; would not act alone), Russia (fleets in 1863; goodwill). None recognised the Confederacy.
- **Ending**: Atlanta (2 September 1864), Lincoln's re-election (8 November), Sherman's march, Appomattox (9 April 1865).
- **Judgement**: resources made Northern victory likely; leadership, strategy, political will and the defensive advantage decided how long it took.
:::
