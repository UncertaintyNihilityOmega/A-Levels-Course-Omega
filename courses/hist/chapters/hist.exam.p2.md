Includes a mark-band explorer.

:::why
Paper 2 is the larger of the two AS papers: 60 marks, and 60% of an AS grade. It is the essay paper, and it rewards the skill that history students find hardest: not knowing what happened, but arguing why it happened and how much each cause mattered. Most students know enough to score well. What separates a Level 3 from a Level 5 is almost never facts. It is whether the answer **explains** instead of describing, **connects** causes instead of listing them, and **judges** instead of just presenting both sides. This chapter shows each step.
:::

## What the paper looks like

**The front page.** It is the same front page as every 9489 paper. It says you answer on the enclosed answer booklet. The instruction is "Answer two questions from one section only", and the sections are A (the European option), B (the American option) and C (the International option). The information box says the total mark is 60 and that the marks for each question or part are shown in square brackets. Nothing else is supplied. You have no sources, no map and no list of dates, so everything must come from your memory.

**The questions.** In the 2027 specimen paper, each section has **two** questions, one on each of that year's two Paper 2 topics, so there are six questions in all. Each question has two parts, (a) and (b). Because you must answer two questions and the section has two, you answer **both**, and **both parts of both**. If the live papers follow the specimen, as the syllabus design suggests, there is no choice of questions within your option.

:::warn The old papers had a choice
Papers from the 2024–26 syllabus offered a choice of three questions in each section, so a student could leave out a topic. The 2027 specimen does not. You cannot avoid a topic. Revise **every key question of both** of your Paper 2 topics. If you practise on an old paper, remember that it overstates your freedom.
:::

**How a section is headed.** A section begins with the option title (for example "Modern Europe, 1774–1924"), then each question has the title of its topic above it. Each question then reads "(a) Explain why …" and "(b) …".

## Which topics are on your paper?

Each AS option has three topics. In any exam year, **two** of them are on Paper 2 and the third is the Paper 1 sources topic. The pair changes every year in a fixed rotation.

| Exam year | European option | American option | International option |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2027 | Germany; Russia | Civil War and Reconstruction; Great Crash, Great Depression and New Deal | International relations 1919–29; International history 1929–39 |
| 2028 | France; Russia | Civil War and Reconstruction; Gilded Age and Progressive Era | Imperialism and world powers; International history 1929–39 |
| 2029 | France; Germany | Gilded Age and Progressive Era; Great Crash, Great Depression and New Deal | Imperialism and world powers; International relations 1919–29 |

Check the pairs against the Paper 1 rotation in [Paper 1: Historical sources](#hist.exam.p1): every topic is on Paper 1 in one year and on Paper 2 in the other two. A topic that is not on Paper 1 in your year will certainly be on Paper 2.

**A question can be set on any part of the topic.** Each topic has four key questions, and the examiners may ask part (a) on any content in any of them. So revise all four key questions of both topics. The specimen mark schemes list "indicative content" for each question, and always add "Accept any other valid responses". A valid point that is not on the list can still earn full marks.

## What the examiners are testing

:::def AO2: Historical explanation, analysis and judgement
Identify, explain and analyse the past using historical concepts: **cause and consequence**, **change and continuity**, and **significance**; explain and analyse **connections** between different aspects of the past; and **reach a judgement**. On Paper 2 it is half of the marks.
:::

:::def AO1: Historical knowledge
Recall, select and use appropriate historical knowledge. On Paper 2 it is the other half of the marks. It does not mean writing everything you know: it means choosing the facts that **prove the point you are making**.
:::

The three concepts appear in question wording. The questions below are examples written for this guide; they are not past-paper questions.

| Concept | What the question asks | Example wording |
|---|---|---|
| Cause and consequence | Why something happened; what it led to | "Explain why the Directory collapsed in 1799." |
| Change and continuity | How much changed and how much stayed the same | "How far did Napoleon's rule change the lives of ordinary people in France?" |
| Significance | How much something mattered, and why | "How important was the railway to the growth of the American economy after 1870?" |

## Command words: what each one asks you to do

The syllabus defines four command words. Only the first is the command of Paper 2 part (a); the others you meet on the other papers, and knowing all four helps you read any question.

| Command word | Meaning | Where you mostly meet it |
|---|---|---|
| **Explain** | Set out purposes or reasons; make the relationships between things clear; say why and/or how, and support with relevant evidence | Paper 2 part (a): "Explain why …" |
| **Assess** | Make an informed judgement | Paper 4 |
| **Evaluate** | Judge the quality, importance, amount or value of something | Paper 4 |
| **Compare** | Identify and comment on similarities and/or differences | Paper 1 part (a) |

Part (b) of Paper 2 does not use a single command word. It is set in these forms:
- "'…' How far do you agree with this view?"
- "To what extent …?"
- "How far was … successful / unsuccessful?"
- "How far was … the key factor / the main reason …?"
- "How successful / how important was …?"

## Part (a) [10]: Explain why

### What the question asks

"Explain why [something happened or mattered]." The answer is a set of **reasons**, each explained, and the links between them. A good answer to part (a) is not a story of the event.

### The levels of Table A

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 9–10 | **Connects factors** to reach a reasoned conclusion: well focused; explains a range of factors with relevant information; shows a clear understanding of the connections between causes; gives a supported conclusion |
| 3 | 6–8 | **Explains factor(s)**: good knowledge and understanding of what the question demands; explained factor(s) supported by relevant information |
| 2 | 3–5 | **Describes factor(s)**: some knowledge; addresses causation, but may be entirely descriptive |
| 1 | 1–2 | **Describes the topic or issue**: some relevant material but no reference to causation |
| 0 | 0 | No creditable response |

:::key The step from level to level in part (a)
- **Level 1 to 2**: use words that **cause**: "because", "so", "led to". Name factors.
- **Level 2 to 3**: **explain** each factor: say how it brought about the event, with a date, name or number as evidence.
- **Level 3 to 4**: **range, connection and conclusion**. Explain two or three factors, show how they were **linked**, and finish by saying which mattered most (or how they combined) and why.
:::

### The ladder: one question, four levels

:::eg Part (a) ladder: Explain why Nicholas II abdicated in March 1917.
All dates are Old Style, the calendar Russia used until 1918, with the New Style date at first mention. See [Russia (3)](#hist.eu.russia.3) for the history.

**Level 1 (1–2 marks): describes the topic.**
"Nicholas II was the last Tsar of Russia. He became Tsar in 1894 and he was married to Alexandra. In March 1917 he gave up the throne and Russia was no longer a monarchy."

*Commentary.* Relevant and accurate, but it never says why. There is no reference to causation. Level 1.

**Level 2 (3–5 marks): describes factors and addresses causation, but does not explain.**
"Russia was doing badly in the war and there was not enough food in Petrograd. There were strikes and the army joined the strikers. So the Tsar had to abdicate."

*Commentary.* Three relevant factors and a causal word ("so"), but each is only stated. The reader is not told how a food shortage or an army mutiny forced a Tsar to give up power. Level 2.

**Level 3 (6–8 marks): explains factors.**
"One reason was the failure of the war. From 1915 the army was driven back out of Poland, short of shells and rifles, and millions of men were killed, wounded or captured. When Nicholas made himself commander-in-chief in August 1915 (September New Style) and went to the front, the defeats became his personal failures, and the government at home was left to the Tsarina and ministers who changed repeatedly.

A second reason was hardship. The government printed money to pay for the war, so prices rose faster than wages, and the railways were choked with military traffic, so bread and fuel did not reach the cities. On 23 February 1917 (8 March New Style) women textile workers in Petrograd went on strike for bread, and within two days the strike had become a general strike. The Tsar's authority was being challenged on the streets."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with accurate support (dates, the printing of money, the railways). Good knowledge and understanding. But the factors are in separate boxes and there is no conclusion about which mattered or how they fit together. Level 3.

**Level 4 (9–10 marks): connects the factors and reaches a conclusion.** Keep the two paragraphs above and add:

"These two factors were linked. The war caused the hardship, because it cost the lives of the men who would have worked the land and it broke the railways. The strain turned a bread strike into a challenge to the regime. But neither factor alone ended the monarchy. A crowd can be dispersed; what decided the matter was the army. On 27 February the Petrograd garrison, mostly raw recruits and peasants afraid of being sent to the front, refused to fire on the crowds and joined them. On 1 March the Tsar's train was stopped at Pskov, where General Ruzsky told him that only a change of government could save the situation, and the front commanders then advised him to abdicate to save the army and the war effort. He abdicated on 2 March (15 March New Style).

So the main reason was that the war, by wearing out both the people and the army, left the Tsar with no force he could trust. Hardship put the people on the streets, but the loss of the army's loyalty, itself caused by the war, is what turned a strike into an abdication."

*Commentary.* A range of factors, each supported; explicit connections (the war caused the hardship and the army's disloyalty); and a supported conclusion that says which factor was the most important and why. Level 4, 9–10 marks. This takes about 350 to 450 words and 14 minutes.
:::

### How to write part (a)

1. **Name the question's focus in the first sentence.** "Nicholas abdicated because a long war had exhausted the people and turned the army against him." That sentence is already a line of argument.
2. **Two or three reasons, one paragraph each.** Start each paragraph with the reason, then **how** it led to the outcome, then the evidence (a date, a name, a number). Do not give four or five thin reasons: two or three explained ones are better.
3. **Link the reasons.** One sentence is enough: "This made the second factor worse because …"; "Without the first, the second would not have mattered."
4. **End with a short conclusion** that says which reason mattered most, or how they combined. It does not need to be long; it needs to be supported by what you have written.

:::exam Technique for part (a)
- 12 to 15 minutes for 10 marks. A paragraph of 100 words, a conclusion of 40 words. No long introduction.
- The word "why" asks for reasons, not a narrative. If a sentence tells the story without saying how it caused the outcome, rewrite it.
- Use a precise piece of evidence in every paragraph: a date, a name, a figure.
- You do not have to cover every possible reason. The mark scheme says "Accept any other valid responses". A smaller number of well-explained reasons beats a long list.
- Part (a) is worth one third of the marks of a question, so give it about a third of the time (14 of the 46 minutes in the plan below), and no more.
:::

## Part (b) [20]: how far do you agree?

### What the question asks

Part (b) gives you a statement, a claim or a question and asks you to weigh it. It is an **argument**: you decide how far the claim is true, and you prove your decision with evidence. Examiners call the different sides of the argument **alternative perspectives**.

:::def Alternative perspective
A different explanation or factor that could be set against the one in the question. If the question asks how far one factor was the main reason, the alternative perspectives are the other factors; if it asks whether a policy was successful, they are the aspects in which it failed.
:::

### The levels of Table B

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 17–20 | **A comparative judgement based on analysis of the alternative perspectives**: well focused, clear analysis that supports and challenges, consistently supported by relevant, detailed evidence, and a clear comparative judgement of the relative strength of the perspectives |
| 4 | 13–16 | **Explanation that considers alternative perspectives**: supports and challenges, with mostly relevant detailed evidence; may form a conclusion that lacks a clear comparative judgement |
| 3 | 9–12 | **Explanation of a single perspective**: supports or challenges, with mostly relevant evidence; other perspectives described rather than explained |
| 2 | 5–8 | **Considers aspects of the question without explanation**: relevant description, few explicit links; valid assertions with limited support |
| 1 | 1–4 | **General information** loosely linked to the question; fragmentary points; unsupported assertions |
| 0 | 0 | No creditable response |

:::key The step from level to level in part (b)
- **Level 1 to 2**: use relevant facts in place of general statements.
- **Level 2 to 3**: stop describing and **explain** how one factor or argument answers the question.
- **Level 3 to 4**: explain **both sides**: the case for the view and an alternative perspective, each with evidence. This is the largest jump.
- **Level 4 to 5**: **compare** the sides. Do not stop at "on the one hand … on the other hand". Say which is stronger, using a criterion, and why.
:::

:::warn "Balance" is not the same as "judgement"
The Level 4 descriptor says the answer "may form a conclusion that lacks clear comparative judgement". That describes the most common conclusion in the exam hall: "In conclusion, there were many causes, some more important than others." Level 5 needs a **comparison**: "Factor X was more important than factor Y because …". Choose a **criterion** (what made the outcome possible; what made it last; what affected most people) and rank the factors by it.
:::

### Planning part (b): templates for each kind of question

Spend three to four minutes planning. In every case the plan has three parts: **the case for**, **the case against (the alternatives)** and **the judgement and its criterion**.

| Form of the question | What to weigh | Plan |
|---|---|---|
| "'…' How far do you agree with this view?" (a claim with one factor in it) | The factor in the claim against other factors | For: the claim's factor, 2 pieces of evidence. Against: 2 other factors with evidence. Judgement: which is stronger by your criterion |
| "To what extent …?" | The size of an effect or the strength of a cause | Decide the measure ("extent" of what?). Show where it was large, where it was limited. Judge |
| "How far was X the key factor / the main reason …?" | X against the other causes | Rank X against 2 or 3 others: decisive, necessary, only a trigger? Explain how they are linked. Judge |
| "How far was X successful / unsuccessful?" or "How successful was X?" | Success against the **aims** of X | State the aims first. Success against each aim (short term, long term). Failure against each. Overall verdict |
| "How important was X?" | The importance of X, compared with other things | Define "important" (what changed because of X?). Compare with the alternatives. Judge |

Use the plan that fits the form, and then check that your last paragraph answers the exact words of the question.

### How to write part (b)

1. **Introduction (3 to 4 sentences).** Say what the key words mean ("the main reason" means the one without which … would not have happened), and give your line of argument: "I will argue that …".
2. **The case for the claim** (one or two paragraphs). **Point**, **evidence**, **explanation of how it answers the question**, and a link to the question's words.
3. **The alternative perspectives** (one or two paragraphs). The same, for the other factors or the weaknesses of the claim. Show how they interact with the first factor: "this made X more serious because …".
4. **Conclusion (4 to 6 sentences).** Answer "how far", name your criterion, say which perspective is stronger, and say why. Do not introduce new evidence; do not repeat the whole essay.

Every paragraph ends with a sentence that says what it means for the question ("so the war was a cause of the government's collapse, but it did not decide who replaced it").

### The ladder: one question, from Level 2 to Level 5

:::eg Part (b) ladder: 'The Provisional Government failed because it continued the war.' How far do you agree with this view? [20]
All dates are Old Style. See [Russia (3)](#hist.eu.russia.3).

**Plan.**
- *For:* the June offensive (18 June) and its collapse; desertions; the army's loss of trust in the government; the Kornilov affair.
- *Against 1, land:* the peasants wanted the landowners' estates, the government said only the Constituent Assembly could decide, and the peasants seized the land anyway.
- *Against 2, the economy:* inflation, shortages and strikes went on; workers formed factory committees.
- *Against 3, dual power:* the Soviet held the real power in the garrison, the railways and the post, so the government could not act alone.
- *Judgement, with criterion:* what cost the government the support it needed to survive. The war made every other problem worse, so it was the most important, but it was not the only reason.

**Level 2 (5–8 marks): description with few links.**
"The Provisional Government continued the war. There was an offensive in June 1917 and many soldiers were killed. There were also food shortages and the peasants wanted land. The Bolsheviks took power in October."

*Commentary.* Relevant material, but only listed. No factor is explained and there is no argument. Level 2.

**Level 3 (9–12 marks): explains a single perspective.**
"The Provisional Government failed largely because it continued the war. The government felt bound by Russia's alliance with Britain and France, but many soldiers wanted the fighting to end. On 18 June 1917 Alexander Kerensky, the War Minister, ordered an offensive in Galicia. It gained a little ground and was then thrown back by a German counter-attack in July. Thousands of soldiers deserted and the army, which had been the last support of any government, never recovered. Soldiers who were willing to defend Russia but not to attack blamed the government, so by the time of the Kornilov affair in late August nobody in the army trusted Kerensky. The war therefore destroyed the government's base of support."

*Commentary.* A single perspective, explained with accurate evidence and linked to the question. Other factors (land, the economy) might be mentioned but are not explained. Level 3.

**Level 4 (13–16 marks): adds an explained alternative perspective.** Add:
"However, the war was not the only reason. The land question cost the government the support of the peasants, who made up most of the population. They wanted the estates of the landowners and could not understand why they should wait. The government replied that only the Constituent Assembly could decide something so important, but it could not meet while the war went on. From the spring the villages took the land themselves, and the government was seen as the landlords' friend. Dual power also weakened it from the start: Order No. 1 of 1 March told the soldiers of the Petrograd garrison to obey the government only where the Soviet agreed, so the government held the legal authority but the Soviet held the force. Inflation and shortages continued, and workers who had hoped the revolution would improve their lives turned to factory committees and, later, the Bolsheviks."

*Commentary.* Both sides explained with detailed evidence. The answer is now at Level 4. If the conclusion simply said "the war and the land question were both important", it would stay here.

**Level 5 (17–20 marks): a comparative judgement.** Add a conclusion such as:
"Overall, the view is largely correct, but the war was a cause of failure rather than the whole explanation. The criterion is what removed the government's support at the moments that decided its fate. Land and the economy lost it the peasants and workers over months. But it was the war that made these problems impossible to solve: it postponed the Constituent Assembly, so the land question could not be settled; it fed inflation and shortages; and the June offensive wrecked the army, which meant that when Kornilov moved on Petrograd in August Kerensky had no troops he could trust and had to ask the Soviet for help, which armed the Bolsheviks' Red Guards. The war was therefore the cause that made the others fatal. Without it, a government with a settled land policy might have survived. Because it fought on, it could not, and that makes the war the most important single reason, though not the only one."

*Commentary.* The conclusion sets a criterion, **compares** the factors, shows how they interact, and gives a firm judgement on "how far". With the paragraphs above it, this is Level 5, in the top two marks, 19–20.
:::

### The commonest weak paragraph

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was Prussia's military strength the main reason for German unification by 1871?" [20]

A student wrote:

"Prussia fought three wars. In 1864 Prussia and Austria fought Denmark. In 1866 Prussia fought Austria and won at Königgrätz on 3 July. In 1870 Prussia fought France and won at Sedan on 1 September, and the German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles on 18 January 1871. So Prussia's military strength was the main reason for unification."

**The level.** Accurate, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story of the wars without arguing that the army was the cause. It never explains **how** military strength produced unification, never compares it with any other factor, and its last sentence is an assertion. It is Level 2 (5–8): relevant description, few explicit links.

**The fix.** Start with the argument, use only the evidence that proves it, then compare. "Military strength was necessary because only victories could remove Austria from Germany and bring the southern states into a union under Prussia: the defeat of Austria in 1866 ended Austrian influence and led to the North German Confederation, and the defeat of France in 1870 brought in the southern states. But the army did not act alone. Bismarck's diplomacy kept other powers out of the wars, so Prussia fought one enemy at a time; and the Zollverein, which did not include Austria, had already tied the economies of the German states to Prussia. So the army was the necessary means, but diplomacy decided which wars Prussia fought and against whom." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement, and a second factor is explained.
:::

## Timing

105 minutes for 60 marks: 1.75 minutes a mark. A suggested plan, which Cambridge does not set:

| Minutes | Task | Marks |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | Read both questions; jot the factors for each (a); plan both (b)s roughly | n/a |
| 14 | Question 1 (a): write | 10 |
| 32 | Question 1 (b): plan (4 min), write (28 min) | 20 |
| 14 | Question 2 (a): write | 10 |
| 32 | Question 2 (b): plan (4 min), write (28 min) | 20 |
| 7 | Check both questions; finish any unfinished conclusion | n/a |
| **105** | **Total** | **60** |

Practise the times at home. The two most common timing errors are to spend 25 minutes on a part (a) worth 10 marks, and to run out of time before the conclusion of the second part (b). If time runs short, write the conclusion of the part (b), even in two sentences: a judgement is the step to Level 5.

## Common mistakes

These come from the descriptors: each is a feature the mark scheme says does **not** earn a higher level.

:::warn Ten ways to lose marks on Paper 2
1. **Narrative** (part (a) Level 2; part (b) Level 2). Telling what happened instead of why.
2. **Listing reasons without explaining them.** "The reasons were X, Y and Z." Each needs "because", the evidence and the link to the outcome.
3. **No connections** (part (a) cannot reach Level 4). Factors in separate boxes with no sentence relating them.
4. **One-sided (b) answer** (Level 3). Only the case for or only the case against.
5. **"On the one hand / on the other hand" with no verdict** (Level 4). Both sides but no comparison.
6. **A conclusion that repeats the introduction** or says "there were many factors". Say which was stronger and why.
7. **Ignoring the key word of the question** ("main", "successful", "key", "extent", "view"). Underline it and use it in every paragraph.
8. **Vague evidence**: "a lot of people", "in the 1920s". Use names, dates, places and figures.
9. **Writing an essay on the topic** rather than the question. If you cannot say how a paragraph answers the question, cut it.
10. **Poor timing.** An unfinished second (b) loses far more marks than a shorter first one.
:::

## Revising for Paper 2

Because there is no choice within your option, your revision has to cover both topics fully.

:::steps
1. **Turn each key question into questions.** For each chapter, write three "Explain why …" questions and three "How far …" statements, using the section headings and the key boxes of the topic chapter.
2. **Build one-page summaries**: five or six factors, each with two precise pieces of evidence (a date, a name, a number), and the links between the factors.
3. **Practise plans**: take a statement and plan the for, against and judgement in four minutes. Do ten of these a week.
4. **Write timed answers**: one (a) in 14 minutes, one (b) in 32 minutes, and mark each one with the tables above.
5. **Rewrite the weakest paragraph** of each answer at the next level up, with the level descriptor open.
:::

Each topic chapter in this course ends with Paper 2 practice and worked examples in the real style. The skills are taught in [Skills: writing the analytical essay (Papers 2 and 4)](#hist.skills.essays).

## Using past papers and mark schemes

- **The 2027 specimen Paper 2** has six questions, two in each section, each with parts (a) [10] and (b) [20]. It is the best guide to what the 2027–2029 papers will look like. Cambridge's past-papers page has it with its mark scheme.
- **Old papers (2024–26 syllabus)** have the same front page but offered a choice of three questions per section. Use them for topics that survive in the new syllabus.
- **The mark scheme's indicative content** for part (a) is a list of reasons; for part (b) it is a "Support" list and a "Challenge" list. Always followed by "Accept any other valid responses". Compare your plan with it, and note any valid points you missed.

:::recap
- Paper 2: **1 h 45 min, 60 marks**; two questions from **one section** (your option); each has (a) [10] "Explain why …" and (b) [20] "How far …?"; no sources; booklet only. The 2027 specimen has six questions, two per section, so you answer both questions of your section.
- Weights: AS 60%, A Level 30%; AO1 50% and AO2 50%.
- **Rotation:** 2027 Germany and Russia; Civil War and Great Crash; 1919–29 and 1929–39. 2028 France and Russia; Civil War and Gilded Age; Imperialism and 1929–39. 2029 France and Germany; Gilded Age and Great Crash; Imperialism and 1919–29.
- **(a)** Level 1 describes the topic; Level 2 describes factors; Level 3 explains factors; Level 4 connects factors to a reasoned conclusion.
- **(b)** Level 1 general information; Level 2 description with few links; Level 3 explains a single perspective; Level 4 explains alternative perspectives; Level 5 gives a comparative judgement.
- Level 4 to 5 is the step from "balance" to **judgement**: use a criterion and say which side is stronger.
- Within a level: **just** (lowest mark), **adequately** (middle), **convincingly** (highest). Marks are positive and never deducted.
- Plan every (b): case for, case against, judgement with its criterion.
- Suggested timing: 6 min reading, then 14 + 32 minutes per question, 7 minutes checking.
