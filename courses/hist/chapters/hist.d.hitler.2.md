## "Effectiveness": what the question is asking

The key question is "How effectively did the Nazis govern Germany?" A good answer first decides what "effectively" means. Four measures are useful and the chapter uses them.

1. **Control:** could the regime stop opposition and make people obey? (the terror state, the courts and the camps)
2. **Consent:** did it win active or passive support? (propaganda, culture, rallies and the Olympics)
3. **Limits:** how much opposition was there, and what did it achieve? (youth, the churches, the left and passive resistance)
4. **Aims and results abroad:** did foreign policy do what the regime wanted, and was it planned? (the steps from 1933 to 1941)

You will see that the regime was very effective at control and fairly effective at consent, and that opposition existed but was weak. In foreign policy it was effective at first and ended in a war that it could not win.

## The terror state

A **terror state** is one in which the government uses fear, arbitrary arrest and violence as regular tools of rule. The syllabus lists six parts: the Gestapo, block wardens, the police and SD, the judiciary and courts, the SS and the concentration camps. The sketch below shows how they fitted together.

### The Gestapo

The **Gestapo** (*Geheime Staatspolizei*, "secret state police") began in April 1933 as the political police of Prussia, set up by Hermann Göring, then Prussia's Interior Minister. In April 1934 Heinrich Himmler, the head of the SS, took control of it, with **Reinhard Heydrich** as its chief, and by a law of 1936 its actions were put beyond the review of the ordinary courts. Its officers could arrest people and hold them in "protective custody" without trial, and send them to a camp.

:::def Gestapo
The Secret State Police of Nazi Germany, created in Prussia in 1933 and extended across the Reich under Himmler and Heydrich in 1934–36. It investigated "enemies of the state", could arrest without trial, and was not subject to review by the courts after the law of 1936.
:::

The Gestapo's reputation was greater than its size. Estimates are that it had about **6,500** officers in 1937 and perhaps **32,000** staff, including clerks, by 1944. In some areas the numbers were very small: in Lower Franconia, around Würzburg, a district of more than 800,000 people, the local office had about two dozen officers. It could not watch the whole population, so it depended on the public. Robert Gellately, in *The Gestapo and German Society* (1990), studied the case files of Würzburg and found that in a large share of cases the Gestapo began an investigation because somebody **denounced** a neighbour, a workmate or a relative. In the files on "race defilement" (sexual relations between Jews and non-Jews, forbidden after 1935), more than half had started in this way. Denunciations had many motives: some came from loyalty to the regime, others from personal quarrels, jealousy or the wish for a flat or a job. The Gestapo was therefore a system in which fear of informers did much of the work of the police.

### Block wardens

The Nazi Party organised the whole country in a ladder: the *Gau* (region), *Kreis* (district), *Ortsgruppe* (local group), *Zelle* (cell) and **Block**. A **block warden** (*Blockleiter*) was responsible for about 40 to 60 households in his street or apartment buildings. He was a volunteer of the Party and he knew his neighbours. He collected donations for the Party's charities (the Winter Relief), kept a record of each household, promoted the Party's message and reported anything that suggested hostility to the regime (a flag not flown on a holiday, a person who did not give the Hitler salute, a remark about the war) to the Party and the Gestapo. By the end of the war the Party had perhaps half a million block wardens. Their power came not from police rank but from the fact that a bad report could cost someone a job, a ration or a flat.

### The police and the SD

The ordinary police were brought under the same control. Himmler became **Chief of German Police** on 17 June 1936 and divided the police into two branches: the **Order Police** (the uniformed Orpo) and the **Security Police** (Sipo), made up of the Gestapo and the criminal police (Kripo). The **SD** (*Sicherheitsdienst*, "security service") was a different body: it was founded by Himmler in 1931 with Heydrich as its leader and became the intelligence service of the Party and the SS, which collected information on opinion and on opponents (the SD reports on public morale are a historian's source). On 27 September 1939 the Security Police and the SD were joined in the **Reich Security Main Office (RSHA)**, led by Heydrich. This fusion of the Party and the state meant that the police were no longer bound by law.

### The judiciary and the courts

The courts were brought into line with the regime in four ways. Judges and prosecutors who were Jews or opponents were dismissed in 1933 under the Civil Service Law. **Special Courts** (*Sondergerichte*) were set up in March 1933 to try political offences swiftly, with limited rights for the accused. The **People's Court** (*Volksgerichtshof*) was created on 24 April 1934 in Berlin to try treason, after the Nazis were dissatisfied with the verdict in the Reichstag Fire trial, in which the Communists were acquitted. Its judges were chosen for their loyalty and it passed about 5,200 death sentences between 1934 and 1945, most of them from 1942, when it was led by **Roland Freisler**. And in 1934 Hitler himself had claimed the right to act as supreme judge (see [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1)). Ordinary criminal and civil justice continued in many cases, so most Germans saw the courts working as before; but for political cases, justice was not independent of the regime.

### The SS and the concentration camps

The **SS** (*Schutzstaffel*, "protection squad") had begun as Hitler's bodyguard, a small elite unit within the SA. Himmler took command in 1929 and made it a separate force, based on racial selection and absolute loyalty to Hitler. After the Night of the Long Knives it became independent of the SA (20 July 1934) and after 1936 it controlled the police. It also ran the **concentration camps**.

:::def Concentration camp
A camp in which people were held without trial or after arbitrary arrest, for being opponents or members of groups the Nazis defined as enemies or "inferior", and where prisoners were forced to work, beaten and often killed. They were run by the SS and were separate from the ordinary prison system.
:::

The first camps of 1933 were improvised by the SA, SS and local police in disused factories and cellars, and held tens of thousands of Communists, Social Democrats and trade unionists. Most closed within a year or two, but **Dachau**, opened on 22 March 1933 near Munich, became the model. Under its commandant Theodor Eicke, the SS developed a system of strict rules and brutal punishments there, and Eicke later became the head of the camp system. New camps were built in 1936–39: Sachsenhausen (1936), Buchenwald (1937), Flossenbürg (1938), Mauthausen (1938) and Ravensbrück, for women (1939), so by September 1939 there were six main camps. The prisoners at first were political opponents; later came Jehovah's Witnesses (who refused to serve), men accused of homosexuality, people classed as "asocial" or work-shy, Sinti and Roma, and, after the pogrom of 9–10 November 1938 (Kristallnacht), about 30,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen. The camps are dealt with further in [The Holocaust (1): from antisemitism to genocide](#hist.interp.holocaust.1).

:::key How the terror state worked
1. **Few officers, many informers.** The Gestapo had about 6,500 officers in 1937; it relied on denunciations from the public and on the Party's block wardens.
2. **No legal limit.** The 1936 law removed the Gestapo from court review; "protective custody" meant arrest without trial.
3. **Courts that served the regime.** Special Courts (1933) and the People's Court (1934, about 5,200 death sentences by 1945) tried political cases.
4. **The SS state.** Himmler controlled the SS, the police (from 1936) and the camps; the RSHA (1939) joined the Party's SD and the state's police.
5. **The camps as a threat.** Dachau (1933) and five more main camps by 1939 held opponents and, increasingly, groups the regime defined as enemies.
:::

### How far did terror explain Nazi rule?

Historians disagree about how important terror was and whom it reached. Two views are often contrasted.

- **Eric Johnson**, in *Nazi Terror* (1999), argued that the terror was **selective**: it was aimed at Communists, Social Democrats, Jews and a few other groups, and the great majority of non-Jewish Germans who obeyed the law had little to fear from the Gestapo. The Gestapo was small and its files show that most Germans never came to its attention.
- **Robert Gellately**, in *The Gestapo and German Society* (1990) and *Backing Hitler* (2001), argued that the terror was widely **known and approved**: it was reported in the press, many Germans welcomed the persecution of "outsiders" such as criminals and Communists, and many collaborated by denouncing neighbours. The regime was in his view based on consent and coercion together.

The two accounts agree on one important point: the regime did not need to arrest most people. A small number of arrests, well publicised, was enough to make others careful.

:::warn Terror was not the whole story
Do not write that Hitler ruled "only by terror" or "only by consent". The terror hit selected groups hard (Communists in 1933, Jews from 1933, Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexuals, the "work-shy"), and for those groups it was total. For the majority it was a background threat. The regime also offered jobs, order and national pride, and it is the combination that makes the regime effective. Use both sides in an essay on "terror and consent".
:::

## Propaganda

**Propaganda** is the deliberate spreading of information and ideas to shape opinion and behaviour. The Nazis were exceptionally interested in it: Hitler had written in *Mein Kampf* about its power, and the regime had a ministry for it. The syllabus lists the Ministry and Goebbels, the media (radio, newspapers, film, art and culture), the Nuremberg Rallies and the 1936 Olympics.

### Goebbels and the Ministry of Propaganda

**Joseph Goebbels** had directed the Party's propaganda since 1930. On **13 March 1933**, soon after the election, Hitler made him head of the new **Reich Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda**. His task was not only to spread Nazi ideas but to control everything that reached the public: the ministry directed the press, radio, film, theatre, literature, music and art. On 22 September 1933 a **Reich Chamber of Culture** was created, with a chamber for each art; no one could work as a writer, journalist, actor, musician or artist without membership, and the Chamber could refuse Jews and political opponents. This meant that control was not only censorship after the fact but a licence to work.

:::def Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda
The ministry established on 13 March 1933 and led by Joseph Goebbels. It controlled the press, radio, film, theatre, literature, music and the arts, through the Reich Chamber of Culture and by direct orders to editors and broadcasters, in order to promote Nazi ideas and remove all others.
:::

### The media

**Radio.** The regime took control of the broadcasting companies in 1933, and from then on programmes were planned by the ministry. Radio was the main means for Hitler and other leaders to reach the whole country at once. Goebbels wanted every household to have a set, so in August 1933 the cheap **People's Receiver** (*Volksempfänger*, model VE301) went on sale at 76 marks, and in 1938 an even cheaper set at 35 marks. By 1941 about two-thirds of German households had a radio. Loudspeakers were put in factories, cafés and public squares, and workers were gathered to listen to important speeches. Listening to foreign stations became a crime when the war began in September 1939.

**Newspapers.** The Editors' Law of 4 October 1933 made editors responsible to the state and required them to be "Aryan" and loyal. Jewish and left-wing journalists were dismissed, opposition papers were closed, and the Nazi publishing house, the Eher Verlag under Max Amann, bought up many others, so that a large share of the press was owned by the Party. The ministry held a press conference each day to tell editors what to print and how to present it, and the result was a press with little variety. Some papers, such as the *Frankfurter Zeitung*, kept a degree of independence for several years by using hints, but they too were closed or changed in the end.

**Film.** The Reich Film Chamber controlled the industry, and Goebbels watched it closely. Most films were entertainment (comedies, romances, adventure), which was deliberate: Goebbels thought that people who were entertained were also content. Others carried the message directly. **Leni Riefenstahl's** *Triumph of the Will* (1935), a film of the 1934 Nuremberg Rally commissioned by Hitler, presented a united people following the Führer, and her *Olympia* (1938) glorified the 1936 Games. Antisemitic films such as *Jud Süß* (1940) were made later, in the war.

**Art and culture.** The regime wished to remove "un-German" influences and to promote art that glorified race, nation, work and the countryside. On **10 May 1933**, students and the SA burned books by Jewish, liberal and left-wing authors (Heinrich Heine, Heinrich Mann, Sigmund Freud and many others) in Berlin and other university towns. In 1937 the Nazis staged two exhibitions in Munich. The **Great German Art Exhibition** opened on 18 July in the new House of German Art and showed approved works of heroic figures, landscapes and healthy peasants. The next day, 19 July 1937, the **"Degenerate Art" exhibition** opened. It showed 650 paintings, sculptures and prints by 112 artists, taken from museums and hung to be mocked (works by Expressionists and others, among them Kandinsky, Nolde, Kirchner and Chagall). About two million people visited it, more than visited the approved show. In all about 5,000 works were taken from state collections. Jazz, which was described as "Negro" and "Jewish" music, was discouraged, and Jewish and modern composers were banned.

:::def Degenerate art
A Nazi term for modern art (Expressionism, abstract art, much of the work of Jewish and left-wing artists) that was said to reflect the sickness of a race and was condemned as un-German. The "Degenerate Art" exhibition of 1937 mocked it, and thousands of works were taken from German museums.
:::

### The Nuremberg Rallies

The Party held a rally each year at Nuremberg, in September, from 1933 to 1938: a week of marches, speeches, ceremonies and displays, with hundreds of thousands of members of the Party, the SA, SS, Hitler Youth and Labour Service in uniform, lit by torches and searchlights, in settings designed by the architect Albert Speer. The rally of 1933 was called the Rally of Victory, that of 1934 the Rally of Unity and Strength (the one filmed by Riefenstahl), and that of 1935 the Rally of Freedom, at which the **Nuremberg Laws** against the Jews were announced (15 September 1935). The 1938 rally celebrated "Greater Germany", and the rally planned for 1939, the "Rally of Peace", was cancelled when war began. The rallies had several purposes. They showed to Germans and to the world a disciplined, united people. They gave members of the Party a sense of belonging and of mass emotion, and they presented Hitler as the object of a quasi-religious loyalty.

### The 1936 Olympics

The Olympic Games of **1–16 August 1936** in Berlin (the Winter Games had been held at Garmisch-Partenkirchen in February) were awarded to Germany in 1931, before the Nazis took power. Although there were calls to boycott them, in the United States and Britain among other places, the attempts failed. The regime treated them as a chance to show a modern, hospitable and peaceful Germany: before the Games, signs saying "Jews not welcome" were taken down, and anti-Jewish measures were relaxed for the visitors. New stadiums were built and the first Olympic torch relay from Greece to Berlin was staged. The festival was filmed by Riefenstahl. The 49 nations that took part included a German team, which had one athlete of part-Jewish descent, the fencer Helene Mayer, who won silver. German athletes topped the medal table, ahead of the United States, and the African American athlete Jesse Owens won four gold medals (100 m, 200 m, long jump and 4 × 100 m relay), which spoiled the Nazis' claims of racial superiority. The Games gave Hitler a propaganda success abroad, and many visitors were impressed by the order and the welcome. The measures against the Jews continued after the visitors left.

:::key What propaganda was meant to do, and how well it worked
1. **Control the message.** The ministry, the Chamber of Culture, the Editors' Law and the daily press conference ensured that only approved views reached the public.
2. **Reach everyone.** The People's Receiver (76 marks, August 1933), loudspeakers and cheap cinema tickets brought the message to nearly every household.
3. **Entertain as well as instruct.** Most films and broadcasts were not political, which kept audiences.
4. **Make Hitler a symbol.** The Führer cult, the rallies and *Triumph of the Will* put Hitler above the Party and above criticism (Kershaw's "Hitler myth", see [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1)).
5. **Limits.** Propaganda worked best when it agreed with what people already believed (national pride, hatred of Versailles, order). It worked less well on Catholics and on the old working-class milieu, and it could not make people believe the news when reality (bombing, defeat) contradicted it.
:::

Ian Kershaw's study *The 'Hitler Myth'* (1987) argued that propaganda succeeded in making Hitler popular, as a man above party who had restored national pride, even when the Party itself was disliked. Reports of the SD and of the exiled Social Democrats show that people grumbled about shortages and the Party's officials, but approved of Hitler's foreign successes. The weakness of the argument is that the evidence is hard to measure: it is difficult to know what people truly thought when it was dangerous to say it.

## Opposition

The syllabus lists four kinds of opposition: youth, the churches, the left, and passive resistance. They differed greatly in aims and in effect, and the first lesson is that there was **opposition but not an effective challenge** to the regime. Before looking at them, it helps to know why historians distinguish open **resistance** (acts meant to overthrow or damage the regime, usually punished by death) from **dissent** or **non-conformity** (grumbling, avoiding rituals, listening to forbidden music). Martin Broszat and Detlev Peukert stressed this wider range of everyday behaviour; Peukert's *Inside Nazi Germany* (published in German in 1982) shows how young people, workers and others found ways of avoiding the regime's demands without trying to overthrow it.

### Opposition from youth

The Hitler Youth had been made a state organisation and, by a law of 1 December 1936, was meant to include all young people, though many did not like its drill and its demands. Young people who rejected it found other forms of dissent.

- **The Swing Youth** (*Swingjugend*) were teenagers and young adults, mostly from comfortable homes in cities such as Hamburg and Berlin, from about 1939. They loved American and British jazz and swing, which the regime banned, danced at private parties, wore English-style clothes, spoke English phrases and mocked the Nazi salute. Their "crime" was a way of life. On 18 August 1941 more than 300 Swing Youth were arrested in Hamburg, and some of the leaders were sent to youth camps and concentration camps.
- **The Edelweiss Pirates** were groups of young people aged mostly 14 to 18, in the industrial cities of western Germany (Cologne, Düsseldorf, Essen), who wanted freedom from the Hitler Youth. They hiked and camped, fought with Hitler Youth patrols, and in the war some helped deserters and distributed Allied leaflets. In November 1944, thirteen people from the Ehrenfeld group in Cologne, six of them teenagers, were hanged in public after an attack in which the local Gestapo chief was killed.
- **The White Rose** was a small group of students at Munich University, led by Hans Scholl and his sister Sophie, who wrote and distributed leaflets in 1942–43 that denounced the regime and its crimes. They were arrested on 18 February 1943 and executed on 22 February after a trial before the People's Court.

These groups were not a threat to the regime, and most were not political, but they show that the Nazi effort to win the young was not complete, and the regime's reaction shows how seriously it took non-conformity.

### The churches

The Christian churches were the only large organisations that were not simply closed down, and their record was mixed. About two-thirds of Germans were Protestants and one-third Catholics.

**The Catholic Church.** The Catholic hierarchy had been wary of Nazism before 1933. On 20 July 1933 the Vatican signed the **Concordat** with Hitler, which guaranteed the Church's rights, its schools and its organisations if it kept out of politics. This gave the regime prestige, and the regime then broke it: the government soon closed Catholic organisations, put pressure on Catholic schools and arrested priests. In March 1937 Pope Pius XI issued the encyclical ***Mit brennender Sorge*** ("With burning concern"), written in German and read from Catholic pulpits on 21 March, which protested against the violation of the Concordat and condemned the worship of race and state. The most famous protest was by **Clemens August von Galen**, Bishop of Münster. In three sermons (13 July, 20 July and 3 August 1941) he attacked Gestapo lawlessness and then condemned the killing of mentally ill and disabled people under the **Aktion T4** "euthanasia" programme (more than 70,000 people had been murdered between September 1939 and August 1941). His sermons were printed and circulated in secret in thousands of copies. The regime did not arrest him: Goebbels and others advised waiting until after the war, for fear of losing support in a Catholic region. Hitler ordered the central gassing of the T4 programme to stop on 24 August 1941, though the killing went on by other means, and historians debate how far Galen's protest caused this.

**The Protestant churches.** The Protestant church was split. The **German Christians**, a Nazi-backed movement, wanted a church that accepted Nazi racial ideas, and with state support their leader **Ludwig Müller** was made Reich Bishop in September 1933. In reaction, **Martin Niemöller**, a Berlin pastor and former U-boat commander, founded the **Pastors' Emergency League** on 21 September 1933, which had about 6,000 members by the end of the year. At the Barmen Synod of 29–31 May 1934 the opposition formed the **Confessing Church**, whose Barmen Declaration, written chiefly by the theologian Karl Barth, rejected the idea that the church could be subject to a political leader. In 1935 about 700 pastors were arrested. Niemöller was arrested on **1 July 1937** and held as Hitler's "personal prisoner" in the camps of Sachsenhausen and Dachau until 1945. The theologian **Dietrich Bonhoeffer**, a Confessing Church leader who later joined the military resistance, was hanged at Flossenbürg on 9 April 1945.

**Judging the churches.** The churches defended their own members and institutions, and their protests had some success on matters that touched their faith (the central T4 gassing was halted in August 1941, and an order removing crucifixes from Bavarian schools was withdrawn in 1941 after protests). But they did not speak out against the persecution of the Jews as a whole, and most church leaders supported the regime's foreign policy and the war. Most Protestant pastors did not join the Confessing Church, and in October 1945 the Protestant church's own Stuttgart Declaration confessed that it had failed to oppose the Nazis strongly enough. The best summary is that the churches resisted when the regime threatened them, but few resisted for the sake of others.

### Political opposition from the left

The **Communist Party (KPD)** and the **Social Democrats (SPD)** were the first targets, and they suffered the most. Their leaders were arrested in 1933 or went into exile; the SPD leadership set up in exile in Prague (the *Sopade*), and sent reports on conditions in Germany. The KPD continued underground, with cells in factories and printed leaflets, but the Gestapo, with its informers, repeatedly broke up the organisation, and thousands of members were arrested, tortured or executed. Communists also suffered from the line of the Soviet Union, which until 1935 had treated the SPD as an enemy. In the war, the **"Red Orchestra"** (*Rote Kapelle*), a network in Berlin led by Harro Schulze-Boysen, an air force officer, and Arvid Harnack, a civil servant, passed information about German war plans to the Soviet Union and distributed leaflets; the Gestapo found it in 1942 and dozens of its members were executed in 1942–43. The left's opposition was brave, but it was isolated from most of the people, and it was destroyed again and again.

### Passive resistance and non-conformity

Most opposition by ordinary Germans took the form of **passive resistance**: acts that expressed disapproval without trying to overthrow the regime. Examples include telling jokes about the Nazis in private, avoiding the "Heil Hitler" greeting (saying "Grüss Gott" instead), not giving to the Winter Relief or avoiding Party events, listening to the BBC (an offence from September 1939), helping Jewish neighbours or hiding them, absenteeism and slow working in factories, and the shared grumbling over shortages. Such behaviour could be punished with a fine, a camp or in wartime death, but it was widespread. It showed that the regime's control was not total. It did not threaten the regime.

### Other opposition

Two other forms are worth knowing in outline. Within the **army and the conservative elite**, a few officers and officials were increasingly disturbed by Hitler's risks in foreign policy and by his crimes: some plotted in 1938 to arrest Hitler if he went to war over Czechoslovakia (the plot lapsed after the Munich agreement), and the **20 July 1944** plot of Claus von Stauffenberg, which was a bomb attempt, failed. And a lone carpenter from Württemberg, **Georg Elser**, planted a bomb in a Munich beer hall on 8 November 1939; Hitler left early and eight people were killed.

:::key Why opposition was weak
1. **Terror.** Arrests, torture, camps and executions deterred and destroyed groups.
2. **Fragmentation.** The left, the churches, the youth groups and the officers did not work together; each acted from different motives.
3. **Popularity of Hitler's successes.** Jobs, the end of Versailles and bloodless foreign victories made many Germans reluctant to oppose him (the war then made opposition seem treason).
4. **The oath and obedience.** The army's oath of 1934 and German habits of obedience made resistance harder.
5. **No allies.** Until the war went badly, there was no outside power to help and no legal channel for protest.
:::

## Nazi foreign policy

The syllabus asks about the **aims** of Nazi foreign policy, the **methods** used between 1933 and 1941 and the **extent of planning and consistency**. Because the events are covered from the British and French side in the AS International chapters, this section gives the sequence from the German side and spends most of its effort on the question the exam asks: what did Hitler want, and was there a plan?

### Aims

Four aims run through Hitler's speeches and writings, from *Mein Kampf* (1925–26) and the unpublished "Second Book" of 1928 to the 1930s.

1. **Reversal of the Treaty of Versailles.** This was the aim that most Germans shared: to end disarmament and reparations, regain lost territory (the Saar, the Rhineland, the Polish Corridor and Danzig) and restore Germany's status.
2. **Pan-Germanism.** Hitler wanted to bring all Germans into one Reich (*Grossdeutschland*), including the Germans of Austria, the Sudetenland and Poland. He was an Austrian himself.
3. **Lebensraum** ("living space"). The central aim: to win land in the east, above all in Russia and Ukraine, to feed a larger German people and to ensure German power by taking land from the "inferior" Slavs. It was an aim that went far beyond the revision of Versailles.
4. **Defeating communism.** Hitler linked the Soviet Union with Jewish influence and described "Jewish Bolshevism" as the great enemy, and the destruction of the Soviet state would also serve Lebensraum.

:::def Lebensraum
"Living space": the Nazi aim of conquering territory in eastern Europe, above all in the Soviet Union, to settle Germans on it, feed the German people and create a self-sufficient empire, at the expense of the people who lived there. It was a racial and imperial aim, different from the simple revision of the Treaty of Versailles.
:::

:::note Hitler's plans in 1937: provenance and use
*Adapted from the Hossbach Memorandum, minutes of a meeting in Berlin on 5 November 1937 written by Colonel Friedrich Hossbach, Hitler's army adjutant, and dated 10 November 1937, in Documents on German Foreign Policy 1918–1945, Series D, volume 1 (Washington, 1949), as printed by the Avalon Project (Yale Law School).*

"The aim of German policy was to make secure and to preserve the racial community [Volksmasse] and to enlarge it." … "it was his unalterable resolve to solve Germany's problem of space at the latest by 1943-45."

**How to use it as evidence:** the minutes are a record of a meeting of Hitler with his War Minister, the heads of the army, navy and air force and the Foreign Minister, so they show what Hitler told the military and diplomatic leaders in private. They support the view that he had expansionist aims and a rough timetable. But Hossbach wrote them five days afterwards from memory and Hitler did not check them; the meeting was also about a dispute over raw materials for the services, and historians disagree about whether it records a plan for war or a sales talk to win the support of cautious generals. A. J. P. Taylor thought the second more likely; others, such as Gerhard Weinberg and Andreas Hillgruber, saw it as a statement of intent.
:::

### Methods, 1933–41

| Date | Step | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 14 Oct 1933 | Leaves the Disarmament Conference and the League | Ended the pretence of cooperation; a plebiscite on 12 November approved it (about 95 per cent, officially) |
| 26 Jan 1934 | Non-aggression pact with Poland | Calmed Poland and divided it from France |
| 25 Jul 1934 | Austrian Nazi putsch fails | Dollfuss murdered; Mussolini moved troops to the Brenner; Hitler backed off |

| 18 Jun 1935 | Anglo-German Naval Agreement | Britain accepted a German fleet of 35 per cent of its own, splitting Britain from France |

| 25 Nov 1936 | Anti-Comintern Pact | An agreement with Japan against communism (Italy joined in 1937) |

| 29–30 Sep 1938 | Munich: Sudetenland | Britain and France agreed the cession; German troops took it between 1 and 10 October |

| 22 May 1939 | Pact of Steel | A military alliance with Italy |

Three patterns stand out. First, the early steps (1933–36) were in each case a **revision of Versailles** that many countries, even Britain, found understandable. Second, from 1938 the steps went beyond revision: the Sudetenland was a German-speaking area, but the occupation of Prague in March 1939 absorbed Czechs, and the policy was shown to be **expansion**, which led Britain and France to guarantee Poland (31 March 1939). Third, Hitler's **methods** were flexible: he used a legal plebiscite in the Saar, diplomacy at Munich, a surprise move in the Rhineland and the Anschluss, an agreement with an enemy in the Nazi–Soviet Pact and war against Poland.

### How far was there a plan? The debate on planning and consistency

Historians have argued for decades over whether Hitler followed a consistent plan or improvised.

- **The "programme" view.** Hugh Trevor-Roper, in *The Mind of Adolf Hitler* (1953) and in later articles, argued that Hitler had a consistent set of aims since *Mein Kampf*, to destroy the Soviet Union and win Lebensraum, and that his steps in the 1930s were stages towards this. Intentionalist historians such as Gerhard Weinberg and Andreas Hillgruber developed this view, and said that the war in the east was the real aim and that the war in the west was a means or a by-product.
- **The "opportunist" view.** A. J. P. Taylor, in *The Origins of the Second World War* (1961), argued that Hitler was less a planner than an opportunist: he wanted what most Germans wanted (a Germany free of Versailles and strong in Europe), and he took chances as the mistakes of other statesmen gave them. Taylor saw the war of 1939 as the result of miscalculation on all sides as much as of a plan.
- **A middle view.** Many historians now argue for consistent aims but flexible methods and timing. Ian Kershaw's idea of "working towards the Führer" (set out in his *Hitler*, 1998 and 2000) is that Hitler's broad aims set a direction and officials and rivals competed to carry them out, so that policy came partly from below. For foreign policy this means that Lebensraum and anti-communism were fixed, while the route changed.

**Evidence for planning and consistency.** The writings from 1925 to 1928 and the Hossbach meeting show consistent aims; the shape of the steps (the Rhineland, then Austria, then Czechoslovakia, then Poland, then the USSR) fits the aims; and the Four Year Plan of 1936, which prepared the economy for war, and rearmament from 1933 suggest preparation. **Evidence for improvisation.** The Anschluss came sooner than expected (Hitler decided in a few days, when Schuschnigg called a plebiscite in Austria). The Sudeten crisis was meant to lead to war and instead led to Munich, which Hitler is reported to have resented. The Nazi–Soviet Pact, signed with the regime he had called the great enemy, showed that tactics could override ideology, and the war with Britain and France in 1939 was not the war he had planned for. The invasion of Poland was meant to be local, and the attack on the USSR was made before Britain was beaten.

A balanced conclusion: Hitler's long-term aims (Lebensraum, anti-communism, a racial empire) were consistent from the 1920s to 1941; his short-term policy was often improvised, shaped by opportunity, by the reactions of Britain and France, by his own impatience and by his officials.

:::key Nazi foreign policy: aims, methods and consistency
1. **Aims:** revise Versailles; unite all Germans; win Lebensraum in the east; defeat communism.
2. **Methods:** diplomacy and legal moves (League exit, Saar, Naval Agreement), surprise (Rhineland, Anschluss), pressure (Sudetenland, Prague), tactical alliance (Nazi–Soviet Pact), war (Poland, then the USSR).
3. **Consistency:** consistent in the long term, flexible in method and timing; the 1939 war with Britain and France was not planned.
4. **The debate:** Trevor-Roper (programme), Taylor (opportunism), Kershaw (working towards the Führer: consistent aims and improvised means).
:::

## How effectively did the Nazis govern? Weighing it up

| Measure | The case that the regime was effective | The case against |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Opposition parties crushed in 1933; Gestapo, camps and courts deterred most people; little organised resistance | The Gestapo was small and relied on informers; the regime did not reach into every area; opposition and non-conformity persisted |
| Consent | Propaganda made Hitler popular; radio and film reached nearly all; rallies and the Olympics built pride; plebiscites showed large majorities | The plebiscites were not free; people grumbled about shortages and Party officials; Catholics and workers were harder to win |
| Dealing with opposition | Youth groups, churches and the left were contained | Contained, not eliminated: churches protested and the White Rose and the Edelweiss Pirates existed |
| Foreign policy | Versailles overturned, Austria and the Sudetenland gained without war, rearmament | Led to war with Britain and France in 1939 that Hitler had not planned for; the attack on the USSR started a war that could not be won |

A strong answer sorts the evidence by **what the regime was trying to do**. As a system of control it was very effective, because it destroyed organised opposition in months and kept it destroyed. As a system for winning consent it was effective when its message matched people's beliefs and weaker when it did not. In foreign policy it was effective in the short term, but the long-term aim of Lebensraum led to a war of conquest that Germany lost. So "effective" depends on the measure.

:::note The historians' debate on terror and consent (stretch: useful for A*)
- **Robert Gellately**, *The Gestapo and German Society* (1990) and *Backing Hitler: Consent and Coercion in Nazi Germany* (2001), argued that most Germans knew about the terror and many supported or helped it, so that coercion and consent worked together.
- **Eric Johnson**, *Nazi Terror* (1999), argued that the terror was selective and that the Gestapo was a small force that could not have controlled the population by fear alone.
- **Ian Kershaw**, *The 'Hitler Myth'* (1987), stressed Hitler's popularity as a source of consent, separate from the Party's.
- **Detlev Peukert**, *Inside Nazi Germany* (1982), stressed that everyday life included conformity and non-conformity, so that a simple picture of either terror or consent is misleading.
:::

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 3.2
1. **Define "effectively" first.** For "Assess how effectively the Nazis governed", criteria could be *control* (could they stop opposition?), *consent* (did they win support?) and *results* (did policy achieve its aims?).
2. **Split a "terror or consent" question.** Give terror its evidence (Gestapo, camps, courts) and consent its evidence (propaganda, Hitler's popularity), then argue how they worked together.
3. **Use precise evidence**: 22 March 1933 (Dachau), 24 April 1934 (People's Court), 13 March 1933 (Propaganda Ministry), 76 marks (the People's Receiver), 19 July 1937 (Degenerate Art), 1–16 August 1936, 6,500 Gestapo officers in 1937, 18 August 1941 (Swing), 7 March 1936, 12 March 1938, 23 August 1939.
4. **Do not use a prepared answer.** Churches, foreign policy and the Gestapo recur; adapt the same facts to the exact words of the question.
5. **For foreign policy questions**, separate the *aims* from the *methods* and say whether "consistency" means in aims, in methods or in results.
6. **Judge.** End with a view that says which measure matters most and what would change the verdict.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, and the total is out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'The Nazi regime relied on terror rather than popular support.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement partly substantiated; a good range of mostly precise points with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criterion: could the regime have survived and functioned if either terror or support had been taken away? (the "remove one" test)
- For terror: the Fire Decree and arrests of 1933; Dachau from 22 March 1933 and six camps by 1939; the Gestapo's powers (1936 law) and informers; Special Courts and the People's Court (about 5,200 death sentences); the destruction of the KPD and SPD; the fate of Swing Youth, Edelweiss Pirates and the White Rose.
- For support: Hitler's popularity (Kershaw); propaganda and the People's Receiver; the rallies and the Olympics; foreign successes (Saar, Rhineland, Anschluss); plebiscite results (though not free); Johnson's view that terror was selective.
- Judgement: terror was the basis in 1933–34 and for the minorities and opponents, support was the basis for the majority; they worked together, but without support the regime would have needed more terror than a Gestapo of 6,500 could supply.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Nazi regime relied on terror. The Gestapo arrested anyone who opposed Hitler and sent them to concentration camps like Dachau. People were afraid to speak against the Nazis in case they were reported, so they did what they were told. There were also courts that sentenced people to death. So the Nazis ruled mostly by terror and not by support."

*Commentary.* Relevant and broadly accurate, but general ("anyone who opposed", "afraid") and one-sided: it does not consider support at all, though the question asks for a comparison. There are no dates, numbers or mechanisms. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 2 (about 5). To move up: give precise evidence for terror, set out the case for support, and compare them.

**A Level 4 paragraph (both sides, but no weighing).** "The regime used terror on a large scale. The Gestapo had power to arrest without trial after the law of 1936, Dachau opened on 22 March 1933 and there were six camps by 1939, and the People's Court, set up in April 1934, passed about 5,200 death sentences. However, the regime also had popular support. Goebbels's ministry controlled the press, radio and film, and from August 1933 the People's Receiver, at 76 marks, put a radio in most homes. Hitler was popular, as Kershaw argued, partly because of foreign successes like the Rhineland in March 1936. So the regime used both terror and support."

*Commentary.* Precise and balanced in coverage, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). But the last sentence only repeats that both existed. It never says which mattered more, to whom, or how they worked together. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs a **criterion, a comparison and a judgement**.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The view can be tested by asking whether the regime could have functioned if either terror or support had been removed. On that test terror was essential to the creation of the regime and to its control of minorities and opponents, but it was not enough by itself to run a country, so the view is partly right but wrongly sets terror against support, because the two worked together.

The case for terror is strong in the years of consolidation. The Reichstag Fire Decree of 28 February 1933 suspended civil rights and made arrest without trial possible; thousands of Communists and Social Democrats were seized, and Dachau opened on 22 March 1933. The Gestapo was put beyond the review of the courts in 1936, the Special Courts (1933) and the People's Court (1934, about 5,200 death sentences by 1945) dealt with political cases, and by 1939 six main camps were in operation. For those targeted, the Communists, Jews, Jehovah's Witnesses and others, terror was total. The trade unions, the SPD and the KPD were destroyed in months, and the regime answered even small acts of non-conformity, such as those of the Swing Youth in Hamburg, with arrests, and killed the White Rose students in February 1943.

But terror alone cannot explain how the regime governed 65 million people. The Gestapo had only about 6,500 officers in 1937 and depended on informers: Gellately found that many Gestapo cases began with a denunciation, which implies that many Germans were willing to help the regime. Johnson argued that the terror was selective and that most Germans had little to fear. The regime built consent as well: the Propaganda Ministry controlled the press, radio and film; the People's Receiver of 1933 and loudspeakers in public spaces reached nearly every household; the rallies and the 1936 Olympics gave Germans a sense of pride and unity. Above all, Hitler's own popularity grew, as Kershaw's study of the 'Hitler myth' shows, because he was seen to have ended mass unemployment and overturned the Treaty of Versailles in the Saar, the Rhineland and Austria. Plebiscites in which 90 to 99 per cent said yes were not free, but even allowing for pressure the regime had real backing in the late 1930s.

The two cannot be separated. Terror made support easier to produce, since opposition parties and papers had been shut, critics were afraid and the only voice was the regime's; and the visible success of propaganda and foreign policy made the terror seem acceptable to those who were not its targets, who saw it as the price of order. Opposition shows this: the Edelweiss Pirates, Swing Youth and the churches existed, but they were limited, because those who protested were afraid and few were supported by their neighbours.

Overall, the regime did not rely on terror rather than support: it relied on terror for the minorities and the opponents, and on support (or acceptance) for the majority, and each made the other effective. If terror had been removed, opposition parties and the unions would have survived and the regime might have fallen in 1933–34; if support had been removed, 6,500 Gestapo officers could not have kept control of the country. The better view is that terror created the conditions in which support could be built, so that it came first, but support sustained the regime."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** (the "remove one" test) and uses it in every paragraph. It gives each side precise evidence (28 February 1933, 22 March 1933, 1936, 6,500 officers, 5,200 sentences, the People's Receiver, Kershaw, Johnson, Gellately) (Table B, Level 5: about 14). It avoids the trap of treating terror and consent as exclusive, shows how they connect and reaches a judgement that depends on the groups concerned (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30.
:::

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the consistency of Hitler's foreign policy aims, 1933–41.
**What the levels want.** This is an "Assess" question about *consistency*, a word that needs defining. Level 5 asks for criteria (consistent in aims, in methods, in results), a balanced use of the evidence for planning and improvisation, and a judgement.

**Plan.**
- Criterion: were the aims the same throughout (consistency of aim), were the methods the same (consistency of method), and did events follow the plan (consistency of result)?
- For consistency of aims: Mein Kampf (1925–26) and the Second Book; the Hossbach meeting (5 November 1937); Lebensraum and anti-communism; the steps from Rhineland to Austria to Czechoslovakia to Poland to the USSR; Barbarossa on 22 June 1941.
- Against: the methods varied (League exit, Naval Agreement, Rhineland surprise, Munich, the Nazi–Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939); the Anschluss was improvised; the 1939 war with Britain and France was unplanned; the Pact contradicted anti-communism; Taylor's opportunism.
- Judgement: consistent in long-term aims, inconsistent in methods, timing and results.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"To assess consistency one must separate aims, methods and results. On that division Hitler's foreign policy was consistent in its long-term aims, much less consistent in its methods, and largely inconsistent in its results, so that the claim of a fixed plan is wrong but the claim of pure opportunism is also wrong.

The evidence for consistent aims is strong. From *Mein Kampf* in 1925–26 and the 'Second Book' of 1928, Hitler named the same objectives: the end of the Treaty of Versailles, the union of Germans in one state, living space in the east and the destruction of Bolshevism. When he told his commanders on 5 November 1937, as recorded in the Hossbach memorandum, that the problem of space had to be solved by 1943–45 at the latest, starting with Austria and Czechoslovakia, he was saying the same thing in more concrete terms. The sequence of steps also fits: the Rhineland (7 March 1936), the Anschluss (12 March 1938), the Sudetenland (October 1938), Prague (15 March 1939), Poland (1 September 1939) and finally the invasion of the Soviet Union (22 June 1941), the war for Lebensraum which he had described since the 1920s. Rearmament from 1933 and the Four Year Plan of 1936 prepared the economy for it. Historians such as Trevor-Roper and Weinberg stress this continuity.

But the methods were not consistent, and here the opportunist reading of A. J. P. Taylor has force. The policy changed with circumstances. In 1933–35 Hitler spoke of peace, signed a pact with Poland (26 January 1934) and made a naval agreement with Britain (18 June 1935) while rearming in secret. In 1936 he took a risk in the Rhineland, and in March 1938 he moved on Austria sooner than he had intended, when Schuschnigg announced a plebiscite. The most striking inconsistency was the Nazi–Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939, an agreement with the state he had called the main enemy, made to remove the risk of a war on two fronts so that he could attack Poland. It shows that Hitler would put tactics before ideology, and it delayed the war against the USSR by almost two years.

The results were also not as planned. Hitler had expected that Britain and France would not fight over Poland, as they had not fought over Czechoslovakia, and the war of 3 September 1939 was one he had not wanted at that stage. Munich in September 1938 gave him the Sudetenland but denied him the war against Czechoslovakia he had wanted. By 1941 Germany was fighting Britain in the west and attacking the USSR in the east, the war on two fronts that Mein Kampf had warned against.

Overall, the best view is that Hitler's foreign policy was consistent in its ends and flexible in its means, and so a mixture of intention and improvisation. The aims of Lebensraum and anti-communism did not change between 1925 and 1941, and Barbarossa carried them out. But the route was improvised, and the war that came in 1939 was not the war he planned. For that reason Kershaw's idea that Hitler set a direction, and that events and others' choices shaped the path, is more convincing than either a blueprint or a series of accidents."

*Commentary.* The essay opens by **defining consistency** (aims, methods, results), which turns the question into three clear tests. It uses precise evidence for each (Hossbach, 5 November 1937; the dates of the steps; the Pact of 23 August 1939; Schuschnigg's plebiscite; 3 September 1939) (Table B, Level 5: about 14). It uses the historians as part of the argument and does not just name them, and it reaches a judgement that matches the question's wording (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Evaluate the view that the Christian churches did more to accommodate than to resist the Nazi regime." [30]

A student wrote:

"The churches did not oppose the Nazis very much. The Catholic Church signed a Concordat with Hitler in 1933. Some people like Niemöller and Bonhoeffer did oppose, but most pastors did nothing. Galen was a bishop who spoke out about euthanasia. So the churches mostly accommodated the Nazis and did not resist."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are broadly right, but the paragraph **asserts** and does not argue. It names people and the Concordat without saying what each shows, it gives no dates or numbers, and it treats "the churches" as one body when the Catholic and Protestant churches behaved differently. "Mostly accommodated" is a conclusion that has not been tested: nothing is said about what *resist* means or about what the churches did when their own interests were attacked.

**The fix.** Define the terms, give the evidence for both sides and separate the churches. "The churches accommodated the regime on most matters but resisted when their own beliefs and organisations were attacked. The Catholic Church signed a Concordat on 20 July 1933 that gave the regime prestige in exchange for protection of its schools and organisations, and the Protestant church was split between the pro-Nazi German Christians and the Confessing Church, which formed in May 1934 at Barmen. Most Protestant pastors did not join it. Yet the churches also resisted when the regime threatened them: Pius XI's encyclical *Mit brennender Sorge* of March 1937 protested at the breach of the Concordat, about 700 pastors were arrested in 1935, Niemöller was held from 1937 to 1945, and Bishop Galen's sermons of July and August 1941 condemned the murder of the sick, after which the T4 gassing was halted on 24 August 1941. But neither church protested publicly at the persecution of the Jews as a whole, and in 1945 the Protestant church confessed its failure. The balance therefore favours accommodation, with resistance limited to the defence of the churches' own interests." Now each fact proves a point, the churches are separated, and there is a limit and a judgement.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was the Gestapo, and how did it work with so few officers?
A: The Secret State Police, created in Prussia in 1933 and extended under Himmler and Heydrich in 1934–36, able to arrest without trial and not subject to the courts after the 1936 law. It had about 6,500 officers in 1937, so it relied on denunciations from the public and on the Party's block wardens.
Q: What did a block warden do?
A: He was a Party volunteer responsible for about 40 to 60 households, who collected donations, kept a record of each household, promoted the Party message and reported hostile remarks or behaviour to the Party and Gestapo.
Q: What were the SD and the RSHA?
A: The SD was the intelligence service of the Party and SS, founded by Himmler in 1931 with Heydrich as head. On 27 September 1939 the Security Police (Gestapo and Kripo) and the SD were joined in the Reich Security Main Office under Heydrich.
Q: What were the People's Court and the Special Courts?
A: Special Courts (from March 1933) tried political offences quickly; the People's Court (24 April 1934) tried treason in Berlin, and passed about 5,200 death sentences by 1945, most of them from 1942 under Freisler. The judges were expected to serve the regime.
Q: How did the concentration camp system develop between 1933 and 1939?
A: Improvised camps run by the SA and SS in 1933 held tens of thousands of political opponents; Dachau (22 March 1933) became the model under Eicke; Sachsenhausen (1936), Buchenwald (1937), Flossenbürg (1938), Mauthausen (1938) and Ravensbrück (1939) followed, and new groups such as Jehovah's Witnesses and "asocials" were held; about 30,000 Jewish men were arrested after 9–10 November 1938.
Q: What did Goebbels's Ministry control and how?
A: Set up on 13 March 1933, it controlled the press, radio, film, theatre, literature, music and art through the Reich Chamber of Culture (22 September 1933), the Editors' Law (4 October 1933) and daily instructions to editors.
Q: What was the People's Receiver and why did it matter?
A: A cheap radio (VE301) launched on 18 August 1933 at 76 marks; a cheaper set at 35 marks followed in 1938. It put Hitler's voice in homes, and by 1941 about two-thirds of households had a radio.
Q: What were the Degenerate Art exhibition and the Great German Art Exhibition?
A: Two exhibitions in Munich in July 1937. The Great German Art Exhibition (opened 18 July) showed approved art; the Degenerate Art exhibition (19 July) showed 650 works by 112 modern artists to be mocked, and about two million people saw it.
Q: What were the Edelweiss Pirates and the Swing Youth?
A: Non-conformist youth groups. The Swing Youth (Hamburg and Berlin) loved banned jazz and mocked Nazi rituals, and more than 300 were arrested in Hamburg on 18 August 1941. The Edelweiss Pirates (western cities) rejected the Hitler Youth, and thirteen in Cologne-Ehrenfeld were hanged in November 1944.
Q: What was the Confessing Church?
A: The Protestant group that formed at Barmen (29–31 May 1934) against the pro-Nazi German Christians, after Niemöller's Pastors' Emergency League of September 1933. It rejected state control of the church; Niemöller was arrested on 1 July 1937; Bonhoeffer was hanged in April 1945.
Q: What were the four aims of Nazi foreign policy?
A: Reversal of the Treaty of Versailles; pan-Germanism (all Germans in one Reich); Lebensraum in the east; and the defeat of communism.
Q: Why did Hitler sign the Nazi–Soviet Pact?
A: On 23 August 1939 it removed the risk of a two-front war before the invasion of Poland, and its secret protocol divided eastern Europe with Stalin. It was a tactical move that contradicted his anti-communism and was broken on 22 June 1941.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★ Assess the importance of the Gestapo in the Nazi terror state.
A: Indicative content: created in Prussia in 1933 and extended by Himmler and Heydrich in 1934; the 1936 law removed it from court review; arrest without trial and "protective custody"; informers and denunciations (Gellately); about 6,500 officers in 1937; the link with the SD and the RSHA of 1939. Challenge: the Gestapo was small and relied on the public; the Party's block wardens, the SS, the camps and the courts were also part of the system; Johnson argued that terror was selective. Level 5 sets criteria (reach, power, deterrence) and judges, for example, that the Gestapo's power was in the law and the informers more than in its numbers.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Propaganda was the main reason why Hitler's regime was able to control Germany.' Evaluate this view.
A: Support: Goebbels's ministry from 13 March 1933; control of the press (Editors' Law), radio (People's Receiver), film (*Triumph of the Will*) and culture (Chamber of Culture, book burnings, Degenerate Art); rallies and the 1936 Olympics; the Hitler myth. Challenge: terror (the Gestapo, camps, courts); Hitler's real successes (jobs, Versailles overturned); propaganda worked best when it matched existing beliefs; Catholics and the old left stayed critical. Level 5 judges, for example, that propaganda made Hitler popular but did not control Germany by itself, since it worked within a climate of fear and success.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the success of Nazi policy towards the arts and culture in the years 1933 to 1939.
A: Indicative content: the Reich Chamber of Culture (September 1933), book burnings (10 May 1933), the Great German Art and Degenerate Art exhibitions (July 1937), censorship of music, the exodus of writers and artists, the entertainment film. Challenge: most people liked entertainment, not heavy propaganda; the degenerate show drew more visitors than the approved one; many artists went into exile; the regime failed to produce great art of its own. Level 5 sets criteria (control, popularity, quality) and judges that control was effective, popularity mixed and creative achievement poor.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the Nuremberg Rallies and the 1936 Olympics were the most effective Nazi propaganda.
A: Support: the rallies gave mass emotion and a Hitler cult (1933–38) and were filmed in *Triumph of the Will*; the Olympics won foreign admiration, led to a relaxation of anti-Jewish signs and presented a peaceful face. Challenge: radio, press and film reached everyone daily; the rallies reached members; Owens's victories embarrassed the regime; foreign visitors saw only what was staged; the persecution of Jews continued. Level 5 defines "effective" (reach, depth, persistence) and judges, for example, that the spectacles were most useful for the image of unity, but the media shaped ordinary opinion more.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent of opposition to the Nazi regime from young people.
A: Indicative content: the Swing Youth (Hamburg and Berlin, arrests of 18 August 1941); the Edelweiss Pirates (Cologne-Ehrenfeld hangings, November 1944); the White Rose (Scholls executed 22 February 1943); the Hitler Youth law of 1 December 1936. Challenge: the groups were small and mostly non-political; most young people joined the Hitler Youth and many believed in the regime; the groups were contained or killed. Level 5 sets criteria (scale, aims, effect) and judges that youth non-conformity was real but did not threaten the regime.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Christian churches did more to accommodate than to resist Nazi rule.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate Catholics from Protestants, and name the issues on which each gave way and on which each protested.
H: Ask what "resist" means: defending the church, or defending others?
A: Support: the Concordat of 20 July 1933; the German Christians and Reich Bishop Müller; most pastors did not join the Confessing Church; no public protest against the persecution of Jews as a whole; support for rearmament and the war; the Stuttgart Declaration of 1945. Challenge: *Mit brennender Sorge* (14 March 1937); the Pastors' Emergency League (about 6,000 pastors) and Barmen (May 1934); Niemöller (arrested 1 July 1937), Bonhoeffer (hanged April 1945); Galen's sermons of July and August 1941 and the halt of the T4 gassing. Level 5 defines resistance and judges, for example, that the churches accommodated generally but resisted when their own faith and institutions were attacked, and that the balance favoured accommodation.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why opposition to the Nazi regime from the left was ineffective.
A: Indicative content: arrests of KPD and SPD leaders in 1933; the destruction of the unions on 2 May 1933; the Gestapo's use of informers; exile (Sopade in Prague); the KPD's underground cells repeatedly broken; the Red Orchestra found in 1942; the split between KPD and SPD; Hitler's popularity. Challenge: some bravery and survival of networks; the regime's successes cut off support; lack of allies and a common aim. Level 5 sets criteria (repression, divisions, popularity) and judges that repression was the main cause, because it destroyed the organisations in 1933–34.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Nazi foreign policy between 1933 and 1939 was a series of improvised responses to opportunities.
H: Pick three steps (the Rhineland, the Anschluss, Munich) and ask what was planned.
H: Then use the Hossbach meeting, Mein Kampf and the Nazi–Soviet Pact.
A: Support: Taylor's argument; the Rhineland move was a gamble with orders to withdraw if France acted; the Anschluss came when Schuschnigg called a plebiscite; the Sudeten crisis and Munich; the Pact of 23 August 1939 reversed anti-communism. Challenge: Mein Kampf and the Second Book; Hossbach (space by 1943–45); rearmament and the Four Year Plan; Prague (15 March 1939) went beyond German-speaking areas; Poland was a planned war. Level 5 defines "improvised" and judges, for example, that the means were improvised and the ends were fixed, so the view fits the methods but not the aims.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Nazi regime governed Germany effectively only because it was not challenged by an organised opposition.' Evaluate this view.
H: Why was there no organised opposition? Think of 1933 and of the Gestapo, but also of Hitler's successes and the army oath.
H: Then ask what "governed effectively" means: order, control, consent, results.
A: Support: the left, the unions and parties were crushed by July 1933; the army swore the oath in August 1934; the churches defended their own interests; the youth groups were small; the 1938 military plot lapsed; Elser acted alone; passive resistance was unorganised. Challenge: effectiveness was also due to positive causes (unemployment fell, Versailles overturned, Hitler's popularity, propaganda); terror made opposition costly; the regime's confusion (rival agencies, see the debate on the "polycracy") reduced effectiveness in some areas; the war of 1939–41 shows that foreign policy effectiveness ended in a war it could not win. A* judgement: the absence of organised opposition was a necessary condition, created by terror in 1933–34, but the regime's effectiveness in winning consent depended on successes that were real and visible at the time; the two together explain the regime's grip until the war turned.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Hitler's foreign policy was a plan for conquest in the east from the beginning.
H: Use the programme and opportunist views, and add the middle view.
H: What does the Pact of 23 August 1939 show, and what does Barbarossa show?
A: Support: Mein Kampf (1925–26), the Second Book (1928) and the Hossbach meeting (5 November 1937) name Lebensraum; the sequence Rhineland, Austria, Czechoslovakia, Poland, USSR; Barbarossa on 22 June 1941; Weinberg and Trevor-Roper. Challenge: the methods were flexible; the early steps were revisions of Versailles that most Germans wanted; the Anschluss was a sudden move; the 1939 war with Britain and France was not planned; the Pact; Taylor's opportunism; Kershaw's "working towards the Führer". A* judgement: the end (an empire in the east) was fixed from the 1920s, but the *route* was improvised, so "a plan from the beginning" is right about the destination and wrong about the timetable and the means.
:::
