:::why
In 1929 most of the great powers were trying to settle their quarrels by talking. Ten years later Europe and Asia were at war or on the edge of it. The change did not come from nowhere: the slump that began in 1929 broke governments, ended the hope of agreed disarmament and sorted the powers into new camps. This chapter explains that first step in the road to the Second World War. It also teaches the skill that this topic tests most: judging how far one cause, the economic crisis, explains a whole set of political events in different countries.
:::

## The Great Depression and the rise of extremism

### How the slump reached the world

On 24 October 1929 share prices on the New York stock exchange began to collapse, and by 29 October the Wall Street Crash had wiped out billions of dollars. The crash did not by itself cause the Great Depression, but it started a chain reaction. American banks failed, American buyers stopped buying, and American lenders called in the loans they had made abroad. In 1930 the USA made things worse for everyone with the Smoot–Hawley Tariff (17 June 1930), which put high duties on thousands of imported goods; other countries copied it, and world trade shrank. In 1931 the banking crisis spread through central Europe: the Austrian bank Credit-Anstalt collapsed in May and German banks followed in July. In September 1931 Britain left the gold standard.

:::def Great Depression
The worldwide economic slump that began with the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and lasted in most countries until the mid-1930s: falling output and prices, collapsing trade, bank failures and mass unemployment.
:::

This mattered for international relations for three reasons. First, the democracies were busy with their own unemployed and had less money, attention and will for foreign policy. Second, countries that depended on exports or on foreign loans (Germany and Japan above all) were hit harder than the USA or Britain. Third, ordinary people lost faith in the governments and the system that had failed them, and listened to leaders who promised a quick, radical cure and someone to blame, often a foreign enemy.

:::def Extremism
In this topic, political movements and governments that rejected parliamentary democracy and the peaceful settlement of disputes, and that used violence, dictatorship and aggressive nationalism: Nazism in Germany, militarism in Japan, and (by the mid-1930s) a more aggressive fascism in Italy.
:::

:::def Militarism
A political system or attitude in which the armed forces dominate government and society, and in which war is seen as a normal and glorious way of achieving national aims.
:::

### Germany: the collapse of democracy and the rise of Nazism

Germany was the country most exposed. Since the Dawes Plan of 1924 it had paid reparations with money borrowed from the USA (see the 1919–29 topic). When the loans stopped, the economy fell. The index of German industrial production (1928 = 100) fell from 102 in 1929 to 58 in 1932, a drop of more than 40 per cent. Registered unemployment passed one million in 1929 and reached about six million in 1932 and again in early 1933, roughly three workers in ten; the true figure was higher because many jobless were not counted.

The democratic parties could not agree on a remedy. The coalition government of the Social Democrat Hermann Müller broke up in March 1930 over unemployment insurance. The next chancellor, Heinrich Brüning of the Catholic Centre Party, governed with the President's emergency powers (Article 48 of the constitution) and cut spending and wages to balance the budget, which deepened the slump. Brüning's government did not trust the parliament it could not control, and the parliament, with the Nazis and Communists both growing, could not produce a majority.

The Nazi Party, which won only 2.6 per cent of the vote in May 1928, offered what many wanted: "work and bread", the end of reparations and of "the shame of Versailles", a scapegoat (the Jews, the Communists, the "November criminals" who had signed the Armistice) and a strong leader. Its vote rose as unemployment rose.

The chart shows how closely the Nazi vote followed the slump: from 2.6 per cent in May 1928 to 18.3 per cent in September 1930 (the Nazis became the second largest party in the Reichstag), 37.3 per cent in July 1932 (the largest, with 230 of the 608 seats) and 33.1 per cent in November 1932, when the vote slipped a little as the economy began to recover and the Nazi vote lost its momentum. On 30 January 1933 President Hindenburg, advised by conservative politicians who thought they could control him, appointed Hitler Chancellor. By then the Nazis had never won a majority of the votes cast in a free election; in the election of 5 March 1933, held a week after the Reichstag fire (the burning of the parliament building on 27 February 1933, blamed on the Communists) and with the police under Nazi control, they won 43.9 per cent. Within months Germany was a one-party dictatorship.

Notice what this meant for international relations, which is the focus of this key question. The democratic Weimar governments of Gustav Stresemann had tried to revise the Treaty of Versailles by negotiation: the Dawes and Young Plans, Locarno, entry to the League. The Nazi government came to power on a promise to throw off Versailles, and it did not feel bound by the Republic's habit of negotiating. Hitler's aims in foreign policy went beyond revision, as later chapters explain. In 1933 he was cautious: Germany was weak, and he needed time. According to the notes of one general, on 3 February 1933 he told army and navy chiefs that the armed forces must be rebuilt, but his public message was peace.

:::key Why democracy collapsed in Germany, and what that did to Europe
1. **The slump** destroyed jobs and savings and the credibility of democratic parties.
2. **Weak government**: the parties could not form a majority, Brüning ruled by decree, and the political elite chose Hitler in January 1933 as a way out.
3. **Nazi appeal**: simple answers, a scapegoat, a rejection of Versailles.
4. **International effect**: a government that wanted to overturn the post-war settlement replaced one that wanted to revise it by agreement. Every later arms conference or treaty had to be judged against that.
:::

:::warn The Depression was necessary, not sufficient
Do not write that "the Depression caused Hitler". Hitler and the Nazis existed before 1929 and won very few votes. Other countries hit hard by the slump (Britain, the USA, France) kept their democracies. The Depression explains why so many Germans abandoned the Republic; weak democratic institutions, the legacy of the defeat of 1918, the Nazis' propaganda and the political deals of 1932–33 explain why they turned to Hitler. A top-level answer says "the Depression was a necessary condition, but it worked together with …".
:::

### Japan: the collapse of democratic government and the rise of militarism

Japan had a limited party democracy in the 1920s, with prime ministers who came from the majority party in the Diet. It was a crowded country (about 64 million people in 1930) with few raw materials, which depended on foreign trade. The slump hit it in two ways. Exports collapsed: the USA, the buyer of most Japanese raw silk, stopped buying, and silk prices roughly halved between 1929 and 1931. Government policy made it worse: in January 1930 the Hamaguchi government returned to the gold standard, just before the slump deepened, which brought deflation and unemployment. Farmers, many of whom depended on silk, were ruined in the poorer north-east.

The parties were blamed for corruption and for the hardship. The army and navy, the nationalists and secret societies of young officers told the public the parties were selling out the nation, at home by serving the rich and abroad by obeying the Western powers. The London Naval Treaty of 1930, which accepted a lower ratio for the Japanese navy than the officers wanted, was a target. On 14 November 1930 a young right-wing fanatic shot Prime Minister Hamaguchi at Tokyo station; he never recovered and died in August 1931.

The army had its own plan. Japan held a lease on the South Manchurian Railway and a large share of the investment in Manchuria, the Chinese region beyond the Great Wall that the army regarded as Japan's "lifeline", a source of coal, iron and soya and a land for settlers. In September 1931 officers of the Kwantung Army, Japan's force in the region, staged the Mukden incident without orders from Tokyo and conquered Manchuria ([International history, 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2) follows the League's response). The government could not control them, and the public cheered the conquest. On 15 May 1932 a group of young naval officers murdered Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi, who had tried to resist the army. After that no leader of a party held the premiership until after 1945; "national unity" governments led by admirals and generals ruled in the 1930s. On 26 February 1936 young army officers killed several ministers in a rebellion in Tokyo. It was put down within days, but the generals used the fright to take a larger share in government, and in May 1936 the rule that only serving officers could be army and navy ministers was restored, giving the services the power to bring down any cabinet.

:::key The rise of militarism in Japan
1. **Economic crisis**: silk exports, deflation and rural poverty discredited the party governments.
2. **Resentment of the West**: the naval ratios of 1922 and 1930 and the tariff walls of 1930 made Japanese officers think the Western powers were holding Japan back.
3. **Violence**: assassinations (1930, 1932, 1936) frightened politicians into giving way.
4. **The army abroad**: success in Manchuria made the army popular and independent of the government.
5. **International effect**: a Japanese government that obeyed its army would break treaties (the Nine-Power Treaty and the Pact of Paris, the Kellogg–Briand Pact) and the League's rules whenever the army wished.
:::

### Italy: pressure on Mussolini for a change of policy

Mussolini had ruled Italy since 1922. The Depression hit Italy more slowly than Germany but it hit: industrial production fell, wages were cut several times between 1930 and 1934, and official unemployment passed one million in 1933 (the true number was probably higher). The government rescued the banks by creating the Institute for Industrial Reconstruction (IRI) in January 1933, which gave the state a large share of Italian industry. But Mussolini's regime had promised greatness, not hardship, and the slump showed up the gap between his propaganda and the reality.

Many historians argue that this was one pressure that pushed Mussolini towards a more aggressive foreign policy in the 1930s: a successful colonial war would distract Italians from poverty, make the regime popular again, and secure land and raw materials. There were other pressures too. He wanted to avenge the defeat at Adwa in 1896, and he had long dreamed of making the Mediterranean an Italian sea. That is why the policy changed in two stages:
- Up to 1935 he acted as a defender of the status quo in Europe. In July 1934, when Austrian Nazis murdered the Austrian Chancellor Dollfuss, Mussolini sent four divisions to the Brenner frontier to warn Hitler against seizing Austria. In 1935 he joined Britain and France at Stresa.
- From 1935 he turned to adventure: the invasion of Abyssinia in October 1935 ([International history, 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2)) broke with Britain and France, who imposed sanctions, and pushed him towards the German dictator he had so recently distrusted.

:::key Five events of 1935–36 used in this chapter (the next chapter tells them in full)
1. **Abyssinia.** Abyssinia is the old name for Ethiopia, an independent African state and a League member. Italy invaded it on 3 October 1935 and had conquered it by May 1936.
2. **Sanctions.** The League voted sanctions against Italy in November 1935 (a ban on arms, loans and some imports). They were weak: oil, the fuel Italy's army most needed, was left off the list, and Britain, which controlled the Suez Canal, kept it open to Italian ships.
3. **The Hoare–Laval plan.** In December 1935 the British foreign secretary, Samuel Hoare, and the French leader Pierre Laval secretly agreed to offer Italy large parts of Abyssinia. The plan leaked, the British public was outraged (the Peace Ballot of 1934–35, a private poll in which about 11.5 million Britons answered, had shown strong support for the League and for sanctions), and Hoare resigned. It showed that the League's leaders did not mean the sanctions to work.
4. **The Rhineland.** On 7 March 1936 Hitler sent German troops into the Rhineland, the strip of western Germany that Versailles and Locarno had made a demilitarised buffer; Britain and France protested but did nothing.
5. **The Spanish Civil War.** In July 1936 army officers led by General Francisco Franco rebelled against Spain's elected government. Germany and Italy helped Franco; Britain and France stayed out.
:::

For this key question, the point is the change of intention: the slump added to an ambition Mussolini already had, and the result was a regime that wanted to alter the map by force at the expense of the League.

### The USA: isolationist pressures

The USA was the strongest economy in the world and had not joined the League. In the 1920s it had played a part in European affairs through the Dawes and Young Plans, the Washington Naval Treaty and the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928). The slump turned Americans inwards. Congress cared about American jobs: the Smoot–Hawley Tariff of 1930 raised barriers against the world. Presidents Hoover and Roosevelt gave priority to the American economy; Roosevelt wrecked the London Economic Conference of 1933 by refusing to tie the dollar to other currencies. Most Americans, who had been disappointed by the First World War, felt that they had been drawn into a European quarrel in 1917 for the sake of bankers and arms makers.

:::def Isolationism
The policy, strong in the USA in the 1930s, of staying out of other countries' political and military quarrels, especially in Europe, and of refusing entangling alliances and League commitments.
:::

Isolationism took legal form in the **Neutrality Acts**. The Nye Committee of the Senate (1934–36) investigated the arms industry and suggested that the "merchants of death" had helped drag the USA into the First World War. The first Neutrality Act (31 August 1935) forbade the export of arms to all countries at war; the second (29 February 1936) banned loans to them; the third (1 May 1937) extended the rules to civil wars (it applied to Spain) and let belligerents buy other goods only on a "cash and carry" basis. The Johnson Act of 1934 banned loans to governments that had defaulted on their First World War debts. In January 1935 the Senate refused to approve membership of the Permanent Court of International Justice (the World Court) by 52 votes to 36, short of the two-thirds needed.

Washington did not entirely ignore the world. In January 1932 Secretary of State Stimson announced that the USA would not recognise any situation brought about by means that broke the Pact of Paris (the Kellogg–Briand Pact; the Stimson Doctrine, [International history, 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2)). In November 1933 Roosevelt recognised the USSR. On 5 October 1937 in Chicago he proposed, in his "quarantine" speech, that peace-loving nations should act together against "an epidemic of world lawlessness", but the isolationist press and Congress reacted so fiercely that he pulled back. The effect for international relations was large: the one power that could have supported the League financially and economically stayed out, so Britain and France (see [International history, 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2)) could count on no American help, and the dictators knew it.

:::key How the Depression changed intentions and behaviour
| Country | What the slump did | Effect on international relations |
|---|---|---|
| Germany | Destroyed the Republic; brought Hitler to power | A government set on overturning the 1919 settlement |
| Japan | Discredited party government; strengthened the army | An army that acted without orders from Tokyo, beyond League control |
| Italy | Exposed the regime's weakness; added pressure for success abroad | A shift from guarding the status quo to expansion in Africa |
| USA | Turned attention inwards | Neutrality Acts; no support for collective action |
:::

:::exam What this means for the key question
The Depression did not make war in 1939 inevitable, but it changed the international system in 1929–33. By 1933 three powers that were unhappy with the post-war order (Germany, Japan and Italy) were governed by men who were readier to use force, and the one power that could have balanced them had chosen isolation. The best answers show the **link** from economic crisis to political change to foreign policy, and do not simply describe each country in turn.
:::

## The World Disarmament Conference, 1932–34

### Reasons for the Conference

The Conference that opened at Geneva in February 1932 was the biggest attempt in history to limit armaments by agreement. Delegates from about sixty states, including the USA and the USSR, which were not League members, attended under the chairmanship of the British statesman Arthur Henderson. There were four main reasons for calling it.

1. **A promise already made.** Article 8 of the League Covenant stated that "the maintenance of peace requires the reduction of national armaments to the lowest point consistent with national safety". The Treaty of Versailles had disarmed Germany and said that this was a first step to a general limitation of armaments. Germany, and many others, said that the promise had not been kept.
2. **Years of preparation.** A Preparatory Commission had worked since 1926 and finished a draft convention in December 1930. The Locarno spirit of the late 1920s had made states feel that talk could replace arms races.
3. **Public opinion.** The memory of the First World War, and the fear of bombers, made peace movements strong. Women's and peace organisations delivered petitions with millions of signatures to the Conference.
4. **The Depression.** Governments were short of money. Spending on armaments was a drain they would gladly have cut, if they could be sure their neighbours would cut too.

:::def World Disarmament Conference
The conference organised by the League of Nations that met at Geneva from February 1932, with delegates from about sixty states, to agree general limits on armaments. It ended without an agreement in 1934 after Germany left it in October 1933.
:::

### The effect of international events

The Conference met in the worst possible conditions.
- **Manchuria.** When delegates arrived in February 1932, Japanese forces had been in Manchuria for four months and were fighting at Shanghai. The sight of a great power ignoring the Covenant while the Conference debated disarmament weakened its authority and made states less willing to reduce their own forces.
- **The Depression.** The slump made governments inward-looking; each defended its own interests.
- **Germany's politics.** In September 1932 the German government of Franz von Papen walked out because the other states refused to grant Germany equality. Germany came back only after the **Five-Power Declaration** of 11 December 1932, in which Britain, France and Italy agreed to Germany's demand for "equality of rights in a system which would provide security for all nations".
- **Hitler.** On 30 January 1933 Hitler became Chancellor. A government whose aim was to rearm Germany now sat at a table that was meant to limit armaments.

### The attitudes of the major powers

:::key Positions at the Conference
- **France** wanted **security first**. Its army was the strongest in Europe, but it feared a revived Germany, and it had no guarantee from Britain or the USA beyond Locarno. French plans (the Tardieu plan of February 1932 and the Herriot plan of November 1932) proposed that the League should have a strong international force and that national armies should be reduced to short-service forces; Germany and Britain rejected them. France would not accept that Germany should rearm while its own security was uncertain.
- **Germany** wanted **equality**. Before Hitler, German governments said that the disarmament of 1919 should be matched by others disarming, or that Germany should be free to rearm to the level of its neighbours. Hitler used the same claim, but with a different aim: his government intended to rearm in any case.
- **Britain** wanted agreement and was ready to see Germany treated more equally, but would not take on new commitments in Europe. It also wished to keep the right to use aircraft for policing its empire. Its leaders (Prime Minister Ramsay MacDonald in 1933, Foreign Secretary Sir John Simon) put forward compromise plans.
- **The USA** was not a League member. President Hoover proposed in June 1932 to cut armies and navies by about a third and to abolish tanks, bombers and poison gas. No one accepted it as it stood.
- **Italy** and **the USSR** also took part. The Soviet delegate Maxim Litvinov proposed complete disarmament, and when that was rejected, supported partial schemes.
:::

### How the Conference failed, 1933–34

Three plans failed in turn. In March 1933 MacDonald proposed a convention under which the continental armies would be set at 200,000 men each, so that Germany's would double, in stages, from its 100,000. On 17 May Hitler's "peace speech" to the Reichstag, delivered in an unusually calm tone, accepted this plan in principle, said that Germany was prepared to disband her entire military establishment if other countries did the same, and warned that she would leave if she was treated as unequal. The British and French then insisted on a trial period in which Germany would first prove her good faith, and in the autumn the Allies said that Germany must not rearm for several years. On 14 October 1933 Hitler withdrew Germany from the Conference, on the ground that it was being denied equality, and announced that she would also leave the League. A national plebiscite on 12 November 1933 backed the withdrawal.

The Conference limped on. In January 1934 Britain proposed a convention that would have allowed Germany a short-service army of 300,000. France, now led by Louis Barthou as Foreign Minister, refused: his note of April 1934 said that France would from now on rely on her own means to guarantee her security. The Conference was effectively dead by the summer of 1934. A year later, on 16 March 1935, Hitler announced conscription and an army of 36 divisions, openly tearing up Part V of the Treaty of Versailles. Nothing remained of the system of agreed limits.

:::key Why the World Disarmament Conference failed
1. **France's fear of Germany** meant that it would not disarm, and its demand for security could not be met by Britain or the USA.
2. **Germany's demand for equality** conflicted with France's demand for security. Hitler's government had no wish for a settlement that limited its rearmament.
3. **International events**: Manchuria undermined the League's authority; the Depression made states inward-looking; Hitler's arrival changed the aims of the main negotiating partner.
4. **No means of enforcement**: the Conference could only recommend, and the powers could not agree on definitions (what is an "offensive" weapon?) or on how to verify promises.
5. **Mistrust** between Britain, France and Germany, and the absence of any agreed method for dealing with a state that broke the rules.
:::

:::exam What this means for the key question
The failure of the Conference was the first clear sign that extremism had changed international relations. It showed that a rearming Germany could use the language of peace and equality to tie the democracies in knots, and that Britain and France could not agree between themselves about how to meet the challenge. After 1934 the powers no longer tried to limit armaments by agreement: they began to rearm and to look for allies.
:::

## The changing relationship between the powers

### The London Naval Conference, 1930

The conference met from 21 January to 22 April 1930 in London, with five delegations: Britain, the USA, Japan, France and Italy. It was called to extend the Washington Naval Treaty of 1922, which had fixed the ratio of capital ships (battleships) for the five powers but had not limited smaller warships. On 22 April the three leading naval powers signed a treaty. The details that matter:
- Britain, the USA and Japan limited their cruisers, destroyers and submarines. In heavy cruisers, Japan was allowed about 60 per cent of the American tonnage (108,400 tons against 180,000), and in light cruisers and destroyers about 70 per cent. Submarine tonnage was equal.
- The "battleship holiday" (a ban on building new capital ships) was extended, and each of the three scrapped some battleships.
- France and Italy could not agree between themselves (Italy demanded parity with France) and accepted only some parts of the treaty.

In the 1920s Britain and the USA had been able to settle naval rivalry by agreement. The Conference was, on the surface, the high point of that hope, and it was a partial success: three great naval powers agreed limits. But it also showed the strains. In Japan the navy's leaders said that the government had given way to the USA and Britain, and the "supreme command" of the navy had been overruled; that helped to produce the assassination of Hamaguchi. In December 1934 Japan gave notice that it would end the Washington Treaty, and in January 1936 Japanese delegates walked out of a second London conference. The Second London Naval Treaty of March 1936 was signed only by Britain, the USA and France. After 1936 there were no naval limits at all.

### The Stresa Front, 1935

By the spring of 1935 the Disarmament Conference had collapsed and Hitler had announced conscription (16 March). Britain, France and Italy, the three powers with the most to lose from a revived Germany, met at the Italian resort of Stresa on Lake Maggiore from 11 to 14 April 1935. Britain was represented by the Prime Minister, Ramsay MacDonald; France by the Prime Minister, Pierre-Étienne Flandin, with the Foreign Minister, Pierre Laval; Italy by Mussolini.

:::def Stresa Front
The agreement of April 1935 between Britain, France and Italy to oppose, by all practicable means, any unilateral repudiation of treaties that might endanger the peace of Europe. It reaffirmed the Locarno Treaties and the independence of Austria.
:::

**Purpose.** The three powers agreed to reaffirm the Locarno Treaties (a guarantee of the Franco-German and Belgian-German frontiers, in which Britain and Italy were the guarantors), to oppose any attempt to break the Treaty of Versailles by unilateral action, and to defend the independence of Austria. The League Council later in April condemned Germany's breach of the treaty.

**Weaknesses.** It had real weaknesses from the start:
- It was a statement, not an alliance. There was no promise of military action and no agreed plan for what to do if Hitler moved again; Britain refused to be committed.
- The three powers wanted different things. Italy cared about Austria and the Brenner; France about the Rhine frontier; Britain wanted to avoid being committed to any frontier at all.
- On 18 June 1935 Britain signed the **Anglo-German Naval Agreement** with Germany, allowing Germany a navy of 35 per cent of the British, without telling France or Italy, in breach of the spirit of Stresa. Britain thought it was limiting German rearmament; France and Italy thought it showed that Britain would make separate deals.
- On 3 October 1935 Italy invaded Abyssinia. Britain and France supported League sanctions, and Mussolini concluded that his old friends had betrayed him.
By March 1936, when Hitler sent troops into the Rhineland and Britain and France did nothing to stop him, the Stresa Front no longer existed.

### The Rome–Berlin Axis, 1936

The break with Britain and France drove Mussolini towards Germany, which had not joined the sanctions and sent coal to Italy. In January 1936 Mussolini told the German ambassador that he would no longer oppose Austria becoming a German satellite, and in July 1936 Austria and Germany agreed a treaty that recognised Austria's independence while binding her to German policy. In July 1936 the Spanish Civil War began, and both dictators supported General Franco. In October 1936 the Italian Foreign Minister, Count Galeazzo Ciano, visited Berlin, and he and the German Foreign Minister, Baron Constantin von Neurath, agreed protocols on policy towards Spain, the Danube and Austria, and the League. On 1 November in the cathedral square in Milan Mussolini said that the line between Berlin and Rome was "not a diaphragm but rather an axis" around which all European states that wanted collaboration and peace could collaborate. The name stuck.

:::def Rome–Berlin Axis
The agreement between Germany and Italy of October 1936, named by Mussolini on 1 November 1936. It was an understanding on policy, not yet a military alliance (that came with the Pact of Steel in May 1939).
:::

### The Anti-Comintern Pact, 1936–37

The second new grouping was between Germany and Japan. Japan had left the League in 1933 and wanted a friend against the USSR, with which it had a long frontier in Manchuria. The Nazi Party wanted a counterweight to the USSR, and the German Foreign Ministry's rival "Ribbentrop Bureau" worked with the Japanese military attaché in Berlin, Oshima Hiroshi, to negotiate it. On 25 November 1936 the two states signed the **Anti-Comintern Pact** in Berlin.

:::def Anti-Comintern Pact
The agreement of 25 November 1936 between Germany and Japan to inform each other about the activities of the Comintern, to consult on defensive measures and to invite other states to join. A secret protocol, which came to light only after the war, said that if either was attacked or threatened by the USSR without provocation, the other would not help the USSR. Italy joined on 6 November 1937.
:::

The public text did not mention the USSR at all, only the "Communistic International". But everyone understood that Moscow was the target. The pact was not a military alliance: it committed no one to fight. Its importance was political. It made the three "have-not" powers (as they described themselves) into a bloc with a common enemy, gave Germany and Japan a way to cooperate against the USSR, and gave Britain and France the hope, which the next chapters examine, that the dictators might turn against the USSR instead of against them.

The key question asks how extremism **affected** relations between the powers. These four events are the evidence for "changed alliances". The pattern: in the early 1930s the powers tried to settle matters by conference (London, Geneva). When that failed, they tried a front against Germany (Stresa). When that broke down over Abyssinia, the three dissatisfied powers drew together (Axis, Anti-Comintern Pact), and Britain and France were left looking for a new way to cope.

## The USSR and the fear of communism

### The Soviet response to the rise of Nazism

In the 1920s the USSR and Germany, both outsiders in the post-war system, had been friends. The Treaty of Rapallo (1922) and the Treaty of Berlin (1926) kept the two on good terms, and the German army trained secretly on Soviet soil in the 1920s, in flying, tank and gas schools. Until 1933 Soviet policy was also hostile to the League of Nations, which Moscow called a club of the imperialist powers, and Comintern policy (the "Third Period" of 1928–34) treated socialists as "social fascists", a policy which divided the German left and made it easier for Hitler to win.

:::def Comintern
The Communist International, founded by Lenin in Moscow in 1919 to organise and direct communist parties in other countries and to spread revolution. In practice it was controlled by the Soviet leadership.
:::

Hitler's victory in January 1933 changed that. He was a fanatical enemy of communism: the German Communist Party was crushed after the Reichstag fire of February 1933, and *Mein Kampf* had called for living space in the east at the expense of Russia. Germany ended the secret military cooperation during 1933, though the Treaty of Berlin was still extended in May 1933. The USSR was also anxious about the east: Japan had occupied Manchuria in 1931 and its army was on the Soviet frontier. Stalin, who was building heavy industry in the Five-Year Plans, wanted time and no war on two fronts.

The Soviet answer was a policy that the Commissar for Foreign Affairs, Maxim Litvinov, called **collective security**:

:::def Collective security
The idea that peace is best kept if all states act together against any one that attacks another, so that an aggressor faces the combined strength of the rest. It is the principle behind the League's Covenant.
:::

The steps were these:
- **Treaties with neighbours** (1932–33): non-aggression pacts with Poland (1932) and France (November 1932), and in July 1933 conventions defining "aggression" with the USSR's neighbours.
- **Recognition by the USA** on 16 November 1933, after years of non-recognition.
- **Joining the League of Nations** on 18 September 1934. The Assembly voted to admit the USSR by 39 votes to 3 (with seven abstentions, though reports differ on the number), and it was given a permanent seat on the Council. Litvinov became the League's most vocal champion of collective security.
- **The Franco-Soviet Pact** (2 May 1935): France and the USSR promised each other help if either was attacked without provocation by a European state. It had no military convention behind it, which made it weak, and the French parliament did not ratify it until early 1936. A treaty with Czechoslovakia (16 May 1935) bound the USSR to help the Czechs, but only if France did so first.
- **The Popular Front.** At the Seventh World Congress of the Comintern in July–August 1935 the General Secretary, the Bulgarian Georgi Dimitrov, told Communists to stop attacking socialists and to build a "wide anti-fascist People's Front". Communists joined the Popular Front governments of France (elected in May 1936, with Léon Blum as Prime Minister) and, after the February 1936 election, of Spain.

### The fear of communism in the West

The same changes made the democracies, and the right-wing governments of eastern Europe, deeply uneasy. For them the USSR was not a normal great power. It had been born in a revolution that had seized the property of foreign investors and cancelled the Tsar's debts, and the Comintern openly called for the overthrow of other governments. Conservatives in Britain and France still remembered the strikes and communist risings of 1919–21; in Britain the "Zinoviev letter" of 1924 (a forgery) had been used to damage Labour. Many politicians believed that communism, which threatened property and social order, was a greater danger than fascism, which at least was against it.

The Soviet purges made suspicion worse. After the murder of the Leningrad party leader Kirov in December 1934, Stalin began to remove real and imagined enemies; the show trials began in August 1936, and in June 1937 the Red Army's leading generals were executed. Western governments, and many in Poland and Romania, doubted that a Red Army whose officers had been shot could fight well, and doubted that the USSR could be trusted at all.

:::key How Soviet policy and fear of communism shaped foreign policy, 1933–37
1. **The USSR** abandoned its friendship with Germany and sought allies: the League, France, Czechoslovakia, the Popular Front.
2. **The West** accepted Soviet membership of the League and a pact with France, but would not make a military alliance or hold staff talks with the USSR on a large scale. Britain did not join the Franco-Soviet Pact.
3. **Eastern Europe**: Poland and Romania, fearful of the Red Army, would not agree to Soviet troops crossing their land, which made it hard to help Czechoslovakia.
4. **The dictators** exploited fear of communism: the Anti-Comintern Pact of 1936 presented Germany, Italy and Japan as defenders of Europe and of the world against Moscow, and many conservatives in Britain and France preferred to see them as a barrier against communism rather than as a threat.
5. **Result**: no effective alliance of the democracies and the USSR against Germany formed before 1939.
:::

:::exam What this means for the key question
The rise of Nazism did not only change Germany; it forced the USSR to change from outsider to a supporter of the League, and it divided Europe along a new line, with communism on one side and fascism on the other. The best answers treat the Soviet change as a **response** to Hitler (cause and effect), and show that mutual suspicion between the USSR and the democracies weakened the opposition to the dictators.
:::

## Weighing the evidence: how did extremism affect international relations?

| Effect | Evidence | How strong? |
|---|---|---|
| The end of agreed disarmament | The Conference failed; Germany left (October 1933); Germany announced conscription (March 1935); no naval limits after 1936 | Very strong: the Conference was the main attempt, and it ended in a public breach |
| New aggressors outside the League's control | Japan in Manchuria; Hitler's withdrawal; Italy in Abyssinia (see [International history, 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2)) | Strong, but its full effect is shown in [International history, 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2) |
| The regrouping of the powers | Stresa Front (1935), then Axis (1936) and Anti-Comintern Pact (1936–37) | Strong, but the Axis and Anti-Comintern Pact were not yet military alliances |
| Soviet change of policy | League entry (1934), Franco-Soviet Pact (1935), Popular Front | Strong for the USSR; its effect on the West was limited by mistrust |
| American isolation | Neutrality Acts (1935–37); Senate and World Court (1935) | Strong, but it was a response to the slump and to memories of 1917 rather than to extremism itself |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Extremism affected international relations in **two ways**: it removed the means of peaceful change (disarmament, naval limits and the confidence of the 1920s) and it created a bloc of dissatisfied states with a common enemy. But the damage was done in part by the **democracies' own divisions**: France's insistence on security before disarmament, Britain's refusal to take commitments, American isolationism and Western suspicion of the USSR all helped the extremists. The slump explains the rise of the dictators; the democracies' choices explain why the dictators met so little resistance.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **A.J.P. Taylor**, *The Origins of the Second World War* (1961), argued that the war was not the result of a master plan: Hitler was an opportunist who took what the failures of the other powers offered. His book made the actions of Britain and France as important as those of the dictators.
- **Richard Overy**, *The Origins of the Second World War* (1987), put the world economic crisis near the centre: the slump broke down the international economic order and helped bring to power or encourage governments in Germany, Italy and Japan that thought they could solve their problems by conquest.
- **Zara Steiner**, *The Triumph of the Dark: European International History 1933–1939* (2011), is a very detailed study of all the powers, including the USSR and the smaller states. It treats the Depression and the rise of dictatorships as the setting, and the choices and constraints of each government as the explanation for what followed.
At AS you are rewarded for your own argument from evidence, not for a list of historians. If you mention one, say what the argument is and use it to support a point.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the World Disarmament Conference failed.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The Conference met at Geneva from 1932. France did not want to disarm because it was afraid of Germany. Germany wanted equality with the other powers. Japan had invaded Manchuria. Hitler became Chancellor in 1933 and Germany left the Conference in October 1933. So the Conference failed."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence only says what happened. "So the Conference failed" asserts a link; it is not explained. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was the clash between France's demand for security and Germany's demand for equality. France had the strongest army in Europe but feared a revived Germany, and it had no guarantee from Britain beyond Locarno, so its plans of 1932 asked for a League force and short-service armies before France would cut its own forces. Germany said that it had disarmed in 1919 on the promise that others would follow, and demanded equality of treatment; its delegation walked out in September 1932 until the Five-Power Declaration of December 1932 promised 'equality of rights in a system which would provide security for all nations'. A second reason was that the Conference met under the shadow of other events. Japan was conquering Manchuria and fighting at Shanghai as delegates arrived, and the League could not stop it, which weakened belief that the Covenant would protect anyone who disarmed."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add Hitler, and show how the factors work together:

"A third reason was Hitler's arrival in January 1933. A compromise had been just possible: MacDonald's plan of March 1933 would have allowed Germany an army of 200,000 and Hitler accepted it in principle in his speech of 17 May. But the British and French then asked for a trial period, which Hitler called a refusal of equality, and on 14 October 1933 he withdrew Germany from the Conference and the League. The British plan of January 1934, with a German army of 300,000, was refused by France; Barthou's note of April 1934 ended French interest.

These factors were linked. France's fear made it unwilling to accept German equality; Germany's insistence on equality, and later Hitler's real aim to rearm, made France's fear justified; and the events in Manchuria and the Depression made all the powers less willing to trust the League. The decisive factor was the conflict of aims between France and Germany: before 1933 it blocked agreement and after Hitler came to power it could not be bridged, because he wanted rearmament and France would not allow it. The Conference failed not because of any single event but because the two states whose agreement was needed wanted opposite things."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected**, and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The Great Depression was the main reason for the rise of extremism in the 1930s.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: Germany (production down by over 40 per cent; about 6 million unemployed; Nazi vote 2.6 per cent in 1928 to 37.3 per cent in July 1932); Japan (silk prices, deflation, rural ruin, assassinations of 1930 and 1932); Italy (wage cuts, one million unemployed, pressure for success abroad).
- Against 1, older causes: resentment of Versailles; the Japanese army's plans for Manchuria before 1929; Mussolini's regime from 1922.
- Against 2, political weakness: Germany's emergency decrees and the choice of Hitler in January 1933; Japan's lack of civilian control over the army.
- Against 3, democracies survived: Britain, France and the USA did not turn to extremism.
- Judgement: the Depression was the **trigger** and decisive for Germany and for the end of party government in Japan; but the underlying causes were older and the outcome depended on political choices. Criterion: what changed a discontented country into an extremist one?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The Great Depression was the main reason for extremism because it hit Germany hardest. Industrial production fell by over 40 per cent between 1929 and 1932 and unemployment reached about six million, so people lost hope in democracy. The Nazi vote rose from 2.6 per cent in 1928 to 37.3 per cent in July 1932. In Japan the fall in the price of silk ruined farmers, and the army took power. So the Depression was the main reason for extremism."

*Commentary.* Accurate and well supported, but it argues one side only and its last sentence asserts what it should prove. An answer made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the Depression cannot be the main reason on its own, because the conditions for extremism existed before it. In Japan, the army's wish to control Manchuria and its contempt for the party politicians were years old; the Kwantung Army's attack of September 1931 was planned by officers who believed that Japan's survival required the conquest of the region, and they would probably have tried it without a slump. In Italy the regime had been fascist since 1922, and Mussolini's dreams of a Mediterranean empire predated the slump; the Depression added pressure but did not create the ambition. In Germany, the Nazis had a programme in the 1920s that blamed Versailles for everything, and the Republic had been weak from the start. Meanwhile Britain, the USA and France suffered as severely in output and unemployment but kept their democracies. If the slump alone caused extremism they would have gone the same way.

What decided the outcome was political. In Germany, the democratic parties could not agree on a policy in 1930, Brüning's reliance on decrees taught Germans to do without parliament, and in January 1933 conservative politicians and President Hindenburg chose to bring in Hitler, in the false belief that they could control him; the Nazis had lost two million votes between July and November 1932, so the Depression itself did not hand Hitler power. In Japan, the assassinations of 1930 and 1932 frightened politicians into giving way, and the army's independence in Manchuria meant that the government could no longer control it.

Overall, the Depression was the necessary trigger: without it the Nazi vote would almost certainly have stayed small, and Japanese party government would probably have lasted longer. But it was not the main reason for extremism in the sense of the most important underlying cause. The deeper causes were the resentments of 1919, weak institutions and the choices of the political elites. The statement is true of the timing and force of the rise of extremism, but not of its causes."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (trigger against underlying cause). With an opening paragraph on the case for the Depression, this is Level 5 (18–19). The sentence about the Nazi vote in November 1932 uses the chart: use data like that.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

... fascism in power was correctly described by the Thirteenth Plenum of the Executive Committee of the Communist International as the open terrorist dictatorship of the most reactionary, most chauvinistic and most imperialist elements of finance capital. ... the formation of a wide anti-fascist People's Front on the basis of the proletarian united front ... the Franco-Soviet pact, which defends the cause of peace against the aggression of German fascism ...

*From the main report by Georgi Dimitrov, General Secretary of the Comintern, to the Seventh World Congress of the Communist International, Moscow, 2 August 1935. Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

*Terms in Source A.* Finance capital: the bankers and big industrialists. Chauvinistic: aggressively nationalist. Proletarian: of the industrial workers. The proletarian united front: Communists and socialists acting together. The Thirteenth Plenum: a full meeting of the Comintern's executive committee, held in 1933. Imperialist: out for foreign conquest.

**Source B**

The High Contracting States agree that they will mutually keep each other informed concerning the activities of the Communistic International, will confer upon the necessary measure of defense, and will carry out such measures in close co-operation. The High Contracting States will jointly invite third States whose internal peace is menaced by the disintegrating work of the Communistic International, to adopt defensive measures in the spirit of the present Agreement or to participate in the present Agreement.

*From Articles I and II of the German–Japanese Agreement against the Communist International (the Anti-Comintern Pact), signed at Berlin, 25 November 1936. English translation as printed in the Avalon Project, Yale Law School.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to fascism and communism in the mid-1930s.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says fascism is a terrorist dictatorship and that there should be a People's Front. Source B says Germany and Japan will exchange information about the Comintern and invite other states to join. They are different because Source A is for communism and Source B is against it. Source A is by a communist so it is biased and Source B is a treaty."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a generalisation with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources are opposed in their attitudes, but their methods have common ground. Each presents the other side as a threat to peace and calls for an international combination against it: Source A wants 'a wide anti-fascist People's Front' and praises the Franco-Soviet pact as defending 'the cause of peace against the aggression of German fascism'; Source B commits Germany and Japan to 'keep each other informed', to 'confer upon the necessary measure of defense' and to invite 'third States' to join. Both also claim to be defensive: Source A says the Franco-Soviet pact 'defends the cause of peace'; Source B speaks of 'defensive measures' against a force that 'menaces' the 'internal peace' of other states.

The differences are in whom each regards as the enemy and what kind of threat it is. Source A sees fascism as a class dictatorship, 'the most reactionary … elements of finance capital', and the danger as military aggression by Germany. Source B names no state, only an organisation, the 'Communistic International', and the danger it describes is internal subversion.

The context explains this. Source A is the Comintern's change of line. Until 1934 Communists had treated socialists as 'social fascists'; the Congress of 1935 followed Hitler's destruction of the German Communist Party and the Soviet turn to collective security (League entry in 1934, the Franco-Soviet Pact of 1935), and the speech is meant to persuade Communists, and also socialists and liberal democrats, to cooperate. Its praise for the Franco-Soviet pact shows that it also serves Soviet foreign policy. Source B is a treaty between two regimes that had left the League and wanted to cooperate without saying so openly: the text avoids naming the USSR, which is one reason why it is more useful as evidence of how Germany and Japan wished to present themselves than of what they intended. It also hides that Germany and Japan agreed a secret protocol that promised not to help the USSR if either was attacked by it. So A is good evidence of Soviet and Comintern thinking in 1935 and B of the public face of the German–Japanese relationship in 1936. Each source is persuasive writing for a purpose; each tells the historian about its author's fears more than about the real aims of the other side."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the Comintern's change of line, the Soviet search for allies, the dictators' wish to appear defensive) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final judgement of what each is good evidence for. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

The three Powers, the object of whose policy is the collective maintenance of peace within the framework of the League of Nations, find themselves in complete agreement in opposing, by all practicable means, any unilateral repudiation of treaties which may endanger the peace of Europe.

*Adapted from the Final Declaration of the Stresa Conference, 14 April 1935. Shortened.*

*Adapted from the speech of Sir Samuel Hoare, British Foreign Secretary, to the Assembly of the League of Nations, Geneva, 11 September 1935, as reported in newspapers the next day.*

**Source C**

This Berlin–Rome line is not a diaphragm but rather an axis around which all European states that desire collaboration and peace can also collaborate.

*Adapted from a speech by Benito Mussolini in the Piazza del Duomo, Milan, 1 November 1936. English translations of the Italian vary.*

**Source D**

The peace-loving nations must make a concerted effort in opposition to those violations of treaties and those ignorings of humane instincts which today are creating a state of international anarchy and instability from which there is no escape through mere isolation or neutrality.

*From a speech by President Franklin D. Roosevelt, Chicago, 5 October 1937.*

**Question.** 'Between 1935 and 1937 the powers turned away from collective security.' How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support (a turn away): C (a new grouping that is not the League: an "axis"); D (a President has to plead for a "concerted effort" and says that isolation and neutrality are no escape, which implies that many were choosing them). Challenge: A and B (in 1935 the powers still spoke the language of collective maintenance of peace and resistance to aggression); D (in 1937 Roosevelt still asks for collective action); C (Mussolini presents the axis as open to all states that want peace).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context.**
- **A**: an official statement agreed by three governments, which is evidence of what they chose to say in public. It is also evidence of the gap between word and deed: it commits no one to act, it is silent about what happens if Hitler moves again, and within two months Britain broke its spirit by the Anglo-German Naval Agreement. Strong evidence of **intentions in April 1935**, weak evidence of what the powers would do.
- **B**: a Foreign Secretary speaking to the Assembly at the height of the Abyssinian crisis, five months after Stresa, to reassure the League that Britain would defend the Covenant. It is the strongest statement of collective security in the set. But it must be read with what followed: oil was left off the sanctions list, the Suez Canal was left open, and in December 1935 Hoare himself agreed the Hoare–Laval plan to give Italy much of Abyssinia. It shows what British public opinion, which had just voted heavily for the League in the Peace Ballot, demanded, not what the government would do.
- **C**: a speech by a dictator to a large crowd, so propaganda, which presents the axis as a force for peace. But its date, days after the October protocols and after the lifting of sanctions on Italy in July, and its context (Spain, Austria, Abyssinia) show a real shift. Strong evidence of **Mussolini's change of direction**, though the Axis was not yet an alliance.
- **D**: a President speaking in a city whose newspapers were strongly isolationist, three months after Japan began a full-scale war against China (July 1937). It shows that at least one leader wanted collective action. The reaction (Roosevelt backed away and the Neutrality Act of 1937 stayed in force) shows how powerful isolationism was, which supports the view that collective security was in retreat.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "The sources support the view to a moderate extent. Sources A and B show that in 1935 the three powers that mattered most in Europe still spoke of 'the collective maintenance of peace' and 'collective resistance to all acts of unprovoked aggression'; by November 1936 Source C shows Italy organising a group outside the League and in October 1937 Source D shows an American President saying that 'isolation or neutrality' are no escape, which presupposes that most were choosing one or the other. But the sources only support a turn away if they are read against what the powers did. Source A is a statement without commitment, and Source B, the strongest, was followed by sanctions that excluded oil and a plan to reward Italy. So the powers had already turned away from collective security in practice in 1935, while claiming to uphold it; what changed between 1935 and 1937 was that Italy and Germany stopped pretending and that a bloc outside the League formed (Source C), and that the USA, which had never been a member, was still unable to act (Source D). The view is therefore only partly right: the powers did turn away from collective security, but the turn came earlier than the sources' words suggest and was more a matter of deeds than of declarations."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1935–37; the answer explains where the sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the Stresa Front a serious attempt to contain Germany?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Stresa Front was set up in April 1935 at Stresa in Italy. Hitler had announced conscription on 16 March 1935 and Britain, France and Italy were worried. Ramsay MacDonald, Pierre-Étienne Flandin and Mussolini met for three days. They agreed to oppose any breaking of treaties and to support the independence of Austria. In June 1935 Britain signed the Anglo-German Naval Agreement with Germany. In October 1935 Italy invaded Abyssinia and Britain and France imposed sanctions. In March 1936 Hitler sent troops into the Rhineland. So the Stresa Front fell apart."

**The level.** Accurate detail and a sound order, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story without ever saying how serious the attempt was. The only link to the question is the last sentence, which says that the Front fell apart but not why that matters. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the facts that prove it, then qualify. "The Stresa Front was a serious attempt in intention but not in substance. In April 1935 the three powers that had most to lose from German revisionism agreed to oppose treaty-breaking 'by all practicable means', and the League Council condemned Hitler's conscription a few days later. But the declaration contained no promise of military action and no plan, and the three governments wanted different things: Italy the independence of Austria, France the Rhine frontier, and Britain to avoid any new commitment. Within two months Britain's Anglo-German Naval Agreement, signed without consulting France or Italy, showed that it would make separate deals with Germany; and the Abyssinian war split Britain and France from Italy. The Front was therefore a gesture that could contain Germany only as long as the three agreed on everything." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give two ways in which the Great Depression hit Germany, and say what that did to German politics.
A: Industrial production fell by more than 40 per cent (1929–32) and unemployment reached about six million. The democratic parties could not agree a policy, Brüning ruled by decree, and the Nazi vote rose from 2.6 per cent (1928) to 37.3 per cent (July 1932); on 30 January 1933 Hitler became Chancellor.
Q: Why did the slump damage Japan's party government?
A: Silk exports and prices collapsed, farmers were ruined and the return to the gold standard brought deflation, so the parties were blamed for hardship and corruption; the army and nationalists said they were also selling out the nation (the London Naval Treaty). Assassinations (Hamaguchi 1930, Inukai 1932) frightened politicians into giving way.
Q: Name the Neutrality Acts of 1935, 1936 and 1937 and say what each added.
A: 1935: arms embargo on all countries at war; 1936: ban on loans to them; 1937: applied the rules to civil wars (Spain) and allowed other goods to be sold only on "cash and carry" terms.
Q: What did France want at the Disarmament Conference and what did Germany want?
A: France wanted security first (a League force, guarantees) before it would reduce its forces; Germany wanted equality of treatment, either disarmament by others or the right to rearm.
Q: Name the three powers at Stresa, give its purpose and say two reasons it failed.
A: Britain, France and Italy; to oppose unilateral repudiation of treaties, reaffirm Locarno and defend Austria's independence. It failed because there was no commitment to act, because Britain signed the Anglo-German Naval Agreement (June 1935) without consulting the others, and because Italy invaded Abyssinia in October 1935.
Q: How did the Rome–Berlin Axis differ from the Anti-Comintern Pact?
A: The Axis (October–November 1936) was an understanding between Germany and Italy on policy; the Anti-Comintern Pact (25 November 1936) was between Germany and Japan, to exchange information about the Comintern, with Italy joining on 6 November 1937. Neither was yet a military alliance.
Q: Why did the USSR change its foreign policy in 1933–35?
A: Hitler's victory (and the destruction of the German Communist Party), Japan's occupation of Manchuria and the fear of war on two fronts led Stalin to seek collective security: pacts with neighbours, US recognition (1933), League membership (1934), the Franco-Soviet Pact (1935) and the Popular Front.
Q: Give two reasons why the Western powers distrusted the USSR in the mid-1930s.
A: The Comintern called for revolution abroad and communism threatened property and order; the Stalinist purges (from 1934, show trials in 1936, the generals in 1937) made the USSR seem unreliable. Poland and Romania also refused to let Soviet troops cross their land.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Great Depression led to the rise of the Nazi Party in Germany.
A: Indicative content: unemployment (about six million) and the fall in production destroyed faith in the Republic; the democratic parties could not agree a policy and Brüning governed by decree; the Nazis offered work and bread, an end to reparations and a scapegoat, and used mass propaganda; the middle class and the unemployed turned to them (vote 2.6 per cent in 1928, 18.3 per cent in 1930, 37.3 per cent in July 1932). A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these (the slump created the discontent, the weakness of the parties left a political vacuum, the Nazi message filled it) and judges the most important reason, for example that the slump was the necessary condition and the failure of the parties the reason the vote went to extremists and not to the moderate left.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why party government came to an end in Japan in 1932.
A: Indicative content: the slump and deflation (silk, rural hardship); the parties' reputation for corruption; resentment of the London Naval Treaty (1930) and the Western powers; the assassination of Hamaguchi (1930); the Kwantung Army's conquest of Manchuria (1931), popular with the public, which the government could not control; the murder of Inukai on 15 May 1932, after which the army and navy dominated "national unity" governments. Level 4: connect the factors (economic crisis weakened the parties; the army's success abroad strengthened its claim to run the country; violence removed the politicians who opposed it) and judge the decisive factor, for example the army's control in Manchuria, because it made the military independent of the civilian government.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far was the Anglo-German Naval Agreement of 1935 responsible for the collapse of the Stresa Front?
A: For: signed on 18 June 1935 without consulting France or Italy, and allowing Germany a navy 35 per cent of the British, it broke the spirit of the declaration against unilateral breaches of Versailles and showed that Britain would make separate deals; France and Italy lost trust. Against: Mussolini's invasion of Abyssinia (3 October 1935) and the sanctions that followed split Italy from Britain and France; the Front had no military commitment from the start; the three powers wanted different things (Austria, the Rhine, avoiding commitments); the Rhineland (7 March 1936) found no common response. Level 5 uses a criterion, for example "what destroyed the common purpose?", and judges that the Naval Agreement damaged trust but the Abyssinian crisis ended the Front, since Italy would otherwise have stayed in it despite the Agreement.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The USSR changed its foreign policy in the 1930s mainly because of the rise of Hitler.' How far do you agree?
A: For: the destruction of the KPD in 1933 and the end of the military cooperation with Germany; Hitler's anti-communism and *Mein Kampf*; the Soviet search for allies (France, Czechoslovakia, the League 1934); the Popular Front (1935) after the Comintern's old line had helped Hitler. Against: the threat from Japan in Manchuria from 1931; the need for peace for the Five-Year Plans; the Soviet–Polish and Franco-Soviet pacts of 1932 began before Hitler came to power; the weakness of the Western powers; the German–Polish pact (January 1934) that suggested Poland might be looking to Germany. Level 5 weighs these, e.g. that Hitler turned a policy of caution into one of active search for alliances, but that Japan and the need for time to build industry also mattered.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about German attitudes to disarmament in 1933.
A: Similarities: both claim that Germany wants equality and is ready to disarm or accept hardship for principle; both are public statements by the German government for an international audience. Differences: A (May) is conciliatory and offers to disband if others do; B (October) is defiant and conditional, making equality an "inalienable condition" and announcing withdrawal. Explanation: in May Hitler needed time and wanted to put the blame for failure on the others; in October the Allies' demand for a trial period gave him a reason to leave and the plebiscite gave the regime a chance to show unity. Provenance: A is a speech to the Reichstag, delivered to an audience of Nazi deputies and the world's press; B is a government proclamation reported by an American news agency (a secondary report, in English, so the wording may differ). Level 4 explains the change through context and judges that the two sources together show that the offer in A was tactical and the equality of B was a pretext for rearmament; neither alone shows Germany's real intentions.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'American isolationism was the main reason why the USA did not support collective action in the 1930s.' How far do you agree?
H: Set a criterion: what does "support collective action" mean? Joining the League? Imposing sanctions? Selling arms?
H: List the barriers inside the USA (Congress, public opinion, the Neutrality Acts) and then those outside (the League's own weakness, Britain and France's own policies).
A: For: Senate rejection of the League (1919–20) and the World Court (1935, by 52 votes to 36); the Nye Committee and the "merchants of death" argument; the Neutrality Acts (1935, 1936, 1937); the reaction to the quarantine speech (5 October 1937); the Johnson Act of 1934. Against: the Depression made government and voters think about jobs first (the slump, not a principle of isolation); Roosevelt's own caution and his need for Congress's votes for the New Deal; the weakness of the League itself (the USA could not rely on Britain and France to act); the USA did take some action (the Stimson Doctrine 1932, recognition of the USSR 1933, the Good Neighbor policy). Level 5: isolationism was the main reason because it was in law and in the party politics that bound the President, but the slump gave it its strength; the answer should distinguish a reason from a precondition.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent was France responsible for the failure of the World Disarmament Conference?
H: Start with what France actually proposed (security through a League force) and why.
H: Look at Germany's and Britain's roles, and at Hitler's decision of 14 October 1933.
A: For: France's insistence on security first and its rejection of German equality; its refusal of the British plan of January 1934 (Barthou's note, April 1934); the Herriot plan did not give the other powers what they could accept. Against: Hitler's intention to rearm and his withdrawal on 14 October 1933; Britain's reluctance to guarantee French security; the effect of Manchuria and the Depression; the American Hoover plan was ignored by all. Level 5 judgement: France's demand for security was understandable and a precondition for any agreement, but it was Hitler's arrival, and his decision to leave the Conference, that ended any chance of a deal; France hastened the end rather than caused it.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Fear of communism, not fear of Germany, shaped the policy of Britain and France towards the dictators in 1933–37.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which pieces of evidence show fear of communism? (Comintern, purges, Popular Front governments, reluctance to ally with the USSR.)
H: Which show fear of Germany? (Stresa, the Franco-Soviet Pact, rearmament.)
A: Support: the reluctance of Britain to join the Franco-Soviet Pact or hold staff talks; the view of many Conservatives that Germany and Italy were barriers against Bolshevism; the Popular Front in France in 1936; Poland and Romania's refusal to let Soviet troops cross; the Anti-Comintern Pact presented as an anti-communist measure. Challenge: Stresa (1935) was a front against Germany with no mention of the USSR; the Franco-Soviet Pact (1935) was French fear of Germany; British rearmament from 1935; the Rhineland crisis and the Anglo-German Naval Agreement show concern with German power, not communism. Level 5 judgement: both fears shaped policy, but fear of communism limited what Britain and France were ready to do about Germany, so it explains their hesitation more than their aims.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The rise of extremism in the 1930s was the result of the failures of the democracies as much as of the strength of the dictators.' How far do you agree?
H: Separate extremism's causes from its success: what made the dictators come to power, and what let them win so much?
H: Give evidence from the economy, from the Disarmament Conference, from Stresa and from the USSR.
A: Support: the democracies' failure to solve the slump (Brüning's deflation; Smoot–Hawley; Roosevelt's refusal to cooperate at London); France's rigidity and Britain's refusal to commit at Geneva; American isolation; mutual suspicion of the USSR; the failure to respond to Manchuria; the democratic politicians' mistake in bringing Hitler to power. Challenge: the dictators' own programmes and ambitions (Hitler's aims, Japan's army, Mussolini's empire), which existed before the slump; Hitler's skill in using the language of peace; that no democracy could have stopped extremism in Germany or Japan by itself. A* judgement: the democracies did not create the dictators but their divisions determined how far the dictators could go; the more convincing view puts responsibility on different actors at different stages (the Depression and German politicians for 1933, the democratic powers for 1933–37).
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ A.J.P. Taylor argued in 1961 that Hitler acted as an opportunist, taking what the mistakes of others allowed. How far does the evidence of the World Disarmament Conference in 1933–34 support this view?
H: Which of Hitler's moves in 1933 were planned (secret rearmament, the aim stated on 3 February 1933) and which were reactions (the withdrawal of 14 October 1933)?
H: Consider the roles of France and Britain in the outcome, and what "opportunist" means.
A: Support: Hitler accepted the MacDonald plan in principle in May 1933 and left only when the Allies asked for a trial period, which gave him a reason to say he was treated unequally; the British and French lack of a joint policy gave him room; he used the plebiscite for domestic gain. Challenge: the secret rearmament had begun before 1933 and the aim of rearming Germany was clear from the notes of 3 February 1933; the decision to leave Geneva and the League on 14 October 1933 shows a settled intention not to be bound by limits; his speech of 17 May was a tactic. A* judgement: the evidence supports a mixed view: a settled aim (rearmament, then revision) with flexible methods that used opportunities. Taylor's "opportunist" describes the methods more than the aims, and the Conference shows both.
