Includes a mark-band explorer.

:::why
Paper 3 is unlike any other history paper. You are not asked what happened, or why, or whether a claim is true. You are given one paragraph or two of a historian's writing and asked what it shows about **what the historian thinks and how they came to think it**. Students who have learned the topic well still lose marks here, because they answer a different question: they summarise the extract, or argue with it, or tell the story of the topic. This chapter shows exactly what the examiners reward, in the order you will meet it in the exam hall. It is a guide to the paper; the skills themselves are taught in [How historians disagree: reading an interpretation](#hist.interp.skills), and each topic's debate is in its own chapter (links below).
:::

## What the paper looks like

**The front page.** It is the front page of every 9489 paper. It says you answer on the enclosed answer booklet. The instruction is "Answer one question from one section only", and it lists the three sections:

| Section | Topic | Your chapters in this course |
|---|---|---|
| A | The origins of the First World War | [the road to war](#hist.interp.ww1.1) and [the historians' debate](#hist.interp.ww1.2) |
| B | The Holocaust | [from antisemitism to genocide](#hist.interp.holocaust.1) and [the historians' debate](#hist.interp.holocaust.2) |
| C | The origins and development of the Cold War | [the breakdown of the wartime alliance](#hist.interp.coldwar.1) and [the historians' debate](#hist.interp.coldwar.2) |

The information box says the total mark is 40 and that the marks for each question or part of a question are shown in brackets. Nothing else is supplied. You have no notes, no timeline and no second extract.

**The question.** Each section has one question, and it is the same in all three except for the name of the topic. In the 2027 specimen paper and in the June 2024 paper of the old syllabus it reads:

> "What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of [the topic] to explain your answer. [40]"

Above it, the paper says "Read this extract and then answer the question that follows it" (or similar words). There is **no choice** of question inside your section and no part (a) or (b). Everything is one answer, and it is marked twice: once for how well you understand the extract as an interpretation (Table A, AO4) and once for the quality of the historical knowledge you use (Table B, AO1).

**The extract.** It is at most 600 words, a few paragraphs from a historian's book or article. The paper does not tell you who wrote it, and the candidates are never asked to say. In the specimen paper one extract opens by naming a famous earlier view that the historian rejects; real extracts usually have this shape, because a historian writes in answer to other historians. There is no source line, no date and no gloss. You work out what you can from the words in front of you.

:::warn Choose your topic early, and learn it as a debate
You study one of the three topics all year, so you will not choose in the exam hall. But notice what that means for revision. The paper does not ask what happened in July 1914, in the camps or at Yalta. It asks about a historian's view of those events. Learning the events is necessary (it is half the marks, in Table B), and it is not enough. For your topic you must also know the **debate**: who argued what, when, in answer to whom, and why.
:::

## What the examiners are testing

:::def Interpretation
What can be inferred from the extract about the nature of the historian's claims and conclusions: what they conclude happened, why it happened, and who or what mattered most.
:::

:::def Approach
What the historian brings to the study of the past: what they are interested in, the questions they ask, and how these reflect their ideology and beliefs. An approach is not a "bias" to be exposed. Every historian has one.
:::

The syllabus says that you do **not** have to separate the two: they are linked, because the questions a historian asks help decide the answers they find. A historian who asks "who decided on war?" will look at ministers and generals, and will conclude something about intentions. In your answer you can write about both together, as long as you say what the extract claims **and** what kind of historian would write it.

:::def AO4: Historical interpretations
Understand, explain and analyse how historians have interpreted and approached aspects of the past. On Paper 3 it is half of the marks, and Table A is the table that tests it.
:::

:::def AO1: Historical knowledge
Recall, select and use appropriate historical knowledge. On Paper 3 it is the other half of the marks (Table B). It is not a separate essay on the topic. The question says "use the extract **and** your knowledge ... to explain your answer", so the knowledge is there to explain the extract.
:::

The syllabus also gives four reasons why historians differ. They are your toolkit for the "approach" part of an answer, because each one is a way of explaining why this historian wrote this extract:

:::key Why historians differ (the syllabus's four reasons)
1. **The fragmentary nature of the evidence.** The past has left only part of its record, so historians fill gaps by inference.
2. **Selection and interpretation of evidence.** Two historians with the same documents choose different ones and read them differently.
3. **The passage of time.** New evidence appears and later historians answer earlier ones.
4. **The time and place in which historians work.** A West German professor in 1961 and an American in 1959 ask different questions because of their world.
:::

### What the question does not ask

:::warn Three ways to answer a different question
1. **Evaluating.** "The historian is wrong because ..." The question says what can you **learn**, not how convincing the extract is. Knowledge may show where a claim is stretched, but only as part of explaining what the historian thinks.
2. **Summarising.** Going through the extract paragraph by paragraph ("First he says ... then he says ..."). The mark scheme puts a summary at Level 2.
3. **Telling the story of the topic.** Pages on the causes of the war, the Wannsee Conference or the Marshall Plan that are not tied to a claim in the extract. That is knowledge, but it is not **used**.

A fourth, smaller mistake: guessing the author. The mark scheme gives nothing for a name, and the paper never asks for one.
:::

## The levels of Table A and Table B

Both tables have six levels. They are the Cambridge levels for 2027 onwards.

**Table A: interpretation and approach (AO4), out of 20**

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 18–20 | Uses the extract in a detailed and accurate way; **complete understanding** of the interpretation and of the approach(es); **explains all elements** of the interpretation |
| 5 | 15–17 | Detailed, accurate use; **sound understanding**; engages with elements of the interpretation, but **without explaining it as a whole** (may cover less important sub-messages) |
| 4 | 12–14 | Uses the extract but only **partial understanding**; identifies elements without adequately explaining them, typically explaining other, less important messages as equally or more important |
| 3 | 9–11 | Understands that the extract contains interpretations, but explains **only sub-messages**; or uses part of the extract to argue for an interpretation that the whole does not support, or refers to a different interpretation in each paragraph |
| 2 | 5–8 | **Summarises** the main points; explanations of the extract as an interpretation lack validity |
| 1 | 1–4 | References to some aspects of the extract; fragments of relevant material |
| 0 | 0 | No creditable response |

**Table B: knowledge (AO1), out of 20**

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 18–20 | Detailed and accurate historical knowledge that is **entirely relevant** |
| 5 | 15–17 | Detailed and mostly accurate knowledge that is mainly relevant |
| 4 | 12–14 | Mostly relevant and accurate knowledge |
| 3 | 9–11 | Generally accurate and relevant knowledge |
| 2 | 5–8 | Some accurate and relevant knowledge |
| 1 | 1–4 | Limited knowledge |
| 0 | 0 | No creditable response |

**How the marking works.** The examiner finds the **best-fit level** for each table. Then, within the level, an answer that **convincingly** meets it gets the highest mark, one that **adequately** meets it a middle mark, and one that **just** meets it the lowest mark. Marks are whole numbers, they are positive, and nothing is deducted for an error or an omission. The two marks are added. Note the difference from the explorer below: a real examiner takes the **best fit**, so an answer that is strong on one Level 3 feature can still be placed there; the explorer is stricter, and counts a level only when the levels below it are also met.

| If both tables are at | The mark out of 40 is |
|---|---|
| Level 6 | 36–40 |
| Level 5 | 30–34 |
| Level 4 | 24–28 |
| Level 3 | 18–22 |
| Level 2 | 10–16 |
| Level 1 | 2–8 |

The tables need not be at the same level. A student who understands the extract well but writes thin knowledge might get Level 5 for Table A (15–17) and Level 3 for Table B (9–11): 24 to 28 in total.

:::key The step from level to level in Table A
- **Level 1 to 2: say what the extract says.** Stop quoting fragments and summarise the main points.
- **Level 2 to 3: say what it argues.** A summary tells you what the historian wrote; an interpretation is what the historian **claims**. Level 3 sees that the extract contains interpretations, but explains only the small ones.
- **Level 3 to 4: read the extract as one argument.** Do not give a different interpretation to each paragraph, and do not build a conclusion from one part of the extract.
- **Level 4 to 5: rank the messages.** Decide which are main and which are supporting, and explain the main ones. Level 4 treats minor messages as equally or more important.
- **Level 5 to 6: explain the whole.** Explain every element of the interpretation (usually two linked aspects), how they fit together, and the approach behind them.
:::

:::key The step from level to level in Table B
Knowledge climbs from **limited** (Level 1) to **some** (2), **generally** accurate and relevant (3), **mostly** (4), **detailed and mostly accurate, mainly relevant** (5) and **detailed, accurate and entirely relevant** (6). The word that matters most is "relevant". A fact is relevant when it is used to explain or test a claim in the extract. A fact that is only part of "what I know about 1914" is not, however accurate.
:::

## Reading the extract: from the page to a plan

The first twenty-five minutes decide the answer. A student who writes at once starts to summarise; a student who has found the whole argument first writes six paragraphs that all say something about it.

:::steps
1. **Read once for the gist.** What is this about, and what does the historian seem to think? Ask two questions: *who or what does this historian blame or credit?* and *whom or what is the extract arguing against?*
2. **Read again with a pencil.** Mark the claims (sentences that say what happened or why), the words of judgement, the words of contrast, and the people, groups or forces the historian puts at the centre. The table below shows what to look for.
3. **Write the main interpretation as one sentence.** A good sentence has two linked parts. If it has only one, you have probably found a sub-message.
4. **Sort the extract.** Write MAIN, SUPPORT or SUB beside every paragraph. The test: if you deleted this paragraph, would the main interpretation change? If not, it is a sub-message.
5. **Plan by aspect, not by paragraph.** Your paragraphs follow the aspects of the interpretation, and the extract's paragraphs are only your evidence.
6. **Choose your knowledge.** For each aspect, pick two or three precise facts that explain or test the claim. Then list what the extract leaves out.
7. **Note the approach.** What does the historian ask, what evidence do they use, and what does that show about their interests and beliefs?
:::

### What to look for in the extract

| What you notice | What it shows |
|---|---|
| An opening that names an earlier view or says "it has often been said" | The extract is a **reply**. What it rejects is half of the interpretation, and the earlier view is the historian's target |
| Words of judgement: *only, merely, above all, largely, rather than, not ... but* | What the historian thinks matters most. They rank causes for you |
| Words of contrast: *but, however, yet, nor, although* | The place where the argument turns. The claim usually sits just after |
| Who does the verbs: "leaders decided", "the system produced", "circumstances forced" | The **kind of explanation**: individuals and intention, structures, or circumstance |
| The evidence quoted: ministers' letters, diplomatic papers, camp records, memoirs, statistics, speeches | The **approach**: which sources the historian trusts, and so what kind of history it is |
| What is missing (no mention of the victims, of the economy, of one country) | What the approach leaves out. Naming an omission is knowledge used well |
| Moral or emotional words | Whether the historian judges or explains |
| A phrase that belongs to a school (for example, "improvised", "the real aims of the Soviet state") | A possible **label**, to be used only if the extract supports it (next section) |

### The two-aspect shape

In the 2027 specimen mark scheme, the main interpretation of each extract is described as having **two aspects**, and the mark scheme says that showing complete understanding of the interpretation involves discussing **both**. Answers that identify and explain both aspects reach Level 6; those that explain one reach Level 5; those that explain part and misread another aspect fall to Level 4; and those that argue a main interpretation different from the real one fall to Level 3. This is the most useful fact about the table: the levels are largely a measure of **how much of the real argument you have grasped**.

Typical pairs of aspects:
- **a claim and its target**: "X caused it" and "the older view, that Y caused it, is wrong";
- **a cause and what it excludes**: "the war made it possible" and "so it was not planned";
- **who and why**: "these leaders decided" and "they did so because they expected to win";
- **a cause and its limit**: "the system produced the crisis" and "it still needed human choices".

When you have your sentence, check it against the extract's opening and closing sentences. They usually state the claim.

## Labels: when the mark scheme will credit one

Historians' schools have names. The specimen mark schemes list the ones that may be used for each topic, and give credit only when the label is **appropriate to the extract and supported from it**.

| Topic | Labels the mark scheme may credit | In a sentence |
|---|---|---|
| The origins of the First World War | **Revisionism** | The reaction, soon after 1919, against blaming Germany alone: it blamed other nations or shared the blame |
| | **Anti-revisionism** | The return to blaming Germany, with Fischer's work of the early 1960s as the turning point |
| The Holocaust | **Intentionalism** | The murder of the Jews was planned, by Hitler and the Nazis, from the start |
| | **Structuralism** | The nature of the Nazi state, with its chaotic competition for Hitler's approval, produced genocide without a coherent plan |
| | **Functionalism** | The Holocaust was an unplanned, improvised response to wartime developments, especially after the conquest of eastern territories |
| | **Synthesis** | An interpretation that combines features of more than one of these |
| The origins and development of the Cold War | **Traditional (or orthodox)** | Blames the Soviet Union and Stalin's expansionism |
| | **Revisionist** | Shifts the blame to the United States, often through its economic aims |
| | **Post-revisionist** | A more balanced view in which both sides carry some blame |
| | **Post-post-revisionist** | The "New" Cold War history, drawing on archives opened after 1991, with new weight on Stalin's personality and the Soviet system |

Four rules.

1. **A label is a shortcut for a sentence, and you must write the sentence.** "This extract is anti-revisionist **because** it rejects the claim that nobody willed the war and names German leaders who accepted the risk." If you cannot write the "because", leave the label out.
2. **A wrong label costs marks.** In the specimen mark schemes for the Holocaust and Cold War extracts, an answer that used the wrong label was held below the top levels (to Level 3, 4 or 5, depending on the label), even when other parts were sound.
3. **The same word can mean different things.** "Revisionist" in the First World War debate is a view that **reduces** German blame. In the Cold War debate it is a view that **increases** American blame. Think about which debate you are in.
4. **An extract may seem to mix schools.** Decide by the whole, not by one sentence. A paragraph that gives the Soviet side a fair hearing does not make an extract post-revisionist if the whole argues that one man's character made the Cold War unavoidable.

No label is ever required. A top answer that explains the interpretation in its own terms and never uses a school's name loses nothing.

## Knowledge: what to have ready

Table B is half the marks. In Paper 3 it does not reward "everything I know". It rewards knowledge that is **used**, and knowledge has three jobs.

:::key Three jobs for knowledge in a Paper 3 answer
1. **Explain a claim.** The extract says something. You show what it refers to. ("The historian speaks of 'a blank cheque': this is Germany's promise of support to Austria-Hungary on 5 and 6 July 1914.")
2. **Test a claim.** You set it beside what you know and say where it fits and where it is stretched. ("The extract says earlier crises were settled; that is right for Morocco in 1906 and 1911, but in 1908–09 Russia gave way under German pressure, which left a grievance.")
3. **Show what the approach stresses or leaves out.** What the historian omits shows what the approach is interested in. ("Nothing is said of the arms race, which suggests an approach centred on decisions.")
:::

A fact does one of these jobs, or it is padding. Learn your topic's facts as a **checklist of dates and names**, because Table B Level 6 means "detailed and accurate". The lists below are what a strong answer on each topic is likely to draw on. Each fact is taught in the topic chapters.

**The origins of the First World War.**
- *Alliances and understandings:* Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy), 1882; Franco-Russian alliance, agreed 1892–94; Entente Cordiale, 8 April 1904 (not an alliance); Anglo-Russian Convention, 1907.
- *Tension before 1914:* German Naval Laws of 1898 and 1900; HMS *Dreadnought*, 1906; the Moroccan crises of 1905–06 and 1911; the Austrian annexation of Bosnia, October 1908; the Balkan Wars of 1912–13; the Schlieffen Plan.
- *The July Crisis:* Sarajevo, 28 June 1914; the "blank cheque", 5–6 July; the Austrian ultimatum, 23 July; Serbia's reply, 25 July; Austria declares war on Serbia, 28 July; Russian general mobilisation, 30 July; Germany declares war on Russia, 1 August, and on France, 3 August; the invasion of Belgium and Britain's entry, 4 August.
- *The debate:* Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919); the shared-responsibility view of the 1920s and 1930s; Fischer's *Griff nach der Weltmacht* (1961); the German reaction; later views that spread the blame; the centenary of 2014.

**The Holocaust.**
- *Before the war:* Nazi boycott of Jewish businesses, 1 April 1933; the Nuremberg Laws, 15 September 1935; Kristallnacht, 9–10 November 1938.
- *War and the ghettos:* the invasion of Poland, September 1939; the sealing of the Łódź ghetto (spring 1940) and the Warsaw ghetto (November 1940); the invasion of the USSR, 22 June 1941, and the *Einsatzgruppen*.
- *The killing programmes:* Chełmno, December 1941; the Wannsee Conference, 20 January 1942; Operation Reinhard camps (Bełżec, Sobibór, Treblinka) and Auschwitz-Birkenau, 1942; the deportation of Hungarian Jews, 1944; about six million Jewish victims.
- *The debate:* Hilberg's *The Destruction of the European Jews* (1961); intentionalism, structuralism and functionalism; Dawidowicz (1975); Browning's *Ordinary Men* (1992); Goldhagen (1996); the roles of perpetrators, victims and bystanders.

**The origins and development of the Cold War.**
- *Wartime and peace-making:* Yalta, 4–11 February 1945; Potsdam, 17 July to 2 August 1945; the atomic bombs, 6 and 9 August 1945.
- *Hardening positions:* Kennan's Long Telegram, 22 February 1946; Churchill's Fulton speech, 5 March 1946; the Truman Doctrine, 12 March 1947; Marshall's Harvard speech, 5 June 1947; the Cominform, September 1947.
- *1948–49:* the communist takeover in Czechoslovakia, February 1948; the Berlin Blockade, 24 June 1948 to 12 May 1949, and the Airlift; NATO, 4 April 1949.
- *The debate:* the traditional view of the 1940s and 1950s; Williams (1959) and the revisionists; Gaddis (1972) and Leffler (1992) as post-revisionists; Zubok and Pleshakov (1996); the opening of the archives after 1991 and the "New" Cold War history.

The other half of your knowledge is about **time and place**: when and where each school wrote, and why that mattered. An answer that can say "a West German historian writing in 1961, when the Eichmann trial was in the news" or "an American writing at the height of the Vietnam War" has a reason for the approach, and that is Level 6 thinking.

## A worked example: one extract, all the levels

The extract below was written for this guide. It is **not** by a real historian, it does not summarise any one book, and it is shorter than a real extract so that you can see the whole argument at once. Real extracts run to about 600 words. This example uses the First World War; if you study another topic, follow the method here and read the worked answer in your own debate chapter ([hist.interp.holocaust.2](#hist.interp.holocaust.2) or [hist.interp.coldwar.2](#hist.interp.coldwar.2)).

:::eg Paper 3 [40]: What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer.
**Practice extract A** (about 200 words; written for this guide, not a real historian's words)

It has become a habit to read 1914 backwards. We know how the story ended, so we look for the machinery that must have produced it: two armed camps, rigid war plans, a naval race, a press that stirred up hatred. The machinery was real, but it explains only why a great war was possible. It cannot explain why the war came in the summer of 1914 and not in 1905, 1908, 1911 or 1913, when each crisis was settled without it. The alliances were not chains: Britain, above all, had made no promise to fight, and its government was divided almost to the last day. What was different in July 1914 was that men in three capitals made choices. Vienna decided that Serbia must be crushed and asked Berlin for support. Berlin gave it, and accepted the risk of a general war rather than see its one dependable ally humbled. St Petersburg decided that it could not stand aside a second time. At each stage someone could have chosen otherwise. A historian who looks only at the system will describe a world waiting to explode. A historian who looks at the decisions will see that it was lit.

**Plan (ten minutes).**
- *Gist:* 1914 was caused by choices, not by a system.
- *Contrast words:* "It has become a habit ... but"; "explains only why ... was possible"; "cannot explain why"; "were not chains"; "A historian who looks only at ... A historian who looks at".
- *Main interpretation, two aspects.* (1) The alliances, arms race and war plans made a great war **possible**, but not **inevitable**: reading the system as the cause is a hindsight error. (2) The war came because of the **decisions** of leaders in Vienna, Berlin and St Petersburg in July 1914, so responsibility lies in choices and is spread across three powers.
- *Sub-messages:* the crises of 1905–13 were settled; Britain had made no promise and was divided; Berlin took a risk to protect Austria-Hungary; Russia would not stand aside a second time.
- *Approach:* asks "why then?" and not "why was it possible?"; explains events by what individuals decided; distrusts explanations from "machinery"; looks at several capitals, not one; uses diplomatic decisions as its evidence; does not rank the states or judge them morally.
- *Label:* none fits. The extract does not return to blaming Germany, so it is not anti-revisionist, and it does not reduce German blame, so it is not revisionist. Do not use one.
- *Knowledge to use:* the crises (Morocco 1905–06 and the Algeciras Conference of 1906; the Bosnian annexation of October 1908, with Russia giving way in March 1909; Agadir 1911 and the Franco-German agreement of 4 November 1911; the Balkan Wars and the Treaty of London, 30 May 1913); the Entente Cordiale (1904) as a settlement of colonial disputes, not an alliance; the blank cheque (5–6 July); the ultimatum (23 July) and Serbia's reply (25 July); the Kaiser's "halt in Belgrade" proposal (28 July); Russian general mobilisation (30 July); the German declarations (1 and 3 August); Belgium and Britain (4 August).

**A Level 2 answer (Table A 5–8; Table B 5–8; about 14 of 40).**
"The extract says that historians read 1914 backwards. It says the alliances and the arms race were real. It says that earlier crises were settled and that Britain had not promised to fight. It then says that Vienna wanted to crush Serbia, that Berlin gave support and that St Petersburg would not stand aside. So the extract says that many things led to the war."

*Commentary.* Accurate, and it follows the extract paragraph by paragraph: a **summary**. The last sentence ("many things led to the war") is not what the extract argues, so the "explanation" lacks validity. No knowledge is added. Level 2 in both tables.

**A Level 3 answer (Table A 9–11; Table B 9–11; about 20 of 40).**
"The extract argues that the alliance system was weak, because Britain was not bound to fight. This is true because Britain only had the Entente Cordiale of 1904, which was a friendly agreement. The extract also says that Vienna decided to crush Serbia and asked Germany for support. This was the blank cheque of July 1914. So the historian thinks that Austria-Hungary was to blame for the war."

*Commentary.* It explains two **sub-messages** correctly, with accurate knowledge. But it builds a different interpretation out of one of them ("Austria-Hungary was to blame"). The extract names three capitals and says nothing about ranking them. This is the Level 3 pattern: part of the extract used to argue an interpretation the whole does not support.

**A Level 4 answer (Table A 12–14; Table B 12–14; about 26 of 40).**
"The main message is that the war was not caused by the alliance system but by decisions, and the historian says this by arguing that the crises of 1905, 1908, 1911 and 1913 all ended peacefully. The decisions were taken in Vienna, Berlin and St Petersburg. But the most important decision was Germany's, since without the blank cheque of 5–6 July Austria-Hungary could not have acted against Serbia, and the historian says that Berlin 'accepted the risk of a general war'. The historian therefore mainly blames Germany."

*Commentary.* It has found one real element (decisions, not the system) and uses the extract. But it makes the **sub-message** about Berlin the most important, and misreads the extract as blaming Germany most, which is the other aspect of the real interpretation turned upside down. It never explains why the historian rejects the "machinery" view. Knowledge is accurate but used for the wrong claim. Level 4.

**A Level 5 answer (Table A 15–17; Table B 15–17; about 32 of 40)** would explain one aspect fully, with the right emphasis: that the war came from decisions taken in three capitals, tested with the blank cheque, the ultimatum of 23 July and Russia's mobilisation of 30 July. It would then deal with the system, the other aspect, as a separate point ("the extract also says the alliances were not chains") without showing that the two claims are one argument, and it would say little about the approach. Every step is accurate and detailed, but the interpretation is **not explained as a whole**.

**A Level 6 answer (Table A 18–20; Table B 18–20; about 37 of 40).**
"The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. It claims that the alliances, arms race and war plans of 1914 made a great war possible but did not make it inevitable, and that the war came because of decisions taken in Vienna, Berlin and St Petersburg in July 1914. The historian therefore rejects the view that 1914 was the working-out of a system, and does not single out one country.

The first aspect is a denial that the system was the cause. The historian says the machinery 'explains only why a great war was possible' and cannot explain 'why the war came in the summer of 1914 and not in 1905, 1908, 1911 or 1913'. Knowledge supports the evidence offered. The Moroccan crisis of 1905–06 was settled at the Algeciras Conference (a meeting of the powers in Spain in 1906), where Germany found little backing for its challenge to France; Russia accepted the Austrian annexation of Bosnia in March 1909 after German pressure; the Agadir crisis of 1911 ended with a Franco-German agreement on 4 November; and the Balkan Wars of 1912–13 were contained by the Treaty of London of May 1913 (the peace, made by the great powers, that ended the First Balkan War). Each could have led to a general war, and none did. The historian's claim that the alliances 'were not chains' also fits what is known: the Entente Cordiale of 1904 settled colonial disputes and was not an alliance, so Britain had made no promise to fight, and the Cabinet remained divided until Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August. The claim is stretched where it passes over the effect of the earlier crises. Russia had given way in 1908–09 and felt humiliated. So the extract's 'choice' in 1914, that 'St Petersburg decided that it could not stand aside a second time', was a choice made under pressure from that earlier defeat.

The second aspect is the argument from decisions, spread across three capitals. Each decision is named. Vienna decided, after Sarajevo on 28 June, to crush Serbia, and secured the 'blank cheque' on 5–6 July. Berlin gave it, and when Serbia replied to the ultimatum of 23 July on 25 July by accepting most of its terms, Austria-Hungary broke off relations and declared war on 28 July. Russia ordered general mobilisation on 30 July, and Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August. The historian's key sentence is that 'at each stage someone could have chosen otherwise', and knowledge shows this: on 28 July the Kaiser himself suggested that Austria-Hungary should 'halt in Belgrade' after the Serbian reply, and the chancellor pressed Vienna to negotiate on 29 and 30 July. These were choices that were open and were not taken.

The approach follows from this. The historian asks 'why then?', and a historian who asks that will look at the decision-makers and at the documents of diplomacy, and will be sceptical of explanations from large structures. The extract mentions the naval race and the press only to set them aside as 'machinery', and says nothing of the economy, empire or party politics, which fits an approach that explains events by the choices of individuals. It does not rank the powers or judge them, and sets out to explain. It is a mark of this approach that the final sentence puts the burden on the leaders, so that the system becomes the setting for choices and not their cause. No school's label fits the extract, because it neither returns to blaming Germany nor reduces its share, and the mark scheme credits labels only when the extract supports them."

*Commentary.* The first paragraph states the **whole** interpretation and both aspects, and says what it is not. Each aspect is explained with the extract's own words and tested with precise knowledge (dates, treaties, named crises, the Kaiser's proposal), including one place where the claim is stretched. The approach is tied to the interpretation, an omission is named, and the label is rightly refused. Every fact has a job. Table A Level 6 (19), Table B Level 6 (18): about 37 of 40. About 600 words, which is under two pages of handwriting and fits easily in 50 minutes.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
A student wrote this paragraph about Practice extract A:

"The historian says the alliances were not chains. This is true because Britain was not allied to France. Britain had the Entente Cordiale in 1904, which was a friendly agreement. So Britain did not have to join the war, but it did in 1914, after Germany invaded Belgium on 4 August. The historian therefore thinks Britain was to blame for making the war into a world war."

**The level.** Table A **Level 3** (9–11): it explains a **sub-message** (Britain's lack of commitment) and from it builds a **new interpretation** ("Britain was to blame") that the whole extract does not support: the extract names Vienna, Berlin and St Petersburg and does not blame Britain at all. Table B is Level 4 (12–14): the facts are accurate and relevant, but they are tested against the wrong claim.

**The fix.** First, say what the sub-message is for: "The historian argues that Britain was not bound to fight to show that the alliance system was not a chain, so that the war cannot be explained as the working of a machine." Then use the facts to test it: "The Entente Cordiale (1904) settled colonial disputes and Britain made no promise to fight, which supports the claim; and Britain entered on 4 August only after Germany invaded Belgium, which shows that a decision, not an automatic obligation, brought Britain in." Finally, say what the choice of this example shows about the approach: a historian who argues that decisions matter will choose a case in which the decision-makers were free. Now the paragraph explains and tests a claim that is part of the whole.
:::

:::eg Is the label earned?
Each extract below is a few lines written for this guide, with the label a student gave it. Decide whether the label is earned, and say why.

**First World War.** "For a generation after 1919 it was fashionable to say that nobody had willed the catastrophe. The documents tell another story. German leaders encouraged Austria-Hungary's attack on Serbia in July 1914 and accepted the risk of a continental war, because they hoped it would improve Germany's place among the powers." *Student's label: revisionist.*

*Verdict.* **Not earned.** The extract rejects the shared-responsibility view and puts the weight back on German leaders: that is **anti-revisionism**. "Revisionist" in the First World War debate means the reaction against blaming Germany. The student has reversed the label.

**The Holocaust.** "The killing did not follow a plan made in advance. Deportation to the east was the aim until the plans broke down. With the ghettos overcrowded and the eastern territories not ready, local officials improvised mass killing, and Berlin approved what was already happening." *Student's label: structuralist.*

*Verdict.* **Not earned.** The extract explains the killing as an improvised response to wartime obstacles: that is **functionalism**. Structuralism would point to the nature of the Nazi state and the competition among its leaders for Hitler's approval, and the extract says nothing of that.

**The Cold War.** "American leaders after 1945 wanted a world open to American trade and investment, and they used loans, the atomic monopoly and the Marshall Plan to build one. Soviet actions in Eastern Europe were responses, not plans: Stalin's aim was to make secure the frontier through which Germany had invaded twice." *Student's label: post-revisionist, because it mentions both sides.*

*Verdict.* **Not earned.** Mentioning both sides is not the same as sharing the blame. The extract makes American aims the driver and Soviet actions the reaction: that is **revisionism**. A post-revisionist extract would give real weight to the faults of both.
:::

## Timing

75 minutes for 40 marks. A suggested plan, which Cambridge does not set:

| Minutes | Task |
|---|---|
| 15 | Read the extract twice and mark it: claims, judgement words, contrast words, MAIN, SUPPORT or SUB beside each paragraph |
| 10 | Plan: the one-sentence interpretation; the plan by aspect; the knowledge for each aspect; the approach; check for a label |
| 50 | Write |
| **75** | **Total** |

If you finish early, read your answer once for one thing: does every paragraph say something about the **whole** extract, and does every fact do a job? Cut nothing; add the one missing sentence that links two aspects.

**How to build the answer.**
1. **First paragraph:** the whole interpretation in two or three sentences, with its two aspects. Say what it is arguing against.
2. **One paragraph for each aspect.** Start with the claim in your own words. Use two or three of the extract's phrases as evidence. Then use precise knowledge to **explain** and **test** the claim, and end by saying what it shows.
3. **A paragraph on the approach.** What does the historian ask? What evidence do they use? What do they leave out? What does it show about their interests or beliefs? Use the time and place of the historian if you know it from your debate chapters, and a label only if it is earned.
4. **A last sentence** that joins the approach to the interpretation: "A historian who asks ... will find ..."

Four to six paragraphs is typical. Length is not a measure. A top answer is the one that explains the whole extract and uses every fact.

## Common mistakes

These come from the descriptors: each is a feature that the mark scheme says does **not** earn a higher level.

:::warn Ten ways to lose marks on Paper 3
1. **Summary** (Table A Level 2). Following the extract line by line. Your paragraphs should follow the aspects of the interpretation.
2. **Sub-messages taken as the main message** (Levels 3 and 4). Check each against "would the interpretation change if this were deleted?".
3. **A different interpretation in each paragraph** (Level 3). Decide once, before you write, what the whole extract argues.
4. **Reading one part and ignoring the rest.** A sentence that sounds like another school does not make the whole one.
5. **Evaluating** the historian ("this is biased", "this is not convincing"). The question asks what you can learn.
6. **Guessing the author.** It earns no marks, and a stereotype hides what the words say.
7. **A label with no "because"**, or the wrong label. The mark scheme holds the answer back.
8. **Knowledge in a separate essay.** Pages of context that are not tied to a claim in the extract.
9. **Vague knowledge.** "A lot of treaties" or "around then" instead of names, dates and figures; Table B Level 6 needs detail and accuracy.
10. **No approach.** An answer that explains the interpretation well but never says what the historian asks, what evidence they use or what they leave out cannot reach Level 6.
:::

## Revising for Paper 3

:::steps
1. **Build the debate map.** On one page per topic: the schools or positions, their key works with dates, who each was replying to, and the time and place of writing.
2. **Make a facts checklist** like the lists above, and test yourself: can you give the date, the person and the one-line meaning for each?
3. **Practise extracts, not essays.** Use the practice extracts at the end of each debate chapter. For each, write the one-sentence interpretation and the two aspects in five minutes, before any writing.
4. **Write timed answers** of about 50 minutes. Mark yourself with Table A and Table B, and write down what would take each to the next level.
5. **Rewrite the weakest paragraph** at the next level up, with the descriptor open.
6. **Practise the approach paragraph** separately: ten extracts, each with only the approach paragraph written.
:::

:::recap
- Paper 3: **1 h 15 min, 40 marks**; one question from **one section** (your topic); one unseen extract of **up to 600 words**, author not named; booklet only. 20% of A Level; AO4 and AO1 each half.
- The question: "What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of ... to explain your answer. [40]"
- **Interpretation** = what the historian concludes. **Approach** = what they bring: questions, interests, beliefs. They are linked, and need not be separated.
- **Table A (AO4), out of 20:** Level 2 summarises; Level 3 explains only sub-messages; Level 4 identifies elements but misjudges their importance; Level 5 explains elements but not the whole; Level 6 explains all elements, as a whole, with the approach. **Table B (AO1), out of 20:** limited, some, generally, mostly, detailed and mostly accurate, detailed and entirely relevant.
- Both tables are added. Within a level: **just** (lowest mark), **adequately** (middle), **convincingly** (highest). Both at Level 6 is 36–40; both at Level 5 is 30–34.
- The main interpretation usually has **two aspects**. Find both, rank the messages, and plan by aspect.
- Labels are credited only when the extract earns them, and a wrong label holds an answer back. "Revisionist" means different things in the First World War and Cold War debates.
- Knowledge **explains** a claim, **tests** a claim, or **shows what the approach omits**. Padding earns nothing.
- Suggested timing: 15 min reading, 10 min planning, 50 min writing.
:::
