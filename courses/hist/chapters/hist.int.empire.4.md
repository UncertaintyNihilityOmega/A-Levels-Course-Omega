:::why
In 1890 the United States had a small navy, no colonies outside the continent (apart from Alaska), and a tradition, going back to Washington, of keeping out of the quarrels of other powers. By 1918 it ruled the Philippines, Puerto Rico and Hawaii, had dug a canal across Panama, had sent two million soldiers to France and was lending money to every Allied government. It had already been the largest industrial economy in the world for years. The question for this chapter is why a rich, self-satisfied country chose to use its power abroad, and why it did so at that moment: a good test of how to weigh economic causes, ideas, events and individuals against each other.
:::

## Changing attitudes to overseas expansion

Americans had always expanded: across the continent, by purchase (Louisiana, 1803; Alaska, 1867), by war (Mexico, 1846–48) and by settlement. The belief that this was their destiny had a name.

:::def Manifest Destiny
The mid-nineteenth-century belief that the United States was meant by Providence to spread across North America, bringing its institutions and way of life. By the 1890s some Americans used the same ideas to justify expansion overseas.
:::

Until the 1890s, however, expansion beyond the continent was rare. The government feared the cost of a large navy and army, the political danger of ruling people who could not become voting citizens, and the quarrels with European powers that empire would bring. Between 1865 and 1890 America's attention was on Reconstruction, railways, industry and settling the West. In the 1890s several things changed together.

### The closing of the frontier

In 1890 the Census Bureau reported that the unsettled area of the West had been so broken up by settlements that there was no longer a continuous frontier line. In July 1893 the historian **Frederick Jackson Turner** read a paper at the World's Columbian Exposition in Chicago, "The Significance of the Frontier in American History". He argued that the frontier had shaped the American character, making Americans individualistic, practical and democratic, and that its end was a turning point.

:::def Frontier thesis
Frederick Jackson Turner's argument (1893) that the existence of free land in the West, and the continual movement of settlers into it, had shaped American democracy and character. Its closing led many Americans to ask where the next frontier would be.
:::

Turner did not say that America must now take colonies, but others used his idea: if growth and opportunity had come from open land, the nation must find new outlets, and the obvious ones were overseas markets and Pacific islands. The impact of the closing frontier on foreign policy was above all psychological. It made expansion seem a necessity for national health, not just a choice.

### Economic growth, depression and the search for markets

By 1890 the USA was a giant. Its output of coal, iron and steel, and its railways, had grown faster than Britain's or Germany's. By 1913 it produced about a third of the world's manufactured goods (an estimate of the economic historian Paul Bairoch), more than any other state. This created two pressures. Businessmen and farmers produced more than the home market could buy, and the surplus needed foreign buyers. And the economy was unstable.

The **Panic of 1893** began with the collapse of the Philadelphia and Reading Railroad and a run on the gold reserve of the Treasury. It brought a deep depression that lasted to 1897: over 15,000 businesses and over 500 banks failed, and unemployment may have reached about one worker in five at its worst (estimates vary), with armies of the jobless marching on Washington in 1894. Many business leaders, farmers and politicians concluded that the answer was not just to cure the home economy but to find new markets abroad: in Latin America, in China (thought to hold hundreds of millions of customers) and in the islands that lay on the way to it. This is the economic argument for expansion, and it was widely believed at the time. The depression also gave the later war with Spain a certain glamour: it was a chance to shake off the gloom.

### Naval power and Mahan

In 1890 Captain **Alfred Thayer Mahan**, a naval officer who taught at the Naval War College, published *The Influence of Sea Power upon History, 1660–1783*. Using the history of Britain's rise, he argued that:
- national greatness depends on command of the sea, won by a battle fleet that can destroy the enemy's;
- sea power rests on trade, a merchant fleet and overseas bases and coaling stations;
- therefore a rising power needs a modern navy, colonies or bases, and a canal through the isthmus of Central America to link its two coasts.

His book was read by statesmen in Britain, Germany and Japan as well as the USA, and it matched what many Americans already believed. Mahan's friends, **Theodore Roosevelt** (Assistant Secretary of the Navy in 1897–98) and Senator **Henry Cabot Lodge**, urged a "large policy": a big navy, the annexation of Hawaii, a canal, bases in the Caribbean and influence in the Pacific. From 1883 Congress had begun to pay for steel warships, and the Naval Act of 1890 authorised the first modern battleships (*Indiana*, *Massachusetts* and *Oregon*). By 1898 the USA had a fleet of modern ships that could beat Spain's, even though it was still smaller than Britain's.

:::def Mahan's sea power
Alfred Thayer Mahan's argument, in *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890), that a great state needs a powerful navy, a merchant fleet and overseas bases, and that command of the sea decides the fate of nations.
:::

### Ideas of race and mission

Alongside economics and strategy there was a set of ideas. Many Americans accepted theories of racial hierarchy: the clergyman Josiah Strong, in *Our Country* (1885), described the "Anglo-Saxon race" as destined to civilise the world, and writers used Darwin's idea of the survival of the fittest to argue that strong nations must expand. Protestant churches sent missionaries overseas. These ideas gave expansion a moral look: it was a duty, not just an advantage. They also meant that most supporters of empire did not expect Filipinos, Cubans or Hawaiians to be equal citizens. Opponents used a different part of the American tradition, the belief in self-government.

### Hawaii and the early signs

Hawaii showed the new mood. American sugar planters had grown rich there, the USA had a treaty right (1887) to use Pearl Harbor, and in January 1893 the planters, backed by US Marines landed from a warship, overthrew **Queen Liliʻuokalani** and asked for annexation. President Cleveland, who thought the revolution unjust, refused to annex and withdrew the treaty. But the idea survived, and it was McKinley's government that annexed Hawaii in July 1898, on the grounds of naval need in the war.

In 1895 Cleveland also used the Monroe Doctrine (President Monroe's declaration of 1823 that Europe must not found new colonies or interfere in the Americas, in return for which the USA would stay out of Europe's quarrels) in a dispute between Venezuela and Britain over the border with British Guiana. His Secretary of State, Richard Olney, said the USA was "practically sovereign" on the continent, and Britain, after protest, agreed to arbitration. It was a sign that Britain would now yield to American claims in the Western Hemisphere, a vital background to the later canal and Caribbean policy.

:::key Why American attitudes to overseas expansion changed in the 1890s
1. **The frontier closed** (1890; Turner 1893), so growth had to find new outlets.
2. **Economic growth and the Panic of 1893** left a surplus of goods and a search for markets, above all in Asia and Latin America.
3. **Naval thinking** (Mahan) said that a great trading nation needed a fleet, bases and a canal.
4. **Ideas of race and mission** made expansion look like a duty.
5. **Leaders** such as Roosevelt, Lodge and, later, Beveridge pressed for a "large policy", and the example of the European scramble for Africa and Asia (see [Imperialism and world powers (1)](#hist.int.empire.1)) made America feel left behind.
The best answers rank these. Economic need and Mahan's strategy gave the practical case; race and mission supplied the moral case; the Spanish crisis supplied the opportunity.
:::

:::warn Do not say that the USA suddenly became imperialist in 1898
Americans had been expanding for a century, had taken Alaska, Midway and rights in Samoa and Hawaii before 1898, and had a plan for a navy by 1890. What changed in 1898 was that the USA took colonies *outside the continent*, with populations who were not going to become states. Write "1898 completed a shift that had begun in the 1880s and 1890s".
:::

## The Spanish–American War, 1898

### Why the USA went to war

Cuba was the last big colony of Spain in the Caribbean, ninety miles from Florida. Spain was a weak, declining power. Cuban rebels had fought for independence in 1868–78 and rose again in February 1895. The American government and public became involved for several reasons.

- **Sympathy and humanitarian feeling.** The rebels' war was fought by destroying plantations, and Spain's governor, General Valeriano Weyler, answered in 1896 with the policy of **reconcentration**: peasants were herded into camps in the towns, and disease and hunger killed very large numbers (estimates of Cuban deaths run from over 100,000 to several hundred thousand). American newspapers reported it with horror.
- **Yellow journalism.** Two New York rivals, Joseph Pulitzer's *World* and William Randolph Hearst's *Journal*, competed for readers with sensational stories and drawings of Spanish cruelty, some of them exaggerated or invented. They did not cause the war, but they heated public opinion in the weeks before it began.
- **Economic interests.** American investors had about 50 million dollars in Cuba, mainly in sugar and mining, and trade with the island had been worth far more before the rebellion. Business leaders at first opposed war, fearing disruption, but wanted the rebellion ended.
- **Strategic interests.** A Cuba in American hands, or at least friendly, would guard the route to a future canal, and American strategists such as Mahan wanted bases in the Caribbean.
- **Politics.** Congress and the press demanded action. McKinley, a veteran of the Civil War who hated war, resisted for over a year, but he could not ignore the pressure.

:::def Yellow journalism
The sensational, often exaggerated newspaper style of the late 1890s, especially in New York, which used large headlines, drawings and dramatic accounts of events such as the Cuban revolt to win readers. The name is thought to come from a cartoon strip, "The Yellow Kid".
:::

Two events in February 1898 pushed the crisis to war. On **9 February** Hearst's *Journal* printed a private letter written by the Spanish minister in Washington, Enrique Dupuy de Lôme, which had been stolen in Havana. It described President McKinley in insulting terms, and Spain's minister resigned. On **15 February** the American battleship USS *Maine*, which had been sent to Havana to protect American citizens, blew up in the harbour. About 260 of her crew of 355 died. The newspapers blamed Spain, and the slogan "Remember the Maine!" spread. A US Navy court of inquiry (the Sampson Board) reported in March that the ship had been destroyed by an external explosion, a mine, though it did not say who had laid it.

:::warn The Maine did not start the war alone
The explosion did not cause the war. It made war nearly unavoidable by turning public anger into a demand for action, but the pressure to intervene had been growing since 1895. Also, the Spanish government did not want war: it conceded nearly all of America's demands in April 1898 and agreed to an armistice in Cuba. Historians, using later studies, now think the cause of the explosion is uncertain. A private investigation in 1976 (the Rickover study) concluded that a fire in a coal bunker next to a magazine was the likely cause, and a 1998 study for the National Geographic Society found that the evidence fitted either cause. Do not write that Spain blew up the Maine.
:::

**Source A** is the heart of President McKinley's message to Congress on 11 April 1898. He did not ask for war, but for power to intervene.

:::note Source A: McKinley's four grounds for intervention
First. In the cause of humanity and to put an end to the barbarities, bloodshed, starvation, and horrible miseries now existing there. … Second. We owe it to our citizens in Cuba to afford them that protection and indemnity for life and property which no government there can or will afford. … Third. The right to intervene may be justified by the very serious injury to the commerce, trade, and business of our people and by the wanton destruction of property and devastation of the island. Fourth, and which is of the utmost importance. The present condition of affairs in Cuba is a constant menace to our peace and entails upon this Government an enormous expense.

*Shortened from the message of President William McKinley to Congress, 11 April 1898. The sentence from which the first ground is taken is cut short, and the second and third grounds are quoted in full.*
:::

McKinley's list shows the mix of motives: humanity, protection of citizens, commerce, and security. He ended with the words: "In the name of humanity, in the name of civilization, in behalf of endangered American interests which give us the right and the duty to speak and to act, the war in Cuba must stop." Congress responded with a joint resolution on 19–20 April that demanded Spain's withdrawal and authorised the use of force. It included the **Teller Amendment**, proposed by Senator Henry Teller of Colorado, which disclaimed any intention "to exercise sovereignty, jurisdiction, or control over said Island except for the pacification thereof", and promised to leave Cuba "to its people". Spain broke off relations, and Congress declared war on **25 April**, retroactive to 21 April.

The Teller Amendment is a useful clue for essays. It shows that most Americans did not want to annex Cuba. The sequel in the Philippines is a clue that the war's results went beyond its stated aims.

:::key Why the USA went to war with Spain in 1898
1. **Humanitarian outrage** at Spanish rule and Weyler's reconcentration.
2. **The press**: yellow journalism roused opinion.
3. **Economic interests** in Cuba and the wish for stability in a nearby market.
4. **Strategic ambition**: the wish to remove a European power from the Caribbean and secure the route for a canal.
5. **The Maine and the de Lôme letter**, which turned feeling into political pressure McKinley could not resist.
Most historians treat 1 and 2 as the means by which opinion was moved, 3 and 4 as the longer-term interests, and 5 as the immediate triggers.
:::

### The war and the Treaty of Paris

The war was short. On **1 May 1898** Commodore **George Dewey**'s squadron destroyed the old Spanish Pacific fleet in Manila Bay, in the Philippines, without losing a ship. On 1 July American troops, including Theodore Roosevelt's volunteer cavalry (the "Rough Riders"), took San Juan Heights outside Santiago de Cuba, and on 3 July the Spanish Atlantic squadron was destroyed trying to leave the harbour. American forces landed in Puerto Rico in July, and Spain asked for an armistice, which was signed on 12 August. John Hay, then American ambassador in London and soon to be Secretary of State, called it "a splendid little war". For the USA the cost in battle was small: 369 soldiers, 10 sailors and 6 marines were killed, but 2,061 died of disease, and public criticism of the Army's supply and medical services was fierce.

The **Treaty of Paris** (10 December 1898) ended the war:
- Spain gave up its claim to **Cuba**.
- Spain ceded **Puerto Rico** and **Guam** (the island chosen as a coaling station on the way to the Philippines).
- Spain ceded the **Philippines** in return for 20 million dollars.
The Senate approved it on 6 February 1899 by 57 votes to 27, just above the two-thirds majority needed (the Constitution requires two-thirds of the Senate to approve a treaty). In the Pacific, Hawaii was annexed by a joint resolution (the Newlands Resolution, which needed only a simple majority in each house of Congress, so it avoided the two-thirds Senate vote that a treaty would have required) signed on 7 July 1898 as a war measure, and the USA took possession of Wake Island in 1899 and secured Tutuila, with its harbour at Pago Pago, in American Samoa by a treaty of 1899 with Britain and Germany.

### Cuba and the Platt Amendment

The Teller Amendment prevented annexation, but the USA kept its troops in Cuba until 1902 and made sure that Cuba would not be fully independent. In 1901 Congress passed the **Platt Amendment** as part of an Army appropriations bill, and the USA made the Cubans write it into their own constitution as the price of the troops' withdrawal. Article III gave the USA "the right to intervene for the preservation of Cuban independence, the maintenance of a government adequate for the protection of life, property, and individual liberty". Cuba also promised not to make treaties with other powers that impaired its independence or to take on unpayable debts, and it leased naval stations (including Guantánamo Bay, 1903) to the USA. The USA intervened in 1906–09 and again later. The amendment lasted until 1934.

### Impact on US power and on international relations

The war was the turning point in the emergence of the USA as a world power for several reasons:
- It gave the USA an overseas empire with bases in both oceans, which needed a bigger navy to protect it.
- It showed European powers that the USA could win a war away from home, against a European state, with a modern navy. Britain, which was friendly, saw the USA as a useful balance to Germany and Russia.
- It increased American interest in China: John Hay's **Open Door Notes** (September 1899 and July 1900) asked the powers to keep equal trading rights and to respect China's territory.
- It started a debate at home about whether the USA should rule other peoples at all, which broke out into the Philippine war.
- It ended Spain as a colonial power in the Americas, and left the USA as the dominant power in the Caribbean.

## The Philippine–American War, 1899–1902

### Why the USA kept the Philippines

The Philippines had not been the cause of the war with Spain. Dewey's victory at Manila Bay was meant to destroy a Spanish fleet, and in 1898 few Americans knew where the islands were. But once Dewey held the bay and American troops had taken Manila (13 August 1898), the question could not be avoided. What should be done with 7,000 islands and some seven million people?

McKinley and his advisers considered the options: giving the islands back to Spain (dishonourable, they thought), handing them to another power (Germany had ships at Manila, and Japan was a rising rival) or leaving them to the Filipinos (who they thought incapable of governing themselves). The remaining option was to keep them. The reasons were mixed:
- **Strategy.** Manila was a naval base and coaling station 600 miles from China. Mahan's followers argued that the USA needed a Pacific base. Hawaii and Guam were stepping stones.
- **Commerce.** The islands were a source of hemp, sugar and tobacco and a gateway to the "China market". Businessmen and the press spoke of "markets for what our farmers raise".
- **Fear of rivals.** If the USA left, Germany or Japan would take the islands, shutting the Open Door.
- **Mission and race.** Many Americans, and later the President himself, spoke of a duty to educate and civilise the Filipinos, whom they believed unfit for self-rule. Protestant churches saw an opportunity for missions, though most Filipinos were already Catholic.
- **Prestige.** European powers had colonies in Asia, and the USA would not be a lesser power.

**Source B** gives the case for expansion in the words of a leading imperialist, the Indiana lawyer Albert Beveridge, speaking during the election campaign of September 1898.

:::note Source B: Beveridge, "The March of the Flag"
Therefore, in this campaign, the question is larger than a party question. It is an American question. It is a world question. Shall the American people continue their march toward the commercial supremacy of the world? … Hawaii is ours; Porto Rico is to be ours; at the prayer of her people Cuba finally will be ours; in the islands of the East, even to the gates of Asia, coaling stations are to be ours at the very least; the flag of a liberal government is to float over the Philippines … Therefore we must find new markets for our produce.

*Shortened from the speech by Albert J. Beveridge, "The March of the Flag", at Indianapolis, 16 September 1898 (text as printed on Wikisource). Beveridge was campaigning for Republican candidates in 1898 and became a US senator in 1899.*
:::

The speech is a good example of mixed arguments: mission and "destiny", but also "commercial supremacy", "markets" and "coaling stations". Beveridge was a politician in a campaign, so he exaggerates; but Senators such as Lodge, businessmen and the press expressed the same ideas.

The President's own position was more guarded. He sent a proclamation to the commander at Manila on 21 December 1898 that spoke of "benevolent assimilation".

:::note Source C: the Benevolent Assimilation proclamation
… it should be the earnest wish and paramount aim of the military administration to win the confidence, respect, and affection of the inhabitants of the Philippines by assuring to them in every possible way that full measure of individual rights and liberties which is the heritage of free peoples, and by proving to them that the mission of the United States is one of benevolent assimilation substituting the mild sway of justice and right for arbitrary rule.

*Adapted from President McKinley's "Benevolent Assimilation" proclamation, in the form sent through the Secretary of War to the military governor in Manila, 21 December 1898; the opening words are left out. General Otis in Manila published a softened version on 4 January 1899.*
:::

### The opposition: the Anti-Imperialist League

Not everyone agreed. The **Anti-Imperialist League** was founded in Boston in November 1898 and grew to include the steel magnate Andrew Carnegie, the philosopher William James, the writer Mark Twain, the reformer Jane Addams, the labour leader Samuel Gompers and Senator George Hoar of Massachusetts. In October 1899 a Chicago conference adopted a national platform.

:::note Source D: the Anti-Imperialist League's platform
We hold that the policy known as imperialism is hostile to liberty and tends toward militarism, an evil from which it has been our glory to be free. … We maintain that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed. We insist that the subjugation of any people is "criminal aggression" and open disloyalty to the distinctive principles of our Government. … A self-governing state cannot accept sovereignty over an unwilling people. The United States cannot act upon the ancient heresy that might makes right.

*Shortened from the platform of the American Anti-Imperialist League, adopted at the Chicago convention, October 1899 (printed in the Speeches, Correspondence and Political Papers of Carl Schurz, volume 6).*
:::

The opposition's arguments were:
- **Principle:** the Declaration of Independence says that governments rest on "the consent of the governed". To rule the Philippines was to be what America had rebelled against.
- **Danger at home:** empire needed a large army and navy and corrupted democracy ("the real firing line is not in the suburbs of Manila").
- **Race and labour:** some opponents, including Gompers and southern Democrats, did not want Filipino workers or products to compete, or non-white peoples to become American citizens. Opposition was therefore not all idealistic.
- **Cost and risk:** the islands would draw the USA into Asian quarrels.
The League failed to stop the treaty: William Jennings Bryan, the Democratic leader, urged his party to vote for the treaty to end the war and to deal with the Philippines afterwards, which helped it pass by one vote more than the two-thirds required.

The *Puck* cartoon "School Begins" (see the worked example below) and Rudyard Kipling's poem "The White Man's Burden" (published in the USA in February 1899, subtitled "The United States and the Philippine Islands") are two contemporary views on the same event: the first mocks the unhappy "pupils" of Uncle Sam's school, the second urges Americans to take up the burden of ruling "new-caught, sullen peoples, half-devil and half-child".

### The war

The Filipinos had their own independence movement. Filipino revolutionaries had fought Spain since 1896, and their leader **Emilio Aguinaldo** had proclaimed independence on 12 June 1898, after returning to the islands on an American ship. When the Treaty of Paris transferred the islands to the USA without consulting him, Aguinaldo's government at Malolos adopted a constitution (January 1899) and Aguinaldo became president of the First Philippine Republic. Two armies faced each other in Manila. On the night of **4 February 1899** an American sentry shot a Filipino soldier, and fighting began. Two days later the Senate ratified the treaty.

The war had two phases.
- **Conventional war** (February–November 1899). The Filipino army was beaten in open battle; the American forces took Malolos on 31 March 1899. Aguinaldo's government fled.
- **Guerrilla war** (November 1899–1902). Filipino forces split into small groups, and the Americans, with up to about 70,000 troops at peak, had to hold hundreds of garrisons. The American Army's response became harsher: burning villages, forcing civilians into zones near towns (reconcentration, the same policy that had been condemned in Cuba), and the use of the "water cure", a form of torture, to get information. After a surprise attack at **Balangiga** on Samar on 28 September 1901 killed 48 American soldiers, General Jacob Smith's harsh campaign on Samar (he was reported to have told his men to kill and burn) led to his court-martial in 1902; he was admonished and retired.
- Aguinaldo was captured on 23 March 1901 by a trick led by General Frederick Funston. He took an oath to the USA and urged his followers to give up. Fighting went on in some provinces until General Miguel Malvar surrendered in April 1902. On **4 July 1902** President Roosevelt declared the "insurrection" over. Fighting with the Muslim Moro peoples of the south continued for more than ten years.

The Senate held hearings on the conduct of the war in 1902. They exposed the "water cure" and other abuses; some soldiers were punished, but few officers.

:::def Philippine–American War
The war of 1899–1902 between the USA and the First Philippine Republic, begun on 4 February 1899, and fought mainly as a guerrilla war after November 1899. The USA called it an "insurrection"; Filipinos call it a war of independence.
:::

**The cost.** The numbers must be read carefully. About 1,020 American soldiers were killed in action and about 3,176 died of disease. The estimates of Filipino fighters killed range from about 16,000 to 20,000. The estimates of Filipino civilian deaths, most of them from famine and disease that followed the fighting and the reconcentration, run from about 200,000 to 250,000 in the figures most historians now use, with some older estimates reaching a million. Treat all these figures as uncertain, and say so in an exam answer.

### The results

- The USA ruled the Philippines as a colony. William Howard Taft headed a civil commission and became the first civil governor in July 1901. The Philippine Organic Act (1902) set up an elected assembly in 1907, and the Jones Act (1916) promised eventual independence, which came in 1946.
- The USA built schools, roads and health services, and trained Filipino officials; it also kept strict control and favoured the landowning elite.
- The war divided America. It raised a lasting argument about whether the USA should be an imperial power. After 1902 there was little appetite for more colonies, and later American policy relied on influence, loans and occasional force rather than annexation.
- Controversy over motives and actions has never ended. For Filipino historians the war was the loss of a republic. In the USA the debate is between those who saw a well-meant, if flawed, "civilising mission" and those who see a war of conquest driven by markets and racism.

:::key The Philippine–American War: the main points
1. **Why the USA kept the islands:** strategy, trade with China, fear of rivals, the belief in a duty to "civilise", prestige.
2. **Why Filipinos resisted:** Filipino revolutionaries had fought Spain since 1896, Aguinaldo had declared independence on 12 June 1898, and by January 1899 a Filipino republic existed, so they saw the Americans as a new ruler replacing Spain.
3. **How it was fought:** a short conventional phase, then a long guerrilla war with harsh counter-insurgency by the Army.
4. **The controversy:** the Anti-Imperialist League, the 1902 hearings, and disagreement about motives and conduct.
5. **The effect:** an American colony in Asia, a lasting debate about empire, and a stake in East Asia that drew the USA into later disputes with Japan.
:::

:::warn Be careful with numbers and words
Do not write that "millions" died, or that the war was only an "insurrection". Say "most historians estimate 200,000 or more Filipino civilian deaths, most through disease and famine", and note that the USA's name for it (insurrection) and the Filipino name (a war) reflect different views. Write about the abuses factually and without graphic detail.
:::

## The Caribbean, Panama and the Pacific, 1899–1914

### Why a canal, and how Panama came about

The war of 1898 gave the case for a canal across Central America a famous example. The battleship USS *Oregon* took 66 days to steam from San Francisco round South America to join the fleet in the Caribbean. Mahan, Roosevelt and the navy said a canal would let the fleet move quickly between oceans, and commerce would benefit.

Two obstacles had to be removed. The first was British: the Clayton–Bulwer Treaty of 1850 said that neither country would control a canal alone. Britain, wishing to keep American goodwill (its attention was on Germany and South Africa), agreed in the **Hay–Pauncefote Treaty** (1901) that the USA could build and fortify a canal. The second was where to build it. A French company had failed in Panama in the 1880s (disease killed thousands), and the USA first looked at Nicaragua. In the end it chose Panama, then a province of Colombia.

In January 1903 the Hay–Herrán Treaty offered Colombia 10 million dollars and 250,000 dollars a year for a canal zone, but the Colombian Senate rejected it in August, hoping for better terms. Theodore Roosevelt was furious. In November 1903 a revolt in Panama, planned with the help of a French engineer, Philippe Bunau-Varilla, and protected by the arrival of the US warship *Nashville*, which stopped Colombian troops from landing, declared Panama independent on **3 November 1903**. The USA recognised the new state three days later. On **18 November 1903** Bunau-Varilla, acting for Panama though he was not a Panamanian, signed the **Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty**: the USA got a Canal Zone about ten miles wide "in perpetuity", for 10 million dollars and 250,000 dollars a year, and the right to intervene to keep order. Colombia and most Latin Americans regarded it as theft; Roosevelt later said, "I took the Canal Zone". In 1921 the USA ratified a treaty to pay Colombia 25 million dollars to repair relations.

Construction began in 1904. The chief engineer John Stevens, then George Goethals, dug the Culebra Cut and built the locks and a great dam, while Colonel William Gorgas largely eliminated yellow fever and controlled malaria in the Zone. About 5,600 workers, mostly West Indian labourers, died of disease and accidents in the American phase. The canal opened on **15 August 1914**.

### The Roosevelt Corollary and the Caribbean

The Monroe Doctrine (1823) had warned European powers not to colonise the Americas. In 1902–03, Britain, Germany and Italy blockaded Venezuela to force it to pay its debts. Roosevelt feared that European creditors would use debt as an excuse to seize ports near the canal. When the Dominican Republic fell into debt too, he announced a new policy in his annual message of **6 December 1904**, which became known as the **Roosevelt Corollary**.

:::note Source E: the Roosevelt Corollary
Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society, may in America, as elsewhere, ultimately require intervention by some civilized nation, and in the Western Hemisphere the adherence of the United States to the Monroe Doctrine may force the United States, however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power.

*From the annual message of President Theodore Roosevelt to Congress, 6 December 1904.*
:::

The Monroe Doctrine had said "Europe, keep out". The Corollary said that the USA itself had the right to intervene to put things in order, so that Europe had no excuse. In 1905 the USA took over the collection of the Dominican customs revenue, paying out a share to creditors (a **customs receivership**), and set up a treaty in 1907. Roosevelt summed up his style in his motto, "Speak softly and carry a big stick", which he used in 1901 and described as a West African proverb. American Marines and officials went into Cuba (1906–09), Nicaragua (1909–10 and from 1912 to 1933), Haiti (1915–34) and the Dominican Republic (1916–24).

### Taft and Dollar Diplomacy

President **William Howard Taft** (1909–13) and his Secretary of State Philander Knox wanted American influence to rest on loans and investment rather than on Marines. This was **Dollar Diplomacy**: American bankers would lend to poor, unstable governments in Central America and China in return for control of their customs and railways, so that other powers had no reason to interfere. Taft defended it in his annual message of 3 December 1912.

:::note Source F: Dollar Diplomacy
The diplomacy of the present administration has sought to respond to modern ideas of commercial intercourse. This policy has been characterized as substituting dollars for bullets. It is one that appeals alike to idealistic humanitarian sentiments, to the dictates of sound policy and strategy, and to legitimate commercial aims.

*From the annual message of President William Howard Taft to Congress, 3 December 1912.*
:::

The results were mixed. In Nicaragua the policy produced American loans and control of customs, and when Nicaraguans rebelled in 1912 Taft sent in the Marines anyway. In China, American bankers joined a group of European banks to lend for railways, but the policy was cancelled by Wilson in 1913. Latin Americans resented it. Under Wilson, intervention became, if anything, more frequent (Veracruz in Mexico in 1914, Haiti in 1915, the Dominican Republic in 1916), which suggests that economic and military means worked together.

:::def Dollar Diplomacy
The policy of the Taft administration (1909–13) of using American loans and investment, backed by the government, to extend American influence abroad, especially in Central America and China, in place of military action.
:::

### Show of strength: the Great White Fleet

On **16 December 1907** President Roosevelt sent sixteen battleships of the Atlantic Fleet, painted white, on a voyage round the world. They went round South America to California, across the Pacific to Australia, Japan (Yokohama, October 1908) and Asia, then home by the Suez Canal, and returned to Hampton Roads on **22 February 1909**, after covering about 43,000 nautical miles. The cruise had practical goals: to test the fleet, to show Japan after the tension over immigration that America would not be pushed out of the Pacific, and to tell the world that the USA was now a naval power. It was also a great popular success. At a time of friction, the Japanese welcomed the fleet warmly, which helped to ease the dispute.

:::key The American position in the Western Hemisphere by 1914
1. **The Caribbean became an "American lake"**: Puerto Rico, Cuba (the Platt Amendment), the Dominican Republic and Haiti (customs control), the canal.
2. **The Panama Canal** gave the navy a two-ocean fleet and made the USA the pivot of world trade.
3. **The Roosevelt Corollary** replaced the idea that Europe must keep out with the claim that the USA must police the hemisphere.
4. **Dollar Diplomacy** added economic control to military power.
5. **The price** was lasting suspicion in Latin America.
:::

## The USA and the First World War, 1914–18

### Neutrality and the pull of the Allies

When war broke out in Europe in August 1914, President **Woodrow Wilson** proclaimed neutrality. On 19 August he asked Americans to be "impartial in thought as well as in action". The country was a mixture. Many Americans had German or Irish ancestry and distrusted Britain; many others, especially in the east and in the government, felt close to Britain and France by language, law and history. But within a few months the war began to bind the USA to the Allies in three ways.

- **Trade and loans.** The British navy controlled the Atlantic and blockaded Germany, so American firms sold to the Allies and, in effect, only to them. US exports to the belligerents (the countries at war) rose from 824.8 million dollars in 1913 to 2.25 billion dollars in 1917. Loans followed: J.P. Morgan and Company became the Allies' banker, lending first to France (1915) and then to Britain. A ban on loans to the belligerents, imposed by Bryan as Secretary of State, was dropped in practice. Germany's trade with the USA was stopped by the blockade. Americans who had made money out of the war had an interest in an Allied victory (some critics later said this explained American entry, a view that most historians reject as too simple).
- **British interference with American ships.** Britain stopped and searched American ships and put American goods on a blacklist. Wilson protested, but he did not threaten Britain, since Britain's actions cost money, while Germany's cost lives.
- **Propaganda and sabotage.** British propaganda, such as the stories of German atrocities in Belgium, was effective in America, while German agents blew up a munitions depot at Black Tom, New Jersey, in July 1916.

### The submarine

Germany's reply to the blockade was the submarine. Its use against merchant ships broke the old rules of the sea that required a warning and the rescue of crews. The sequence matters.
- **7 May 1915.** A German submarine sank the British liner *Lusitania* off the coast of Ireland. About 1,195 people died, 128 of them Americans. The ship carried some munitions, which Germany used to justify the attack, but the loss of civilian life shocked America. Wilson sent strong notes, and the Secretary of State William Jennings Bryan resigned in June 1915 because he thought the notes too hostile. Germany promised in September 1915 (after the sinking of the *Arabic*) not to sink liners without warning.
- **24 March 1916.** The *Sussex*, a French cross-Channel ferry, was torpedoed with Americans aboard. Wilson threatened to break relations, and on 4 May 1916 Germany made the **Sussex pledge**: to spare merchant ships without warning, provided that Britain obeyed international law as well. Wilson ran for re-election in 1916 on the slogan "He kept us out of war" and won narrowly.
- **22 January 1917.** Wilson asked the belligerents for a "peace without victory". The German government, however, had already decided that it could win only by cutting Britain's supplies.
- **31 January 1917.** Germany announced **unrestricted submarine warfare** from 1 February, accepting that this would probably mean war with the USA. Wilson broke off relations on 3 February. Between 3 February and 4 April, German submarines sank ten American merchant ships. After three more were sunk in mid-March, public opinion in the east and the south moved decisively for war.

### The Zimmermann telegram

British naval intelligence (Room 40, the code-breaking office of the British Admiralty) had broken the German diplomatic code. On 16 January 1917 the German Foreign Secretary, Arthur Zimmermann, had sent a coded telegram to the German minister in Mexico. It was intercepted, decoded and shown to the Americans on 24 February, and published in the American press on **1 March 1917**. **Source G** is the key passage.

:::note Source G: the Zimmermann telegram
We intend to begin on the first of February unrestricted submarine warfare. We shall endeavor in spite of this to keep the United States of America neutral. In the event of this not succeeding, we make Mexico a proposal or alliance on the following basis: make war together, make peace together, generous financial support and an understanding on our part that Mexico is to reconquer the lost territory in Texas, New Mexico, and Arizona.

*From the decoded English text of the telegram sent by Arthur Zimmermann, German Foreign Secretary, to the German minister in Mexico, 16 January 1917, as printed by the US National Archives (the later parts about Japan are left out).*
:::

The effect was great. Americans in the south-west, who might have been neutral, were angered, and the telegram showed that Germany would bring the war to America's doorstep. Zimmermann admitted that it was genuine. Mexico, in the middle of its own revolution, did not take up the offer. The telegram did not cause war on its own, but it removed doubts in the west and south, and showed that the submarine campaign was not Germany's only hostile act.

### Wilson's policy and the opponents of war

Wilson's policy was cautious. He wanted to keep the USA out, to mediate and to shape the peace, and he distrusted both the Allies and the war itself. He moved by stages: from neutrality to "preparedness" (the National Defense Act and a big Navy programme of 1916), and then, when Germany returned to unrestricted submarine warfare, to the view that neutrality could no longer protect American rights.

Pressure to stay out came from many sources:
- **Peace groups.** The Woman's Peace Party (January 1915, led by Jane Addams), the American Union Against Militarism, and Henry Ford's "Peace Ship" of December 1915.
- **Political opponents.** William Jennings Bryan; Progressive senators such as Robert La Follette of Wisconsin and George Norris of Nebraska, who feared that bankers and arms makers were pushing the nation into war; the Socialist Party, which condemned the war at St Louis in April 1917; German- and Irish-Americans; and many farmers in the Midwest.
- **Public opinion.** Opinion was against being dragged into a European quarrel, and Wilson's slogan of 1916 expressed it.

On the other side, the "preparedness" movement (Theodore Roosevelt, General Leonard Wood) argued for a stronger army and for helping the Allies.

On **2 April 1917** Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war. His speech combined the immediate cause, the submarine, with a larger aim.

:::note Source H: Wilson's war message
The present German submarine warfare against commerce is a warfare against mankind. It is a war against all nations. … The world must be made safe for democracy. Its peace must be planted upon the tested foundations of political liberty. We have no selfish ends to serve. We desire no conquest, no dominion.

*Shortened from the address of President Woodrow Wilson to a joint session of Congress, 2 April 1917 (Senate Document 5, 65th Congress, 1st session).*
:::

The Senate voted for war by 82 to 6 (4 April) and the House by 373 to 50 (6 April). Among the votes against was that of Jeannette Rankin of Montana, the first woman in Congress. The USA became an "Associated Power" and not a formal ally of Britain and France, to keep freedom of action.

### Why did the USA enter the war?

| Cause | Evidence | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| German submarine warfare | The *Lusitania* (1915), the *Sussex* (1916), the announcement of 31 January 1917, ten American ships sunk | The immediate cause; without it Wilson would probably not have asked for war |
| Links with the Allies | Trade (824.8 million dollars to 2.25 billion), loans (Morgan), shared culture and law | Made a German victory seem dangerous to the USA and a neutral stance hard to keep |
| German hostility | The Zimmermann telegram, sabotage at Black Tom | Removed doubts, especially in the south and west |
| Wilson's ideals | A league of nations, a "peace without victory", "make the world safe for democracy" | Gave a larger purpose after the decision, and shaped his war aims |
| The Russian Revolution | The Tsar's fall in March 1917 meant the Allies were no longer led by an autocracy | Made the "war for democracy" speech easier to make |

:::key Why the USA entered the First World War
1. **Unrestricted submarine warfare** was the immediate cause: it killed Americans, threatened trade and left Wilson with no way to defend neutral rights.
2. **Economic and cultural ties with Britain and France** had made the USA a partner in all but name; a defeat of the Allies would have cost America its loans and trade.
3. **The Zimmermann telegram** showed German hostility and angered the south and west.
4. **Wilson's war aims** gave the decision a purpose, which allowed him to carry the nation.
The best answers separate the *immediate cause* (the submarine) from the *background* (trade, loans, sympathy), and avoid claiming that either alone explains it.
:::

### The impact of US intervention

- **Finance and supply.** The USA lent about 10 billion dollars to the Allies, through the Liberty Loan campaigns, and its food, steel and shipping filled the Allies' needs.
- **The navy.** American destroyers joined anti-submarine patrols from Queenstown in Ireland in May 1917, and the convoy system (adopted in 1917) cut shipping losses.
- **The army.** The Selective Service Act (18 May 1917) began conscription. The American Expeditionary Forces under General **John J. Pershing** reached France in June 1917, but had little combat until 1918. By the armistice about two million Americans were in France. American forces took part at Cantigny (May 1918), Belleau Wood and Château-Thierry (June 1918), and the Meuse–Argonne offensive (26 September – 11 November 1918). About 53,000 American soldiers were killed in battle, and about 63,000 died of disease, mostly of influenza.
- **Morale and the balance of power.** In spring 1918 Germany, having defeated Russia, made its last great offensive in the west. The arrival of fresh American troops, whom the Germans could not match, helped tip the balance, and the Allies counter-attacked from July.
- **Peace aims.** On 8 January 1918 Wilson set out the **Fourteen Points**, which included free trade, open diplomacy, self-determination and a League of Nations. At Paris in 1919 he was the most powerful figure at the conference. But the Senate rejected the Treaty of Versailles (19 November 1919 and 19 March 1920) and the USA did not join the League.

The impact was great but ambiguous. By 1918 the USA was the world's chief creditor and its greatest economic power, and the war had ended the old Europe-centred order. But the American people, who had fought for an ideal, did not want to take up a permanent role in European affairs. The USA was a world power in strength, but not yet in its own opinion.

## Why did the USA emerge as a world power? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | Rapid economic growth; continental size and population; the end of the frontier; ideas of mission and race | Gave the USA the means, and a motive to look for outlets |
| Medium-term | Panic of 1893 and the search for markets; Mahan and the new navy; the "large policy" of Roosevelt and Lodge | Turned economic power into a plan for overseas power |
| Short-term | Cuba's revolt; the press; the *Maine*; Dewey's victory | Provided the opportunity and the decision of 1898 |
| Opportunity | Spain's weakness; Britain's goodwill after 1895; European rivalries; the First World War | Meant there was little to stop the USA |

:::key A judgement you can defend
**Economic strength** was the necessary cause: without the world's largest economy the USA could not have built a navy or lent to the Allies. But it was not sufficient, because the USA had been rich for years without acting abroad. What turned wealth into power was the *decision* to use it: the shift in ideas and strategy in the 1890s, the opportunity of the war with Spain and, in 1917, the German submarine. The war with Spain was the turning point in the change of attitude; the First World War was the proof of what the USA could do.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **William Appleman Williams**, *The Tragedy of American Diplomacy* (1959), argued that American leaders sought overseas markets and an "open door" for American trade as the answer to the nation's economic problems, and that this drove policy from the 1890s.
- **Walter LaFeber**, *The New Empire* (1963), developed the economic interpretation: the depressions of the 1870s and 1890s convinced American leaders that the country needed foreign markets, and 1898 was the result.
- **Paul Kramer**, *The Blood of Government* (2006), studied the Philippines and argued that ideas of race, and the dealings between Americans and Filipinos, shaped the colonial state and the way the USA dealt with the rest of the world.
For AS, you can use this to show that the causes of expansion are weighed differently: economics (Williams, LaFeber) or race and culture (Kramer). Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the USA went to war with Spain in 1898.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The USA went to war with Spain because of Cuba. The Cubans were rebelling against Spain and Spain was cruel to them. The newspapers wrote about it. The American ship the Maine blew up in Havana harbour in February 1898 and the Americans blamed Spain. Congress declared war in April."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes. The newspapers and the *Maine* are mentioned but not explained, and no sentence shows how one thing led to another. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was American feeling about Cuba. After the revolt of 1895 General Weyler's policy of reconcentration herded peasants into camps where very many died of hunger and disease, and the American press reported it with horror. Because the papers competed for readers (Hearst's *Journal* against Pulitzer's *World*), they printed sensational and sometimes exaggerated stories, and public opinion pressed Congress and the President to act. A second reason was American interests. American investors had about 50 million dollars in Cuban sugar and mines, and the island lay near the route to a future canal, so the USA wanted a stable Cuba that was not under Spain. President McKinley named the harm to 'the commerce, trade, and business of our people' as one reason for intervening."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was the sequence of events in February 1898. On 9 February the *Journal* published the de Lôme letter, in which the Spanish minister insulted McKinley. Six days later the battleship *Maine* blew up in Havana harbour, killing about 260 of her crew. The Navy's inquiry reported an external explosion, and 'Remember the Maine!' became a slogan. No one knew who was responsible, but the incident turned an argument about humanity into a demand for revenge and made it politically impossible for McKinley to resist.

These factors were linked. The suffering in Cuba, magnified by the press, created the public demand; American economic and strategic interests meant that the Government had a stake in ending the war on the island; and the events of February provided the trigger that overcame McKinley's reluctance. The decisive reason was the pressure of public opinion, since McKinley was cautious and Spain had conceded most of America's demands by April, so war was not inevitable on the facts of Cuba alone. The other factors explain why opinion was so strong and why Congress, in the Teller Amendment, was willing to go to war without any plan to annex Cuba."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the feeling and the interests prepared the ground, and the events triggered it), and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

Chronic wrongdoing, or an impotence which results in a general loosening of the ties of civilized society, may in America, as elsewhere, ultimately require intervention by some civilized nation, and in the Western Hemisphere the adherence of the United States to the Monroe Doctrine may force the United States, however reluctantly, in flagrant cases of such wrongdoing or impotence, to the exercise of an international police power.

*From the annual message of President Theodore Roosevelt to Congress, 6 December 1904.*

**Source B**

The diplomacy of the present administration has sought to respond to modern ideas of commercial intercourse. This policy has been characterized as substituting dollars for bullets. It is one that appeals alike to idealistic humanitarian sentiments, to the dictates of sound policy and strategy, and to legitimate commercial aims.

*From the annual message of President William Howard Taft to Congress, 3 December 1912.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about how the USA used its power in the Caribbean and Central America.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is from Roosevelt and says that the USA will act as a police power. Source B is from Taft and says that the USA will use dollars not bullets. They are different because one uses force and the other uses money. Both are from presidents so they are reliable."

*Commentary.* One difference, stated but not supported by words from the sources or explained. No similarity. The comment on reliability is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources agree that the USA has a right and a duty to shape events in its neighbourhood. Source A claims 'an international police power' to deal with 'chronic wrongdoing'; Source B claims that its policy 'appeals alike to idealistic humanitarian sentiments' as well as to 'sound policy and strategy'. American exports to the belligerents had risen from 824.8 million dollars in 1913 to 2.25 billion in 1917, and the Allies had borrowed heavily from American banks, led by J.P. The fall of the Tsar in March 1917 made it possible to describe the war as one for democracy.

Overall, German submarine warfare was the main reason for the timing and the decision, since without the announcement of 31 January Wilson would probably have remained neutral, and he said so himself in explaining the war. :::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the sinking of the Maine the main cause of the Spanish–American War?" [20]

A student wrote:

"On 15 February 1898 the American battleship Maine blew up in Havana harbour. Two hundred and sixty of the crew died. It was a big ship and it had been sent to protect American citizens. A court of inquiry said that it was a mine. The newspapers said 'Remember the Maine' and the Americans were very angry. Then the Americans declared war on 25 April and they won easily. Dewey destroyed the Spanish fleet at Manila Bay and the Americans took Cuba, Puerto Rico and the Philippines."

**The level.** The facts are accurate, but the paragraph is **narrative**: it tells the story of the *Maine* and of the war, without saying whether the explosion caused the war, and it never compares it with other causes. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The sinking of the *Maine* was the trigger of the war but not its main cause. It turned public anger into a demand for action: 'Remember the Maine' was a slogan within days, and the Navy's report of an external explosion made Spain the suspect, although the cause was not known. But the pressure to act had been building since the revolt of 1895, with the horror at Weyler's reconcentration, the campaigns of Hearst and Pulitzer, and American investments in Cuba, and McKinley had resisted for months. Spain also conceded nearly all of America's demands in April, so the *Maine* alone does not explain why Congress still voted for war. The *Maine* was therefore the spark, and the causes were the longer-term feeling and interests." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

Q: What was the frontier thesis, and why did it matter for foreign policy?
A: Frederick Jackson Turner argued in 1893 that the frontier had shaped American democracy and character. After the 1890 census said that the frontier line had gone, many Americans felt that the nation needed new outlets, and overseas markets and bases seemed the answer.
Q: What did Mahan argue in *The Influence of Sea Power upon History* (1890), and who was influenced by it?
A: That national greatness depends on command of the sea, won by a battle fleet, a merchant fleet and overseas bases, and that a canal across Central America was needed. Theodore Roosevelt, Henry Cabot Lodge and naval planners in the USA (and in Britain, Germany and Japan) were influenced by it.
Q: Name three causes of the Spanish–American War and the main terms of the Treaty of Paris.
A: Spanish rule in Cuba and Weyler's reconcentration, the press (yellow journalism), American economic and strategic interests, the *Maine* and the de Lôme letter. Treaty of Paris (10 December 1898): Spain gave up Cuba and ceded Puerto Rico, Guam and the Philippines (for 20 million dollars).
Q: What were the Teller and Platt Amendments?
A: The Teller Amendment (April 1898) disclaimed any intention to annex Cuba. The Platt Amendment (1901), written into Cuba's constitution, gave the USA the right to intervene to protect Cuban independence and a lease of naval bases (Guantánamo).
Q: Why did the Philippine–American War begin, and how was it fought?
A: The Treaty of Paris gave the Philippines to the USA, but Aguinaldo's Republic claimed independence; fighting began on 4 February 1899. It was a conventional war until November 1899 and then a guerrilla war, with harsh methods by the American Army, until the "insurrection" was declared over on 4 July 1902.
Q: How did the USA get the Panama Canal Zone?
A: Colombia's Senate rejected the Hay–Herrán Treaty (August 1903); a revolt in Panama on 3 November 1903, protected by the US Navy, produced a new state; the Hay–Bunau-Varilla Treaty of 18 November gave the USA a Canal Zone ten miles wide for 10 million dollars and 250,000 dollars a year. The canal opened on 15 August 1914.
Q: What were the Roosevelt Corollary and Dollar Diplomacy?
A: The Corollary (6 December 1904) claimed the right of the USA to act as an "international police power" in the Western Hemisphere, to keep European powers out. Dollar Diplomacy (Taft, 1909–13) used loans and investment, supported by the government, in place of force.
Q: List four events that led to the US declaration of war on Germany in April 1917.
A: Any four of: the sinking of the *Lusitania* (7 May 1915); the Sussex pledge (4 May 1916); the German announcement of unrestricted submarine warfare (31 January 1917); the break of relations (3 February); the Zimmermann telegram (published 1 March); the sinking of American ships in March; Wilson's war message (2 April) and the vote of 6 April.

? What did the Roosevelt Corollary (1904) say?
- The USA would stay out of Latin America
+ The USA might have to act as an "international police power" in the Western Hemisphere
- Europe could collect debts by force
- Panama belonged to Colombia
> It turned the Monroe Doctrine from a warning to Europe into a claim to intervene.
? What did Dollar Diplomacy mean?
+ Using loans and investment backed by the government instead of force
- Giving gifts to Latin American states
- Paying Colombia for the Canal Zone
- A tax on imports
> Taft described it as "substituting dollars for bullets"; but Marines were sent to Nicaragua in 1912 anyway.
? Which event of 1917 first moved the USA to break diplomatic relations with Germany?
- The Zimmermann telegram
- The sinking of the Lusitania
+ The announcement of unrestricted submarine warfare on 31 January
- The Russian Revolution
> Relations were broken on 3 February. The Lusitania was in 1915 and the telegram was published on 1 March.
? Which statement about the Zimmermann telegram is correct?
- It was sent by Wilson to Germany
+ It proposed a German–Mexican alliance if the USA entered the war, and was published in the USA on 1 March 1917
- It caused the Lusitania sinking
- Mexico accepted its proposal
> Mexico did not accept the offer; the telegram was decoded by British intelligence (Room 40).
? What did the Senate do with the Treaty of Versailles?
- It ratified it in 1919
+ It rejected it in November 1919 and again in March 1920
- It amended it and ratified it in 1921
- It never voted on it
> The USA therefore did not join the League of Nations.

:::recap
- **Attitudes:** the frontier closed (1890; Turner 12 July 1893); Mahan (1890); the new navy; the Panic of 1893 (over 15,000 firms and over 500 banks failed); ideas of race and mission; Hawaii (overthrow 17 January 1893, annexed 7 July 1898).
- **Spain, 1898:** Cuba's revolt (1895), Weyler's reconcentration, yellow journalism; de Lôme letter (9 February) and the *Maine* (15 February); McKinley's message (11 April); the Teller Amendment; war declared 25 April; Manila Bay (1 May); Treaty of Paris (10 December 1898: Puerto Rico, Guam, the Philippines for 20 million dollars; Senate 57–27 on 6 February 1899). Platt Amendment (1901).
- **Philippines:** war from 4 February 1899; Aguinaldo captured 23 March 1901; Balangiga 28 September 1901; declared over 4 July 1902; about 1,020 US soldiers killed in action and 3,176 dead of disease; Filipino civilian deaths about 200,000–250,000 (estimates vary). Anti-Imperialist League; Beveridge's "March of the Flag" (16 September 1898).
- **Panama and the Caribbean:** Hay–Pauncefote (1901); Panama independent 3 November 1903; Hay–Bunau-Varilla (18 November 1903); canal opened 15 August 1914; Roosevelt Corollary (6 December 1904); Dollar Diplomacy (Taft, 3 December 1912); Great White Fleet (16 December 1907 – 22 February 1909).
- **First World War:** neutrality (19 August 1914); *Lusitania* (7 May 1915); Sussex pledge (4 May 1916); unrestricted U-boat warfare (31 January 1917); Zimmermann telegram (published 1 March); war message (2 April); declaration (6 April); Fourteen Points (8 January 1918); the Senate rejects Versailles.
- **Judgement:** economic strength was the necessary cause; the decision to use it came with the ideas and events of the 1890s; the Spanish war was the turning point in attitude, and the First World War the proof of power.
