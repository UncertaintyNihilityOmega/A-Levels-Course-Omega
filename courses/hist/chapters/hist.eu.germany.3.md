:::why
In September 1862 the King of Prussia was ready to abdicate rather than give way to his own parliament. Four years later the same parliament voted to forgive the government for governing without it, and Prussia had beaten Austria, the power that had led Germany for a thousand years of imperial history. The man in the middle was Otto von Bismarck. Was he a statesman with a plan for German unity, or a gambler who seized each chance as it came? That question is at the centre of this chapter and of many essay titles. It also teaches a skill you need in every period: judging whether a leader's aims were fixed from the start or changed with events.

## Prussia in 1858–62: a new king, a new army

### Wilhelm I and the New Era

King Frederick William IV of Prussia had a stroke in 1857 and could no longer rule. His brother William, born in 1797, took over as Regent on 7 October 1858 and became King Wilhelm I when Frederick William died on 2 January 1861. Wilhelm was 61 when he became Regent and 63 when he became king. He was a soldier first: he had fought against Napoleon in 1814 and had been the "grape-shot prince" who was sent abroad in 1848 for his hard line against the Berlin crowds. He believed that the king ruled by God's grace and that the army was the foundation of the state.

He was not, however, a reactionary like his brother. On becoming Regent he dismissed the conservative ministry of Otto von Manteuffel and appointed moderate ministers, led by Prince Karl Anton of Hohenzollern-Sigmaringen. Liberals called this the **New Era**. They expected a government that would respect the constitution of 1850 and work with the elected House of Representatives. The elections of 1858 gave liberals many seats. Wilhelm wanted good relations with parliament, as long as it did not touch his army.

:::def Landtag and House of Representatives
The Prussian parliament under the constitution of 1850. Its lower house, the House of Representatives (*Abgeordnetenhaus*), was elected by the three-class voting system, which weighted votes by the tax each man paid. The upper house, the *Herrenhaus*, was made up of nobles and nominated members. Money laws needed the consent of both houses and the king.
:::

### Roon and the army reform

The army was the problem. The Prussian army had been organised after the defeats by Napoleon, by the reformers Scharnhorst and Boyen. It rested on universal conscription, but in practice only part of each age group was called up, and the number of recruits had hardly changed since 1815 although the population had grown by well over half. The regular army was small. Behind it stood the **Landwehr**, a reserve of older men organised as a citizens' militia, with many officers drawn from the middle class. When Prussia mobilised in 1859 during the war between France and Austria, the mobilisation went badly, and the Landwehr turned out to be slow and unreliable. Wilhelm drew a conclusion: the army must be reformed.

:::def Landwehr
The Prussian reserve force created in 1813–14. Men served in the regular army for a short period, then in the Landwehr, a militia of older men with its own, largely middle-class, officers. Liberals saw it as the people's army; Wilhelm and his generals thought it militarily weak.
:::

The task fell to **Albrecht von Roon**, who became War Minister on 5 December 1859, with the help of **Helmuth von Moltke**, chief of the General Staff. Their plan, put to the House of Representatives in February 1860, had three parts:
- **More soldiers.** The annual intake of recruits was to rise from about 40,000 to about 63,000, an increase of more than half, so that the whole age group was called up and the peacetime army grew to about 200,000 men. This needed dozens of new regiments and more money.
- **Longer service.** Conscripts were to serve three years with the colours, not two.
- **A smaller role for the Landwehr.** The older Landwehr men would no longer take their place in the first line. The reform tied the reserve closely to the regular army and its aristocratic officers.

:::key What the army reform meant
1. **Military**: a bigger, better-trained, more professional army, close to the king, that Prussia would need if it was ever to face Austria or France.
2. **Political**: a bigger budget, which needed the House of Representatives to vote taxes, and a smaller citizens' militia, which liberals valued.
3. **Constitutional**: a question about who controlled the army, the king or parliament.
:::

### The reaction of the liberals

Most liberals did not oppose a strong army. Their programme (below) said that they would make any sacrifice if the nation's honour and power were at stake. What they objected to was the way it was done. Three years of service cost money and kept young men from work. The weakening of the Landwehr removed the middle class from the army. Most important, the army was the king's, and they wanted to use their power over money to win a share in the government. In 1860 they voted the extra money only as a temporary, one-year grant, without accepting that the reforms were permanent. Wilhelm, though, treated the money as a final decision and went on forming the new regiments.

**The German Progressive Party.** In June 1861 liberal members of the House of Representatives founded the **German Progressive Party** (*Deutsche Fortschrittspartei*), the first modern political party in Germany. Among its founders were the scientist Rudolf Virchow, the historian Theodor Mommsen and the industrialist Werner von Siemens. The programme of 9 June 1861 combined the two aims of the liberal nationalists: German unity under a strong Prussian central power with a common German parliament, and constitutional government at home (ministerial responsibility, meaning ministers answerable to parliament and not only to the king; independent courts, equal rights for all religions and a reform of the aristocratic upper house, the *Herrenhaus*). On the army it asked for strict economy in peacetime, the keeping of the militia and two years of service.

:::def German Progressive Party
The liberal party founded in Prussia in June 1861. It wanted a united Germany under Prussian leadership with an elected parliament, constitutional government in Prussia and two years' military service. It was the largest party in the Prussian House of Representatives from 1861 and led the opposition to the army reform and to Bismarck.
:::

### The constitutional crisis, 1860–62

The Progressives won the elections of December 1861 and became the largest party. Wilhelm had been crowned at Königsberg in October 1861 and, in a ceremony he arranged himself, took the crown from the altar and put it on his own head, to show that he ruled by God's grace and not by a constitution. That is the king the liberals faced. In March 1862 the House passed an amendment requiring a detailed budget for the army, and Wilhelm dissolved it. The new elections in May 1862 gave the liberals an even bigger majority, and the Progressives and the more moderate liberals together held most of the seats. Wilhelm had already replaced his few liberal ministers with conservatives, so the king's cabinet and the House now faced each other with no one in between.

:::def Constitutional conflict (Verfassungskonflikt)
The struggle in Prussia from 1860 to 1866 between the king and the liberal majority of the House of Representatives over the army reform and, behind it, over who held real power: the king, who commanded the army, or the parliament, which voted the money. Historians call it the constitutional conflict or constitutional crisis.
:::

The crisis came to a head in September 1862. The House struck the new money for the army out of the budget again. Wilhelm refused to give way, saying that he could not rule if he gave up control of the army. He drafted a document of abdication in favour of his son, Crown Prince Frederick, a liberal who was married to the daughter of Queen Victoria. The Crown Prince declined to take the throne in such a way, and refused to rule on liberal terms against his father's wishes. The king was left with a choice: give way to parliament, abdicate, or find a minister who would govern without the consent of the House.

:::key The causes of the constitutional crisis
1. **A strong king who would not compromise** on the army: Wilhelm saw command of the army as the heart of his power.
2. **A strong liberal party** that had won two elections and believed it could force the king to accept parliament's right to control the budget.
3. **A real military need**: the army reform was not a whim, and the military men Roon and Moltke were sure it was needed.
4. **A constitution with a gap**: the Prussian constitution of 1850 said the budget must be fixed by law, but did not say what to do if the king and the House could not agree.
:::

## Bismarck's appointment, 1862

### Who was Bismarck?

**Otto von Bismarck** was born on 1 April 1815 on a Junker (landowning noble) estate at Schönhausen, in Brandenburg. As the chapter on [Germany (2)](#hist.eu.germany.2) showed, he had come to notice as a hard-line royalist in 1848–50. From 1851 to 1859 he represented Prussia at the Federal Diet in Frankfurt, where he learned that Austria would not treat Prussia as an equal. He was then Prussia's ambassador in St Petersburg (1859–62), where he won the friendship of the Russian court, and in May 1862 he was sent to Paris for a short time. He was 47 in September 1862. Liberals knew him as an arrogant, reactionary landowner, and the king did not like him: Wilhelm thought him unreliable and too ready to use revolutionary means for conservative ends. Wilhelm turned to him only because there was no one else.

### The appointment

The man who persuaded the king was Roon. In mid-September, with the king close to abdication, Roon sent Bismarck, who was on holiday in the south of France, a telegram with the words *periculum in mora* ("danger in delay"). Bismarck came to Berlin. On 22 September 1862 he met the king in the park of Babelsberg Palace, near Potsdam. In his memoirs, written many years later, Bismarck says that Wilhelm feared the fate of Charles I of England and Louis XVI of France, who both lost their heads, and that he replied that one had to die some day, and that it was better to die fighting for one's king. Whether the conversation was as dramatic as Bismarck recalled we cannot know (he was telling his own story), but the outcome is certain. Bismarck promised to carry out the army reform even without a majority in parliament. Wilhelm dropped the idea of abdication. Bismarck was named Minister-President on 23 September 1862 and was confirmed in that office, with the Foreign Ministry as well, on 8 October.

:::def Minister-President
The head of the Prussian government. He chaired the council of ministers, but until 1862 was not very powerful: each minister reported to the king separately. Bismarck made the post the centre of government, combined with the Foreign Ministry.
:::

:::key Why Wilhelm appointed Bismarck
1. **Wilhelm would not give way**: he saw the army reform as essential and had no wish to give parliament control of it.
2. **The liberals would not give way** either, so the usual ministers could not govern, and abdication was the alternative.
3. **No other minister was willing** to rule against the House, but Bismarck would.
4. **Roon's backing**: the War Minister, whom Wilhelm trusted, argued that Bismarck was the only man for the job.
5. **Bismarck's qualities**: a strong will, a record as an uncompromising royalist, foreign policy experience, and the belief that a crisis at home could be solved by success abroad.
:::

### "Iron and blood"

A week later, on 30 September 1862, Bismarck spoke to the Budget Committee of the House of Representatives. He went to the meeting hoping to calm the committee, but his words were remembered for their hardness. He said that Germany looked to Prussia's power, not its liberalism, and that Prussia must gather its strength for the favourable moment. His most famous sentence said that the great questions of the day would not be decided by speeches and majority decisions, which he called the great mistake of 1848 and 1849, but "by iron and blood" (in German *durch Eisen und Blut*). It is often quoted as "blood and iron", but he said iron first.

The speech did not announce a plan to unify Germany by war. It was a warning to the liberals that the government would use its power, and its reference to 1848–49 was a jab at the Frankfurt Parliament, which had tried to make Germany by speeches. For the liberals it seemed to confirm that Bismarck was a danger. For Bismarck's supporters it showed that Prussia now had a leader who understood power.

### How Bismarck managed the crisis

Bismarck did not solve the dispute with the liberals; he sidestepped it. His method rested on the **gap theory** (*Lückentheorie*).

:::def Gap theory (Lückentheorie)
Bismarck's argument that the Prussian constitution contained a gap: it said the budget needed the agreement of king and parliament but not what should happen when they disagreed. In that case, he said, the state could not stop, so the government must carry on collecting taxes and spending under the old budget until the two sides agreed.
:::

In practice, from 1862 to 1866 the government raised taxes and paid for the new army without a legal budget. The House of Representatives passed resolutions calling this unconstitutional; Bismarck ignored them. He also used other tools:
- **Dissolving the House.** New elections in 1862–63 still returned liberal majorities, so Bismarck had no hope of winning a majority, but he did not need one. The upper house and the king backed him.
- **Controlling the press.** A press ordinance of 1 June 1863 allowed the government to suspend newspapers that were critical. Even the Crown Prince spoke publicly against it, and Wilhelm was angry.
- **The civil service and the army.** Officials who sided with the opposition were disciplined. The army, which now numbered about 200,000, remained loyal to the king.
- **Foreign policy.** The most important tool was the one Bismarck turned to from 1863: a successful foreign policy that would win support at home.

:::key Why Bismarck survived the constitutional conflict
1. **The king backed him** against parliament and the Crown Prince.
2. **The army and civil service were loyal**, and the liberals had no way to force a change, short of revolution, which most liberals did not want.
3. **The liberals were divided** between those who put constitutional rights first and those who put German unity first.
4. **The gap theory gave him a legal excuse** to carry on collecting taxes.
5. **Success abroad** in 1864 and above all 1866 weakened the opposition (see the Indemnity Bill, below).
:::

:::warn Do not write that Bismarck "ended" the crisis in 1862
The crisis did not end in 1862. Bismarck's appointment kept the king from abdicating and let the army reform go ahead, but the dispute with parliament lasted until the Indemnity Bill of September 1866. If a question asks how Bismarck "resolved" the crisis, explain both stages: managing it by the gap theory and unconstitutional rule in 1862–66, and ending it by victory and the Indemnity Bill in 1866.
:::

## The Congress of Princes and relations with Austria, 1863

By 1863 the Austrian emperor Francis Joseph was worried that Prussia's liberal nationalists might create a small German nation-state under Prussia, and that Bismarck might one day use them. Austria needed a reform of the German Confederation that would give nationalists something without breaking up the system that Austria led.

Two events in the first half of 1863 influenced Prussia's position. In January 1863 the Poles rose against Russia. Bismarck wished to keep Russia friendly and in February 1863 Prussia signed the **Alvensleben Convention**, which allowed each side's troops to cross the border in pursuit of Polish rebels. It was never put into effect, but it pleased the Tsar's government. It also angered Britain, France and Austria, and the Prussian liberals, many of whom sympathised with the Poles. It proved to be a good investment: Bismarck later counted on Russian friendliness in 1864, 1866 and 1870.

Then Austria took the initiative. In summer 1863 Francis Joseph put to Wilhelm a plan for the reform of the Confederation and invited the German princes to meet at Frankfurt. The plan proposed a directing board with a stronger executive, an assembly of delegates chosen by the parliaments of the separate states, a federal court and regular meetings of the sovereigns. It rejected a parliament elected directly by the people. Austria would keep its place as the senior power.

Wilhelm was inclined to go. Bismarck was alarmed: to accept the plan would be to accept Austria's lead and to give up any chance of Prussian equality. He argued with the king for days, and Wilhelm, who disliked offending Francis Joseph, finally agreed to stay away. The Congress of Princes met in August 1863 without Prussia, and almost all the other states were represented. Bismarck then made a counter-proposal: an assembly elected directly by the people, and equality between Austria and Prussia. This was an idea that Austria could not accept, and it was also a way of appealing to the liberals over the heads of the princes.

:::key Why the Congress of Princes failed
1. **Prussia stayed away**: without Prussia, the biggest of the German states except Austria, no reform of the Confederation could work.
2. **The plan was weak**: it did not offer a directly elected parliament, so liberal nationalists were not interested.
3. **Bismarck was determined**: he persuaded Wilhelm to refuse, as he believed that Austrian leadership of any reformed Confederation would end Prussia's hopes.
4. **Prussia's own proposal was clever**: a parliament elected by the people could not be accepted by Austria, but it showed that Prussia and not Austria spoke for the nation.
:::

The result was an important change in relations with Austria. It was Austria's last serious attempt to reform the Confederation from within, and it failed. It also showed that Bismarck was willing to defy the king's own wishes to keep Prussia from being treated as a junior partner. Yet in the very next months Prussia and Austria were to fight side by side.

## War with Denmark, 1864

### The Schleswig-Holstein question

:::def The Schleswig-Holstein question
The dispute over the duchies of Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg, which the King of Denmark ruled as duke. Holstein and Lauenburg were German-speaking and members of the German Confederation. Schleswig, with a mixed German and Danish population, was not in the Confederation, but it was linked to Holstein by ancient custom. The question was whether the duchies would be tied to Denmark, remain separate or become German.
:::

The problem was old. In 1848 Danish attempts to bind Schleswig closer to Denmark had led to a war in which Prussia fought for the duchies, until it gave way under pressure from Russia and Britain (the Malmö armistice of 1848, which you met in [Germany (2)](#hist.eu.germany.2)). The Great Powers settled the matter in the **London Protocol** of 1852. It said that the duchies would stay with the Danish crown, without being absorbed into Denmark, and that after the death of the childless King Frederick VII the crown would pass to Prince Christian of Glücksburg. The head of the rival Augustenburg family, which had a claim to the duchies under German law, gave up his family's claim in return for money. His son Frederick later said that he was not bound by that.

In the 1850s Danish nationalists, the "Eider Danes", demanded that Schleswig be made a proper part of Denmark. On 13 November 1863 the Danish parliament passed the **November Constitution**, which tied Schleswig to Denmark and left Holstein out. Frederick VII died on 15 November. His heir, Christian IX, signed the constitution on 18 November. Germans said that this broke the London Protocol, and the son of the old claimant, Frederick of Augustenburg, now declared himself Duke Frederick VIII of the duchies. German public opinion supported him almost everywhere, and many of the smaller German states were ready to recognise him. The Federal Diet did not recognise him but, in December 1863, it ordered a **federal execution** (military action by Confederation troops against a state, under the execution law that went with the Carlsbad Decrees), and Saxon and Hanoverian troops entered Holstein.

### Why did Bismarck get involved?

Bismarck was in a delicate position. If the small states and the nationalists had their way, a new little state under the Duke of Augustenburg would be created, one more vote for the middle states at the Diet and a loss for Prussia. If Prussia acted alone, it would break the London Protocol and might provoke Britain and Russia. So he turned the problem upside down: he persuaded Austria to act jointly with Prussia, in the name of the London Protocol, which both powers had signed. By keeping the Diet out and holding to the treaty, Bismarck kept the other powers quiet; at the same time he insisted that Denmark must withdraw its November Constitution.

:::key Bismarck's reasons for involvement in Schleswig-Holstein
1. **Prevent an Augustenburg state**: a new middle state would add to the small states' bloc against Prussia. Bismarck preferred annexation, or at least a duchy under Prussian control.
2. **Strategic value**: Kiel and the North Sea–Baltic route were a chance for a Prussian navy and a canal. Prussia had hardly any coast.
3. **Win national credit at home**: the nationalist cause was popular, and a success might weaken the liberal opposition in the constitutional conflict.
4. **Draw Austria into partnership**: after the Congress of Princes, Austria's cooperation split the Austrians from the middle states and made Austria share the risk.
5. **The chance was good**: Russia was friendly after the Alvensleben Convention; Napoleon III was sympathetic to nationalism; Britain would protest but not fight.
6. **Test the new army**: the war would show the value of Roon's reforms.
:::

### The course of the war

On 14 January 1864 Austria and Prussia agreed to act together without the Diet. On 16 January they gave Denmark 48 hours to cancel the November Constitution. Denmark refused, and on 1 February 1864 Prussian and Austrian troops crossed the Eider. The Prussian commander was the old Field Marshal Wrangel, later replaced by Prince Frederick Charles. The Danish army abandoned the ancient defence line of the Danevirke in February and fell back on the fortified position at Düppel (Danish: Dybbøl). On 18 April 1864 Prussian troops took the Düppel redoubts after a bombardment, and on 29 June they crossed to the island of Als. Prussian and Austrian troops occupied Jutland. Britain called a conference at London (25 April to 25 June 1864) but offered Denmark no help, and the talks failed. Palmerston's threats in 1863 had been a bluff (Palmerston was the British Prime Minister, who had hinted that Britain would help Denmark).

Under the **Treaty of Vienna** of 30 October 1864 Denmark gave up Schleswig, Holstein and Lauenburg to Austria and Prussia jointly. Denmark lost about two fifths of its population.

### The outcome: tensions between Austria and Prussia

On the surface the war was a success for both powers, and for the army. Prussia gained credit, and its soldiers had shown the effect of the reforms at Düppel. But the settlement made a new quarrel almost certain. Austria and Prussia now ruled the duchies together (a **condominium**), and they did not agree on what to do with them.

- **Austria** favoured Duke Frederick of Augustenburg's state and wanted a settlement approved by the Diet. It also had little to gain from the duchies, which were far from its borders.
- **Prussia** wanted the duchies for itself. In February 1865 Bismarck set out conditions under which Prussia would accept an Augustenburg state: Prussian control of Kiel, the Kiel Canal route and the duchies' army and navy. They amounted to annexation in all but name. The Prussian Crown Council agreed in May 1865 that Prussia had a right to annex the duchies.
- **The middle states and the liberals** supported Augustenburg and were angry at both powers.

Austria could not force Prussia out of Schleswig, while Prussia could not simply push Austria out of Holstein without war. In summer 1865 both sides drew back.

:::def The Convention of Gastein
The treaty of 14 August 1865 between Austria and Prussia that ended their joint rule of the duchies. Prussia took over the administration of Schleswig, and Austria that of Holstein. Prussia bought the rights to Lauenburg from Austria for 2.5 million Danish rigsdalers, and Kiel was to be a federal naval port under Prussian command.
:::

Gastein was a patch, not a solution. Bismarck himself described it as papering over the cracks in the building. The duchies were still formally under joint sovereignty, so the dispute could return whenever either side wished. Because Holstein was surrounded by Prussian-held territory, Austria's position there was weak, while the Prussian right to move troops along a military road through Holstein meant that Prussia could always interfere. And Bismarck had bought time to prepare for war.

## Preparing for war with Austria, 1865–66

### Isolating Austria

Bismarck now saw that Austria could be beaten if the other great powers stayed out. He tackled them one by one.
- **Russia** was friendly after the Alvensleben Convention. Austria had annoyed Russia by not supporting it in the Crimean War.
- **Britain** had shown in 1864 that it would not fight for the duchies and was busy elsewhere.
- **France.** In October 1865 Bismarck travelled to **Biarritz** to meet Napoleon III. Napoleon wanted to be a friend of nationalism, and he thought that a war between Austria and Prussia would be long and exhausting, so that France would be the judge of Europe at the end. Bismarck hinted at some compensation to France, perhaps in Belgium or Luxembourg, but put nothing in writing. Napoleon promised nothing definite, but his goodwill was enough.
- **Italy.** Italy wanted Venetia (the region round Venice, still Austrian) from Austria. On 8 April 1866 Prussia and Italy signed a secret alliance: if Prussia went to war with Austria within three months, Italy would join in. The time limit was a trap for Bismarck as well as for Austria, because it pushed him to start the war before the treaty ran out.

### Bismarck's proposal to the Federal Diet

On 9 April 1866 Bismarck made a proposal to the Federal Diet that astonished Europe. He called for a national parliament for the whole Confederation, elected by **universal manhood suffrage**, to draw up a new constitution. The motive was tactical. It would pose as the friend of German nationalism and embarrass Austria, which could not accept it, and it would split the opposition in Prussia, because even the liberals wanted a German parliament. A conservative Junker who had despised the Frankfurt Parliament of 1848 was now offering the vote to every man. Conservatives were shocked. Bismarck's view was that ordinary voters, especially in the countryside, were more loyal to the crown than the liberal middle class. It is an early example of what the historian Lothar Gall later called his "white revolutionary" method: a conservative using revolutionary means for conservative ends.

### Why war broke out

The immediate cause was the question of the duchies. Austria was ready to hand the question of the duchies to the Diet, where it had a majority. Bismarck had always said that Gastein excluded the Diet. On 1 June 1866 Austria did just that, and Bismarck called it a violation of the Convention of Gastein. Prussian troops entered Holstein in the first days of June, and Austrian troops withdrew. On 14 June the Diet voted, by a majority, for a mobilisation against Prussia, and Prussia declared that the Confederation was at an end and put forward its own plan for Germany without Austria. On 15 June Prussian armies invaded Hanover, Saxony and Hesse-Kassel, which sided with Austria. Italy joined the war on 20 June.

:::key Why war broke out in 1866
1. **The duchies**: the Gastein arrangement left Holstein and Schleswig as a standing dispute, and Austria's appeal to the Diet in June 1866 gave Bismarck a reason to say it was broken.
2. **Prussia's determination to end Austria's leadership**: Bismarck believed that Germany was too small for two great powers (see [Germany (2)](#hist.eu.germany.2)).
3. **Bismarck's diplomacy**: Russia friendly, Britain uninterested, France's vague neutrality, and an Italian ally.
4. **His reform proposal**: it made Prussia look like the champion of nationalism and Austria like its enemy.
5. **Prussia's new army** and Moltke's plans, which gave Bismarck confidence that Prussia would win.
6. **Austria's mistakes**: it appealed to the Diet in breach of Gastein and let the war come on Bismarck's terms.
:::

Even then, war was not certain until the last moment. Wilhelm did not want to fight another German monarch, and many of his advisers, including the Crown Prince, warned against it. Public opinion in Prussia was against the war. Bismarck was shot at on 7 May 1866 in Berlin by a student, Ferdinand Cohen-Blind, who missed; this made him briefly more popular.

## The Seven Weeks' War, 1866

### Königgrätz

Moltke planned a war of speed. Using the railways and the telegraph he brought three armies to Bohemia, to meet near Königgrätz (Sadowa in Czech). The Prussian infantry had the Dreyse **needle gun**, a breech-loading rifle that could be loaded lying down and fired much faster than the muzzle-loading Lorenz rifles of the Austrians. On **3 July 1866** about 220,000 Prussians fought about 215,000 Austrians and Saxons. The Austrian commander, Benedek, had taken a position with a river at his back. In the afternoon the Crown Prince's Second Army, with nearly 100,000 men, arrived on the Austrian flank. Prussia lost about 9,000 men. Austria lost about three times that number, and thousands more were taken prisoner. The war lasted about two and a half weeks from the invasion of 15 June to the battle, and it was decided in a single day. The name "Seven Weeks' War" is used for the whole conflict.

### The peace: Nikolsburg and Prague

After the victory, the king and the generals wanted to march on Vienna and take land from Austria. Bismarck wanted a quick, moderate peace. In his memoirs he says that he argued in a council of war on 23 July that Austria should not be wounded so badly that she would seek revenge, since Prussia might want her as a friend again; that a crushed Austria might join France; and that Prussia could not govern Vienna from Berlin. He added that cholera had spread among the troops. (This is Bismarck's own account, written years later, so treat it with care. But it fits his behaviour: the terms really were mild.) After a bitter quarrel Wilhelm gave way, and a preliminary peace was signed at **Nikolsburg** on 26 July. The **Treaty of Prague** of 23 August 1866 ended the war.

:::key The Treaty of Prague, 1866: what it did
1. **The German Confederation was dissolved**, and Austria accepted a new organisation of Germany without Austria.
2. **Austria lost no land to Prussia.** It gave up Venetia (it handed the region to Napoleon III, who passed it to Italy) and paid a war indemnity of about 20 million thalers.
3. **Prussia annexed** Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, the free city of Frankfurt, and Schleswig and Holstein: about four million people. Prussia now stretched without a break from the Rhine to the east.
4. **The North German Confederation** was set up north of the River Main, led by Prussia, with Saxony kept in existence but forced to join.
5. **The southern states** (Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden and part of Hesse-Darmstadt) were left outside, free to form their own union, but in practice joined to Prussia by secret military alliances.
:::

### The North German Confederation

The **North German Confederation** was a federal state of 22 states with about 30 million people, of whom roughly four fifths lived in Prussia. Its constitution, which came into force on 1 July 1867, was drafted by Bismarck. The King of Prussia was its president, and Bismarck was Chancellor. Laws needed the agreement of two bodies: the **Bundesrat**, made up of delegates of the governments, in which Prussia had the largest number of votes, and the **Reichstag**, elected by all men over 25. The army and foreign policy were in Prussia's hands. The confederation was Bismarck's way of tying the north to Prussia, offering liberals a national parliament and keeping real power with the Prussian king. The constitution of 1871 would copy it closely (see [Germany (4)](#hist.eu.germany.4)).

### The Liberals' change of attitude and the Indemnity Bill

The effect of Königgrätz on the liberals was dramatic. The Prussian elections held on the very day of the battle, 3 July 1866, had already been a blow: the conservatives, who supported Bismarck, rose from about 35 seats to about 140. After the victory, many of those who had fought Bismarck for four years began to see him differently.

:::def The Indemnity Bill (1866)
A law passed by the Prussian House of Representatives on 3 September 1866 (by 230 votes to 75) and signed on 14 September, which approved the government's spending in the years 1862–65 after the event and authorised spending for 1866. Bismarck presented it as an offer of peace. The government admitted that the budget law was needed, but nobody was punished for having ruled without one.
:::

Why did so many liberals change sides?
1. **Nationalism beat liberalism.** For most liberals the unity of Germany had always been the greater aim. Bismarck had done more in four years to bring about a Prussian-led Germany than all their speeches. Many came to think that freedom might follow once the nation was made.
2. **Success.** The victory showed that his policy worked and that the army reform they had resisted had delivered an army capable of winning.
3. **Bismarck's concessions.** The North German Confederation had a Reichstag elected by universal suffrage, and the Indemnity Bill itself was an olive branch: in his speech of 1 September 1866 he said that the government sought peace, that neither side had convinced the other in four years, and that each had believed it acted rightly.
4. **Fear of the left.** A moderate liberal would rather deal with Bismarck than see a radical movement grow.

In September 1866 the Progressives split. Those who voted for the bill, with leaders such as Eduard Lasker and Karl Twesten in the Prussian House and Rudolf von Bennigsen in the wider movement, formed in 1866–67 the **National Liberal Party**. The rest, including Virchow, kept up their opposition and voted against.

:::def National Liberal Party
The party formed in 1866–67 by liberals who supported Bismarck's national policy and accepted the Indemnity Bill. It put German unity first and constitutional demands second, and was the main party in the Reichstag until the late 1870s.
:::

:::key What Bismarck gained by 1866
1. **Prussia's victory over Austria**: Austria was shut out of Germany, and Prussia led the north.
2. **Peace at home**: the constitutional conflict was over, the Indemnity Bill left the army reform and Bismarck's power intact, and the liberals were split.
3. **A new popular base**: the National Liberals and, later, a national parliament elected by universal suffrage.
4. **A step towards unification**, but not a completed one: the south was still outside and France was hostile.
:::

## What were Bismarck's aims? Master plan or opportunism?

This is the question behind the second half of the key question. Look at what he did in these years.

| Stage | What Bismarck did | Does it suggest a plan, or a response to events? |
|---|---|---|
| Sept 1862 | Took office, ruled without a budget | He needed to save the army reform; no plan for unification stated |
| Aug 1863 | Kept Wilhelm away from the Congress of Princes | Consistent: no Austrian leadership of Germany |
| 1863–64 | Joined Austria against Denmark | Opportunist: used a crisis created by Danish nationalism |
| Aug 1865 | Gastein | Flexible: bought time, kept options open |
| Oct 1865 – Apr 1866 | Biarritz, Italy, Diet proposal | Planned: isolating Austria |
| June 1866 | Used Austria's appeal to the Diet | Opportunist in timing, but war had been prepared |
| July 1866 | Imposed a moderate peace | Clear aims: a Prussia dominant in the north and a friendly Austria later |

:::key The case that Bismarck had a plan
1. **A consistent aim**: from 1856 he argued that Germany could not hold both Prussia and Austria. Everything from the Congress of Princes to Prague pushed Austria out.
2. **Preparation**: Biarritz, the Italian alliance and the diplomatic isolation of Austria were long in preparation.
3. **His own later account** says he was working to unify Germany (but this is self-serving).
4. **Gall** (*Bismarck: The White Revolutionary*, 1980) saw a statesman who planned to preserve the Prussian monarchy by taking over the nationalist and democratic forces.
:::

:::key The case that Bismarck was an opportunist
1. **He changed course often**: he opposed Augustenburg and the Diet in 1863 and then relied on a popular vote and a parliament in 1866; he allied with Austria in 1864 and fought it in 1866.
2. **Events led him**: the Polish revolt, the Danish constitution and Austria's appeal to the Diet were not his doing; he used them.
3. **His goals were narrow**: Prussian dominance in the north was enough in 1866. He did not try to take the south or add German-Austrian lands.
4. **Taylor** (*Bismarck: The Man and the Statesman*, 1955) argued that Bismarck had no master plan: he improvised, took what chance offered and kept every option open. **Pflanze** (*Bismarck and the Development of Germany*, 1963, revised 1990) described a politician who kept several lines open and chose among them as events allowed, and **Steinberg** (*Bismarck: A Life*, 2011) stressed his ruthlessness and his skill in making others act as he wished.
:::

:::note A judgement you can defend
Most historians stand between the two. Bismarck's aim before 1866 was clear and narrow: to make Prussia the leading power in Germany and to end Austria's role. His methods were flexible: he held several policies at once and picked the one that events favoured. German unification as a nation-state was not a fixed aim in 1866; it was something the war with France would later make possible. A strong answer to "Bismarck had a clear plan" says that there was a plan for **Prussian power** but not for **a united Germany**, and uses Taylor and Gall only to sharpen the argument, not to replace the evidence.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Bismarck was appointed Minister-President in 1862.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Wilhelm I wanted to reform the army but the liberals in the House of Representatives would not agree. The Progressive Party won the elections in 1861 and 1862. Wilhelm was going to abdicate. Roon sent a telegram to Bismarck, who met the king at Babelsberg. Bismarck was made Minister-President on 23 September 1862."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it tells a story. It never says why these events led the king to choose Bismarck. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was the deadlock over the army. Roon's reform, put to the House in 1860, would raise the annual intake of recruits from about 40,000 to about 63,000 and make service three years. The liberals, who wanted two years and a strong Landwehr, voted only temporary money and then, after winning the elections of December 1861 and May 1862, refused it. Wilhelm saw command of the army as the heart of the monarchy, so he could not give way, and his ordinary ministers could not govern against the House. A second reason was that nobody else would do the job. By September 1862 the king had drafted his abdication, and the only alternative to abdication was a minister ready to carry out the reform without the consent of parliament. Bismarck, a Junker royalist, promised at Babelsberg to do so."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was Roon's influence. The War Minister trusted Bismarck and sent him the telegram *periculum in mora* in mid-September, and Wilhelm, who distrusted Bismarck and thought him too ready to use revolutionary methods, trusted Roon.

These reasons were linked. The deadlock over the army was the cause of the crisis, and the king's refusal to compromise made abdication or a minister who would rule without a budget the only two choices. Wilhelm did not appoint Bismarck because he liked him, but because the crisis had closed every other route, and Roon's recommendation made the choice acceptable to him. The decisive factor was the king's refusal to give up control of the army: once he had decided that he would rather abdicate than yield, he needed a man who would not yield either, and Bismarck was the one man who offered it."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the deadlock created the need; the lack of alternatives and Roon's backing made Bismarck the answer), with a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

Prussia's strength depends on the union of Germany. That union needs a strong central power in Prussian hands and a common German parliament. At home the constitutional rights of citizens must be respected by every official, and the law on ministerial responsibility promised by the constitution must at last be passed.

*Adapted (closely paraphrased) from the Founding Programme of the German Progressive Party, Berlin, June 1861.*

**Source B**

The German states must remain independent members bound by a federal tie. A single head of government or a parliament elected directly by the people would dissolve them into one state. We therefore propose a directing board of the Confederation with a stronger executive, an assembly of delegates sent from time to time by the parliaments of the separate states, a federal court and regular meetings of the sovereigns. Prussia's cooperation is essential, since without it no far-reaching reform can be carried out.

*Adapted from an Austrian memorandum proposing the reform of the German Confederation, summer 1863 (translated in German History in Documents and Images).*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the kind of German unity that was wanted in the early 1860s.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that Germany should be united under Prussia with a parliament. Source B says that the states should stay independent and has no parliament elected by the people. So they disagree. Source A is from a party and Source B is from Austria, so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources have some common ground. Both want the Confederation reformed and both accept that representative bodies should have a part in it: Source A calls for 'a common German parliament', and Source B proposes 'an assembly of delegates'. Both also accept that Prussia is central: Source A says that unity needs 'a strong central power in Prussian hands', and Source B says that 'Prussia's cooperation is essential'.

On the kind of unity, however, they are opposed. Source A wants a stronger union under one power, Prussia, with a parliament for all Germany. Source B explicitly rejects that: it fears that 'a single head of government or a parliament elected directly by the people' would dissolve the states, and offers an assembly whose members are sent by the state parliaments, and a directorate and meetings of princes that keep the states and their rulers in control. Source A puts a people's parliament and a strong centre first, Source B the independence of the states and their rulers.

The context explains this difference. Source A was written by liberal nationalists, founding a party in the middle of the Prussian army dispute. Their purpose was to show that they were both loyal to the king and committed to German unity and a constitution, so the programme speaks of Prussian leadership because they saw Prussia, with its army and its Zollverein, as the only state able to unite Germany, and of ministerial responsibility because they were fighting for it at home. Source B comes from the Habsburg government, which ruled many nationalities and feared a nation-state. Austria's purpose was to head off the nationalists by reforming the Confederation on its own terms, before the Congress of Princes in August 1863, while keeping the princes and the federal system through which it led Germany. Its language about Prussia's cooperation reflects the reality that Prussia, as the second power, could block any reform, as it did when Bismarck persuaded Wilhelm to stay away. So Source A is strong evidence of what liberal nationalists wanted, but it says little about what other Germans or other states wanted. Source B is strong evidence of what Austria was prepared to offer and what it feared, but as an official proposal it presents Austria's interest as the interest of the states. For the question of what kind of unity was wanted, they show two competing visions, and they do not show which of them had most support."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the army dispute, the Habsburg fear of nationalism, the Congress of Princes) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Bismarck had a clear plan to unify Germany by 1866.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: consistent policy against Austria (1856 letter, Congress of Princes 1863, Gastein, Biarritz, Italy, the Diet proposal, Prague); preparation of the army; careful diplomacy.
- Against 1, events: the Danish crisis (November Constitution, death of Frederick VII) was not his making; Gastein, the alliance with Austria in 1864, and the timing of war in 1866 show improvisation.
- Against 2, "unify": in 1866 he made a north German confederation, left the south out and left Austria untouched; unity of Germany came in 1871 after a war with France.
- Judgement: a plan for Prussian dominance, not for German unity. Criterion: what the plan was aimed at, and what actually changed by 1866.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Bismarck did have a plan. He had said in the 1850s that Germany was too small for Prussia and Austria. In 1863 he persuaded Wilhelm not to go to the Congress of Princes. He joined Austria against Denmark in 1864, got Gastein in 1865 and then met Napoleon III at Biarritz and made an alliance with Italy in 1866. He proposed a parliament elected by universal suffrage, and then he used Austria's appeal to the Diet to start the war. After Königgrätz he made the Treaty of Prague and set up the North German Confederation. This shows that he had a clear plan to unify Germany."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: the evidence shows a plan against Austria, not necessarily a plan to unify Germany. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with the alternative merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet much of what Bismarck did looks like improvisation. The crisis over Schleswig-Holstein began with the Danish November Constitution and the death of Frederick VII in November 1863, events he did not control, and his policy shifted several times as it unfolded: he first used the London Protocol to bind Austria, then asked for conditions that amounted to annexation, then signed Gastein in August 1865, a compromise that he himself described as papering over the cracks. His proposal of April 1866 for a parliament elected by universal suffrage was a tactical move to embarrass Austria and divide the liberals, not a statement of belief. Taylor argued that Bismarck had no master plan and took what chance offered, and the timing of the war, forced by the three-month limit of the Italian treaty and by Austria's appeal to the Diet on 1 June, supports him.

The word 'unify' is also a problem. The settlement that Bismarck imposed in 1866 did not unify Germany. The Treaty of Prague set up a confederation north of the Main with about 30 million people, left Bavaria, Württemberg, Baden and part of Hesse-Darmstadt outside, and shut Austria out. Bismarck refused to annex any Austrian land, and in his memoirs he recalls telling the council of war of 23 July that Prussia was there to pursue a German policy and not to punish. The south joined only after the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71.

Overall, the statement is partly true. Bismarck did have a clear aim between 1862 and 1866: to end Austria's leadership and make Prussia the leading power of Germany, and he prepared carefully for it. But that was a plan for Prussian power, carried out flexibly, not a plan to unify Germany, which was not yet the aim. The statement is therefore right about the direction and wrong about the destination."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (what the plan aimed at) and separates plan from outcome. With an opening paragraph on the case for a plan, this is Level 5 (19–20). Note how the word "unify" was challenged: a strong answer does not just agree or disagree with the question, it tests its words.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

The party will make every sacrifice when the nation's honour and power are at stake in war. In peace it demands strict thrift in the military budget. It wishes the militia to be kept, able-bodied men to be called up for two years, and the aristocratic upper house to be reformed, without which all other reforms remain a pious hope.

*Adapted (closely paraphrased) from the Founding Programme of the German Progressive Party, Berlin, June 1861.*

**Source B**

Germany does not look to Prussia's liberalism but to its power. Prussia must gather its strength and keep it for the favourable moment, which has been missed several times. The great questions of the day will not be decided by speeches and majority decisions, which was the great mistake of 1848 and 1849, but by iron and blood.

*Adapted from a speech by Otto von Bismarck, Minister-President of Prussia, to the Budget Committee of the Prussian House of Representatives, 30 September 1862.*

**Source C**

The government wants peace sincerely and will not now defend or attack what is past. For four years neither side has convinced the other, and each believed it acted rightly. The government seeks peace not because it cannot fight but because the country needs it, and it hopes the House will take the outstretched hand. Foreign policy is not finished: the army's victories have raised the stakes, and the government must stay united at home.

*Adapted (paraphrased) from a speech by Bismarck to the Prussian House of Representatives on the Indemnity Bill, 1 September 1866.*

**Source D**

The government is granted indemnity for its conduct of the budget in the years 1862 to 1865, as if the budgets had been lawfully fixed. Spending of up to 154 million thalers is authorised for 1866. The government will present the House with an account of its income and spending.

*Adapted from the Prussian Indemnity Law, 14 September 1866.*

**Question.** How far do these sources show that the constitutional conflict ended in a victory for the government?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support (victory for the government): B (power, not speeches, decides), C (the government offers peace from strength: "not because it cannot fight"), D (the government is indemnified for four years without a lawful budget). Challenge or qualify: A (what the liberals wanted: two years, the militia, a reformed upper house; none of it achieved); C (admits that neither side convinced the other and speaks of an "outstretched hand"); D (the government accounts to the House and must have a lawful budget in future).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a party programme, intended to rally support in 1861 and to show that the liberals were loyal to the king and not against the army. It shows what the liberals wanted, so it is strong evidence of the starting position, but a programme is not a record of what was achieved. The two years' service and the militia were not won.
- **B**: a speech to a hostile committee a week after Bismarck took office. Its purpose was to show the committee that the government would not be pushed around. It is strong evidence of his attitude and weak evidence of his plans: he did not announce a war, and the "iron and blood" sentence, in the context of the debate on the budget, is a warning to the liberals. The speech came before any of the successes of 1864–66.
- **C**: spoken after Königgrätz to win a vote, so its tone is conciliatory by design: Bismarck wanted the liberals to accept the bill. It shows his victory (he can afford to offer peace), but its message that neither side had convinced the other is the language of a man trying to make it easy for the House to change sides.
- **D**: an official law, which is the strongest evidence of what the settlement actually was. It forgives the past and authorises the 1866 spending. But it takes the form of a law passed by the House of Representatives, so it also shows that the government sought the House's agreement.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources B, C and D support the view that the government won the conflict. Source B shows the attitude with which Bismarck began: the government would not accept that a majority could settle the matter. Source D shows the outcome: for four years the government had collected taxes and paid for the army without a lawful budget, and the House now approved it after the event, so Bismarck's gap theory was in effect accepted. Source C shows why: he could offer peace as a victor, in a speech made two months after the victory at Königgrätz. Source A shows what the liberals lost: the two years' service and the citizens' militia, and the reform of the upper house on which they said other reforms depended, were not achieved. On the other hand, the sources suggest that the conflict ended in a compromise as well as a victory. Source D is a law passed by the House, and it asks for indemnity: the government accepts that it needed the House's permission, even if only after the event, and Source C speaks of an 'outstretched hand'. In my own knowledge, the bill was passed by 230 votes to 75, because many liberals thought that unity mattered more than the principle, and the Progressives split, with the National Liberals going over to Bismarck. So the sources agree to a large extent that the government won: the king's army was untouched and Bismarck's power was secure. But they also show that it was a victory for the government of a particular kind, won by success abroad, which persuaded a part of the opposition to change its mind, and not by the defeat of the liberals' arguments."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1861–66; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "To what extent was the war with Denmark in 1864 caused by Bismarck?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The war with Denmark began on 1 February 1864 when Prussian and Austrian troops crossed the Eider. It was fought over Schleswig and Holstein. In November 1863 the King of Denmark, Frederick VII, died and Christian IX signed the November Constitution. The Prussians stormed the Düppel redoubts on 18 April 1864. The war ended with the Treaty of Vienna on 30 October 1864, when Denmark gave up the duchies to Austria and Prussia. So Bismarck was an important cause of the war."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it describes events and says nothing about Bismarck's part in them. The last sentence asserts a link that nothing earlier in the paragraph has shown, and there is no alternative cause. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then weigh. "The war was caused to a large extent by Danish action, but Bismarck decided what would be done with it. The trigger was Denmark's November Constitution of 1863, which tied Schleswig to Denmark and broke the London Protocol of 1852 in German eyes; it was Danish nationalism, not Bismarck, that created the crisis. However, Bismarck turned a crisis into a war. He persuaded Austria to act with Prussia under the London Protocol, kept the Diet out, and on 16 January 1864 demanded in an ultimatum with a 48-hour limit that Denmark withdraw a constitution it could not cancel in so short a time. He wanted the duchies for Prussia and preferred war to an Augustenburg state. So the Danes provided the cause, but Bismarck chose war." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What were the three main changes proposed in Roon's army reform?
A: More recruits each year (from about 40,000 to about 63,000, so a peacetime army of about 200,000); three years' service instead of two; a smaller role for the Landwehr, the older militia reserve.
Q: Why did the liberals oppose the army reform even though most were not against a strong army?
A: They wanted two years' service and a strong militia; and they saw the money for the army as their chance to force the king to accept parliament's control of the budget and a share in government.
Q: What was the gap theory?
A: Bismarck's claim that the constitution left a gap on what to do when king and House could not agree on the budget, so the government could carry on collecting taxes and spending under the old budget.
Q: Why did Wilhelm I appoint Bismarck in September 1862?
A: Wilhelm would not give up control of the army and the liberals would not give way; he was ready to abdicate; no other minister would govern without a budget, Roon recommended Bismarck, and Bismarck promised to carry out the reform.
Q: Why did Wilhelm not go to the Congress of Princes in 1863?
A: Bismarck persuaded him that to accept Austria's plan would be to accept Austrian leadership of the Confederation and give up Prussian equality; the plan did not offer a directly elected parliament.
Q: Give two reasons for Bismarck's involvement in the war with Denmark.
A: To prevent a new middle state under Augustenburg and gain the duchies for Prussia (Kiel and the coast); to win national credit and weaken the opposition at home; to bind Austria into partnership; the great powers would not stop him.
Q: What were the terms of the Convention of Gastein?
A: Prussia administered Schleswig and Austria Holstein; Prussia bought Austria's rights in Lauenburg for 2.5 million Danish rigsdalers; Kiel was to be a federal naval port under Prussian command.
Q: What did the Treaty of Prague of 1866 do?
A: Dissolved the German Confederation; Austria lost no land to Prussia but gave up Venetia and paid an indemnity; Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, Frankfurt and the duchies; the North German Confederation was set up north of the Main.
Q: Why did many liberals vote for the Indemnity Bill in 1866?
A: Success in 1866 showed Bismarck's policy worked; unity mattered more to them than the budget principle; he offered a parliament elected by universal suffrage and an olive branch; they feared the radical left. The bill passed 230 to 75, and the National Liberal Party formed.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Prussian liberals opposed Roon's army reform between 1860 and 1862.
A: Indicative content: two years' service rather than three; the Landwehr, the citizens' militia, was being weakened; cost; fear that the army would become the king's tool; above all, the money gave the House its main power, and refusing the budget was a way to force the king to accept parliament's right to control it and ministerial responsibility; the Progressives won the elections of December 1861 and May 1862 and felt they spoke for the people. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: the military objections were real, but the underlying issue was constitutional, and the liberals' election successes made them unwilling to compromise.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Wilhelm I refused to attend the Congress of Princes in 1863.
A: Indicative content: Austria's plan kept Austria as the senior power and offered no directly elected parliament; Bismarck argued that attending would accept Austrian leadership; the princes could out-vote Prussia; Wilhelm disliked offending Francis Joseph and was tempted, but Bismarck's pressure and the risk of damaging Prussia's equality prevailed; Prussia's alternative (a parliament elected directly) appealed to the nationalists. Level 4: connect the king's reluctance to Bismarck's influence and conclude that Bismarck's fear of Austrian leadership was decisive.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Bismarck solved the constitutional crisis in Prussia between 1862 and 1866 by force.' How far do you agree?
A: For: ruling without a budget, the gap theory, the press ordinance, the loyal army and officials, ignoring the House's resolutions. Against: he also used foreign policy to win consent; he offered the Indemnity Bill and the universal suffrage; the liberals were divided and many gave way because of nationalism, not force; the king's backing; the conflict only ended in 1866. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that force and the gap theory let him survive, but success abroad ended the conflict, so force was necessary to avoid defeat but not what resolved it.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was Bismarck responsible for the war with Austria in 1866?
A: For: isolation of Austria (Biarritz, Italy, Russia), the Diet proposal, the use of the Gastein quarrel, the intention to end Austrian leadership, preparation by Roon and Moltke. Against: Austria's appeal to the Diet on 1 June broke Gastein; Austria's support for Augustenburg; the Confederation's weakness; the competition for leadership that had begun at Olmütz; Austrian and middle-state mobilisation on 14 June; Wilhelm's reluctance. Level 5: a criterion such as "who chose the timing and the terms of the quarrel?" and a judgement, e.g. that war was likely given the rivalry, but Bismarck made it happen on his terms.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about Bismarck's approach to his opponents in 1866.
A: Similarities: both show a government that speaks from victory but wants to avoid humiliating an opponent (A: Austria must not be wounded; B: the government seeks peace), and both link the peace to Prussia's wider interest (a later friendship with Austria; unity at home before more foreign policy). Differences: A is about Austria and foreign policy, B about the Prussian liberals and domestic policy; A is a private memoir argument for a limit on victory, B a public speech to win a vote. Provenance: A was written decades later in Bismarck's memoirs and may exaggerate his foresight; B was a speech with a practical purpose. Level 4 explains the shared tone through his belief in limits and the differences through audience and purpose, and judges which is better evidence.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources show that Prussian leadership in Germany would depend on force rather than agreement?
H: Decide which sources show force (D) and which show limits to force or agreement (A, B, C).
H: Use your own knowledge: how did Prussia in fact win leadership between 1862 and 1866?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose, and note when it was written.
A: Support: D (Prussia's power, not liberalism, matters; keep it for the moment); B (the government has the strength to fight); C (Austria says Prussian cooperation is essential, so Prussia has a veto and can block reform, which is power). Challenge: A (restraint towards Austria; Prussia is pursuing a German policy), B (peace offered), C (Austria's plan was an offer of agreement, though on Austrian terms). Evaluation with knowledge: D is a threat to a hostile committee in 1862; A is a retrospective, self-justifying account of 1866; B was spoken to win a vote; C is Austrian and wants Prussia's agreement for its own plan. Knowledge: war in 1864 and 1866, but also the Diet proposal, the Italian alliance and a moderate peace. Level 5 judges that force was decisive in the end, but Bismarck chose when and how far to use it and tried agreement where he could.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was the victory at Königgrätz more important than Bismarck's diplomacy in establishing Prussian leadership of Germany by 1866?
H: Set a criterion: what does "leadership" mean? Military power, diplomatic position, control of the north German states?
H: What would Königgrätz have achieved without the diplomatic isolation of Austria?
A: Königgrätz: ended Austria's chance to resist, made the Treaty of Prague and the North German Confederation possible, split the liberals; the needle gun, railways and Moltke's plans. Diplomacy: Russia and Britain neutral, Biarritz and Italy, the Diet proposal, and keeping the terms moderate so that France and Austria did not combine against Prussia. Level 5: judge that Königgrätz was decisive for leadership, but only because diplomacy first meant the war was fought on one front against Austria alone, and diplomacy after the victory decided what was done with it.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The Prussian liberals were defeated in the constitutional conflict by 1866.' Discuss.
H: List what the liberals wanted in 1861 (Source A of the worked example) and check what they got.
H: Think about what the Indemnity Bill admitted, and whether the National Liberals saw themselves as defeated.
A: Defeat: no ministerial responsibility, the army reform stood, three years of service, Bismarck ruled four years without a budget, the House forgave him; the Progressives split; the old Landwehr's role was reduced. Not defeat: the Indemnity Bill admitted the need for a lawful budget; universal suffrage and a parliament in the North German Confederation; the National Liberals won the unification they wanted; the Reichstag later forced concessions. A* judgement: the liberals lost the argument over who controlled the army, but chose to; most of their programme on German unity was achieved on Bismarck's terms, so it was a defeat for liberalism and a victory for nationalism.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Bismarck's later memoirs describe the years before 1866 as a steady march towards a goal he had always had in view. How far can a historian rely on the memoirs as evidence of his aims?
H: Who was the memoirs' audience, and when were they written?
H: Compare them with what he did at the time: Gastein, Nikolsburg, the proposal to the Diet.
A: Strengths: the author was there; they give details no other source has; the argument at Nikolsburg fits the moderate peace he got. Weaknesses: written (published 1898) after he had been dismissed in 1890, to defend his reputation and criticise his successors; they stress foresight and cleverness and play down luck and mistakes; some scenes cannot be checked; the account of the war council is his own. Compare: contemporary letters and actions show he often changed course, which is hard to reconcile with a steady march. Judgement: reliable on events he observed and on his reasoning at certain moments, unreliable on his claims of a master plan; use it with other evidence.

:::recap
- **Crisis in Prussia**: Wilhelm I (Regent 1858, king 1861) and Roon planned a bigger army with three years' service and a smaller Landwehr (Roon War Minister 5 December 1859). The liberals, the Progressive Party of June 1861, wanted two years and the militia, and used the budget to claim power. They won the elections of December 1861 and May 1862. In September 1862 Wilhelm was ready to abdicate.
- **Bismarck's appointment**: Roon's *periculum in mora*; Babelsberg 22 September; Minister-President on 23 September 1862. "Iron and blood" speech, 30 September.
- **Managing the crisis**: gap theory, taxes collected without a budget, press ordinance (1 June 1863), loyal army; the crisis ended in 1866.
- **1863**: Alvensleben Convention (8 February); Congress of Princes in August, Wilhelm stayed away on Bismarck's advice.
- **Denmark, 1864**: November Constitution; Frederick VII died 15 November 1863; war from 1 February; Düppel 18 April; Treaty of Vienna 30 October. Bismarck wanted the duchies for Prussia and to bind Austria. Gastein, 14 August 1865: Prussia took Schleswig, Austria Holstein.
- **Road to war**: Biarritz (October 1865), Italian alliance (8 April 1866), proposal for a parliament elected by universal manhood suffrage (9 April), Austria's appeal to the Diet (1 June), invasion (15 June).
- **Seven Weeks' War**: Königgrätz 3 July; Nikolsburg 26 July; Prague 23 August: the Confederation was dissolved, Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel, Nassau, Frankfurt and the duchies, and the North German Confederation was formed.
- **Liberals**: the Indemnity Bill, passed 230 to 75 on 3 September 1866; the Progressives split and the National Liberals formed.
- **Aims**: a plan for Prussian dominance and the end of Austria's role, carried out flexibly; not yet a plan for a united Germany.
