Q: [40 marks] (#hist.eu.france.2) **France, 1774–1814.** Read the sources and then answer both parts of the question.

**Source A**

But times have changed. This so-called convention between a people and its king no longer exists. Louis has abdicated the throne. From now on Louis is nothing to us, unless he become our enemy. … it can no longer close its eyes to the fact that monarchy, above all hereditary monarchy, is incompatible with liberty. … We therefore call upon you, in the name of the fatherland, to declare immediately that France is no longer a monarchy, but rather that it is a republic. Or at a minimum, wait until all the departments and all of the primary assemblies have expressed their opinion on this important question …

*Shortened from an address by the Cordeliers club (the Society of Friends of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen) to the National Assembly ("the Representatives of the Nation"), June 1791 (dated 21 June in the collection), as translated by the project "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity" (George Mason University and the City University of New York) from Albert Mathiez, Le Club des Cordeliers pendant la crise de Varennes.*

**Source B**

Sire, … the continuing troubles have their source in the criminal preparations of French émigrés. … Sire, it is your role to make them stop. It is your role to address these foreign powers with a language worthy of the King of the French People. … let the hordes of émigrés be dissipated this instant. … Let your declaration be underscored by the movement of the forces that have been entrusted to you, so that the nation is aware of who are enemies and who are friends. With these bold steps, we shall recognize the defender of the Constitution.

*Shortened from the address of the Legislative Assembly to Louis XVI, 29 November 1791, as translated by the project "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity" from the* Archives parlementaires.

**Source C**

Ferment is extreme in all corners of the Empire and will explode in a terrible way unless reasonable confidence in Your Majesty's intentions finally calm them. This confidence however cannot be based on protestations. It can only be based on deeds. … Already public opinion compromises Your Majesty's goals. The people will be saddened to think their King is acting as a friend and accomplice of the conspirators. … I know that the stern language of the truth is rarely welcome close to the throne. I also know that, because it is almost never heard there, that revolutions become necessary.

*Shortened from a letter by Jean-Marie Roland, a Girondin minister in the King's government, to Louis XVI, dated 10 June 1792 and read to the Legislative Assembly on 13 June, after the King had dismissed him, as translated by the project "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity" from the* Archives parlementaires.

**Source D**

… upon entering the Assembly hall, I was greatly surprised to find the King, the Queen, the Prince, the King's oldest sister, Madame Elisabeth, and others … all very carefully dressed, with heads lowered like wet hens; they had all taken refuge in the National Assembly to seek there the safety which could no longer be found in the palace. The cannoneers … had instead simply unloaded their cannon … The King has been suspended from all his functions and powers; we have driven out his counterrevolutionary ministers and have named others worthy of public confidence.

*Shortened from a letter written at midnight on 10 August 1792 by Michel Azema, a deputy of the Legislative Assembly, as translated by the project "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity" from Camille Bloch, ed.,* La Révolution française *(1894).*

**Answer both parts of the question with reference to the sources.**

(a) Read Sources A and B. Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to Louis XVI in 1791. [15]

(b) Read all of the sources. "The monarchy was overthrown in 1792 mainly because of Louis XVI's own actions." How far do these sources support this view? [25]

A: **Mark scheme, Section A.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [15]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO3).**

*Similarities.* Both sources judge the King by the Revolution's own principles. Source A says "monarchy, above all hereditary monarchy, is incompatible with liberty"; Source B asks him to show himself "the defender of the Constitution". Both speak for the nation against a danger and both treat the King's conduct as the test: A says he "has abdicated the throne" and speaks "in the name of the fatherland"; B says that "the nation" must know "who are enemies and who are friends" and that his "bold steps" will decide whether the deputies "recognize" him. Both are demands made to a person or body with power.

*Differences.* Source A has given up on the King. The "convention between a people and its king no longer exists", "Louis is nothing to us", and France should be "a republic" (or, at the least, wait for the departments to speak). Source B still needs a king: it addresses him as "Sire", wants a "language worthy of the King of the French People", and offers him the chance to become "the defender of the Constitution". A blames the King; B blames the émigrés and the German princes who shelter them ("the continuing troubles have their source in the criminal preparations of French émigrés"). A asks for a change of regime; B asks the existing regime to act.

*Why they differ (the step to Level 4).* **Date and events.** Source A was written as the news of the flight to Varennes (20–21 June 1791) broke, when a king who had secretly left Paris and protested against the Revolution in a declaration he left behind had shocked patriots; the idea of a republic became speakable for the first time. It was still a minority view: the Assembly declared the King "kidnapped" and, on 15 July, inviolable, and on 17 July the National Guard fired on the crowd that came to sign a petition at the Champ de Mars. Source B comes after the settlement: the King accepted the constitution in September 1791, the Legislative Assembly met on 1 October, and the deputies had sworn to a constitution with a king in it. They now feared invasion by the émigrés' backers and had passed a decree against the émigrés (9 November) that the King was soon to veto. **Author and purpose.** The Cordeliers were a small, radical club whose aim was to move opinion towards a republic; the address was meant to be printed and circulated, so it argues, it does not describe. The Assembly's address is formal and carefully polite, and its purpose is to press the King to act and to expose him in public if he does not: its flattering language ("a language worthy of the King of the French People") may hide deputies' real suspicion. **Value as evidence.** A is strong evidence of what Varennes did to radical opinion, weak evidence of the views of most French people or deputies in 1791. B is strong evidence of the Assembly's official position and of its fear of the émigrés in November 1791, weaker evidence of what individual deputies privately thought of Louis.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–3).** Describes each source ("A is a petition that says Louis has abdicated; B is the Assembly telling the King about émigrés") or makes a simple point such as "one is from a club, the other from the Assembly" that is not developed.
- **Level 2 (4–7).** Identifies a difference or a similarity, but only one aspect is explained ("A wants a republic and B does not"), or lists both without support from the sources.
- **Level 3 (8–11).** Gives similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations. The explanation is generic ("A is from radicals so it is biased").
- **Level 4 (12–15).** A developed comparison that explains why the sources agree and differ, using context (Varennes, or the settlement of September 1791) and evaluation (who is speaking, to whom, and why). The top of the level compares the value of the two sources for the question; the bottom of the level explains only one of them.

*A Level 4 paragraph on the "why".* "The sources differ because they were written at opposite ends of 1791. Source A, written within days of Varennes, comes from a club that had never trusted the monarchy and now had proof that the King had tried to leave the country: 'Louis has abdicated the throne' is the argument of men who want a republic and are using the flight to get it. Source B was written after the King had accepted the constitution in September and the deputies had sworn to it, so the Assembly could not say it wanted a republic; it needed the King to be 'the defender of the Constitution'. Its address blames the émigrés rather than the King because the deputies were trying to bind him to their side, and the request is also a test he might fail. So A is good evidence of how far radical opinion had moved, but B, though more cautious, is better evidence of what the majority of the Assembly could openly say in 1791."

**Part (b) [25]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO3).**

*Support: the King's own actions.* **Source A** treats the flight as the King's own act of desertion that ended the "convention between a people and its king"; own knowledge: Varennes (20–21 June 1791), the declaration he left behind, and his devout Catholic opposition to the Civil Constitution of the Clergy. **Source C**, written a year later, says "reasonable confidence in Your Majesty's intentions" can rest only on "deeds", and warns that the people think the King a "friend and accomplice of the conspirators"; own knowledge: his vetoes of the decrees against émigrés and refractory priests (late 1791) and, in June 1792, of the decree for a camp of 20,000 volunteers; his dismissal of the Girondin ministers on 13 June; the court's secret appeals to foreign rulers; the Paris crowd invaded the Tuileries on 20 June and he did not withdraw the vetoes. **Source D** shows a King who had to flee to the Assembly and whose own gunners had "unloaded their cannon", and says that the "counterrevolutionary ministers" had been driven out: the court is seen as the enemy's party. Own knowledge: the Brunswick Manifesto (25 July) and the belief that the court had helped to write it.

*Challenge: other causes.* **Source B** blames the émigrés and the German princes, and shows that in November 1791 the Assembly was trying to keep the monarchy by giving the King a way to earn trust; it hints at a second cause, the threat of foreign war. **Source D** shows that the final blow was struck by force in Paris: the royal family had fled to the Assembly for "the safety which could no longer be found in the palace", and the King's own cannoneers had "simply unloaded their cannon"; only then was the King "suspended from all his functions and powers". The deputies were reacting to an insurrection, not judging the King's conduct. Own knowledge: the war (declared 20 April 1792) and its early defeats; "the fatherland in danger" (11 July); the arrival of the fédérés and the Marseillais; the Paris sections' ultimatum of 4 August and the Insurrectionary Commune of the night of 9–10 August; the radical clubs and the press; the fall in the assignat and high prices; a constitution that gave the King a veto and no way to settle a dispute with the Assembly (the flaw in the design, not only in the man); the Girondins' decision to make war, which put the King under suspicion.

*Evaluation of the sources (Level 5).* **A** is a radical club's address, written to push the Assembly towards a republic in the heat of the flight: strong evidence of how Varennes changed opinion in Paris, weak evidence of the national mood or of what made a republic happen a year later (the Assembly declined to depose the King in July 1791). **B** is an official address, formal in tone and aimed at persuading a King the deputies still needed; it is strong evidence of the Assembly's position and fears in November 1791, but its blame of the émigrés is partly a diplomatic device. **C** is a minister's letter, a political act as well as a warning: Roland was a Girondin who wanted the King to accept the war and the decrees, and the King dismissed him; the letter is strong evidence of the Girondins' view that the King's behaviour was the problem, but it is one party's view. **D** is a deputy's eyewitness letter, written on the night itself; strong evidence of what the deputy saw and of the speed with which authority passed to the sections, but he writes as a supporter of the insurrection (the royal family "with heads lowered like wet hens"), and he reports the King's flight to the Assembly from the outside and gives no causes.

*Judgement.* A strong answer decides what "mainly" means (the cause without which the monarchy would have survived, or the cause that decided the outcome) and weighs the sources against it. Support is strongest for the King's actions as the cause of the loss of trust (A and C), and the sources agree with each other more than they disagree on that. But C and D show that the loss of trust became overthrow only when the war and the Paris crowd forced the issue, and B shows that in 1791 the Assembly wanted to keep the King. So the sources support the view as an explanation of the King's isolation, and only in part as an explanation of the overthrow. A defensible judgement is that the King's actions were the main cause of the monarchy's weakness and the war and the sections the main cause of its fall; another is that the constitution made a clash inevitable. Accept either if the sources are used to reach it.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–5).** Describes the content of the sources, or writes an essay on the fall of the monarchy with the sources as illustration, or makes assertions without reference to them.
- **Level 2 (6–10).** Uses the sources on one side only (all four "show the King was to blame"), or tries both sides but misreads one (for example, treats B as an attack on the King).
- **Level 3 (11–15).** Uses the sources to support and to challenge; points are made from the content on both sides; comments on bias or reliability are generic ("Source A is from the radicals so it is biased"); the link to "mainly" is implicit.
- **Level 4 (16–20).** A developed answer that uses all four sources accurately, explains how each supports or challenges "mainly because of Louis XVI's own actions", and uses context to read them (the date of each, who is speaking, the war). May show nuance, such as seeing that C, a hostile critic, also proves how much the Girondins needed the King's cooperation.
- **Level 5 (21–25).** Evaluates the sources to reach a judgement: understanding of all four; detailed support and challenge; specific own knowledge used to assess nature, origin or purpose; says whether each source is stronger or weaker as evidence for this question; and a judgement on which side is stronger that answers "how far".

*A Level 5 judgement.* "On balance the sources support the claim less than they first seem to. A and C, the two sources that blame Louis, are partisan: a club that wanted a republic and a minister who wanted a war. Source D, the one eyewitness, shows the monarchy falling because armed men from the sections arrived and the King's own gunners would not fire, not because the deputies judged his conduct. And B shows that in November 1791 the Assembly was still trying to bind the King into the constitution. The King's own actions, above all Varennes and the vetoes, destroyed the trust on which a constitutional monarchy depended. But they were a necessary cause and not the decisive one: the monarchy survived Varennes and fell only when the war and the Paris sections made trust a matter of national survival."

## Section B: American option

:::try
Q: [40 marks] (#hist.us.gilded.2) **The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920.** Read the sources and then answer both parts of the question.

**Source A**

Whereas, by reason of unlawful obstructions, combinations, and assemblages of persons, it has become impracticable, in the judgment of the President, to enforce by the ordinary course of judicial proceedings the laws of the United States within the State of Illinois, and especially in the city of Chicago … and Whereas, for the purpose of enforcing the faithful execution of the laws of the United States and protecting its property and removing obstructions to the United States mails in the State and city aforesaid, the President has employed a part of the military forces of the United States: … I hereby warn all persons engaged in or in any way connected with such unlawful obstructions, combinations, and assemblages to disperse and retire peaceably to their respective abodes …

*Shortened from the proclamation of President Grover Cleveland, "Law and Order in the State of Illinois", 8 July 1894.*

**Source B**

We, the people of Pullman, who, by the greed and oppression of George M. Pullman, have been brought to a condition where starvation stares us in the face, do hereby appeal to you for aid in this our hour of need. We have been refused employment and have no means of leaving this vicinity, and our families are starving. Our places have been filled with workmen from all over the United States, brought here by the Pullman Company … There are over 1600 families here in destitution and want, and their condition is pitiful.

*Shortened from an appeal by a committee of the Pullman workers to John P. Altgeld, Governor of Illinois, Kensington, Illinois, 17 August 1894.*

**Source C**

Now these people live in your town and were your employees. … Assuming that they were wrong and foolish, they had yet served you long and well and you must feel some interest in them. They do not stand on the same footing with you, so that much must be overlooked. The State of Illinois has not the least desire to meddle in the affairs of your company, but it cannot allow a whole community within its borders to perish of hunger. … It seems to me that you would prefer to relieve the situation yourself, especially as it has just cost the State upwards of fifty thousand dollars to protect your property, and both the State and the public have suffered enormous loss and expense on account of disturbances that grew out of trouble between your company and its workmen. I am going to Chicago to-night to make a personal investigation before taking any official action.

*Shortened from a letter by John P. Altgeld, Governor of Illinois, to George M. Pullman, president of the Pullman Palace Car Company, 19 August 1894.*

**Source D**

The entire strength of the nation may be used to enforce in any part of the land the full and free exercise of all national powers and the security of all rights intrusted by the constitution to its care. The strong arm of the national government may be put forth to brush away all obstructions to the freedom of interstate commerce or the transportation of the mails. If the emergency arises, the army of the nation, and all its militia, are at the service of the nation, to compel obedience to its laws.

*From the opinion of the Supreme Court, delivered by Justice David J. Brewer, in* In re Debs*, 27 May 1895, the case of Eugene V. Debs and other leaders of the American Railway Union, who had been held in contempt of court for disobeying an injunction against the Pullman boycott.*

**Answer both parts of the question with reference to the sources.**

(a) Read Sources A and C. Compare these two sources as evidence about the role of government in the Pullman strike of 1894. [15]

(b) Read all of the sources. "In labour disputes in the 1890s, government acted in the interests of employers." How far do these sources support this view? [25]

A: **Mark scheme, Section B.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [15]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO3).**

*Similarities.* Both sources come from governments, and both speak of protecting property and the law while the strike has disturbed them. Source A says the government is "protecting its property" and "enforcing the faithful execution of the laws"; Source C says the state has spent "upwards of fifty thousand dollars to protect your property". Both see the disorder as costly and the cause of "obstructions" or "disturbances"; both are written by an official who has taken action.

*Differences.* Source A is a warning to the strikers and their supporters, backed by "the military forces of the United States": they are "unlawful" and must "disperse". Source C is a rebuke to the employer on behalf of the workers: they "served you long and well", Illinois "cannot allow a whole community … to perish of hunger", and the company should "relieve the situation yourself". A is about federal law, the mails and order; C is about distress and the employer's duty. A does not mention the workers' grievances or the company; C does not criticise the strikers' conduct. A speaks as the national government asserting its power over "the State of Illinois"; C speaks as a state government stressing that it has "not the least desire to meddle", and asking for the company to move.

*Why they differ (the step to Level 4).* **Date and events.** A was issued on 8 July 1894, in the middle of the dispute: the Pullman workers had struck on 11 May after wage cuts of about a quarter, the American Railway Union (ARU) had begun its boycott on 26 June, a court injunction had been granted, and federal troops had been sent to Chicago on 4 July. The law required a warning to disperse before troops were used. C was written six weeks later, on 19 August, when the strike had been broken, Debs had been arrested, and 1,600 families, according to the workers' committee, were destitute and had appealed to the governor (Source B is that appeal). **Author and purpose.** Cleveland, a Democratic President whose Attorney General, Richard Olney, had been a railroad lawyer, was justifying a controversial use of the army, over the objection of Governor Altgeld; he therefore puts it in terms of law, the mails and interstate commerce, and says nothing of wages. Altgeld, a Democrat elected governor of Illinois in 1892, had pardoned the Haymarket prisoners in 1893 and had protested against the troops; his letter is partly pressure on the company, partly a reminder that the state, not the federal government, was meeting the cost. **Value as evidence.** A is strong evidence of the stated purpose of federal action and of how the government wanted it seen, weak evidence of the motives of those who advised it. C is strong evidence that some governments sympathised with the workers, but it is the view of one state governor who was already opposed to Cleveland's policy.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–3).** Describes each source ("A is a proclamation about unlawful assemblies; C is a letter about starving workers").
- **Level 2 (4–7).** One difference or similarity, one-sided or without support ("A is on the side of the government and C is on the side of the workers").
- **Level 3 (8–11).** Both similarities and differences, supported by quotation; the explanation is generic ("the President and the governor have different views").
- **Level 4 (12–15).** A developed comparison explaining the differences through context (the date: during and after the strike; federal against state; Cleveland's and Altgeld's positions) and through source evaluation (a proclamation to deter against a letter to persuade).

*A Level 4 paragraph on the "why".* "The two sources look at the same strike but at different moments and from different levels of government. Source A, written on 8 July while troops were in Chicago, is a legal document: the 'unlawful obstructions' are those on the railroads and the mails, and the President says nothing about wages because his case for using the army depended on federal law, not on the dispute between the company and its workers. Source C was written six weeks later, when the strike was over and the workers were starving. Altgeld, who had already protested against the troops, uses the same word, 'protect', in a way that shows how little the state's protection had done for the people of Pullman. So A is good evidence of how the federal government explained its action, and C of how a hostile state governor judged its results; neither tells us what ordinary workers wanted, which Source B does."

**Part (b) [25]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO3).**

*Support.* **Source A** shows the federal government using troops against a strike; its stated aim is to protect the mails and the law, but the effect was to break the boycott: own knowledge, the injunction obtained by Olney under the Sherman Act, the arrest of Debs and the collapse of the ARU, and the fact that Olney was a former railroad lawyer and the railroads' General Managers' Association had pressed for action. **Source D** shows the Supreme Court approving the use of "the strong arm of the national government" and "the army of the nation" against an "obstruction"; own knowledge: Debs was sentenced to six months in jail for contempt and the decision upheld the use of injunctions against strikes, which became the employers' main weapon until the 1930s. **Source B** shows the outcome for the workers: "workmen from all over the United States" brought in to take their places, 1,600 families destitute, and they blame "the greed and oppression" of Pullman, not the government: the strike was lost, and the employer had won with the help of the law. Own knowledge: earlier uses of force (federal troops in 1877; state militia at Homestead in 1892).

*Challenge.* **Source C** shows a government on the workers' side: the governor of Illinois rebukes Pullman, wants the company itself to relieve the starving, and says that the state's protection of Pullman's property has been costly. **Source B**, by appealing to the governor "for aid", shows that the workers expected a government, at least a state government, to help them. **Sources A and D** state their purpose as the enforcement of federal law, the mails and interstate commerce, not the interests of the company; Cleveland also signed the law making Labor Day a national holiday in June 1894 and appointed the United States Strike Commission, whose report at the end of 1894 criticised the Pullman company's refusal to arbitrate. Own knowledge: Congress barred the federal government from hiring Pinkerton guards after Homestead (1893); the Contract Labor Law (1885); some states passed laws on hours and child labour; many Progressive laws (the Clayton Act of 1914) later recognised unions; the dispute turned on the idea that strikes were an attack on public order and property, which many middle-class Americans shared.

*Evaluation of the sources (Level 5).* **A** is a formal, official proclamation, written to justify and to deter; strong evidence of the government's declared reasons and of the law it relied on, but its silence about wages and Pullman is part of its purpose, so it is weak evidence of who benefited or of the motives of the Attorney General. **B** is a petition written by a committee to a governor, asking for relief: its language ("greed and oppression") is chosen to win sympathy, but the facts it cites, the dismissals and the numbers, are repeated by Altgeld in C, who was about to investigate them in person. **C** is a governor's letter to an employer, strong evidence of the attitude of a state government and the cost of the dispute to it, but written by a man who was a known opponent of the use of troops and who was a politician as well as a defender of labour. **D** is a judgment of the highest court, reasoned and formal and strong evidence of the legal position; its concern is the power of the national government, and the effect on the union is a consequence rather than the Court's stated aim.

*Judgement.* A strong answer sets a test for "acted in the interests of employers": intention, effect, or the balance of actions across all governments. On the effect of federal action, A, B and D support the view: the boycott was broken, Debs was jailed and the injunction became the employers' weapon. On intention and the whole of government action, the support is weaker: Cleveland and the Court rested on federal power and the mails, C shows a state siding with the workers, and the Strike Commission criticised the employer. A good judgement is that the federal government's actions helped employers in effect and (through Olney's links) partly in intention, so the view is largely supported for federal action, but it overstates "government" as a whole.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–5).** Describes the sources, writes an essay on the Pullman strike or on labour in the 1890s using the sources as illustration, or asserts without using them.
- **Level 2 (6–10).** Uses the sources on one side only (all four "show government on the employers' side"), or misreads one (for example, takes C as supporting the view because it mentions protecting property).
- **Level 3 (11–15).** Support and challenge from the sources on both sides; comments on bias are generic ("Altgeld was pro-labour"); the link to the statement is implicit.
- **Level 4 (16–20).** Uses all four sources accurately, explains how each supports or challenges "government acted in the interests of employers", and uses context to read them (federal against state; the date of each; the injunction; the mails). May show nuance, such as seeing that A's silence about wages is part of its purpose.
- **Level 5 (21–25).** Evaluates the sources to reach a judgement: all four understood; detailed explanation of support and challenge; specific own knowledge used to assess nature, origin or purpose; assesses whether each is stronger or weaker evidence for this question; a judgement on which side is stronger that answers "how far".

*A Level 5 judgement.* "The sources support the view more strongly about what the federal government did than about why it did it, and say less about government as a whole. A and D show troops and a court used to remove a boycott, and B shows the result: replaced workers and hungry families. But Cleveland and Brewer both argue from the mails, interstate commerce and federal law, and a proclamation that says nothing of wages is not evidence of a wish to help the company, only of what it chose to say. Source C shows that a government could be on the other side, and it is the only source written by an official in answer to the Pullman workers' own appeal. The honest conclusion is that federal power was used in a way that helped employers, whatever its stated purpose, and that this was possible because the law treated a boycott as an obstruction while it had no law on the employers' conduct; but 'government' was divided, and the view is true of Washington more than of Springfield."

## Section C: International option

:::try
Q: [40 marks] (#hist.int.empire.1) **Imperialism and the emergence of world powers, c.1870–1918.** Read the sources and then answer both parts of the question.

**Source A**

All the men and all the money used in civilizing and Christianizing the New Hebrides have been British. … it would be unwise to let any other power now take possession and reap the fruits of all this British outlay. … The islands on this group are generally very rich in soil and in tropical products so that if a possession of Great Britain … they would soon, and for ages to come, become rich sources of tropical wealth to these colonies … Because if any other nation takes possession of them, their excellent and spacious harbors … would in time of war make them dangerous to British interests and commerce in the South Seas and her colonies. … Whether looked at in the interests of humanity, or of Christianity, or commercially, or politically, sure it is most desirable that they should at once be British possessions.

*Shortened from a letter published by the Rev. John G. Paton of the New Hebrides Mission, urging the British government to annex the New Hebrides, in* Accounts and Papers *(London: HMSO, 1883).*

**Source B**

In spite of the fact that we have no such fleet as we should have, we have conquered for ourselves a place in the sun. It will now be my task to see to it that this place in the sun shall remain our undisputed possession, in order that the sun's rays may fall fruitfully upon our activity and trade in foreign parts, that our industry and agriculture may develop within the state and our sailing sports upon the water, for our future lies upon the water. … May it be the function of my Hansa during many years of peace to protect and advance commerce and trade!

*Shortened from a speech by Kaiser Wilhelm II to the North German Regatta Association, 1901, in the translation printed in C. Gauss,* The German Kaiser as Shown in His Public Utterances *(New York, 1915).*

**Source C**

I have had it out with George [Wyndham, parliamentary under-secretary in the War Office] about Fashoda. He states the English case with brutal frankness. "The day of talking," he says, "about legality in Africa is over, all the international law there is there consists of interest and understandings. It is generally agreed by all the powers that the end of African operations is to 'civilize' it in the interests of Europe, and that to gain that end all means are good. … England intends to do it on the Nile … our end, which is the railway from Cairo to the Cape. We don't care whether the Nile is called English or Egyptian or what it is called, but we mean to have it and we don't mean the French to have it."

*Shortened from the diary of Wilfrid Scawen Blunt, an English poet and critic of imperial policy, entry for 17 October 1898, in his* My Diaries *(1921), in the text as modernised in the Internet Modern History Sourcebook.*

**Source D**

… But while drawing the juices out of this gigantic continent, and, wherever possible, holding hundreds of millions in a state of economic slavery, do the pioneers of civilization hope for final success? … The idea of invading a complex foreign life, of using Asia as a tool for the advancement of the selfish interests of modern, so-called civilized, mankind, was repugnant to us. … The strangers have dethroned and oppressed the East. Coming here to live and make money, they do not find a home …

*Shortened from Prince E. Ukhtomskii, a Russian writer who accompanied the Tsarevich Nicholas on his journey through Asia in 1890–91, in* Travels in the East of Nicholas II, Emperor of Russia, When Cesarewitch, 1890–1891 *(London, 1896).*

**Answer both parts of the question with reference to the sources.**

(a) Read Sources A and C. Compare these two sources as evidence about the motives for imperial expansion. [15]

(b) Read all of the sources. "The main motive for European imperial expansion was economic gain." How far do these sources support this view? [25]

A: **Mark scheme, Section C.** Marks are never deducted. Choose the best-fit level, then the top mark if the answer meets it convincingly, a middle mark if adequately, the lowest if it just meets it. Accept any other valid response.

**Part (a) [15]: indicative content (Table A, AO1 and AO3).**

*Similarities.* Both sources see other European powers as a reason to act. Source A fears that "any other power" will "take possession" and make the islands "dangerous to British interests and commerce"; Source C says the French must be kept out: "we don't mean the French to have it". Both link the high-sounding reasons to interests: A lists "humanity" and "Christianity" next to "commercially, or politically" and "British interests"; C says the end is "to 'civilize' it in the interests of Europe". Both want territory for Britain and both include a practical aim, harbours and trade in A and a railway "from Cairo to the Cape" in C.

*Differences.* Source A is a public appeal. It puts religion and humanity first ("civilizing and Christianizing"; "the interests of humanity, or of Christianity"), then money ("rich sources of tropical wealth", "British outlay") and strategy ("harbors"); it wants an official decision to annex some islands. Source C is a private diary that reports a minister's blunt view: "civilize" is in quotation marks, "all means are good", and "legality" does not matter, because "all the international law there is there consists of interest and understandings". A is about a small island group in the South Pacific; C is about a continent and the Nile, and about a rivalry which could lead to war. A argues that Britain has earned the right by its missionaries' "outlay"; C does not argue at all, it states what the government means to do.

*Why they differ (the step to Level 4).* **Date and events.** A is from 1883, before the Scramble for Africa had properly begun, when British governments were reluctant to take on new colonies and missionaries and Australian colonists were lobbying them; it asks Britain to forestall France, which already held New Caledonia. It failed: in the New Hebrides Britain and France set up a joint naval commission in 1887 and agreed to govern jointly in 1906. C was written in October 1898, during the Fashoda crisis, when a French expedition under Marchand, which had reached Fashoda on 10 July, faced Kitchener, who arrived on 19 September; Britain and France came close to war before France gave way. By then the powers were in a race to claim territory. **Author and purpose.** The missionaries want to move a government and so use every argument that might: a religious and humanitarian case first, then the commercial and strategic. Blunt was a critic of imperial policy who wrote in private, and he probably heard Wyndham's frankness as proof of what he already believed; he also reports it from memory and with his own wording ("brutal frankness"). **Value as evidence.** A is good evidence of the range of arguments used to win support for expansion and of local pressure from below, weaker on what governments thought; C is good evidence of the strategic reasoning of one member of a government in a crisis, but it is one man's words as recorded by a hostile witness.

**Part (a): levels (Table A).**
- **Level 1 (1–3).** Describes the content of each source ("A is about missionaries and the New Hebrides; C is about Fashoda") or makes an undeveloped point ("one is a letter and one is a diary").
- **Level 2 (4–7).** Identifies similarities or differences, but one-sided, or asserts both without quotation ("A is about religion and C is about the French").
- **Level 3 (8–11).** Both similarities and differences, supported from the sources; the explanation is generic ("A is by missionaries so it is biased; C is by someone who dislikes empire").
- **Level 4 (12–15).** A developed comparison that explains why the sources agree and differ through context (the Pacific in 1883 and Fashoda in 1898; a public appeal and a private diary) and source evaluation (who wrote, to whom and why), and says what each is worth as evidence of motives.

*A Level 4 paragraph on the "why".* "The sources differ because one is written to persuade and the other is a private record. Source A is a public appeal by missionaries in 1883 asking the government to annex islands, so it uses every argument that might work: humanity and Christianity first, then trade ('tropical wealth') and strategy ('harbors'). Source C is Blunt's private diary of a conversation at the height of the Fashoda crisis, and it records a minister saying openly what A could not: that 'civilize' is a word to put in quotation marks and that 'interests' and rivalry with France decide the matter. This does not prove that the missionaries were insincere, nor that Wyndham spoke for the whole government, since Blunt hated imperialism and reports the conversation in his own words. But they agree on one thing: whatever the language, the fear that 'any other power' would take the territory first was a strong motive."

**Part (b) [25]: indicative content (Table B, AO1 and AO3).**

*Support: economic gain.* **Source A** puts commerce and wealth among its arguments: the islands are "very rich in soil and in tropical products" and would become "rich sources of tropical wealth", and "British outlay" should not be lost to a rival. **Source B** speaks of "our activity and trade in foreign parts", of "industry and agriculture" and of the duty "to protect and advance commerce and trade". **Source D** says that Europeans are "drawing the juices out of this gigantic continent" and have come "to live and make money". **Source C** speaks of "the railway from Cairo to the Cape", which was about trade and communications as well as control. Own knowledge: the long depression from 1873 and the rise of tariffs, which made governments and businessmen look for markets and raw materials; rubber, palm oil, diamonds (Kimberley) and gold (the Witwatersrand, from 1886); the chartered companies (the Royal Niger Company, the British South Africa Company); Leopold's Congo; Hobson's argument in *Imperialism* (1902) that investors pushed governments into empire.

*Challenge: other motives.* **Source B** ties trade to a navy and to national status: "a place in the sun" and "our undisputed possession"; own knowledge: *Weltpolitik*, the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900, and Bülow's use of the phrase in the Reichstag in December 1897. **Source C** makes rivalry with France the point: "we don't mean the French to have it", and puts law aside; own knowledge: Fashoda, the Suez Canal and the Cape route, and the fear of rivals that drove the speed of the Scramble. **Source A** also gives religion and humanity as reasons and strategy ("harbors" for war). **Source D**, though it concentrates on money, also describes the Europeans as having "dethroned and oppressed the East", which suggests a motive of power as well as profit. Own knowledge: religion and the "civilising mission" (Livingstone's "Christianity, commerce and civilisation", 1857; Ferry in 1885); national prestige and the sense that Britain, France and Germany must not be left behind; domestic politics and the popularity of empire; the "men on the spot" who made policy; the fact that most colonies brought small returns and most British and French investment went elsewhere (so economic gain was often the hope and not the result).

*Evaluation of the sources (Level 5).* **A** is a missionary letter, intended to persuade the government; it is strong evidence of the arguments that campaigners thought would work (and so of what they believed the government would care about), weak evidence of why governments acted: Britain did not annex the islands. **B** is a public speech to a sailing association in the middle of the campaign for a bigger navy; strong evidence of how the Kaiser wished to present German expansion, as trade protected by a fleet, but a speech by a ruler to an audience is a poor guide to private decisions. **C** is a private diary of an opponent of empire reporting a minister's words; strong evidence of strategic thinking in a crisis, but second-hand and unchecked, and concerned with one region and moment. **D** is the work of a Russian writer who accompanied the Tsarevich on a tour that ended with the start of the Trans-Siberian Railway, published in 1896: he is attacking Western Europeans' "economic slavery" and "selfish interests" in order to contrast them with a Russian mission in Asia, so he is strong evidence of how a rival power saw European motives, but a hostile and self-interested witness.

*Judgement.* A strong answer defines "main" (the most frequent, the most powerful, or the cause that explains the timing) and weighs the sources against it. Economic gain appears in A, B and D, but never alone: A combines it with religion and strategy, B with national status and the navy, and C, the most candid source, puts rivalry first and does not mention money. So the sources support the view that economic gain was a major motive, and challenge the claim that it was the main one: the evidence in C and B suggests that national rivalry, once the race began, explains the speed and the scale. A good judgement is that economic ideas made empire attractive and national rivalry made it urgent; another is that different motives mattered for different powers and places. Accept any judgement supported by the sources and own knowledge.

**Part (b): levels (Table B).**
- **Level 1 (1–5).** Describes the content of the sources, or writes an essay on the causes of imperialism with the sources as illustration, or asserts without using them.
- **Level 2 (6–10).** Uses the sources on one side only ("all four mention money"), or misreads one (for example, takes C as a source about trade because of the railway).
- **Level 3 (11–15).** Uses the sources to support and challenge; comments on bias or reliability are generic ("Source A is by missionaries so it is biased"); the link to "main" is implicit.
- **Level 4 (16–20).** Uses all four sources accurately and explains how each supports or challenges the view, using context (1883, 1898, the navy campaign) to interpret them; may show nuance, such as seeing that A uses commerce because it was an argument that persuaded governments, whatever the missionaries' own motives.
- **Level 5 (21–25).** Evaluates the sources to reach a judgement: all four understood; detailed explanation of support and challenge; specific own knowledge used to assess nature, origin or purpose; assesses whether each is stronger or weaker evidence of motives; and a judgement that answers "how far".

*A Level 5 judgement.* "The four sources agree that money mattered and disagree about whether it came first. Three of them mention trade or wealth, but each does so in a text written to persuade: a missionary lobbying for annexation, a Kaiser raising support for a navy, and a Russian prince who wanted Asia to look like his empire's future. The one source that is private and frank, Source C, reports a government that is thinking about the French and the Nile, not about profit. That does not prove that economic motives were weak, since own knowledge of the depression, the tariffs and the search for markets shows that they made empire attractive. But the speed of the Scramble after 1884, with Fashoda as its crisis, is better explained by the fear of rivals. On this evidence economic gain was an important motive and a poor explanation of the timing, so 'main' is too strong."

## After the paper: marking yourself

**Mark each part separately, using the levels in the mark scheme.** First decide the level that best fits the whole answer, then place it within the level: the top mark if the answer meets the level convincingly, the middle if adequately, the lowest if it only just meets it. Do not add a mark for each good point: the real mark scheme rewards the quality of the whole answer. Then add your two marks (part (a) out of 15 and part (b) out of 25) and enter the total out of 40 in the box under the question you answered; leave the other two boxes blank. Cambridge sets its grade boundaries after every exam series, so this course gives no fixed grade for a mark: use the level of each part instead.

| Part (a) level | What it tells you | What to practise next |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (1–3) | You described the two sources one after the other | Write two columns, "alike" and "different", before you write a word. Take one quotation for each point |
| 2 (4–7) | You found one kind of point, or made points without quotations | Look for the other kind of point (if you found differences, look for similarities) and quote each time |
| 3 (8–11) | You compared well but did not explain why | Add a "because" to every difference: date, author, audience, purpose |
| 4 (12–15) | You explained the differences through context and evaluation | Finish with a sentence on what each source is worth as evidence for the question set |

| Part (b) level | What it tells you | What to practise next |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (1–5) | You wrote about the topic or described the sources | Before writing, list for each source: does it support or challenge the statement, and the words that show it |
| 2 (6–10) | You used the sources on one side only, or misread one | Find at least one source that challenges the statement; read each source's last sentence again |
| 3 (11–15) | You used both sides but your comments on reliability were generic | Replace "it is biased" with the author, the date, the purpose and what difference that makes to this question |
| 4 (16–20) | You used all the sources and context well | Say whether each source is stronger or weaker evidence for this question, and weigh the two sides |
| 5 (21–25) | You evaluated the sources and reached a judgement | Check that your judgement answers "how far" and says why one side is stronger |

:::warn The five mistakes that cost the most marks on this paper
1. **Describing instead of comparing.** In part (a) a paragraph on Source A followed by a paragraph on Source B scores at Level 1 or 2, however accurate. Compare in the same sentence: "Source A …, whereas Source B …".
2. **Using Sources C and D in part (a), or only two sources in part (b).** Part (a) names two sources; part (b) needs all four. Leaving a source out caps the level.
3. **Treating a source as one-sided because of its label.** Every source can support and challenge a statement. A government source can be critical of the government, and a hostile source can contain facts.
4. **Generic reliability comments.** "It is biased because the author is X" is a Level 3 comment. Say who wrote it, when, to whom and why, and then say what it is good and bad evidence for.
5. **No judgement.** Part (b) asks "how far". The last paragraph must say how far, and which side of the evidence is stronger, and why.
:::
