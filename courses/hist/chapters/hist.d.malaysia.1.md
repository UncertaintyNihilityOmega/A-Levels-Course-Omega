:::why
In 1941 Malaya was one of Britain's most valuable possessions. It produced a large part of the world's tin and rubber, it was guarded by the naval base at Singapore, and it was ruled by a handful of British officials with the agreement of nine Malay sultans. Within sixteen years it was independent, and within twenty-two it had become part of a new country, Malaysia. This is the first of four chapters on that route to independence, and it asks what the British left behind them and what the Japanese occupation did to it. The answer matters for everything that follows: a country in which the three main communities lived apart, in which most people did not yet think of themselves as one nation, and in which the war had broken the idea that Britain could not be defeated. The key question asks **how widespread** nationalism and hostility to colonial rule were, so this chapter is about numbers of people as well as about ideas: who felt hostile to whom, and how many of them.
:::

## What is the question really asking?

The key question asks about **growth** ("how widespread was the growth of nationalism") and about **hostility to colonial rule**, over a long period, to 1945. That makes four demands on you.
1. **Whose nationalism?** In 1941 there was not one nationalism in Malaya but several: a Malay nationalism, a Chinese nationalism whose heart was in China, an Indian nationalism whose heart was in India, and a communist movement that wanted a revolution. Did any of them want a single independent Malaya?
2. **How widespread?** This is a question of numbers and reach: a few hundred students and teachers, or tens of thousands of villagers?
3. **Nationalism or only hostility?** A person can dislike the British or the Japanese without wanting a nation of their own. Keep the two apart.
4. **Before or during the occupation?** Did the British years produce nationalism, or did the Japanese occupation do it? This is the question most essays turn on.

The last section, "How widespread was nationalism by 1945?", comes back to these.

:::def Malaya
In this course, the Malay peninsula and its British-ruled territories, taken together: the Straits Settlements, the Federated Malay States and the Unfederated Malay States. Singapore, which sits at its southern tip, was part of British Malaya in these years. **Malaysia** is a different, later thing: the federation formed on 16 September 1963 (see [The route to independence (2): the formation of Malaysia](#hist.d.malaysia.2)). Use "Malaya" for events up to 1963 and "Malaysia" only from then on.
:::

:::def Nationalism
The belief that a people with a shared identity (a language, a history, a culture or a homeland) should govern themselves in a state of their own, and the movement that works for it. In Malaya the difficulty was to decide who the nation was: all who lived in Malaya, or the Malays alone.
:::

:::note Terms for the communities
The British counted people by race, and the census of 1947 gave the main groups as Malays, Chinese, Indians and others. This chapter uses those words as historical terms, as the sources do. **Malay** here means the people who spoke Malay, were Muslims and were subjects of a sultan, including settlers from Sumatra, Java and elsewhere in the archipelago who had taken Malay ways. **Chinese** and **Indian** mean people of Chinese or Indian descent, whether they had been born in Malaya or had come there to work. Each group was divided inside (Hokkien, Cantonese and Hakka Chinese; Tamils and Punjabis), and many people in each group were born in Malaya and had no wish to leave it. The groups were not blocs that all thought alike, and the best essays say so.
:::

## The nature of colonial rule

### Three groups of territory

Britain did not rule Malaya as one colony. By 1914 it ruled the peninsula in **three different ways**, which grew up one after another, and the pattern is the key to understanding what followed.

**The Straits Settlements.** Britain took Penang from the Sultan of Kedah in 1786, founded Singapore in 1819 and received Malacca from the Dutch under the Anglo-Dutch Treaty of 1824. The three were joined as the Straits Settlements in 1826 and became a **Crown colony**, ruled directly from London, in 1867. They were trading ports with large Chinese populations and many Chinese, Indian and Eurasian families who had lived there for generations. Singapore became the most important port in the region and the headquarters of British power in South-East Asia.

**The Federated Malay States.** In the 1860s and 1870s Chinese miners fought one another over the tin of Perak and Selangor and Malay chiefs fought over the succession. British traders in Singapore wanted order. In **January 1874** Sir Andrew Clarke, Governor of the Straits Settlements, made the **Treaty of Pangkor** with the claimant to the throne of Perak. The Sultan agreed to accept a British **Resident** whose advice had to be asked and followed on everything except Malay religion and custom. Selangor and Negeri Sembilan took Residents in the same year and Pahang in 1888. In 1895–96 the four states were joined as the **Federated Malay States (FMS)**, with a Resident-General (later called the Chief Secretary) at Kuala Lumpur. The sultans kept their thrones, their ceremonies and their authority over Islam, but the British ran the government, the courts, the police and the finances. This is **indirect rule**: the Residents did not command, they "advised", and the sultans signed.

**The Unfederated Malay States.** The remaining states were Johor in the south and four in the north, Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu. They were not joined to the FMS and for a long time Britain did not control them. How it gained them is the next topic.

### Why Britain gained protection of the Unfederated Malay States: the Bangkok Treaty, 1909

The four northern states, Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu, had been vassals of Siam (Thailand) for centuries and sent it tribute in the form of gold and silver flowers. In the nineteenth century Siam tried to turn this loose overlordship into real control. This worried Britain, which already ruled the states to their south and to their west.

On **10 March 1909** the British Minister in Bangkok, Sir Ralph Paget, and the Siamese Foreign Minister, Prince Devawongse, signed the **Bangkok Treaty** (also called the Anglo-Siamese Treaty of 1909). Siam gave up "all rights of suzerainty, protection, administration and control" over Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah and Perlis and the islands next to them. In return Britain gave up some of the special legal rights that British subjects had in Siam, and the Federated Malay States lent Siam money for a railway in the south. Siam kept Patani and the districts around it. The Siamese were not asked what the people of the four states thought.

**Why did Britain want the states, and why did Siam agree?** Historians give a mixture of reasons.
- **Security.** Britain's position in Malaya depended on the sea route through the Strait of Malacca and the naval base and port at Singapore. It did not want another European power to gain a foothold on the peninsula or the coast beside it, and the north was the weakest point. An earlier secret convention of 1897 had already bound Siam not to cede territory south of a certain line on the peninsula to a third power without British consent. The treaty of 1909 completed this plan by bringing the territory itself under British protection.
- **Economic interest.** British planters and miners were already at work in the northern states, with concessions in Kelantan in particular, and tin and rubber were the base of Malaya's wealth. British businessmen wanted law, courts and land titles of the kind that the FMS had.
- **Siam's weakness.** Siam wanted the British to give up their special legal privileges, which it saw as an insult to a sovereign state, and it wanted money for railway building. It also had to fear the French on its eastern frontier. Giving up distant tributary states cost it less than the concessions were worth.

**Why does the treaty matter?** Its importance has three parts.
1. It **completed British control** of the peninsula. After 1909, with Johor to follow, no Malay state was outside the British system.
2. It created the **Unfederated Malay States (UMS)**, five states with five different treaties, which gave the British a way to rule that was different again from the FMS. The sultans of the UMS kept more of their internal authority, and each state took a British Adviser, in Johor's case a General Adviser accepted in **1914**. Johor, which had been the most independent of the Malay states, was the last to give way.
3. It **kept Malaya divided**. The UMS were not joined to the FMS, so there were now three administrations, with different laws, different budgets and different attitudes. In the Second World War the four northern states were handed to Thailand again (1943), and the lines of 1909 became a political fact that Malay nationalists and the British alike had to deal with after 1945.

:::key Why the Bangkok Treaty of 1909 mattered
1. It brought **the last northern Malay states** under British protection, completing the British hold on the peninsula (Johor followed in 1914).
2. It created the **Unfederated Malay States**, a third kind of British rule, with the sultans keeping more power than in the FMS.
3. It left Malaya **in three administrative parts**, which made later unification, by Clementi or by the Malayan Union, a political problem as well as an administrative one.
:::

### Divide and rule

**Divide and rule** is the old idea that a colonial power keeps control by keeping its subjects apart. In British Malaya the historians' question is whether it was a **policy** (an intention) or a **result** (the outcome of policies made for other reasons). The facts are not in doubt.

- **Different jobs.** The British protected the **Malays as farmers and as rulers**. The Malay Reservation Enactment of 1913 in the FMS set aside land that could only be owned by Malays. Malays were recruited to the Malay Administrative Service, and the sons of aristocrats were taught English at the Malay College at Kuala Kangsar (1905) to become junior officials. **Chinese** worked as miners, traders and shopkeepers and soon ran much of the economy of the towns. **Indians** came in large numbers, mostly Tamils from southern India, to work on the rubber estates and on the railways and roads, and also as clerks and doctors.
- **Different schools.** Malay children could attend village schools that taught in Malay and gave a few years of basic schooling, designed to fit them for rural life rather than for the towns. Chinese children went to Chinese-language schools run and paid for by their own communities, many of them following the curriculum of China, and Indian children on the estates had Tamil schools, where they had schools at all. English schools were in the towns and led to jobs in government and business. There was no common school and no common language.
- **Different places.** Malays lived in villages, Chinese in the towns and mining areas, Indians on the estates. Each community lived with its own customs, associations and newspapers.

Was it a plan? The British said they were **protecting the Malays** and obeying the treaties with the sultans, and they were also keeping costs low. Critics, then and since, point out that the result was to keep the three communities apart, to turn Malays into rural subjects of the sultans, and to leave economic power with Chinese and British businessmen. The better answer is that the policy was **a mixture of protection, economy and habit** which, whatever its purpose, had the **effect** of dividing. It also gave each community a reason for suspicion: Malays feared the economic strength of the immigrants and Chinese and Indians resented a state that offered them no part in its government.

### Immigration and the polarisation of society

The British economy in Malaya needed workers who could not be found among the Malay villagers. Tin mining boomed from the 1840s and rubber, planted from about 1900, boomed with the car industry. The British and Chinese owners of the mines and plantations encouraged the migration of workers from China and India, and the Chinese and Indian communities grew very fast.

By the 1947 census the population of Malaya was divided almost into halves: about **49.5 per cent Malays**, **38.4 per cent Chinese**, **10.8 per cent Indians** and 1.3 per cent others. The Chinese share had been about 29 per cent from 1901 to 1921, then 34 per cent in 1931 and 38 per cent in 1947. Taken together, non-Malays were about 50.5 per cent of the population, slightly more than the Malays.

Look at the chart. Malays were the largest group but not the majority of the whole, and in many towns, in the tin mines and in Singapore the Chinese were the largest group, often the majority. A Malay who looked at the towns saw a country that was being taken from him. A Chinese or Indian who was born in Malaya, who had never seen China or India, saw that he was treated as a visitor.

**Polarisation** means that people divide into two or more camps and move apart. In Malaya it had these causes.
1. **Economic.** Jobs, trades and land followed community lines. Malays were farmers, fishermen and junior officials, Chinese were miners and traders, Indians were estate workers.
2. **Educational.** Four kinds of school taught in four languages, with different books and different aims.
3. **Political.** The British gave a say in government only to the sultans and to a few Straits-born and English-educated Chinese. Each community's loyalty pointed elsewhere: Malays to their sultans, many Chinese to China (the Kuomintang had branches in Malaya in the 1920s, and the British acted against them), many Indians to India.
4. **Controls on immigration.** In the Depression the British cut immigration. In the 1930s they limited Chinese entry, and in 1938 the Indian government itself banned the recruitment of unskilled labourers for Malaya. These controls slowed the growth but did not undo the division.

:::warn Do not call it a Malay country
Do not describe British Malaya as a Malay country with some immigrants in it. In 1941 a large share of the Chinese and Indian population had been born in Malaya and many of the rest were permanent settlers. But do not describe it as a mixed society either: the three communities lived largely apart, and few people thought of **Malayan** as an identity. "Polarised" fits better than either.
:::

### Sir Cecil Clementi and the failure of Malayanisation

Sir Cecil Clementi was Governor of the Straits Settlements and High Commissioner for the Malay States from **5 February 1930** to **17 February 1934**. He came with a plan, and it failed. This part of the syllabus is easy to misunderstand, so take the story in steps.

**What he wanted.** The FMS had grown so centralised that the Chief Secretary at Kuala Lumpur, a British official, held most of the power, and the sultans and their state councils felt that the states had been turned into districts. Clementi's aim was **decentralisation**: to hand powers back from Kuala Lumpur to the states, which would please the sultans, with the further aim of bringing the three groups of British territory closer together into a wider Malayan union, for which the syllabus uses the word **Malayanisation**. In August 1931 he announced his scheme at the **Sri Menanti** durbar of the rulers of the FMS. It proposed to end the post of Chief Secretary and to give the state councils more power. He saw this as a first step to a **Pan-Malayan federation** or customs union that would bring in the Unfederated Malay States and perhaps the Straits Settlements.

**Why it failed.** Clementi's plan met opposition from several sides.
- **Business.** Businessmen in the FMS and in Singapore wanted a strong central government in Kuala Lumpur, a single customs system and efficient administration, and they feared that decentralisation would bring a set of small, slow and costly state governments. When a customs union was proposed, 85 of the 111 members of the Singapore Chamber of Commerce signed a memorandum against it, about 77 per cent.
- **Chinese opinion.** Chinese in the FMS, who had no voice in the Malay rulers' councils, feared that handing powers to the states would weaken their position. His education and immigration policies and his action against the Kuomintang had already angered prominent Chinese leaders.
- **The Unfederated Malay States.** The rulers of Johor and the northern states did not want to be drawn into a union under Kuala Lumpur or under the High Commissioner. They had accepted British advice, not British federation, and they feared the loss of their independence.
- **The Colonial Office.** London sent Sir Samuel Wilson to report. His report of 1933 largely supported Clementi's aims but recommended going slowly, and with the Depression and opposition the plan stalled. Clementi left because of ill health in February 1934, and by late 1933 the scheme was reported to be dead.

**What it showed.** Three points matter for the key question.
1. Britain wanted **a more united Malaya** more than twenty years before the war, so the Malayan Union of 1946 (see [The route to independence (2)](#hist.d.malaysia.2)) was not a sudden idea. The same pressure, from the same business interests, returned after the war.
2. Every community that resisted did so to defend **its own interest**: the rulers their power, Chinese businessmen their trade and their voice. Malaya had no group that wanted unity for its own sake, and so no ground for a **Malayan nationalism**.
3. The scheme gave Malays a **foretaste**. Many saw that a plan to unite Malaya under British direction could threaten their rulers and their status as the people of the land. The same fear came back, in a much stronger form, in 1946.

:::key Three features of British rule that shaped nationalism
1. **Three kinds of administration** (Straits Settlements, FMS, UMS), with no Malayan state to inherit and no Malayan citizenship.
2. **Divide and rule in effect**: separate jobs, schools and places for Malays, Chinese and Indians; the Malays protected as rural subjects of the sultans.
3. **Mass immigration**: by 1947 about half of Malaya's people were not Malays, and few of the communities thought of the others as fellow-citizens. Clementi's attempt to bring the territories together failed in 1931–34 for lack of any group that wanted it.
:::

## The growth of nationalism before 1941

### Several nationalisms, not one

To ask "how widespread" you first need to count the movements. Before the Japanese came there were four main kinds of political feeling, and they pointed in different directions.
- **Malay feeling**: religious reform, Malay associations and, on the left, the KMM. It was about the position of Malays in their own land.
- **Chinese feeling**: for many Chinese, especially the China-born, the cause was China, where there was a republic from 1911 and a war with Japan from 1937. Money was raised in Malaya to help China, and boycotts of Japanese goods were organised.
- **Indian feeling**: a smaller group of Indian associations, concerned with the treatment of estate workers and with the independence of India.
- **Communism**: the **Malayan Communist Party (MCP)**, founded in 1930, was run by Chinese, drew mainly on Chinese workers and by 1941 was the only party that tried to bring Chinese, Indians and Malays together, though its Malay and Indian members were few.

None of them in 1941 asked for an independent, multi-racial Malaya. The first real nationalism was Malay, and it grew in three streams.

### The Kaum Muda

The **Kaum Muda** (the "Young Group") were Muslim reformers who in the early twentieth century argued that Malays had fallen behind because they clung to custom and to unquestioned teachers, and that they should return to the Koran and the traditions of the Prophet, learn modern subjects, and use reason. They were influenced by reformers in Egypt, notably Muhammad Abduh and his follower Rashid Rida, whose journal *al-Manar* circulated in the Muslim world. Their leaders in Malaya included Sheikh Tahir Jalaluddin and **Syed Sheikh al-Hadi**, and in **1906** they began a journal, ***al-Imam***, in Singapore. It ran until 1908 and was followed by other papers, such as *al-Ikhwan*, which al-Hadi published in Penang, and by schools.

The **Kaum Tua** ("Old Group"), the traditional religious teachers who were close to the sultans and the state religious officials, opposed them, and the quarrel was bitter and was fought in print and in the mosques.

**How important were they?** There are three answers. First, they began a **public debate** among Malays about why they were poor and powerless, and so they trained a generation to think of a Malay community with a need for reform. William Roff, in *The Origins of Malay Nationalism* (1967), treats this and the Malay press as the beginning of the story. Second, they challenged the **authority of the sultans' religious establishment**, which the British had left in place, so in a small way they were a challenge to the traditional order. Third, they were **few and mostly urban**: their paper circulated in the Straits Settlements and among traders and teachers, not in most Malay villages, and the rulers and the British (who left religion alone and protected the established religious officials) did not support them. Kaum Muda was a movement of ideas, not of numbers.

:::def Kaum Muda
The "Young Group": Malay Muslim reformers of the early twentieth century, led by men such as Syed Sheikh al-Hadi and Sheikh Tahir Jalaluddin and publishing *al-Imam* (1906–08), who argued for religious reform, modern education and Malay self-improvement. They were opposed by the traditionalist Kaum Tua. They matter as the first challenge to the established order among Malays, though their reach was narrow.
:::

### English-educated Malays and the Malay associations

A second stream came from Malays with an **English education**: the sons of aristocrats from the Malay College at Kuala Kangsar and others from English schools, who became junior officials, teachers, clerks and journalists. They were more at home in the colonial system than the village-school graduates, and many were loyal to the British and to their rulers. But they were also well placed to see how little the British gave them. They noticed that senior jobs in the Malayan Civil Service went to Britons, and many of the clerks and shopkeepers around them were Chinese and Indian. Their **opposition** to the British was mostly that of insiders: they wanted a larger share of the posts and a surer protection of Malay interests, rather than the end of British rule. It was also opposition to **non-Malay competition**, and to the way the British, as they saw it, let immigrants into government and the economy.

They formed **associations**, which were the first modern Malay political bodies. The **Kesatuan Melayu Singapura** (Singapore Malay Union) was founded on 14 May 1926, with Mohamed Eunos Abdullah, a Malay newspaper editor, as its first president. In the late 1930s there were Malay associations in nearly every state, such as the Persatuan Melayu Selangor, which in several states took the lead in asking for more places for Malays in government. In 1939 **Utusan Melayu** was started in Singapore, the first Malay newspaper wholly owned and managed by Malays. In **August 1939** a first Malay congress met in Kuala Lumpur, and a second followed in Singapore in December 1940, to which delegates came from associations in several states. A third was planned for Ipoh in 1941 but never took place.

**What did they want?** They asked for the safeguarding of Malay jobs and land, for more Malay education, and for fairer treatment of Malays in their own country. They were **loyal to the sultans** and mostly to Britain, and they spoke for the Malays only, which means that they were Malay nationalists in a **narrow** sense: they defended a community and a way of life but did not ask for independence. Their effect was to teach Malays to organise and to hold meetings, and to link people in different states, while at the same time reinforcing the separation of the Malay and non-Malay communities.

### The radicals: Malay-educated teachers and the KMM

The third stream was more radical and came from a different school. The **Sultan Idris Training College** at Tanjong Malim, which trained Malay teachers for the village schools, taught its students Malay history and literature, and they met others from all over the peninsula. Some of its graduates, with little hope of promotion in a system that kept them in village schools, turned to journalism and politics. In **1938** a group of them, led by **Ibrahim Yaacob** (a teacher turned journalist from Pahang) and including **Ishak Haji Muhammad** and Mustapha Hussain, founded the **Kesatuan Melayu Muda (KMM)**, the "Young Malay Union".

The KMM was **left-wing, anti-colonial and pan-Malay**. It wanted the end of British rule and, in the longer term, a union of the Malay peoples of the peninsula with those of the Dutch East Indies in a **Melayu Raya** ("Greater Malaya", later "Indonesia Raya"), inspired by the Indonesian nationalists. It was critical of the sultans and the aristocrats and of the English-educated associations, which it thought were too ready to cooperate with the British. By the end of 1941 the British, who regarded it as dangerous, had arrested Ibrahim and other leaders. It was **small**: by 1945 it had about sixty members and was found only in a few towns. But it was the first Malay body to ask outright for independence, and it is important for the connection it made with the Indonesian movement.

:::key The growth of Malay nationalism before 1941
1. **Kaum Muda (from 1906)**: religious reform and education, a debate among Malays, but few in number and mostly urban.
2. **English-educated Malays and associations (from 1926)**: organised and loyal, defending Malay jobs, land and status; no demand for independence.
3. **The KMM (1938)**: radical, anti-British, pan-Malay, the first to seek independence; about sixty members by 1945.
4. **Overall**: a **narrow** movement. It spoke for Malays only, it was divided by class and outlook and it had no mass base.
:::

### Non-Malay politics

Chinese and Indians had their own organisations, and most were not nationalist in the Malayan sense. The Kuomintang and then the war with Japan gave many Chinese a focus in China, and the **MCP** (1930) was the only party that tried to unite the communities, but in practice it was a Chinese body. Its main strength before the war was in trade unions and Chinese schools, and the British banned it and arrested its leaders and kept it under watch. The Indian associations were concerned with labour conditions and with Indian independence. Few Chinese or Indians, before the war, would have said that Malaya was their country in the way a nationalist would mean it.

### How widespread was nationalism by 1941?

Put the evidence together. There were a few thousand Malays in associations, perhaps a few hundred in radical groups, a larger body of Chinese who followed the China question and a small communist party. Nearly all of the population of about five million was in villages, mines and estates, outside politics, and the British and the sultans between them kept the system working with very little overt opposition. The conclusion is that **nationalism was real but narrow**: it existed among a small educated minority, it was communal not national, and its aims were mostly the **defence of a community** rather than independence. **Hostility to colonial rule** as such was even narrower. It was the occupation of 1941–45 that changed the scale.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: where did Malay nationalism come from?)
William Roff, in *The Origins of Malay Nationalism* (1967), traced Malay nationalism to the religious reformers, the Malay press and the associations of the early twentieth century, in a history of several strands in which the Kaum Muda, the English-educated and the radicals each play a part. Barbara Watson Andaya and Leonard Andaya, in *A History of Malaysia* (1982; later editions), give a broader account in which the British pattern of economic development and protection of the Malay sultans shaped the identities of all the communities. For an essay, you can use these views as one question: did Malay nationalism grow **from within Malay society** (religious reform, education, the press) or **from the way the British ruled** (protection, immigration, jobs)? The best answers say both, and that the Japanese occupation came before either had produced a mass movement.
:::

## Japanese rule, 1941–45

### The invasion and the new rulers

Japanese forces landed at Kota Bharu in Kelantan just after midnight on **8 December 1941**, about an hour before the attack on Pearl Harbor. They advanced down the peninsula in a campaign of about ten weeks (Penang was occupied on 19 December and Kuala Lumpur fell on 13 January 1942), crossed the causeway to Singapore, and the British forces under General Percival surrendered there on **15 February 1942**, 69 days after the first landing. It was the largest surrender in British history and it destroyed British prestige: the rulers who had said that they were there to protect Malaya had been unable to defend it.

Malaya was put under a **Japanese military administration**, called the **Malayan Military Administration (MMA)** in the syllabus (in Japanese, the *Malai Gunsei Kanbu*). It was run by the **25th Army** from Singapore, which was renamed **Syonan-to** ("Light of the South"). The Straits Settlements were governed directly by the army, and the Federated and Unfederated Malay States by Japanese officers beside the sultans, who kept their thrones but lost real power. The **Kempeitai**, the military police, had powers of arrest and torture and was feared by everyone. The Japanese changed the calendar, the clock and the street names, made Japanese the language of government and taught it in schools, and required people to bow to Japanese soldiers.

### Hardship

The occupation was an **economic disaster**. Before the war Malaya had produced about 40 per cent of the world's rubber, and it had imported over half of its rice. With the sea routes cut by Allied submarines and the rubber and tin markets lost, the economy collapsed. Rice was short, and many families lived on tapioca and sweet potatoes grown in gardens. The Japanese printed money without limit, the notes (called "banana money" from the pictures of banana trees on them) lost their value, and prices rose thousands of times by 1945. Real income per head fell to about half of its 1941 level by 1944. About **73,000 Malayans** were forced to work on the Thailand–Burma railway, many of them Tamils from the estates, and estimates suggest that about 25,000 of them died, about a third. For almost everyone the occupation meant hunger, fear and lost earnings. This was a basic reason for **hostility** to the Japanese among all communities.

### The treatment of the different races

The Japanese did not treat the communities alike, and the difference is the heart of this part of the key question.

**Chinese.** The Japanese saw the Chinese as their main enemies. China was at war with Japan, Chinese in Malaya had raised money for China, and many were members of the MCP. After the fall of Singapore the army ordered anti-Japanese elements to be found and removed, and the **Sook Ching** ("purification by elimination") followed in February and March 1942. Men were made to report to screening centres, and those thought to be hostile were taken away and killed. Similar actions followed in Malaya, especially in Johor and Negeri Sembilan. The true numbers are not known, and **estimates for Singapore and Malaya together range from about 25,000 to 50,000 or more**. The Japanese also demanded a "gift" of **50 million dollars** from the Chinese community, who raised only about 28 million, 56 per cent, and had to borrow the rest from a Japanese bank. Chinese schools and newspapers were closed. The massacres were never forgotten, and they turned many Chinese towards resistance and towards the communists, who alone organised an armed force against the Japanese.

**Malays.** The Japanese tried to win the Malays over, and Malays were treated better than the Chinese. The sultans were kept and their status as heads of Islam was respected. Malays were taken into the police and the lower ranks of the administration, and the slogan of "Asia for the Asians" had an appeal, since it offered the end of British rule. But Malays also suffered from the economic collapse, from forced labour and from Japanese demands, and they were angered when, in **1943**, Japan handed the four northern states, Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu, to Thailand (a transfer announced in July and in effect from 18 October 1943). Japan's treatment of Malays was **favourable but not generous**, and the independence that Malay radicals hoped for was never granted.

**Indians.** The Japanese used Indians against Britain. Indian prisoners of war from the British army were recruited into the **Indian National Army (INA)**, and after **Subhas Chandra Bose** arrived in 1943 a Provisional Government of Free India was proclaimed in Singapore (21 October 1943). Many Indian civilians in Malaya joined the Indian Independence League, some from conviction and some under pressure. Others were among the forced labourers on the Thailand–Burma railway. For Indians, the occupation gave India's independence cause a new force and a base, and also brought great suffering.

**Others.** Europeans were interned. Eurasians, who were often loyal to Britain, were treated with suspicion.

:::key How the Japanese treated the communities
1. **Chinese**: the main target. The Sook Ching massacres (February–March 1942), a 50 million dollar levy and the closing of Chinese schools. This produced the strongest **hostility** and the strongest resistance.
2. **Malays**: favoured, with the sultans kept and Malays taken into the police and the administration, but hit by hardship and angered by the transfer of the northern states to Thailand (1943).
3. **Indians**: used for the Indian National Army and the Indian Independence League, and also forced into labour on the Thailand–Burma railway.
4. **Result**: the occupation sharpened the **divisions between communities** while it weakened British prestige in all of them.
:::

### The growth of resistance: the MPAJA

The **Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army (MPAJA)** was the main armed resistance to the Japanese. It grew from a decision of the **Malayan Communist Party**, which had been banned by the British, to offer help to the British in December 1941. Some 165 communists were given a short military course at a British training school in Singapore, and these men formed the core of the first guerrilla bands. After the fall of Singapore these bands retreated to the jungle, and from March 1942 they were organised as the MPAJA, with "independent regiments" in the different states. Its fighters were **mostly Chinese**, with a few Malays and Indians, and it was controlled by the MCP, whose general secretary was **Lai Teck**.

The MPAJA's early record was a story of danger and disaster. On **1 September 1942** the Japanese attacked a meeting of party leaders at the Batu Caves, near Kuala Lumpur, and killed dozens of the leaders. The attack was made possible by Lai Teck, a mysterious figure who had been working as an agent for the British before the war and was caught by the Japanese and turned. A much younger man, **Chin Peng**, became important in the party in these years. The MPAJA then retreated and spent much of 1943 trying to survive, living with Chinese villagers on the jungle edge, who gave it food and information.

In **1943** British agents of **Force 136**, a branch of the Special Operations Executive, landed on the Perak coast (the first party under Colonel John Davis arrived on 24 May 1943). At Bukit Bidor in Perak, on **1 January 1944**, the MPAJA leaders agreed to accept orders from the British commander in exchange for arms, money, training and supplies. The British dropped supplies in the months that followed. By the end of 1944 the MPAJA had more than 7,000 members, and a smaller number of Malays led by local figures, such as the force raised in Pahang with the Sultan's consent, fought as well.

Its real effect on the Japanese was **limited**, since it was never strong enough to defeat them in battle, and its strength was in how it was **organised**. It created a **disciplined movement**, with arms, a network of villagers who supported it and prestige as the only force that had fought the Japanese. And it had **ambitions**. Historians disagree about how far the MCP planned to seize power in 1945. Cheah Boon Kheng, in *Red Star over Malaya* (1983), the standard study of the MPAJA and the weeks after the surrender, and others argue that its long-term aim was a communist republic, while others argue that during the war its only target was Japan. What is certain is that after the Japanese surrender on 15 August 1945 and before British troops landed, MPAJA units came out of the jungle, took control of many towns and villages and punished people they accused of collaboration, and that in some places this led to violent clashes between Chinese and Malays. The British Military Administration began on **12 September 1945**, and the MPAJA was disbanded on 1 December 1945: some 6,800 guerrillas handed in 5,497 weapons, and the British suspected that others were hidden.

## Increasing hostility and nationalism under the occupation

The syllabus asks about four things that fed hostility and nationalism. Take them in turn, and keep in mind the difference between **hostility** (to the Japanese or to the British) and **nationalism** (a wish for a nation of one's own).

### The Japanese contribution to the growth of nationalism

The Japanese did more to produce Malay nationalism than the British had done in a generation, although they did not intend a free Malaya. They did it in five ways.
1. **They broke British prestige.** The surrender of Singapore showed that the British could be beaten by Asians. Even those who hoped for a British return no longer thought the British would stay for ever.
2. **They gave nationalists freedom and resources.** They released the KMM leaders from British prisons, helped them to buy a newspaper and gave them posts.
3. **They gave Malays arms and military training.** Under Japanese direction the **Pembela Tanah Air (PETA)**, the volunteer army, was formed in 1943 with Ibrahim Yaacob as its commander. It trained a force of about 2,000 Malay soldiers, the first Malay army, and it gave the nationalists a military base.
4. **They used the language of Asian liberation**, "Asia for the Asians", which invited every community to think about the end of colonial rule.
5. **They created hardship and fear** that made every community look for protection, and for Malays this meant looking to Malay leaders and Malay organisations.

But the Japanese limited the effect. They refused the KMM's request in January 1942, the first call by a body from the whole peninsula for Malayan independence, and dissolved the KMM within a few months. They kept power in their own hands and used nationalism for their own ends.

### The role of the KMM

The KMM had the most direct link to the occupation. Its leaders had been in British gaols, and its members had supported the Japanese advance, and in January 1942 it asked for independence, which was refused. Within months it was dissolved. But its leaders, led by Ibrahim Yaacob, were put in charge of **PETA** and later of the plans for independence that appeared in 1945. For the KMM the occupation was both an opportunity and a failure: it gave it a place and an army but not a country.

### Pembela Tanah Air (PETA) and Kesatuan Rakyat Indonesia Semenanjung (KRIS)

By 1945 Japan was losing the war and hoped to win the loyalty of the peoples of South-East Asia. In the summer of 1945, with Japanese encouragement, Ibrahim Yaacob and Dr **Burhanuddin al-Helmy**, a Malay nationalist and Islamic scholar, formed the **Kesatuan Rakyat Indonesia Semenanjung (KRIS)**. Its aim was an independent Malaya as part of a **union with Indonesia**, in a Greater Indonesia (*Indonesia Raya*). Ibrahim met Sukarno and Hatta, the leaders of the Indonesian nationalists, on 12 August 1945 to plan it. Three days later Japan surrendered, and Indonesia declared independence on 17 August, **without** Malaya. KRIS could not act and faded. Ibrahim went to Indonesia, where he lived under another name until his death in 1979.

KRIS shows both the **strength and the weakness** of this nationalism. It was **bold**, since it was the first Malay plan for an independent state, and it linked Malaya to the nationalist movement in the Dutch East Indies. But it was **narrow and late**: it was led by a handful of men, it was supported by the Japanese, and it was ended by their defeat. It would also have put Malaya under a union with a country with a very different character, which most Malays, with their sultans and their villages, did not want. This is why, after the war, the main Malay nationalist movement chose the road of **UMNO** instead (see [The route to independence (2)](#hist.d.malaysia.2)).

### The ambitions of the MPAJA

The MPAJA's ambitions have been discussed above. For the key question the point is that the MPAJA and the MCP offered a **different** kind of nationalism, a non-communal, anti-Japanese and anti-colonial movement that spoke in the name of all Malaya. In practice it was led by Chinese, and its support among Malays was small, so that to many Malays it was a Chinese force. The weeks of interregnum in August and September 1945 made that picture stronger. The MPAJA was thus the most **effective** organisation but not a **national** one.

## How widespread was nationalism by 1945?

Use the four questions at the start of the chapter.

**Whose nationalism?** By 1945 there were still several. The Malay nationalists were bolder and better organised than in 1941, the MCP and MPAJA were the strongest organisation, the Indian nationalists had been given a role, and no group spoke for all the people of Malaya.

**How widespread?** **Hostility to Japanese rule was widespread**, since almost every family suffered from hunger and fear, and among the Chinese it was very strong. **Nationalism**, a wish for a country of one's own, was **much less widespread**. It was held by a minority of Malay radicals, partly by the MCP, and by some Indians in the INA, and the great majority of Malay villagers still looked to their sultans and to the British to return.

**Nationalism or only hostility?** The occupation made it hard to tell. Hostility to the Japanese, which was real, was **not the same thing** as nationalism, and it often went with the hope that the British would come back. The Malay nationalists, who were nationalists in the full sense, were helped by the Japanese and ended the war discredited by that help.

**Before or during the occupation?** The answer for a Level 5 essay is: **the British years supplied the conditions** (three administrations, separate communities, a small educated Malay elite with grievances, and a banned communist party), and **the occupation supplied the force** (broken prestige, arms, organisation, suffering and a new sense that politics mattered). It also made communal division **worse**, since the Chinese were persecuted, the Malays were favoured and the MPAJA's actions at the end of the war led to Sino-Malay clashes.

:::key A judgement you can defend
By 1945 **hostility to Japanese rule was widespread** in every community, but **nationalism, in the sense of a movement for an independent Malaya, was narrow and divided**. The British left a society of three communities with no common loyalty and an elite of Malay associations, and the Japanese occupation created stronger organisations (the MPAJA, PETA, the KMM's links to Indonesia) while it deepened the division. When the British returned in September 1945 they came back to a country that was **more politically aware but less united** than in 1941.
:::

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is largely valid in its effects, but it needs two qualifications: that the division was more often the result of how Britain ruled than a deliberate plan, and that Britain did try, late and without success, to unite. I shall test it by two criteria: whether British policies kept the communities apart, and whether they created a Malayan political structure.

On the first criterion the view is strongly supported. Britain protected Malays as farmers and as subjects of the sultans: the Malay Reservation Enactment of 1913 reserved land for them, the Malay College at Kuala Kangsar (1905) prepared aristocrats for the Malay Administrative Service, and the village schools taught in Malay for a few years to fit boys for rural life. Chinese worked in the mines and towns, and Indians, mostly Tamils, on the estates and the railways, each with schools of their own in their own language. By 1947 Malays were about 49.5 per cent of the people and the Chinese had risen from about 29 per cent in 1921 to 38 per cent, and few families crossed the lines. The result was a society in which, as the British counted it, there were three races and no Malayans.

On the second criterion the evidence is stronger still. Britain ruled in three ways: directly in the Straits Settlements (a Crown colony since 1867), through Residents in the Federated Malay States (1895) and through the weaker Advisers of the Unfederated Malay States, which had been completed when Siam handed over Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu in the Bangkok Treaty of 10 March 1909, and Johor accepted an adviser in 1914. There was no Malayan government, citizenship or budget, and so nothing for a Malayan loyalty to attach itself to.

Against the view, three points must be weighed. First, Britain did not plan for division. It wanted cheap order, it protected Malays because its treaties with the sultans required it to, and the large Chinese and Indian communities came because mines and plantations needed workers. Second, there was an attempt at unity: Sir Cecil Clementi, High Commissioner from 1930, proposed in 1931 to decentralise the Federated Malay States as a first step to a wider union. It failed, because 85 of 111 members of the Singapore Chamber of Commerce opposed a customs union, and Chinese opinion, the Unfederated rulers and business in the FMS also opposed it. This shows that Britain wanted unity and was prevented from getting it by the communities themselves. Third, the communities kept their own divisions: Chinese schools followed China, and many Chinese and Indians looked beyond Malaya.

On balance, I judge that British policies did more to divide than to unite, in **effect**: they set up separate economic roles and separate administrations, and the one serious attempt at unity failed. But the claim is too simple if it implies design. Division was the **product of protection, economic demand and low-cost rule**, and its strongest causes were the immigration and the mines and plantations that Britain wanted. The fair verdict is that Britain did not set out to divide but, by what it chose to protect and what it left alone, it made unity far harder to build than division."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): explicit criteria, a balanced argument that tests intention against effect, a sustained judgement, and the counter-evidence (Clementi) used and not merely mentioned. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise, selected detail (1913, 1905, 1895, 10 March 1909, 1914, 85 of 111, 49.5 and 38.4 per cent). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 650 words, 35 to 40 minutes.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Evaluate the view that the Japanese occupation was the main cause of the growth of nationalism in Malaya by 1945." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Japanese occupation caused nationalism to grow in Malaya. The Japanese defeated the British so people saw that they could be beaten. The Japanese were cruel to the Chinese in the Sook Ching and the Chinese joined the MPAJA. The Japanese also let Malays have an army called PETA. So the occupation was the main cause because lots of things happened in it that made nationalism grow."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): one claim, supported by three facts, with no criteria, no comparison with the earlier years and no definition of "nationalism". It also mixes up **hostility to Japan** (the Chinese in the MPAJA) with **nationalism** (the wish for a nation), and concludes by repeating the question. Table B Level 2 (4–6): the facts are accurate but vague (no dates, no figures), the dates and organisations (February 1942, PETA formed in 1943, KRIS in 1945) are missing, and nothing is said about the years before 1941.

**The fix.** Define the term, choose a criterion and compare. "Before the occupation, nationalism in Malaya was narrow: the KMM, the only group demanding independence, was founded in 1938 and had about sixty members by 1945, and the associations defended Malay interests, not independence. The occupation changed three things. It broke British prestige on 15 February 1942; it armed and organised two movements, the MPAJA (about 7,000 members by the end of 1944) and PETA (about 2,000 Malay soldiers); and it let Ibrahim Yaacob and Burhanuddin al-Helmy form KRIS in 1945 with a plan for union with Indonesia. But much of what grew was **hostility to Japan**, especially among Chinese after the Sook Ching, and not nationalism, and the Japanese refused the KMM's request for independence in January 1942." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement that separates the two things, and the paragraph is on its way to Level 4 or 5.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name the three groups of territory in British Malaya before 1941 and say how each was ruled.
A: The **Straits Settlements** (Singapore, Penang, Malacca), a Crown colony ruled directly; the **Federated Malay States** (Perak, Selangor, Negeri Sembilan, Pahang, joined in 1895–96), each with a British Resident and a Chief Secretary at Kuala Lumpur; and the **Unfederated Malay States** (Johor, Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan, Terengganu), under British protection (the north from 1909 and Johor from 1914) with Advisers and more internal self-government.
Q: What was the Bangkok Treaty of 1909, and why did it matter?
A: It was signed on 10 March 1909, when Siam handed Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu to British protection, in return for British concessions over legal rights and a railway loan. It completed British control of the peninsula, created the Unfederated Malay States and left Malaya under three administrations.
Q: What was meant by divide and rule in British Malaya?
A: The effect of British policies that kept Malays, Chinese and Indians apart: Malays as protected rural subjects of the sultans with village schools and reserved land, Chinese in mines and towns, Indians on estates, with separate schools. Historians debate whether it was a deliberate policy or the result of protection, economy and the demand for labour.
Q: What proportion of Malaya's population was Malay, Chinese and Indian in 1947?
A: About 49.5 per cent Malay, 38.4 per cent Chinese and 10.8 per cent Indian, with 1.3 per cent others (census of 1947). The Chinese share had risen from about 29 per cent in 1921 to 34 in 1931.
Q: What was Sir Cecil Clementi's plan, and why did it fail?
A: High Commissioner from 1930 to 1934, he announced a scheme of decentralisation at Sri Menanti in August 1931, to give the states more power and as a first step to a wider Malayan union. Business in Singapore and the FMS (85 of 111 Chamber of Commerce members opposed a customs union), Chinese opinion and the rulers of the Unfederated States opposed it, and it stalled, helped by the Depression and his ill health.
Q: Who were the Kaum Muda, and what was *al-Imam*?
A: Muslim reformers who wanted religious reform, modern education and self-improvement for Malays. *Al-Imam* was their journal, published in Singapore from 1906 to 1908. They were opposed by the traditionalist Kaum Tua. Their influence was real but limited to a small, mostly urban group.
Q: What was the KMM?
A: The Kesatuan Melayu Muda (Young Malay Union), founded in 1938 by Ibrahim Yaacob and others, many of them teachers trained at the Sultan Idris Training College. It was left-wing and anti-colonial, wanted independence and a union with the Indonesian nationalists in a Greater Malaya, and had about sixty members by 1945.
Q: What was the Sook Ching?
A: The massacres of Chinese by the Japanese army in Singapore and Malaya from February 1942, when men were screened and those judged hostile were killed. Estimates range from about 25,000 to 50,000 or more. The Japanese also demanded a 50 million dollar levy from the Chinese community.
Q: What was the MPAJA, and who led it?
A: The Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army, the main resistance to the Japanese, set up by the Malayan Communist Party in 1941–42 and drawing mostly on Chinese. Lai Teck was the party's general secretary and Chin Peng a rising leader. It accepted British direction at Bukit Bidor on 1 January 1944, had more than 7,000 members by the end of 1944, and was disbanded on 1 December 1945.
Q: What were PETA and KRIS?
A: PETA (Pembela Tanah Air) was the Malay volunteer army formed under Japanese direction in 1943 with Ibrahim Yaacob as commander. KRIS (Kesatuan Rakyat Indonesia Semenanjung) was a body formed in 1945 by Ibrahim Yaacob and Burhanuddin al-Helmy that sought independence for Malaya in union with Indonesia. Japan's surrender on 15 August 1945 ended the plan.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the importance of the Bangkok Treaty of 1909 in the establishment of British control of Malaya.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: how far it completed control; how far it changed the way Malaya was ruled; its longer effects. For: Siam gave up Kedah, Perlis, Kelantan and Terengganu on 10 March 1909, ending any other power's chance of influence; it created the Unfederated Malay States; Johor followed in 1914; Malaya had been completely under British protection. Against: the FMS (1895–96) and the Straits Settlements were the real centres of British power; British influence in the north had been growing since the 1890s, and the Unfederated states were governed lightly; Siam's own motives; the treaty did not unite Malaya. A Level 5 answer judges that it completed the territorial map but left the administration divided, and that its importance was to fix three kinds of rule.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Divide and rule was the main feature of British government in Malaya before 1941.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: separate jobs, schools and places for Malays, Chinese and Indians; the Malay Reservation Enactment (1913); three administrations. Challenge: it was an effect and not a plan; protection of the sultans' treaties; economic demand for labour; indirect rule through the sultans and Residents; Clementi's attempt at unity in 1931. Level 5 argues that divisions were the pattern, but that "divide and rule" mistakes the cause, which was economy and protection.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons for the failure of Sir Cecil Clementi's plans in the 1930s.
A: Indicative content. His aim (decentralisation as a step to Pan-Malayan union; Sri Menanti, August 1931); opposition from business (85 of 111 on the customs union), from Chinese opinion, and from the Unfederated Malay States; the Wilson report of 1933 asking for a slow pace; the Depression; his ill health and departure in February 1934. Level 5 weighs the reasons (which was decisive: the opposition of interest groups, or the lack of a group that wanted a united Malaya?) and judges.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the contribution of the Kaum Muda to the growth of nationalism in Malaya.
A: Indicative content. For: *al-Imam* (1906–08) and the debate on Malay backwardness and reform; challenge to the traditional religious establishment; education; influence on later nationalists (Roff). Against: small and urban; the Kaum Tua and the rulers' opposition; its aims were religious reform and not independence; the associations and the KMM were more political. Level 5 sets criteria (reach, aims, legacy) and judges it as the first step in an intellectual movement but not a political force.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Nationalism in Malaya before 1941 was weak because Malaya was so divided.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which divisions mattered: race, class, the three administrations?
H: What other causes were there (the strength of the sultans, British protection, economic prosperity)?
A: Indicative content. Support: Malays, Chinese and Indians had different aims and loyalties; three administrations; no Malayan citizenship; Malay associations against non-Malays; the Chinese looking to China. Challenge: the sultans' authority and British indirect rule kept political life quiet; prosperity before the Depression; the small number of educated people; the repression of the KMM and the MCP; Malay nationalism was weak even within the Malay community. Level 5 judges that division was a main cause because it prevented a nation from forming, but ranks it with the loyalty to rulers and the small educated class.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the Japanese treatment of the different races did more to divide than to unite the people of Malaya.
H: Compare the Chinese, Malays and Indians in turn.
H: What common experiences did they share?
A: Indicative content. Divide: the Sook Ching and the levy against Chinese; favour to Malays; the use of Indians in the INA; the MPAJA as a Chinese force; the Thai transfer; the clashes of August–September 1945. Unite: shared hardship, hunger and forced labour; hatred of the Japanese in all communities; the ending of British prestige; the Malay and Chinese guerrillas who fought together in some areas. Level 5 judges that the treatment divided and the shared hardship did not produce a common cause.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent of Malay nationalism under the Japanese occupation.
H: Use criteria: numbers, organisation, aims, independence of action.
H: Who gained and lost from Japanese help: KMM, PETA, KRIS?
A: Indicative content. Growth: KMM's release and request for independence (January 1942); PETA (1943, about 2,000 men); KRIS (1945, a plan for union with Indonesia); the end of British prestige; organisation and training. Limits: Japan refused independence and dissolved the KMM; most Malays cooperated with the Japanese or kept to their villages; KRIS came when Japan was already losing and was ended by the surrender; the leaders were tainted by collaboration. Level 5 judges that the occupation made Malay nationalism more organised and more confident, but did not make it broad, and gave no state.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the impact of the Malayan People's Anti-Japanese Army (MPAJA) on Malaya by the end of 1945.
A: Indicative content. Impact on the Japanese: limited (Batu Caves, 1 September 1942; supply from Force 136 from 1944). Impact on the MCP: a disciplined armed force, over 7,000 men by 1944, village support. Impact on communities: Chinese prestige and Malay fear; the clashes after August 1945; the mix of heroism and fear. The weapons hidden when it disbanded (6,800 men, 5,497 weapons, 1 December 1945). Level 5 weighs its effect on the war against its effect on the politics of 1945–48.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Japanese occupation did more than British rule to create nationalism in Malaya.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate the conditions the British made from the forces the Japanese released.
H: Is hostility to the Japanese the same thing as nationalism?
A: Support: the occupation broke British prestige (15 February 1942); it armed the MPAJA and PETA; it freed the KMM; it gave KRIS a chance; it created hardship that made politics matter to ordinary people. Challenge: British rule produced the associations, schools and press that nationalists used (Kaum Muda, associations, KMM in 1938); hostility to Japan was not nationalism; the occupation also deepened division; the Japanese gave no independence; most nationalism after 1945 grew from the British return and from the Malayan Union (a theme of the next chapter). A* judgement: British rule made the leaders and the grievances, the occupation made the opportunity and the organisations, and neither made a nation, since Malaya's communities still had no common cause.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The communities of Malaya were more divided in 1945 than in 1941.' Evaluate this view.
H: What happened to the Chinese, the Malays and the Indians?
H: Compare the MPAJA's actions in August 1945 with the Japanese treatment of the Chinese.
A: Support: the Sook Ching and the 50 million dollar levy; favour for Malays; the Thai transfer; the MPAJA's actions and Sino-Malay clashes in August–September 1945; separate military forces (MPAJA, PETA, INA). Challenge: the divisions were already deep before 1941 (three administrations, separate schools, immigration); shared hardship; the communist party's claim to represent all; the fear of Japan was common. A* judgement: the occupation made existing divisions sharper and more violent, but it did not create them, and it also showed, through the MPAJA's claim and the Malay nationalists' plans, that Malayans could think beyond their own communities.

- **Three kinds of rule**: Straits Settlements (Crown colony from 1867); Federated Malay States (Residents, 1895–96); Unfederated Malay States (Advisers; the north from the Bangkok Treaty of 10 March 1909, Johor from 1914).
- **Divide and rule**: separate jobs, schools and places for Malays, Chinese and Indians; Malay Reservation Enactment (1913); 1947 census 49.5, 38.4 and 10.8 per cent; an effect of protection, economy and habit more than a plan.
- **Clementi (High Commissioner 5 February 1930 to 17 February 1934)**: decentralisation at Sri Menanti (August 1931) as a step to wider union; opposed by business (85 of 111 on the customs union), Chinese opinion and the Unfederated rulers; failed.
- **Nationalism before 1941**: Kaum Muda and *al-Imam* (1906–08); English-educated Malays and associations (Kesatuan Melayu Singapura, 14 May 1926; congresses 1939 and 1940); KMM (1938, Ibrahim Yaacob; about sixty members by 1945). Narrow and communal.
- **Japanese occupation**: invasion 8 December 1941; Singapore 15 February 1942; the MMA under the 25th Army; hardship and 73,000 forced to the railway; Sook Ching (February–March 1942; 25,000 to 50,000 or more; 50 million dollar levy); Malays favoured; Indians in the INA; Thailand takes the north from 18 October 1943.
- **Resistance**: MPAJA (MCP, 1941–42; Batu Caves 1 September 1942; Bukit Bidor 1 January 1944; over 7,000 by end of 1944; disbanded 1 December 1945).
- **Nationalism under Japan**: Japan broke British prestige and armed Malays (PETA, 1943); KMM refused independence (January 1942); KRIS (1945) planned union with Indonesia and was ended by the surrender (15 August 1945).
