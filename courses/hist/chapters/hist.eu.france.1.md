## France before 1787: the Ancien Régime

France in 1774 had about 28 million people, more than any other European state except Russia. It was rich in land and trade, and its king lived in the greatest palace in Europe at Versailles. Yet the way it was governed and taxed was the product of centuries of compromise, and by the 1780s it could no longer pay its bills.

:::def Ancien Régime
"The old order": the political and social system of France before 1789, based on an absolute monarchy, a society divided into three legally unequal orders (Estates), and privileges held by groups, regions and towns.
:::

### Absolute monarchy and royal government

In theory Louis XVI was an **absolute monarch**: he ruled by divine right, made the law, chose his ministers and was answerable only to God. There was no national parliament. The Estates-General, the only body that represented the whole kingdom, had not met since 1614.

:::def Absolute monarchy
A system in which the king holds all political authority himself, by divine right, with no elected body able to limit him by law.
:::

In practice the king's power had limits:
- **Ministers and councils.** The king governed through his councils and a handful of ministers. The most important for this story was the **Controller-General of Finances**, who managed taxation and borrowing. Ministers rose and fell through court favour, so policy changed with personnel.
- **Intendants.** In each of the provinces (*généralités*) a royal official, the intendant, collected taxes, supervised roads and kept order. They were the king's eyes in the regions.
- **The parlements.** Thirteen high law courts, the most powerful being the **Parlement of Paris**. A royal edict became law in their area only once a parlement registered it. They could send **remonstrances** (formal objections) and delay; the king could force registration in a ceremony called a *lit de justice*. The judges were nobles who had bought or inherited their offices, and they presented themselves as defenders of the nation's liberties against "ministerial despotism".
- **Privilege everywhere.** Provinces such as Brittany and Languedoc kept their own estates and tax deals; towns, guilds and the Church had their own rights. Internal customs barriers divided the country.
- **Lettres de cachet.** Sealed royal orders could imprison a person without trial. They were rare by the 1780s but symbolised arbitrary power, which is why the cahiers of 1789 demanded an end to them.

:::def Parlement
One of the thirteen sovereign law courts of Ancien Régime France. Parlements registered royal edicts, and could delay them with remonstrances; the Parlement of Paris was the most important.
:::

Louis XVI himself mattered. He was well-meaning, pious and interested in geography and locks, but indecisive. He often backed a reforming minister, then abandoned him when the court and the queen, Marie Antoinette, turned hostile. Marie Antoinette, an Austrian princess, was unpopular for her spending and her nationality; the Diamond Necklace Affair of 1785–86, a fraud in which she was innocent, damaged her reputation anyway.

### The society of orders: the three Estates

French law divided people into three **Estates** (orders), each with different rights and duties.

**The First Estate: the clergy** (roughly 130,000 people). The Catholic Church owned perhaps 6–10 per cent of the land, collected the **tithe** (a share of each harvest) and ran almost all schools, hospitals and poor relief. It paid no direct taxes; instead it voted the king a "free gift" (*don gratuit*) every few years. The clergy was deeply divided: bishops and abbots were almost all nobles with large incomes, while many parish priests (*curés*) lived close to poverty among the peasants. In 1789 that division mattered, because many curés sided with the Third Estate.

**The Second Estate: the nobility.** Nobles held perhaps a quarter of the land, the top posts in the army, Church and government, and **seigneurial rights** over the peasants on their estates: dues in money or produce, labour services, the right to hunt, and *banalités*, fees for using the lord's mill, oven or wine press. They were exempt from the main direct tax, the *taille*. The nobility was not one block either: old "nobles of the sword" looked down on "nobles of the robe" who had bought legal offices, and many provincial nobles were poor. Rich commoners could buy their way in by purchasing offices that carried noble status.

**The Third Estate: everyone else.** About 98 per cent of the population, from rich bankers to landless labourers.
- The **bourgeoisie**: merchants, manufacturers, lawyers, doctors, officials. Some were wealthier than nobles. Their grievance was less poverty than exclusion: the **Ségur Ordinance** of 22 May 1781 required four generations of nobility for an army officer's commission, and top posts in the Church and government were closing to commoners. Lawyers in particular read the Enlightenment writers and dominated the Third Estate's deputies in 1789.
- **Town workers**: artisans, journeymen, shopkeepers, servants and labourers. In Paris (about 600,000 people) a labouring family might spend half its income on bread in a normal year. A rise in the price of bread was a direct threat to survival.
- **Peasants**: about 80 per cent of the population. A minority owned enough land to prosper; most rented, sharecropped or laboured. They paid the *taille*, the *gabelle* (salt tax), the *corvée* (unpaid labour on royal roads), the tithe and seigneurial dues. The specimen mark scheme notes peasants paid up to about a tenth of their income in state taxes alone, before the tithe and dues.

:::def Taille
The main direct tax of Ancien Régime France, paid on land or income. The clergy, the nobility and many towns and office-holders were exempt, so the burden fell on the peasants.
:::

:::def Corvée
Compulsory unpaid labour, in particular the royal corvée: days of work each year building and repairing roads, demanded of peasants.
:::

:::def Seigneurial dues
Payments and services owed by peasants to the lord (seigneur) of the land: money rents, a share of crops, labour, and fees (banalités) for using his mill, oven or wine press.
:::

### The discontent of the Third Estate

Put the three groups together and you have three different grievances, which is why the Third Estate could unite against privilege in 1789 and then split apart later.

:::key Three grievances inside the Third Estate
1. **The bourgeoisie** resented legal inequality and blocked careers, and wanted a say in government and taxation.
2. **Town workers** feared hunger: food prices, wages and jobs.
3. **Peasants** resented the tithe, seigneurial dues and royal taxes that took a large share of their harvest, while the privileged paid little.
Unequal taxation linked all three: the people with most wealth paid least.
:::

:::warn Do not say nobles and clergy paid no tax at all
By the 1780s nobles paid some newer taxes, such as the *capitation* (a poll tax from 1695) and the *vingtièmes* (taxes on income from 1749), though often at favourable rates. Their key exemption was from the *taille*. Write "the privileged orders were exempt from the main direct tax, the taille" rather than "they paid no tax". Precise claims earn the higher levels.
:::

## Pressure for change, 1774–87

### The American War of Independence

France joined the American colonists against Britain with a treaty of alliance on 6 February 1778, partly for revenge after losing the Seven Years' War (1756–63). The war ended in victory with the Peace of Paris in September 1783, but it cost over a thousand million livres, almost all of it borrowed. By the late 1780s interest on the debt was the largest item in the royal budget.

The war also carried ideas. French officers such as the Marquis de Lafayette returned having fought for "no taxation without representation" and for a republic built on a written constitution and a declaration of rights. The American Declaration of Independence (1776) showed that rights could be proclaimed and governments rebuilt.

### The influence of the Enlightenment

The **Enlightenment** was an eighteenth-century movement of writers (the *philosophes*) who argued that reason, not tradition or religious authority, should judge institutions.

:::def Enlightenment
An eighteenth-century intellectual movement that used reason and evidence to criticise tradition, religious authority and arbitrary government, and promoted ideas such as natural rights, religious toleration and government by consent.
:::

| Writer | Key work | Idea that mattered in 1789 |
|---|---|---|
| Montesquieu | *The Spirit of the Laws* (1748) | Separate the powers (law-making, executing, judging) to prevent despotism; the parlements quoted him |
| Voltaire | essays, letters, *Candide* (1759) | Attacked Church intolerance and arbitrary imprisonment |
| Rousseau | *The Social Contract* (1762) | Sovereignty belongs to the people; law should express the "general will" |
| Diderot and d'Alembert | the *Encyclopédie* (from 1751) | Spread critical, practical knowledge across Europe |
| The Physiocrats (e.g. Quesnay) | economic writings | Free trade in grain and a single tax on land; they influenced Turgot |

The ideas spread through salons, cafés, Masonic lodges, provincial academies and a flood of pamphlets. They did not cause the Revolution on their own; few peasants had read Rousseau. What they did was give educated people a **language** to describe the crisis of 1787–89 ("the nation", "rights", "despotism", "the general will"), so that a quarrel about taxes became a quarrel about who should rule.

### Money: the attempts at reform, 1774–87

The Crown's problem was simple to state: spending, especially on war and on interest, exceeded revenue, and the tax system could not be changed without challenging privilege.

**Anne-Robert-Jacques Turgot** (Controller-General, August 1774 to May 1776) promised the king "no bankruptcy, no tax increases, no borrowing". He freed the trade in grain in September 1774, but a poor harvest followed and prices rose, provoking riots around Paris in spring 1775 known as the **Flour War**. In 1776 his **Six Edicts** abolished the royal corvée (replaced by a tax on all landowners) and the guilds. The Parlement of Paris refused to register them; Louis forced them through with a *lit de justice* but, faced with opposition at court, dismissed Turgot in May 1776 and the reforms were reversed.

**Jacques Necker**, a Swiss Protestant banker, ran the finances from 1777 to 1781 (as a Protestant he took the title Director-General rather than Controller-General). He paid for the American war with loans instead of new taxes, which made him popular. In February 1781 he published the *Compte rendu au roi*, the first public account of royal finances, which claimed a surplus in ordinary spending by leaving out the war costs. It made him a hero of public opinion, but misled people about the real state of the finances. He resigned in May 1781 when the king refused him more power.

**Charles-Alexandre de Calonne** (Controller-General, November 1783 to April 1787) at first borrowed and spent to keep up confidence. In August 1786, with loans drying up, he told the king the state was close to bankruptcy and proposed a sweeping plan:
- a **universal land tax** (*subvention territoriale*) paid by all landowners, privileged or not;
- **provincial assemblies** elected by landowners regardless of order, to share out the tax;
- free trade in grain, abolition of internal customs, and replacing the corvée with a money tax.

:::key Why the reforms of 1774–87 failed
1. **Privilege**: every serious reform meant taxing the clergy and nobility, who controlled the parlements and the court.
2. **The parlements** could delay and obstruct, and claimed to speak for the nation.
3. **The king** would not back reforming ministers to the end.
4. **Borrowing** (Necker, then Calonne) postponed the problem and made it worse, because interest grew every year.
:::

## The financial and political crisis, 1787–89

### The Assembly of Notables, 1787

To avoid the parlements, Calonne persuaded the king to call an **Assembly of Notables**, 144 hand-picked nobles, bishops, judges and officials, who met on 22 February 1787. He expected them to approve his plan. They did not. They demanded to see the accounts, attacked Calonne personally (he had a reputation for extravagance), and argued that only the nation could consent to a new permanent tax. Louis dismissed Calonne on 8 April 1787.

His successor, **Étienne-Charles de Loménie de Brienne**, Archbishop of Toulouse and one of Calonne's critics among the Notables, took over in May 1787. The Notables would not approve his modified plan either, and were sent home on 25 May.

### Brienne and the Parlement of Paris

Brienne then had to send the reforms to the Parlement of Paris. It registered some (provincial assemblies, free grain trade, the corvée commuted, and in early 1788 civil rights for Protestants) but refused the land tax and a new stamp duty, declaring in July 1787 that only the **Estates-General** could consent to new taxes. The king exiled the judges to Troyes in August 1787, then recalled them in September.

Brienne's own published budget for 1788 showed how desperate things were:

| Royal budget for 1788 | million livres |
|---|---|
| Expenditure | 629 |
| Revenue | 503 |
| Deficit | 126 |
| Interest on debts (part of expenditure) | 318 |

So about half of all spending went on interest before a single soldier or official was paid.

On 8 May 1788 the government struck back with the **May Edicts** of the Keeper of the Seals, Lamoignon, which took away the parlements' right to register laws. This provoked what historians call the **aristocratic revolt**: parlements, provincial nobles and clergy resisted across France. In Grenoble on 7 June 1788 townspeople threw roof tiles at troops sent to exile the local judges (the "Day of the Tiles"), and on 21 July 1788 an assembly of all three orders of the Dauphiné met at Vizille and demanded the Estates-General, with double representation for the Third Estate and voting by head. The clergy's assembly cut its "free gift" to a fraction of what Brienne asked.

:::def Aristocratic revolt
The resistance of the privileged orders, led by the parlements, to royal reforms in 1787–88. By refusing new taxes and demanding the Estates-General, it forced the Crown to give way and opened the door to wider political change.
:::

### The king calls the Estates-General

With no one willing to lend, Brienne gave in. On **8 August 1788** the Estates-General was summoned for 1 May 1789. On 16 August the treasury suspended some payments: in effect, the state was bankrupt. Brienne resigned in late August and the king **reappointed Necker**, the one minister the public and the bankers trusted, as Director-General of Finances. The May Edicts were withdrawn and the Parlement returned in triumph.

Then the political question took over. On 25 September 1788 the Parlement of Paris ruled that the Estates-General should meet **as in 1614**: the three orders equal in size and voting separately, so that clergy and nobles could always outvote the Third Estate two to one. Overnight the parlements lost their popularity: they were defending privilege, not the nation. A "patriot" party of liberal nobles and lawyers demanded **doubling** (as many Third Estate deputies as the other two combined) and **voting by head**. On 27 December 1788 the royal council agreed to double the Third, but said nothing about voting, which left the central question open.

In January 1789 the Abbé **Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès** published the most famous pamphlet of the year, *What is the Third Estate?* It opened: "What is the Third Estate? Everything. What has it been until now in the political order? Nothing. What does it want to be? Something." He argued that the privileged orders were not really part of the nation at all.

:::def Estates-General
The assembly of representatives of the three orders of France (clergy, nobility, Third Estate), called by the king to advise him and consent to taxes. It had not met since 1614 when it was summoned in 1788.
:::

:::def Cahiers de doléances
"Lists of grievances" drawn up in early 1789 by each electoral assembly of each order, for their deputies to take to the Estates-General.
:::

The elections of spring 1789 produced tens of thousands of **cahiers de doléances**. They show both agreement and division. Many cahiers of all three orders asked for regular meetings of the Estates-General, consent to taxation, an end to arbitrary arrest and freedom of the press, and many noble cahiers accepted equal taxation. They divided over voting by head and over seigneurial rights. Almost none attacked the king himself: in spring 1789 France wanted a reformed monarchy, not a republic.

### The economic crisis of 1788–89

Just as politics reached its crisis, so did the economy.
- **Bad harvests and food shortage.** A violent hailstorm on 13 July 1788 destroyed crops across the Paris region, and the harvest of 1788 was poor across much of France. The winter of 1788–89 was exceptionally harsh. In Paris the price of the standard four-pound loaf rose from about 8 sous in August 1788 to about 14½ sous by February 1789: it almost doubled.
- **Unemployment and price rises.** When bread took most of a family's budget, they stopped buying cloth and other goods, so workshops laid off workers. The **Eden Treaty** of 1786 with Britain had lowered tariffs, letting in cheaper British textiles and adding to unemployment in the cloth towns.
- **Unrest.** Grain convoys were attacked and markets rioted in the spring of 1789. In Paris, rumours that the wallpaper manufacturer Réveillon wanted to cut wages led to the **Réveillon riots** of 27–28 April 1789, put down by troops with dozens of deaths.

:::exam Linking economic and political causes
The best answers connect the two crises. Hunger made ordinary people angry and ready to act in the summer of 1789, and the political crisis gave that anger a target: the "aristocratic plot" blamed for high prices and for threatening the Assembly. Without hunger the deputies would have had no crowd to protect them; without the political crisis the bread riots would have been one more year of local disturbances, as in 1775.
:::

## From Estates-General to National Assembly, May–July 1789

The Estates-General opened at Versailles on **5 May 1789** with roughly 1,200 deputies, about half of them from the Third Estate. Necker spoke for about three hours, mostly about finance, and offered no lead on the voting question. The Third Estate refused to verify its members' credentials separately, because accepting separate verification meant accepting separate voting. For six weeks nothing happened.

On **17 June** the Third Estate, joined by a few parish priests, declared itself the **National Assembly**, claiming to represent the nation. It also declared existing taxes illegal unless it approved them, a direct claim to sovereignty. Two days later the clergy voted narrowly to join it.

On **20 June** the deputies found their hall locked and guarded (it was being prepared for a royal session). They moved to a nearby indoor tennis court and swore the **Tennis Court Oath**: never to separate until they had given France a constitution. All but one of the deputies present signed.

:::def National Assembly
The body formed on 17 June 1789 when the deputies of the Third Estate, with some clergy, declared that they alone represented the nation. On 9 July it named itself the National Constituent Assembly, because its task was to write a constitution.
:::

On **23 June**, at the **royal session** (*séance royale*), Louis XVI offered real reforms: no new taxes without consent, regular meetings of the Estates-General, freedom of the press, and provincial estates. But he declared the decisions of 17 June null, insisted that the three orders meet separately, and kept the tithe and seigneurial dues as property. He ordered the deputies to disperse. The Third Estate stayed; the Comte de Mirabeau said they would leave only by force. Within days most clergy and 47 nobles, including the king's cousin the Duc d'Orléans, joined the Assembly, and on 27 June the king ordered the rest of the first two orders to join too.

It looked like a royal climbdown. But at the same time around 20,000 troops, many from foreign regiments, were being moved into the Paris region. Then on **11 July** the king dismissed Necker. To Parisians this looked like the start of a counter-revolution by force: **Louis' refusal to carry out reform** on the Assembly's terms was now plain.

:::key Louis XVI's refusal to reform: the evidence
1. 23 June: he rejected the National Assembly and voting by head, and kept privilege.
2. Late June to early July: he gathered troops around Paris and Versailles.
3. 11 July: he dismissed Necker, the minister the public trusted.
4. August to October: he delayed accepting the August Decrees and the Declaration of Rights.
Each step made a compromise with the Assembly harder and pushed the people of Paris into action.
:::

## The outbreak of revolution, July–October 1789

### The storming of the Bastille, 14 July 1789

News of Necker's dismissal reached Paris on 12 July. At the Palais-Royal the young journalist Camille Desmoulins urged the crowd to arm itself. Crowds burned most of the customs posts on the city wall (which taxed food entering Paris) and searched for weapons and grain. On 13 July the electors of Paris formed a committee and a citizens' militia. On the morning of **14 July** crowds took around 30,000 muskets from the Invalides, then went to the **Bastille**, a royal fortress and prison in the east of the city, to seize its gunpowder.

After a confused fight, joined by some soldiers of the French Guards with cannon, the garrison surrendered. About a hundred attackers had been killed. The governor, the Marquis de Launay, was killed by the crowd. Only seven prisoners were inside.

The consequences mattered more than the fight:
- The king **withdrew the troops**, recalled Necker (16 July) and on 17 July came to Paris, where he accepted the red, white and blue cockade.
- Paris got a new **revolutionary city government** (the astronomer Bailly as mayor) and a **National Guard** under Lafayette. Towns across France copied this "municipal revolution".
- The Assembly was **safe**: the threat of force had failed. The first nobles, including the king's brother the Comte d'Artois, left France as *émigrés*.

### The Great Fear, July–August 1789

In the countryside, hunger, rumours and news from Paris combined. From about 20 July to 6 August a panic known as the **Great Fear** spread across much of France: rumours that the nobles had hired "brigands" to destroy the crops in revenge. Villagers armed themselves; when no brigands came, many turned on the châteaux, burning the *terriers* (the records of seigneurial dues) and sometimes the houses.

:::def The Great Fear
The rural panic of late July and early August 1789, in which rumours of an aristocratic plot and of brigands led peasants to arm and attack châteaux, destroying the records of seigneurial dues.
:::

### The August Decrees

The Assembly had to restore order without using force against the peasants. On the night of **4 August 1789**, in a carefully prepared session, liberal nobles (the Vicomte de Noailles, the Duc d'Aiguillon) proposed giving up feudal rights, and deputy after deputy renounced privileges. The **August Decrees** (finalised 4–11 August) declared that the Assembly "completely abolishes the feudal system". They ended:
- personal servitude and seigneurial justice, without compensation;
- the tithe, tax privileges, the sale of public offices, and the special privileges of provinces and towns;
- barriers to careers: all citizens could hold any office.

But dues attached to land, the largest part of what peasants paid, were to be **redeemed** (bought out) by the peasants. Many refused to pay, and rural unrest continued until these dues were abolished without compensation in 1793.

:::def August Decrees
The decrees of 4–11 August 1789 by which the National Assembly abolished the feudal system, the tithe and privileges of orders, provinces and towns, while requiring peasants to buy out most dues attached to land.
:::

### The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen

On **26 August 1789** the Assembly approved the **Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen**, seventeen articles intended as the preamble to the future constitution. Its first article reads: "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be founded only upon the general good." Article 3 placed sovereignty "essentially in the nation", Article 6 made law "the expression of the general will", and Article 17 called property "an inviolable and sacred right".

It drew on Enlightenment ideas and the American declarations. Its limits are as revealing: it said nothing about women's rights (Olympe de Gouges answered it with a *Declaration of the Rights of Woman* in 1791) or about slavery in France's colonies.

:::def Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen
The statement of principles adopted by the National Assembly on 26 August 1789: natural rights of liberty, property, security and resistance to oppression; equality before the law; sovereignty of the nation; law as the general will.
:::

### The March of the Women, 5–6 October 1789

Louis XVI delayed approving the August Decrees and the Declaration, and the Assembly argued about how much power he should keep (in September it gave him a **suspensive veto**, able to delay laws but not block them for ever). Bread was still scarce in Paris. When news spread that at a banquet at Versailles on 1 October officers had trampled the revolutionary cockade, anger boiled over.

On **5 October** several thousand women gathered at the Hôtel de Ville and marched about 20 kilometres to Versailles to demand bread. Thousands of National Guards followed under Lafayette. That evening the king accepted the August Decrees and the Declaration. Early on 6 October crowds broke into the palace and killed two of the royal guards. To calm them the royal family agreed to move to Paris, escorted back by the crowd, who called them "the baker, the baker's wife and the baker's boy". The Assembly followed within two weeks.

:::def March of the Women (October Days)
The march of Parisian women, followed by the National Guard, to Versailles on 5–6 October 1789, which forced Louis XVI to accept the August Decrees and the Declaration and to move with his family to Paris.
:::

### Immediate consequences: France by October 1789

:::key What had changed between May and October 1789
1. **Sovereignty**: power had passed from the king to the nation, represented by the National Assembly.
2. **Privilege**: the society of orders and the feudal system were legally abolished.
3. **Rights**: the Declaration set out equality before the law and the sovereignty of the nation.
4. **The king**: still on the throne, but in Paris, under the eye of the crowd, with only a suspensive veto.
5. **The people**: the crowds of Paris and the peasants had shown they could change national politics by force.
6. **Division**: émigrés abroad and uneasy nobles and clergy at home began a counter-revolution that would shape the next three years.
:::

## Why did revolution break out in 1789? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | Society of orders and privilege; fiscal system; Enlightenment ideas; resentment of the bourgeoisie | Created grievances and a language of rights, but had existed for decades without revolution |
| Medium-term | War debt (1778–83); failure of Turgot, Necker, Calonne | Turned a tax problem into bankruptcy |
| Short-term | Aristocratic revolt 1787–88; the 1614 ruling; harvest failure 1788; Louis' choices in June–July 1789 | Forced the Estates-General, split the orders, and mobilised the crowds |
| Triggers | Necker's dismissal (11 July); the cockade banquet (1 October) | Set off the Bastille and the October Days |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The financial crisis was the **necessary** cause: without bankruptcy the king would not have called the Estates-General. But it was not **sufficient**. What turned a reform crisis into a revolution was the political struggle over representation (the 1614 ruling and voting by head), the king's attempt to reverse it by force, and the hunger that brought the crowds onto the streets to defend the Assembly.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **Georges Lefebvre**, *Quatre-vingt-neuf* (1939; in English *The Coming of the French Revolution*, 1947), a Marxist historian, saw four revolutions in 1789: aristocratic, bourgeois, popular (urban) and peasant. The rising bourgeoisie overthrew a feudal order.
- **Alfred Cobban** challenged this "social interpretation" in a 1954 lecture, *The Myth of the French Revolution*, and in *The Social Interpretation of the French Revolution* (1964): the revolutionary "bourgeoisie" were mostly lawyers and office-holders, not capitalists, and feudalism was largely gone already. This began the **revisionist** school.
- **William Doyle**, *Origins of the French Revolution* (1980), stressed the political and financial collapse of the state and the choices of the elites.
- **François Furet**, *Interpreting the French Revolution* (1978 in French), stressed political culture: the new language of the nation and the general will drove events.
You can use this at AS to show why causes are weighed differently, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Louis XVI agreed in 1788 to call the Estates-General.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "France had a lot of debt from the American War. The Assembly of Notables met in 1787 and did not agree to Calonne's reforms. The Parlement of Paris said only the Estates-General could agree to taxes. There was the Day of the Tiles in Grenoble. So the king called the Estates-General in August 1788."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes an event; none says how it made the king call the Estates-General. Causation is implied by the word "so", not explained.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was bankruptcy. The war of 1778–83 had cost over a thousand million livres, almost all borrowed, so by 1788 interest took about half of royal spending and the budget showed a deficit of 126 million livres. Bankers would no longer lend unless the Crown found new revenue, and the only large untapped source was the privileged orders. A second reason was that every other route had been blocked. Calonne tried the Assembly of Notables in 1787 to avoid the parlements, but the Notables refused his land tax and said a new permanent tax needed the nation's consent. Brienne then tried the Parlement of Paris, which refused the land tax and in July 1787 demanded the Estates-General."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was the aristocratic revolt of 1788. When Lamoignon's May Edicts removed the parlements' power to register laws, the parlements, provincial nobles and clergy resisted across France: at Grenoble crowds attacked troops on the Day of the Tiles (7 June) and the Vizille assembly (21 July) demanded the Estates-General. The clergy cut its free gift. The Crown could no longer govern or borrow without the cooperation of the privileged, and they would cooperate only through the Estates-General.

These factors were linked. Bankruptcy made new taxes unavoidable; the privileged used the parlements and the Notables to block them; and their revolt in 1788 made it impossible to force reform through. The decisive factor was the empty treasury: on 8 August 1788 Brienne summoned the Estates-General, and on 16 August payments were suspended. The king called the Estates-General not because he wanted to share power, but because it was the only body whose consent might persuade lenders that new taxes would be paid."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (bankruptcy made reform necessary, privilege made it impossible by other means), and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

The National Assembly, considering that it has been called to establish the constitution of the kingdom, to bring about the regeneration of public order and to maintain the true principles of the monarchy, and that nothing can prevent it from continuing its deliberations wherever it is forced to meet, decrees that all members of this Assembly shall immediately take a solemn oath never to separate, and to reassemble wherever circumstances require, until the constitution of the kingdom is established on firm foundations.

*Adapted from the Tennis Court Oath, sworn by the deputies of the National Assembly at Versailles, 20 June 1789.*

**Source B**

The King wishes the ancient distinction of the three orders of the State to be preserved in its entirety, as essentially linked to the constitution of his kingdom. He declares null the decisions taken by the deputies of the Third Estate on 17 June, and those which followed, as illegal and unconstitutional. No new tax shall be established without the consent of the representatives of the nation. I order you, gentlemen, to separate at once, and to go tomorrow morning each to the chamber set aside for your order.

*Adapted from the declarations read on behalf of Louis XVI, and his closing words, at the royal session (séance royale) of the Estates-General, Versailles, 23 June 1789.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the dispute over who had the authority to reform France in June 1789.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says the Assembly will not separate until there is a constitution. Source B says the three orders must stay separate and the decisions of 17 June are null. So they disagree about the Assembly. Source A is from the deputies and Source B is from the king, so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share some common ground. Both claim to be acting within the monarchy and its constitution: Source A says the Assembly will maintain 'the true principles of the monarchy', and Source B speaks of preserving the 'constitution of his kingdom'. Both also accept that the nation must consent to taxation: Source A's purpose is a constitution, and in Source B the king himself promises that no new tax will be established 'without the consent of the representatives of the nation'. By June 1789 even the king accepted the cahiers' central demand that taxation needed consent.

On authority, however, they are directly opposed. Source A claims that the Assembly alone has been 'called to establish the constitution', and that nothing can stop it meeting 'wherever it is forced to'. Source B denies that the Assembly exists at all: it declares the decisions of 17 June, when the Third Estate named itself the National Assembly, 'null' and 'illegal', and orders the deputies to meet as separate orders, which would let clergy and nobles outvote the Third Estate. Source A rests authority on the nation; Source B rests it on the king and the traditional orders.

The context explains this clash. Source A was sworn in a tennis court because the deputies had been locked out of their hall on 20 June; the words 'wherever it is forced to meet' and 'never to separate' are a direct answer to that lockout and to fears that the king would dissolve them. It is a public, collective oath meant to bind the deputies together and to rally Paris, so it presents the Assembly as defending the monarchy rather than attacking it. Source B is the king's response three days later at the royal session. Its purpose was to regain control by offering concessions (tax consent) while restoring the old voting by order. The king's real weakness shows in what followed: the deputies refused to leave, and on 27 June Louis ordered the other two orders to join the Assembly. So Source B is strong evidence of what the king wanted, but weak evidence of where authority actually lay by the end of June; Source A, though one-sided, better reflects the shift of power that the events of the next weeks confirmed."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the lockout, the royal session) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Financial problems were the main reason for the outbreak of revolution in 1789.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: debt and deficit (war of 1778–83; 1788 budget: 629 spending, 503 revenue, 318 interest); failed reforms; bankruptcy forced the Estates-General (8 and 16 August 1788).
- Against 1, politics: the 1614 ruling and the battle over voting by head turned a tax crisis into a crisis of sovereignty (17 June, 20 June).
- Against 2, the king: 23 June, troops, Necker's dismissal (11 July) provoked the Bastille.
- Against 3, hunger and the people: harvest 1788, bread nearly doubled, Réveillon riots, the Great Fear, October Days.
- Judgement: finance was the necessary starting point; the political struggle plus popular hunger made it a revolution. Criterion: what changed a crisis into a revolution.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Financial problems were very important. France had spent over a thousand million livres on the American War and had borrowed most of it. By 1788 the deficit was 126 million livres and interest was 318 million livres, about half of spending. Turgot, Necker and Calonne all failed to fix this, and when the Notables and the Parlement refused new taxes the king had to call the Estates-General. This shows that financial problems caused the Revolution."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: calling the Estates-General is not the same as a revolution. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet financial problems explain why the Estates-General met, not why it turned into a revolution. That change came from the political struggle over representation. Once the Parlement of Paris ruled on 25 September 1788 that the Estates-General should meet as in 1614, the debate stopped being about taxes and became about who represented the nation. Sieyès's pamphlet of January 1789 asked what the Third Estate had been 'in the political order' and answered 'Nothing'. The six-week deadlock over verifying credentials, and the Third Estate's decision on 17 June to call itself the National Assembly and to declare existing taxes illegal unless it approved them, were claims to sovereignty, not to budget reform. The financial crisis provided the occasion; the claim of the nation to be sovereign turned it into a revolution.

Popular action then decided the outcome, and here economic hardship, a different kind of problem from the Crown's finances, was central. The hailstorm of 13 July 1788 and the poor harvest pushed the price of a four-pound loaf in Paris from about 8 to about 14½ sous. Hungry crowds blamed hoarders and an 'aristocratic plot', and when the king dismissed Necker on 11 July they armed themselves and took the Bastille. Without that force the Assembly might well have been dissolved, as the king's troop movements suggest he intended. In the countryside the same hunger fed the Great Fear, which forced the Assembly to abolish the feudal system in August.

Overall, financial problems were the necessary cause, because without bankruptcy Louis XVI would never have called the Estates-General. But they were not the main reason for the outbreak of revolution. The decisive factors were the political conflict over representation, which turned a tax crisis into a crisis of sovereignty, and the economic distress that put the people of Paris and the countryside behind the Assembly at the critical moments of July and October. The king's own choices made this combination explosive. The statement is therefore true of the origins of the crisis, but not of the revolution itself."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (what turned crisis into revolution) and distinguishes necessary from decisive causes. With an opening paragraph on the case for finance, this is Level 5 (19–20). Note that "financial problems" (the state's finances) and "economic problems" (harvests, prices, jobs) are different factors: separating them is itself a mark of a strong answer.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

What is the Third Estate? Everything. What has it been until now in the political order? Nothing. What does it want to be? Something. The privileged orders are a people apart within the nation. If they were removed, the nation would not be smaller but greater.

*From What is the Third Estate?, a pamphlet by the Abbé Sieyès, January 1789. The first three sentences are quoted; the rest is adapted.*

**Source B**

Walking up a long hill, I was joined by a poor woman who complained of the times. Her husband had only a morsel of land, one cow and a poor little horse, yet they had to pay a measure of wheat and three chickens to one lord, and oats, a chicken and money to another, besides very heavy royal taxes. She said that something was to be done by some great folks for such poor ones, for the taxes and the dues are crushing us.

*Adapted from the journal of Arthur Young, an English farmer and writer on agriculture travelling in France, entry for 12 July 1789, published in 1792.*

**Source C**

A print showing an old peasant, bent over a stick, carrying on his back a clergyman and a nobleman, who ride him comfortably. Its title, in peasant dialect, means "Let's hope this game ends soon".

*A French cartoon published in 1789.*

**Source D**

Men are born and remain free and equal in rights. Social distinctions may be founded only upon the general good. The principle of all sovereignty resides essentially in the nation.

*From Articles 1 and 3 of the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, adopted by the National Assembly, 26 August 1789.*

**Question.** How far do these sources agree that resentment of privilege was the main cause of discontent in France in 1789?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (privileged orders "a people apart"), B (dues and taxes "crushing"), C (clergy and noble riding the peasant). Challenge or qualify: D (the main principle is sovereignty and rights, not only privilege); B (the royal taxes are also a burden, and her complaint is poverty); A (Sieyès's target is political exclusion: "in the political order").

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: written by a clergyman for the Third Estate's cause during the election campaign; a persuasive pamphlet, so it exaggerates. But its huge sales (it went through several editions in 1789) and the Third Estate's actions on 17 June show it expressed a widely shared view among educated commoners. Strong evidence of **bourgeois** opinion, weak evidence about peasants.
- **B**: an outsider with no stake in French politics and an expert's interest in farming, recording a specific conversation; that makes it valuable. Yet it is one woman, retold in English and published in 1792, after the Revolution, which may shape what Young chose to print. Its detail matches what we know of seigneurial dues and the *taille*, and it was written two days before the Bastille, during the harvest crisis.
- **C**: propaganda, simplified for people who could not read well, so it shows the message revolutionaries wanted to spread rather than measured opinion. Its existence in 1789 is still evidence that attacks on privilege had a popular audience.
- **D**: an official statement of principle, debated and voted by the Assembly after the August Decrees. It shows what the deputies thought the Revolution was about, and its focus is as much on sovereignty and rights as on privilege.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A, B and C agree that privilege was a central grievance, but they are describing different resentments. For Sieyès in Source A, privilege matters because it excludes the Third Estate from 'the political order'; for the woman in Source B it matters because dues and taxes take her family's food; Source C fuses the two into one image of the burden of the privileged orders. Source D shows that by August the deputies had turned resentment of privilege into a wider claim: social distinctions only 'upon the general good' and sovereignty in 'the nation'. On balance the sources strongly support the view that resentment of privilege was the most widespread cause of discontent, because it is the one grievance shared by the pamphleteer, the peasant and the Assembly. But B's stress on royal taxes and poverty, written at the height of the bread crisis, and D's focus on sovereignty, show that hunger and the struggle for political power were equally important in 1789. The sources agree only to a large extent: privilege was the common thread that joined different discontents, rather than their single main cause."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1789; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the storming of the Bastille the most important event of 1789?" [20]

A student wrote:

"On 14 July 1789 the people of Paris stormed the Bastille. First they went to the Invalides and took about 30,000 muskets. Then they went to the Bastille to get gunpowder. The governor de Launay surrendered after a fight in which about a hundred attackers died. He was then killed by the crowd and his head was put on a pike. There were only seven prisoners inside. Afterwards the king came to Paris on 17 July and wore the tricolour cockade. The Bastille is now remembered every year in France on 14 July."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story without saying why it matters for the question, and it never compares the Bastille with other events. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The storming of the Bastille was important because it saved the National Assembly. Necker's dismissal on 11 July and the troops gathering round Paris suggested the king meant to dissolve the Assembly by force; the fall of the Bastille showed that the troops could not be relied on, and on 15 July Louis withdrew them and recalled Necker. However, it did less than the August Decrees to change how France was governed: the Bastille protected the revolution of 17 June, while the decrees of 4–11 August actually abolished the society of orders." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name the three Estates and say roughly what share of the population each made up.
A: First Estate, the clergy (about 0.5 per cent); Second Estate, the nobility (about 1–1.5 per cent); Third Estate, everyone else (about 98 per cent, about 80 per cent of the whole population being peasants).
Q: What was the role of the parlements, and why did they matter in 1787–88?
A: They were high law courts that had to register royal edicts and could object (remonstrances). In 1787–88 the Parlement of Paris refused to register the land tax and demanded the Estates-General, leading the aristocratic revolt that forced the king to call it.
Q: Give one reform each of Turgot and Calonne, and say why each failed.
A: Turgot's Six Edicts (1776) abolished the corvée and the guilds; court and Parlement opposition led the king to dismiss him and reverse them. Calonne's universal land tax (1786–87) was rejected by the Assembly of Notables, who distrusted him and said only the nation could consent to new taxes.
Q: What did the Parlement of Paris rule on 25 September 1788, and why did it matter?
A: That the Estates-General should meet as in 1614, with the orders voting separately. This meant the clergy and nobility could outvote the Third Estate, so the parlements lost their popularity and the struggle became one over representation.
Q: Why did the deputies swear the Tennis Court Oath on 20 June 1789?
A: They found their hall locked for the coming royal session and feared the king would dissolve them, so they swore never to separate until they had given France a constitution.
Q: Give three consequences of the storming of the Bastille.
A: The king withdrew the troops and recalled Necker; Paris set up a new city government and a National Guard under Lafayette; the National Assembly was safe from dissolution by force. (Also: the first émigrés, including the Comte d'Artois, left France; towns copied the "municipal revolution".)
Q: Why did the August Decrees not end rural unrest?
A: Dues attached to land had to be bought out (redeemed) by the peasants, which most could not or would not pay; unrest continued until these dues were abolished without compensation in 1793.
Q: What were the results of the March of the Women in October 1789?
A: Louis XVI accepted the August Decrees and the Declaration of Rights, and the royal family, followed by the Assembly, moved from Versailles to Paris, where the king was under the eye of the Paris crowd.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Assembly of Notables rejected Calonne's reforms in 1787.
A: Indicative content: the Notables distrusted Calonne (his reputation for borrowing and extravagance; his figures contradicted Necker's *Compte rendu* of 1781, which had claimed a surplus); the universal land tax attacked their own exemptions; they demanded to see the accounts before approving anything; many argued that only the nation (the Estates-General) could consent to a new permanent tax, an argument shaped by Enlightenment and American ideas; the provincial assemblies, elected without regard to order, threatened the privileged orders' control of local government. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: self-interest (privilege) was presented in the language of national consent, which made refusal respectable, and distrust of Calonne gave them a reason to refuse that did not look selfish. It ends with a judgement on the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Great Fear broke out in the summer of 1789.
A: Indicative content: hunger after the 1788 harvest failure, with grain prices at their peak in July before the new harvest; news from Paris of the Bastille and rumours of an aristocratic plot to starve the people or to use foreign troops; real bands of wandering unemployed and beggars that could be mistaken for "brigands"; long resentment of seigneurial dues, which gave the peasants a target (the *terriers*); the collapse of royal authority, so no one stopped the panic. Level 4: connect the background (hunger and dues) to the trigger (rumours after 14 July) and judge which mattered most; e.g. the rumours triggered the panic, but resentment of dues explains why it turned into attacks on châteaux.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the Enlightenment responsible for the outbreak of revolution in 1789?
A: For: ideas of rights, popular sovereignty and the separation of powers (Rousseau, Montesquieu) shaped the demands of 1788–89; Sieyès's pamphlet and the cahiers use this language; the Declaration of 26 August is Enlightenment thinking in law; the American example showed ideas could be put into practice. Against: few peasants or town workers read the philosophes, and their revolt was about bread, dues and taxes; the parlements used "liberty" to defend privilege; bankruptcy, not ideas, forced the Estates-General; the king's choices in June and July triggered the violence. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that the Enlightenment shaped the form the revolution took (its aims and language) more than it caused the outbreak, which came from the financial, political and economic crises.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Louis XVI's own actions were the main reason for the collapse of royal authority in 1789.' How far do you agree?
A: For: abandoning Turgot (1776) and Calonne (1787); calling the Estates-General without deciding the voting question; the royal session of 23 June; troops around Paris and Necker's dismissal on 11 July, which triggered the Bastille; delaying the August Decrees until the October Days. Against: structural bankruptcy and privilege that no king could easily reform; the aristocratic revolt of 1787–88; the Third Estate's claim to sovereignty on 17 June; hunger and popular action in July and October. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "did the king create the crisis or fail to manage it?", and judges, e.g., that he did not create the crisis but his choices in June–July 1789 turned it into a collapse of authority.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the mood in Paris in the summer of 1789.
A: Similarities: both show intense political excitement and hostility to the old order (A: pamphlets "violent against the clergy and nobility"; B: fear of a "massacre of patriots"); both show public opinion mobilised in Paris. Differences: A describes debate and print, a mood of argument; B describes fear and a call to arms, a mood of violence; A is in June, before the crisis, B on 12 July after Necker's dismissal. Explanation: the change from 9 June to 12 July reflects events (the royal session, troops near Paris, Necker's dismissal). Provenance: A is an English observer writing a journal (detached, but published after the Revolution in 1792); B is a participant justifying his own role to his father, so he may exaggerate both the danger and his importance. Level 4 explains the differences through this context and judges which is better evidence of the general mood.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources agree that the revolution of 1789 was driven by the people of Paris?
H: Decide which sources show Paris acting (A, B) and which show the National Assembly acting (C, D).
H: Use your own knowledge of the Great Fear to judge Source C: who forced the Assembly to act on 4 August?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: A shows Parisian opinion inflamed by pamphlets; B shows a Parisian crowd arming itself on 12 July, the start of the events that led to the Bastille. Challenge: C and D are the work of the National Assembly, showing that the legal changes of August were made by deputies, not by crowds; D's protection of property shows a cautious, propertied Assembly with its own aims. Evaluation with knowledge: B is a participant's self-justifying account, but the Bastille confirms its picture; C was passed because of the Great Fear, a rural rising, so C actually shows the **countryside** driving the Assembly, which challenges the view from a different direction; D reflects deputies' principles (Enlightenment, property-owners) not crowd demands. Level 5 judges how far: e.g. the people of Paris drove the revolution at its decisive moments (July, October), but the sources show the Assembly shaped its content and the peasants forced its biggest social change, so the agreement is limited.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was the March of the Women in October 1789 more significant than the storming of the Bastille in ending royal power?
H: Set a criterion: what does "ending royal power" mean? Freedom of action? Control of law-making? Control of force?
H: Compare what changed for the king after 14 July with what changed after 6 October.
A: Bastille: saved the Assembly, showed the king could not rely on his army, started the municipal revolution and the National Guard; but the king stayed at Versailles with his veto and delayed the August Decrees. October Days: forced acceptance of the August Decrees and the Declaration; moved king and Assembly to Paris under the crowd's eye, ending his independence of action. Level 5: e.g. the Bastille ended the king's power to use force against the Assembly; the October Days ended his freedom to obstruct it, so October was more significant for royal power, though it depended on the precedent of July.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Georges Lefebvre argued that there were four revolutions in 1789: aristocratic, bourgeois, popular and peasant. How far does the evidence of 1787–89 support this view?
H: Match each of Lefebvre's "revolutions" to events in this chapter.
H: Then ask whether they were separate revolutions or one connected process, and whether "bourgeois" is the right word for the Third Estate's deputies.
A: Support: aristocratic revolt 1787–88 (Notables, parlements, Vizille); bourgeois revolution 17–20 June (lawyers dominate the National Assembly; Sieyès); popular revolution in Paris (Réveillon riots, Bastille, October Days); peasant revolution (Great Fear, forcing the August Decrees). Challenge: the four were connected, not separate: the aristocratic revolt opened the door, the popular revolution protected the Assembly, the peasant revolt pushed it to abolish feudalism; Cobban's revisionist point that the deputies were mainly lawyers and office-holders rather than a capitalist bourgeoisie; liberal nobles (Lafayette, Noailles, d'Aiguillon) and parish priests played key roles in the "bourgeois" revolution. A* judgement: Lefebvre's model is a useful map of the different actors, but the events were one interconnected crisis in which each group's action changed the others' options.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ The cahiers de doléances are sometimes used as an "opinion poll" of France in 1789. How far are they reliable evidence of what ordinary French people wanted?
H: Who actually drafted a parish cahier, and who wrote the general cahier of an electoral district?
H: What were the cahiers for, and how might that shape what they said?
A: Strengths: tens of thousands written across all orders and regions; they record concrete local grievances (dues, taxes, the gabelle) and broad shared demands (consent to taxation, regular Estates-General, an end to arbitrary arrest). Weaknesses: drafted mostly by literate men (lawyers, notaries, curés), often using model texts that circulated in pamphlets; the Third Estate's general cahiers were written by electors who were mostly bourgeois, and dropped many peasant demands; written to persuade the king and to fit official forms, so loyal and moderate in tone; produced before the summer of 1789 radicalised opinion. Judgement: reliable evidence of the issues that concerned people and of the language educated Frenchmen used in spring 1789, less reliable for the views of the poor and unreliable as a guide to what people would accept after July.
:::

:::recap
- **Ancien Régime**: absolute monarchy limited in practice by privilege, the parlements and the king's weakness; a society of three orders in which the clergy and nobility were exempt from the taille.
- **Third Estate** (about 98 per cent): the bourgeoisie wanted equality and a political voice; town workers feared for bread; peasants paid taxes, tithe and seigneurial dues.
- **Pressure for change**: the American war (1778–83) cost over a thousand million livres and spread ideas of rights; the Enlightenment gave a language of rights and the nation.
- **Failed reforms**: Turgot (1774–76), Necker (1777–81, borrowing, *Compte rendu*), Calonne (1783–87, universal land tax).
- **1787–88**: Notables reject Calonne (dismissed 8 April 1787); Parlement demands the Estates-General; May Edicts and the aristocratic revolt; 1788 budget deficit 126 million livres, interest 318 of 629 million spent; Estates-General called 8 August 1788; Necker reappointed.
- **Politics of representation**: the 1614 ruling (25 September 1788); doubling the Third (27 December 1788) without voting by head; Sieyès (January 1789); the cahiers.
- **Economic crisis**: hailstorm 13 July 1788; bread almost doubled in Paris; unemployment (Eden Treaty 1786); Réveillon riots (April 1789).
- **1789**: Estates-General 5 May; National Assembly 17 June; Tennis Court Oath 20 June; royal session 23 June; Necker dismissed 11 July; Bastille 14 July; Great Fear late July–early August; August Decrees 4–11 August; Declaration 26 August; March of the Women 5–6 October.
- **Judgement**: finance was the necessary cause; the struggle over representation, the king's choices and popular hunger turned a crisis into a revolution.
:::
