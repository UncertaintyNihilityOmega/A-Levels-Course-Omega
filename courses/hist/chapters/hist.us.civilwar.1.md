## Two societies: the South and the North

By 1820 the United States was a young country of about ten million people, but two different ways of life had grown up inside it. To see why they clashed, begin with how each earned its living.

:::def Sectionalism
Loyalty to the interests of one region (a "section") of a country, in this case the North or the South, above the interests of the nation as a whole.
:::

### The South: cotton and slavery

In 1860 the United States had about 31.4 million people. Of these, 3,953,760 were enslaved, about one person in eight. Almost all lived in the South. In the fifteen slave states, roughly 12.2 million people lived, so about a third of the South's population was enslaved. Enslaved people were men, women and children who were bought and sold as property, forced to work without wages, and kept in bondage by law and by violence. Families were broken up by sale. Children were born into slavery. In Southern law an enslaved person had almost no rights.

The slave system grew, it did not fade, as the nineteenth century went on. The reason was cotton. After Eli Whitney's cotton gin (1793) made it easy to clean short-staple cotton, planters spread across the "Deep South" (Georgia, Alabama, Mississippi, Louisiana, later Texas), and the demand of the textile mills of Britain and New England drove the price up. By 1860 cotton made up almost 60 per cent of all American exports, and Southern planters supplied roughly three-quarters of the cotton the world's mills used. That wealth rested on enslaved labour, so the number of enslaved people rose every decade.

Slavery was not spread evenly among white Southerners. Roughly a quarter of white families in the slave states held any enslaved person at all, and a small number of planters held dozens or hundreds. Most white Southerners were small farmers who worked their own land. Yet they mostly supported the system: slavery gave them a place above the Black population, many hoped to become enslavers themselves, and planters dominated Southern politics, newspapers, churches and the law. There were about 250,000 free Black people in the South in 1860 as well as 3.95 million enslaved, and their position was precarious.

The South had few big cities, little factory industry and few railways in proportion to its size. Its capital was tied up in land and in enslaved people, who were the most valuable form of property in the region. This matters for what happened next: when Northerners spoke of limiting slavery, Southern planters heard a threat to the biggest part of their wealth, as well as to their social order.

Southern leaders did not apologise for slavery. In the 1830s many stopped calling it a "necessary evil" and began defending it as a "positive good": John C. Calhoun of South Carolina used those words in the Senate in 1837. They argued from the Bible, from claims about race that we now know to be false, and from comparisons with Northern factory workers. This defence is the background to everything in this chapter. It meant that a compromise which merely "contained" slavery felt to Southerners like an insult, and that attacks on slavery felt like attacks on their way of life.

:::def Slave Power
The Northern belief that a small group of Southern slaveholders controlled the federal government and used it to protect and expand slavery. Many Northerners who disliked slavery but did not call for its abolition were drawn to the Republican Party by this fear.
:::

### The North: free labour, factories and immigrants

The North had a very different economy. Northern farmers worked family farms in the Midwest (Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin), while New England and the Mid-Atlantic built mills, ironworks and shipyards. By 1860 the free states produced roughly nine-tenths of American manufactured goods (historians' estimates vary a little by source). The North had most of the country's roughly 30,000 miles of railway, with a far denser network than the South: railways tied the Midwest to the East coast, not down the Mississippi to the South as they had done before. New York had 813,669 people in 1860 and Philadelphia 565,529, while New Orleans, the biggest city of the Deep South, had only about 169,000.

Immigration made the gap wider. About 2.6 million immigrants arrived in the 1850s, many of them Irish, driven out by the Famine, and Germans. Nearly all settled in the North and Midwest, where there were jobs, and almost none went to the South, where enslaved labour held down wages and opportunities. So the North's population grew far faster than the South's, and that gave it more seats in the House of Representatives and more electoral votes.

Behind these facts lay a belief. Many Northerners held that a free person should be able to work, save, buy land and rise: **free labour**. They saw a society based on slavery as backward, because it denied that hope to both the enslaved and the poor white. Historian Eric Foner, in *Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men* (1970), argued that this free-labour ideology, more than any abolitionist moral appeal, was the core of Northern anti-slavery politics.

:::def Free labour
The Northern ideology that work should be done by free people who could improve themselves through their own effort, buy land and become independent. It saw slavery as unfair competition for free workers and as an obstacle to progress.
:::

### Abolitionism: the moral challenge

A small but growing movement went further and demanded the end of slavery. William Lloyd Garrison began his newspaper *The Liberator* in Boston in 1831 and the American Anti-Slavery Society was founded in 1833. Frederick Douglass, who escaped from slavery in Maryland in 1838, became its most powerful speaker. **Abolitionists** wanted slavery ended everywhere, at once or soon. They were unpopular even in the North: mobs attacked their meetings, and until the 1850s most Northern voters did not support abolition.

The South treated abolitionists as a danger. In 1835 Southern postmasters and mobs blocked abolitionist mail, and from 1836 to 1844 the House of Representatives refused even to read petitions against slavery (the "gag rule"). Notice the effect: the South reacted to a few thousand abolitionists by restricting free speech and a basic right of petition. Many Northerners who disliked abolitionists now began to dislike the South's response.

:::key Why North and South drifted apart
1. **Different economies**: cotton and slavery in the South, factories, free farms and fast-growing cities in the North.
2. **Different populations**: the North grew faster (immigration), so it gained political power over time.
3. **Different values**: "free labour" against a society that defended slavery as a "positive good".
4. **Fear on both sides**: Southerners feared Northern attacks on slavery; Northerners feared a "Slave Power".
Differences alone did not cause a war. Many countries have regions with different economies. What made these differences dangerous was that **slavery was national politics**: every new territory raised the question of which society would grow.
:::

## The tariff and the Nullification crisis

You will see "tariffs" in many textbooks as a cause of the Civil War. They did matter earlier, but they were not the main issue.

:::def Tariff
A tax on imported goods. A **protective tariff** is set high to make imports dearer and protect home industries from foreign competition.
:::

After the War of 1812, Northern manufacturers wanted protection against cheap British goods. The South sold cotton to Britain and bought manufactured goods in return, so a tariff raised the price of what Southerners bought and, they feared, could provoke British retaliation against cotton. Southerners saw a tariff as a tax on the South for the benefit of the North. In 1828 Congress passed a high tariff that the South called the **Tariff of Abominations**. Vice-President John C. Calhoun of South Carolina secretly wrote the *South Carolina Exposition and Protest* (1828), arguing that a state could declare a federal law unconstitutional and refuse to enforce it: this is **nullification**.

When a further tariff came in 1832, South Carolina acted. On 24 November 1832 a state convention adopted an **Ordinance of Nullification**, declaring the tariffs of 1828 and 1832 void in South Carolina from 1 February 1833. President Andrew Jackson, himself a Southerner from Tennessee, answered with a Proclamation (10 December 1832) that nullification was treason, and asked Congress for a Force Bill allowing him to use troops. At the same time Henry Clay of Kentucky steered a Compromise Tariff through Congress that lowered duties gradually over ten years. Both passed on 1 March 1833. South Carolina repealed its ordinance, then, as a face-saving gesture, "nullified" the Force Bill.

Jackson saw further than the tariff. In a letter of May 1833 he predicted that "the tariff was only a pretext": the real object, he thought, was disunion and a Southern confederacy, and the next pretext would be slavery. He was right to see that the ideas of nullification and states' rights could be reused.

:::key What the tariff crisis shows (and does not show)
1. It showed the South's willingness to threaten the Union to defend its interests, and the idea of state sovereignty that secessionists would use in 1860–61.
2. It was settled by compromise, and the tariff lost importance: rates were lowered in 1846 and again in 1857. The Morrill Tariff, which raised duties again, passed in 1861 only after Southern members had left Congress.
3. So the tariff cannot be the main cause of the sectional crisis of the 1850s. Slavery was the issue behind the arguments about territory, laws and parties that follow.
:::

:::warn Do not reduce sectionalism to tariffs
The most common weak answer says the Civil War was "about tariffs" or "about states' rights". The tariff mattered in 1828–33 and then faded. "States' rights" was the argument of the moment, but the question was always: the right to do what? The South's own secession declarations, which you meet in the next chapter, say the answer was to protect slavery. Write "the tariff was a source of tension in 1828–33, but by the 1850s slavery in the territories was the central issue".
:::

## The Missouri Compromise, 1820

Slavery became a national political problem over a question of land: should it be allowed to spread into new states? In 1803 the United States bought the vast Louisiana Territory from France (the Louisiana Purchase). By 1819 there were eleven free and eleven slave states, so the balance of the Senate (two senators each) was even. That balance mattered to the South, because the North already had a majority in the House (it had more people), and so only an equal number of Senate seats guaranteed that no anti-slavery law could pass.

In 1819 Missouri, where about 10,000 enslaved people lived, asked to join the Union as a slave state. Congressman James Tallmadge of New York proposed an amendment that would stop further slaves being brought into Missouri and free the children of the enslaved when they reached 25. The House passed it; the Senate rejected it. Southerners saw it as the first step to abolition.

The crisis was settled in March 1820. The **Missouri Compromise** had three parts:
- **Missouri** entered as a slave state, **Maine** (split off from Massachusetts) as a free state, so the Senate balance stayed even.
- In the rest of the Louisiana Purchase, **slavery was banned north of latitude 36°30′** (the line of Missouri's southern border), and allowed south of it. In practice that left the future Arkansas and Oklahoma open to slavery and the huge land of the northern Plains (the future Kansas and Nebraska and states further north) closed.
- By drawing the line, Congress asserted its right to ban slavery in the territories, a right Southerners had begun to deny.

Former president Thomas Jefferson, who owned more than 600 enslaved people during his life, was not reassured. Writing on 22 April 1820, he said the Missouri question, "like a fire bell in the night, awakened and filled me with terror". He feared that a geographical line between slave and free would harden the divide between sections.

The compromise lasted for 34 years. It worked because it divided future territory in a way both sides could accept, and because few Americans had yet settled in the Plains. It fell apart when the country moved west and wanted that land.

:::def Missouri Compromise
The settlement of 1820 by which Missouri was admitted as a slave state and Maine as a free state, and slavery was prohibited in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase north of latitude 36°30′. It was repealed by the Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854).
:::

## Westward expansion: Texas and the Mexican War

### Manifest Destiny

In the 1840s Americans believed the nation was meant to spread across the continent. In 1845 the journalist John O'Sullivan gave this belief a name, **Manifest Destiny**. It was a patriotic, self-confident idea, often combined with racist assumptions about Native Americans and Mexicans. It drove settlers to Oregon, California and Texas. But expansion meant new states, and new states meant new arguments about slavery. For the first time since 1820, the balance of free and slave states was again in question.

### The absorption of Texas

Texas had been part of Mexico. American settlers, many of them from the South and many bringing enslaved people, moved there from the 1820s. Mexico had abolished slavery in 1829, but did not enforce this fully in Texas. In 1836 the settlers rebelled and declared the independent Republic of Texas. Texas asked to join the United States, but for nearly a decade Presidents refused, because annexation would add a large slave state and risk war with Mexico. Northern Whigs, in particular, opposed it as a plot to extend slavery.

The issue was settled in the 1844 election, when the Democrat James K. Polk, an expansionist who owned enslaved people, won. Before Polk took office, President John Tyler had Congress pass a joint resolution offering Texas statehood (signed 1 March 1845). Texas joined in December 1845 as a slave state. Mexico, which had never recognised Texan independence, broke off relations.

### The Mexican War, 1846–48

Polk wanted more: California and New Mexico. He also claimed that the southern border of Texas was the Rio Grande, not the Nueces River further north. In spring 1846 Polk sent troops into the disputed land. After a clash between American and Mexican soldiers, Polk told Congress that Mexico had "shed American blood upon the American soil", and Congress declared war on 13 May 1846. Many Northerners suspected that the war was a plot of the "Slave Power" to win more slave territory. A young Whig congressman from Illinois, Abraham Lincoln, demanded in 1847 to know the exact "spot" where blood had been shed on American soil.

American armies won. By the **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo** (2 February 1848) Mexico gave up California and the lands that became Nevada, Utah, most of Arizona and parts of New Mexico, Colorado and Wyoming (the **Mexican Cession**, more than half a million square miles) and recognised the Rio Grande border of Texas. The United States paid 15 million dollars. It was the largest single gain of land since the Louisiana Purchase.

:::key Why the Mexican War mattered for sectional tension
1. It added a huge area where the question "free or slave?" had no answer. The 36°30′ line of 1820 applied only to the Louisiana Purchase, so the Mexican Cession was an open question.
2. It split the parties along sectional lines, as Northerners and Southerners disagreed about whether the new land should be open to slavery.
3. It turned a theoretical argument into a practical one: the gold rush of 1848–49 brought so many settlers to California that it needed a state government within a year.
:::

### The Wilmot Proviso, 1846

On 8 August 1846, when Polk asked Congress for 2 million dollars to negotiate a peace with Mexico, David Wilmot, a Democratic congressman from Pennsylvania, moved an amendment. It said that, as "an express and fundamental condition" of acquiring any land from Mexico, "neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall ever exist in any part of said territory, except for crime". This is the **Wilmot Proviso**.

The House passed it by 83 votes to 64, with Northern Democrats and Whigs of both parties voting for it against the wishes of many Southern colleagues. The Senate never voted on it, and it was reintroduced and defeated in later sessions. It never became law. Yet it mattered greatly. For the first time, members of Congress had voted along **sectional**, not party, lines on slavery. Many Northerners had hoped slavery would be "contained", with the Wilmot Proviso as their weapon; Southerners saw it as an attack. The South, said its leaders, had paid in blood in the war and was now told it could not use the land it had helped to win.

:::def Wilmot Proviso
An amendment proposed in August 1846 by Congressman David Wilmot, banning slavery from any territory the United States gained from Mexico. It passed the House but not the Senate, and was never law, but it made the question of slavery in the territories the central issue in American politics.
:::

### Free Soil, popular sovereignty and the 1848 election

Two competing answers to "what should happen to slavery in the territories?" now emerged, with a third just behind.

:::def Free Soil
The belief, and the political movement that held it, that slavery should be kept out of the western territories so that they could be settled by free white farmers. Free Soilers mostly did not demand abolition in the states where slavery already existed.
:::

:::def Popular sovereignty
The idea that the settlers of a territory themselves should decide by vote whether to allow slavery. Its supporters, led by Senator Lewis Cass of Michigan and later Stephen Douglas, argued that this was democratic and kept Congress out of the quarrel.
:::

- **Free Soil**: Congress should ban slavery in the territories (the Wilmot Proviso).
- **Popular sovereignty**: let each territory's settlers vote.
- **Southern rights**: Congress could not ban slavery in the territories at all, because they belonged to all the states. Calhoun argued this in 1847, claiming slaveholders had a constitutional right to take their property anywhere in the common territory.

In 1848 both main parties tried to avoid the issue. The Democrats chose Cass (popular sovereignty); the Whigs chose a Mexican War hero, General Zachary Taylor, a Louisiana slaveholder, and did not write a platform. Anti-slavery Northerners from both parties met at Buffalo, New York, in August 1848 and formed the **Free Soil Party**, with the slogan "Free Soil, Free Speech, Free Labor, Free Men" and former president Martin Van Buren as candidate. Van Buren won about 10 per cent of the popular vote and no electoral votes. Taylor won. But the Free Soilers had shown that opposition to the extension of slavery could win votes, and they had prepared the way for the Republican Party six years later.

## The Compromise of 1850

### The crisis

In 1849, thousands of gold-seekers had poured into California. In late 1849 the Californians drew up a state constitution banning slavery, and applied to join the Union as a free state. President Taylor, himself a slaveholder, supported admission to avoid a long fight. But admitting California would give the free states a majority in the Senate of sixteen to fifteen. Southerners, who had already seen the Wilmot Proviso, were furious. Other disputes piled up: Texas claimed much of the land of New Mexico, slavery and the slave trade were legal in Washington, DC, and Southerners complained that fugitives from slavery were escaping to the North because Northern states did not help to return them. Some Southern leaders spoke openly of secession, and a convention of Southern states was called at Nashville for June 1850.

### The measures

Senator Henry Clay of Kentucky, the "Great Compromiser" who had helped to settle the crisis of 1820, introduced a package of resolutions on 29 January 1850. Calhoun, dying and too weak to speak, had his last speech read for him by Senator James Mason on 4 March. He warned that the ties that held the Union together were snapping one by one, and demanded that the South be given an equal right in the new territories, that fugitive slaves be returned and that attacks on slavery in the North stop. Daniel Webster of Massachusetts, in his Seventh of March speech, supported compromise and a strong fugitive law, to save the Union. Abolitionists called this a betrayal; the minister Theodore Parker wrote that no living man had done so much to corrupt the conscience of the nation. Calhoun died on 31 March, and President Taylor died on 9 July 1850. His successor, Millard Fillmore, supported the compromise.

Clay's package failed as one bill. Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois, a young Democrat, broke it into separate bills so that different majorities could pass each. Between 9 and 20 September 1850 Congress passed five measures:

| Measure | What it did | Who gained |
|---|---|---|
| California admitted | California entered as a **free state** | North |
| Texas boundary | Texas gave up claims to land in New Mexico, in return for 10 million dollars to pay its debt | Mixed (Texas bondholders) |
| Utah and New Mexico | Territories organised with **popular sovereignty**, no ban on slavery | South (in principle) |
| Slave trade in the District of Columbia | The buying and selling of enslaved people was banned in Washington, DC (slavery itself stayed) | North |
| **Fugitive Slave Act** | A strong new law to return people who had escaped slavery | South |

### The Fugitive Slave Act

The Fugitive Slave Act of 18 September 1850 was the harshest part. The Constitution already said that people who escaped slavery must be returned (Article IV), and a law of 1793 had tried to enforce it, but Northern states had passed "personal liberty laws" that made it hard. The 1850 law took the matter out of state hands:

- Federal **commissioners** decided each case, and were paid ten dollars if they ruled that the person was a fugitive and five dollars if not, which many Northerners saw as a bribe to rule for the claimant.
- The alleged fugitive had no right to a jury trial and could not testify in their own defence.
- "All good citizens" were "commanded to aid and assist" in carrying out the law; anyone who helped a fugitive faced a fine up to 1,000 dollars and up to six months in prison.
- The risk fell on free Black Northerners too: someone could be seized on a claim of ownership and have no way to disprove it.

:::def Fugitive Slave Act (1850)
The federal law of 18 September 1850 that required the return of people who had escaped slavery, let federal commissioners decide cases without a jury, forbade the accused to testify, and punished anyone who helped them. It was part of the Compromise of 1850 and a major cause of Northern anger.
:::

In the North the law had a startling effect on people who had never thought about slavery. Now, for the first time, ordinary Northerners could be made to take part in sending a person back into bondage. Thousands of Black people fled to Canada. Some Northern crowds rescued fugitives. In 1854 a Virginian named Anthony Burns, who had escaped to Boston, was arrested, and it took a force of soldiers to march him through the crowded streets to a ship. Many Bostonians who had never been abolitionists joined the crowds that gathered around the courthouse. The novelist Harriet Beecher Stowe wrote *Uncle Tom's Cabin* (1852) partly in anger at the Act (see [the next chapter](#hist.us.civilwar.2)).

### Did the Compromise settle the issue?

At first, many Americans hoped it had. In 1852 both the Democratic and the Whig platforms accepted the Compromise as a "finality" and promised not to reopen the slavery question. But the settlement had weaknesses:
- It gave the South a law that angered the North, and gave the North a free California the South resented.
- **Popular sovereignty** in Utah and New Mexico was vague: it did not say when settlers could decide, so the question was left open.
- It did not touch the biggest territory still unorganised, the land of the Plains north of 36°30′, where the 1820 ban still applied.
- It depended on people not looking too closely. The Fugitive Slave Act made them look.

:::key Why the Compromise of 1850 failed to end tension
1. The **Fugitive Slave Act** made the North take part in slavery and turned moderates against the South.
2. It did not settle the future of the remaining western land, so expansion would reopen the issue.
3. It left both sides with grievances: Southerners felt California was a loss, and many Northerners felt they had been forced to give up their principles.
4. The "finality" depended on both parties, and the parties were about to break up.
:::

## The Kansas–Nebraska Act, 1854

### Why Douglas introduced it

In 1854 Senator Stephen Douglas of Illinois wanted to organise the land west of Missouri and Iowa into territories, so that settlers could move in and a railway could run from Chicago to the Pacific. The land, called Nebraska, lay north of 36°30′ and so was closed to slavery by the Missouri Compromise. Southern senators had no wish to see another free area, and some, such as Senator David Atchison of Missouri, whose state would be surrounded by free territory, threatened to block the bill. Douglas, an ambitious Democrat who hoped to become president and needed Southern votes, then gave way. He split the land into two territories, **Kansas** and **Nebraska**, and added a clause which in effect repealed the Missouri Compromise's ban. A Kentucky Whig, Archibald Dixon, had already proposed this repeal, and Douglas's draft was revised accordingly.

The **Kansas–Nebraska Act** was signed by President Franklin Pierce on 30 May 1854. Section 14 says that the Missouri Compromise, "inconsistent with the principle of non-intervention by Congress", was "inoperative and void", and that it was "the true intent and meaning of this act not to legislate slavery into any Territory or State, nor to exclude it therefrom, but to leave the people thereof perfectly free to form and regulate their domestic institutions in their own way". The Senate passed it 37 to 14 and the House 113 to 100, with most Southern members in favour and Northern members badly split.

:::def Kansas–Nebraska Act
The law of 30 May 1854, introduced by Senator Stephen Douglas, that created Kansas and Nebraska Territories, repealed the Missouri Compromise ban on slavery north of 36°30′ and applied popular sovereignty, so that the settlers would decide whether to allow slavery.
:::

### The reaction

Douglas expected a short storm. Instead, the Act set off the greatest political upheaval since the Constitution. Six anti-slavery members of Congress, led by Salmon P. Chase and Joshua Giddings of Ohio and Charles Sumner of Massachusetts, published an *Appeal of the Independent Democrats* (January 1854) that called it "a gross violation of a sacred pledge". For thirty-four years the Missouri Compromise had stood as a settlement between the sections. Now the South, with the help of a Northern Democrat, had torn up a bargain that had closed a vast area to slavery.

Many Northerners who disliked slavery but cared little about abolition now saw the Slave Power at work. Others feared that settlers from the free states would be shut out of the Plains. The South, for its part, was divided: some Southerners doubted that Kansas would ever really become a slave state, since the climate did not suit cotton, but they felt the principle was important: the North should not be able to veto slavery by law in any territory. In effect the Act made it certain that Kansas would become the battleground.

:::key Why the Kansas–Nebraska Act raised tension so sharply
1. It **repealed the Missouri Compromise**, the 1820 settlement, and so broke the trust that compromises would last.
2. It opened land north of 36°30′ to slavery for the first time, which shocked many Northerners who had accepted the old line.
3. It **destroyed the Whig Party and split the Democrats**, and created the Republican Party (below).
4. It put the slavery question to a vote in a territory where settlers from both sections would rush in to win it, which led directly to violence.
:::

## "Bleeding Kansas", 1854–59

### The struggle for Kansas

If settlers were to decide the future of Kansas, then each side wanted more settlers. Anti-slavery New Englanders formed the **New England Emigrant Aid Company** (1854) and founded the town of Lawrence. Slaveholders from Missouri, just across the border, crossed in large numbers to vote in the elections. In the territorial election of 30 March 1855, thousands of these **border ruffians** voted illegally, and the pro-slavery side won 37 of the 39 seats in the legislature. Governor Andrew Reeder cancelled results in some districts, but a pro-slavery legislature met at Shawnee Mission and passed harsh laws, including heavy penalties for helping fugitives and for denying the right to hold enslaved people in Kansas.

Free-State settlers refused to accept this "bogus" legislature. They held their own convention and wrote a free-state constitution (the **Topeka Constitution**, December 1855). Kansas now had two governments. President Pierce recognised the pro-slavery one. Northern newspapers called it "the rape of Kansas".

:::def Bleeding Kansas
The violent struggle in Kansas Territory between pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers from 1854 to 1859, after the Kansas–Nebraska Act made popular sovereignty the rule. About 56 people were killed (Kansas Historical Society estimate), but its effect on national opinion was greater than its death toll.
:::

### Violence

In May 1856 a pro-slavery posse led by a county sheriff entered Lawrence, destroyed two newspaper offices and burned the Free State Hotel on 21 May (the "Sack of Lawrence"). The abolitionist John Brown, a fervent believer that slavery was a sin that must be ended by force, was enraged. On the night of 24 May 1856 Brown and a small party dragged five pro-slavery settlers from their cabins by Pottawatomie Creek and killed them with broadswords. This is the **Pottawatomie Massacre**. In the following months there were raids, skirmishes (Brown fought at Osawatomie in August 1856) and ambushes; and in 1856 the territory was close to civil war. A new governor, John Geary, and federal troops restored a rough peace in the autumn of 1856, but violence continued off and on until 1859.

### The Brooks–Sumner incident, 22 May 1856

The violence in Kansas had a counterpart in the Senate. On 19 and 20 May 1856 Senator Charles Sumner of Massachusetts, a leading anti-slavery Republican, delivered a long speech called "The Crime against Kansas", attacking the pro-slavery forces and the "Slave Power". He singled out Senator Andrew Butler of South Carolina, an elderly man, and mocked him with a coarse image that linked slavery to a mistress. Two days later, on 22 May, Butler's relative, Congressman Preston Brooks of South Carolina, walked into the Senate chamber while it was nearly empty, found Sumner at his desk and beat him on the head with a heavy gutta-percha cane until the cane broke and Sumner fell unconscious, trapped under the desk.

Sumner's injuries were serious: he was away from the Senate for most of the next three years and suffered from them for the rest of his life. A House vote to expel Brooks fell short of the two-thirds needed (121 to 95). Brooks resigned to give his voters the chance to judge him, was re-elected in August, and was fined 300 dollars in court; no prison sentence followed. Southerners sent him canes in tribute. In the North, Sumner became a martyr and the attack was taken as proof that the South settled arguments by violence: a Northern lithograph by John L. Magee, *Southern Chivalry: Dignity versus Impudence* (1856), showed Brooks beating a defenceless Sumner while other Southerners looked on.

:::key Why Bleeding Kansas and the Brooks–Sumner incident mattered
1. They showed that **popular sovereignty did not work**: it invited both sides to fight for the territory.
2. They made each side believe the worst of the other: Northerners saw a Slave Power ready to cheat and kill, Southerners saw abolitionist invaders and fanatics.
3. They moved politics out of debate and into violence, even inside the Senate.
4. They strengthened the new Republican Party, which used "Bleeding Kansas" in the 1856 campaign.
:::

### The Lecompton Constitution, 1857–58

Kansas Territory entered its last crisis under President James Buchanan, a Pennsylvanian elected in 1856. In September 1857 a convention at Lecompton, elected in a vote that Free-State settlers boycotted, wrote a constitution that protected slavery, including the property rights of existing enslavers. The convention then refused to let the voters accept or reject the whole constitution: they could vote only for the constitution "with slavery" or "without slavery", and even the second option protected slaves already in Kansas. Free-Staters boycotted the vote of 21 December 1857, and "with slavery" won. In a second vote on 4 January 1858, called by the territorial legislature, the constitution as a whole was rejected by 10,226 to 138; supporters of the constitution largely stayed away.

Buchanan, who relied on Southern support, backed the Lecompton Constitution and asked Congress to admit Kansas as a slave state. The Senate agreed (33 to 25 on 23 March 1858), but the House refused. Stephen Douglas, who had championed popular sovereignty, broke with the president: he said the Lecompton vote was a fraud on the settlers' right to decide. Congress offered Kansas land grants if it accepted the constitution (the English Bill), and in a vote on 2 August 1858 the voters rejected it again, by more than 11,000 to fewer than 2,000. Kansas finally entered the Union as a free state on 29 January 1861, after Southern senators had left Congress.

:::key What the Lecompton crisis showed
1. Even a Democratic president could not make popular sovereignty work when the result was clear: the majority in Kansas was against slavery.
2. It **split the Democratic Party**, the last party with strong support in both sections, because Douglas and Northern Democrats would not support a rigged vote.
3. It reinforced Northern suspicion that the Buchanan government was serving the Slave Power.
:::

## Changes in the party system

In 1820 the sections had been divided on slavery; the national parties had mostly managed to hold both sections together. Between 1850 and 1860 that system broke. This section explains how, because a party system that cannot hold North and South together removes a barrier between disagreement and disunion.

### The decline of the Whig Party

The Whigs had been one of two national parties since the 1830s, with support in the North and South. But the Compromise of 1850 divided them: Northern "Conscience Whigs" hated the Fugitive Slave Act, while Southern "Cotton Whigs" demanded protection for slavery. In 1852 the Whig candidate General Winfield Scott lost badly to the Democrat Franklin Pierce, winning only 42 electoral votes to Pierce's 254; Henry Clay and Daniel Webster, the party's leaders, had both died during the year. The Kansas–Nebraska Act finished the job: almost all Northern Whigs opposed it, almost all Southern Whigs supported it, and the party collapsed.

### The Know-Nothings

Between the Whigs and the Republicans lay a short-lived party built on a different issue. Many Americans, especially Protestant workers in the North, feared the arrival of Irish Catholic immigrants. A secret society, the Order of the Star-Spangled Banner, grew into the American Party, nicknamed the **Know-Nothings** because members answered questions about it with "I know nothing". In 1854–55 it won elections in the Northeast. But it too split over slavery in 1855, with Northern members leaving to join the Republicans. Its last candidate was former president Millard Fillmore in 1856.

### The rise of the Republican Party

The Republican Party was born in 1854 in meetings across the North that opposed the Kansas–Nebraska Act (the first to suggest the name was at Ripon, Wisconsin, in March, and a convention near Jackson, Michigan, on 6 July 1854 adopted it for a state ticket). It joined three groups: Free Soilers, anti-slavery ("Conscience") Whigs and Northern Democrats who rejected Douglas's act. Their programme was to stop the spread of slavery into the territories. Most Republicans did **not** propose to end slavery in the states where it existed, and they said so repeatedly. They also appealed to Northern economic interests in free farms and internal improvements.

The Republican Party was unlike any earlier party because it existed only in the North. In 1856 its first presidential candidate, John C. Frémont, an army officer and explorer, won 33.1 per cent of the vote and 114 electoral votes, all from Northern states, running under the slogan "Free Soil, Free Men, Frémont". The Democrat James Buchanan won with 45.3 per cent and 174 electoral votes, carrying every Southern state except Maryland and also Pennsylvania, New Jersey, Indiana, Illinois and California. Fillmore, running for the Know-Nothings, took 21.5 per cent but only Maryland (8 electoral votes). If Frémont had won Pennsylvania and one other state he would have been president without a single Southern vote. That alone alarmed the South.

:::def Republican Party
The anti-slavery-extension party formed in the North in 1854 from Free Soilers, Northern Whigs and anti-Nebraska Democrats. It opposed the spread of slavery into the territories but, before 1861, did not call for its abolition in the states.
:::

:::def Doughface
A Northern politician who supported Southern interests, especially on slavery. Franklin Pierce and James Buchanan were both called doughfaces by their opponents.
:::

### The Lincoln–Douglas debates, 1858

In 1858 the Republicans chose a former Whig congressman from Illinois, **Abraham Lincoln**, to run against Stephen Douglas for the Senate seat from Illinois. Accepting the nomination at Springfield on 16 June 1858, Lincoln gave what became known as the **House Divided** speech. "A house divided against itself cannot stand," he began (borrowing the words of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark): "I believe this government cannot endure, permanently half slave and half free. I do not expect the Union to be dissolved—I do not expect the house to fall—but I do expect it will cease to be divided. It will become all one thing, or all the other." He argued that a "slave power" conspiracy was working to make slavery lawful in every state, and that only the Republicans could stop it.

The seven debates between the two men (21 August to 15 October 1858, at Ottawa, Freeport, Jonesboro, Charleston, Galesburg, Quincy and Alton) were reported across the country. Lincoln pressed the moral wrong of slavery and argued that it should not be allowed to spread; Douglas accused him of favouring racial equality and defended popular sovereignty. Lincoln was not an abolitionist at this time: he said that he did not intend to interfere with slavery in the Southern states and he opposed giving Black Americans equal political and social rights, which shows how limited the Republican position was.

At Freeport on 27 August 1858 Lincoln asked Douglas whether the people of a territory could keep slavery out when the Supreme Court had said in *Dred Scott* (1857; see [the next chapter](#hist.us.civilwar.2)) that Congress could not. Douglas replied that, whatever the Court said, slavery could not exist without local police laws, so settlers could still exclude it by refusing to pass them. This **Freeport Doctrine** saved him in Illinois but angered the South, who now saw him as unreliable. Douglas won the Senate seat in the Illinois legislature, 54 to 46 (January 1859), though Republican candidates had won more votes overall (about 190,000 to 166,000 for the Douglas side). Lincoln lost the seat but won a national reputation.

### Splits within the Democratic Party

By 1860 the Democrats were the only major party still strong in both sections, and they too broke. The split followed the Lecompton fight and the Freeport Doctrine: Southern Democrats demanded a federal **slave code** that would protect slavery in all territories, while Douglas's Northern wing refused. At the party's convention in Charleston, South Carolina (23 April to 3 May 1860), about fifty Southern delegates walked out when the convention would not accept a pro-slavery platform. When the party reassembled at Baltimore in June, the Northern wing nominated Douglas, and the Southern wing nominated Vice-President John C. Breckinridge of Kentucky. With the Republicans now ready to nominate Lincoln, the stage was set for the election of 1860 (see [the next chapter](#hist.us.civilwar.2)).

:::key How the party system fell apart
1. **Whigs** collapsed after 1850–54 (Compromise of 1850, Kansas–Nebraska Act).
2. **Know-Nothings** rose and split in 1854–56 (immigration, then slavery).
3. **Republicans** grew in 1854–56 as a Northern anti-extension party, with no Southern support.
4. **Democrats** split in 1858–60 (Lecompton, the Freeport Doctrine, the slave code).
By 1860 there was no national party left. Without parties that spanned the sections, there was nothing to hold the Union together except the Constitution.
:::

## Why was there increasing tension? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | Different economies and societies; slavery's growth with cotton; abolitionism and the defence of slavery as a "positive good" | Created two different ways of life and mutual fear, but had existed for decades without war |
| Medium-term | The Missouri Compromise; the tariff and Nullification; Texas and the Mexican War; Wilmot Proviso and Free Soil | Made the question of slavery in the territories the central political issue |
| Short-term | The Compromise of 1850, Kansas–Nebraska Act, Bleeding Kansas, Lecompton | Broke trust in compromises and drove voters towards sectional parties |
| Political | Collapse of the Whigs and Know-Nothings, the rise of the Republicans, the split of the Democrats | Removed the national parties that had restrained conflict |
| Triggers and symbols | The Fugitive Slave Act, Brooks–Sumner, Lincoln–Douglas | Turned dislike into anger |

:::key A judgement you can defend
**Slavery** was the underlying cause: it explains why the economies, the societies and the politics of the sections diverged, and it was the subject of every crisis in the chapter. **Territorial expansion** was the *arena*: because each new territory raised the question "slave or free?", expansion turned the existence of slavery into a national political problem. The **breakdown of the party system** was the *mechanism*: when the Whigs and then the Democrats split along sectional lines, politics itself became sectional. The tariff and "states' rights" were arguments used at the time, not the root of the dispute.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **David Potter**, *The Impending Crisis, 1848–1861* (1976), stressed that the argument over slavery in the territories gave the sections a way to attack each other's values and so drove them apart; he also showed how political leaders struggled to contain the crisis.
- **James McPherson**, *Battle Cry of Freedom* (1988), described a North and a South that diverged ever further over slavery, with the Republican rise and the Southern response as the climax.
- **Eric Foner**, *Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men* (1970), argued that Republicans were driven by a free-labour ideology, not primarily by sympathy for the enslaved.
- **Michael Holt**, *The Political Crisis of the 1850s* (1978), stressed the collapse of the old party system as the key change, rather than a simple growth of anti-slavery feeling.
- Older debates: **James G. Randall** argued in 1940 that the war came from a "blundering generation" of politicians; **Allan Nevins**, in *Ordeal of the Union* (from 1947), argued that slavery made the conflict hard to avoid.
At AS, Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the Kansas–Nebraska Act increased tension between North and South.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The Kansas–Nebraska Act was passed in 1854 and it was introduced by Stephen Douglas. It said that the people of Kansas and Nebraska could decide whether they wanted slavery. This repealed the Missouri Compromise. There was fighting in Kansas after that, which was called Bleeding Kansas. So there was more tension between the North and South."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes. "So there was more tension" is asserted, not explained: the answer never says why repeal mattered to Northerners or why settlers fighting made the sections angrier. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The Act increased tension first because it repealed the Missouri Compromise. Since 1820, the 36°30′ line had been accepted as a settlement that closed the northern Plains to slavery. Northerners felt that the South, with Douglas's help, had broken a bargain they had kept for thirty-four years, and the Appeal of the Independent Democrats called it 'a gross violation of a sacred pledge'. A second reason was that popular sovereignty invited both sides to rush settlers into Kansas. Pro-slavery Missourians crossed the border to vote illegally in 1855, winning 37 of the 39 seats in the legislature, while the New England Emigrant Aid Company sent anti-slavery settlers. Each side then saw the other as cheating, and this led to violence."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was the effect on the party system. The Act divided the Northern and Southern Whigs so deeply that the party collapsed, and it split the Northern Democrats, many of whom joined the Free Soilers and anti-slavery Whigs in the new Republican Party, founded in 1854. For the first time a major party existed only in the North, so politics itself became sectional.

These factors were linked. The repeal broke trust, and that made Northerners suspicious of any Southern proposal; popular sovereignty then turned that suspicion into a contest on the ground in Kansas, where the cheating and the killing at Pottawatomie (24 May 1856) seemed to confirm each side's fear; and the party realignment turned that anger into a permanent political force, the Republicans, who won 114 electoral votes in 1856 without a single Southern vote. The most important reason for the increase in tension was the repeal itself, because it reopened a question that had been settled and made every later compromise look unreliable. Without it there would have been no Bleeding Kansas and no Republican Party."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (repeal broke trust, popular sovereignty created the conflict, the party shift made it permanent), and a supported conclusion about the main factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

As an express and fundamental condition to the acquisition of any territory from the Republic of Mexico, … neither slavery nor involuntary servitude shall ever exist in any part of said territory, except for crime.

*Adapted from the Wilmot Proviso, an amendment moved in the House of Representatives by David Wilmot, a Democratic congressman from Pennsylvania, 8 August 1846.*

**Source B**

The Union is being weakened, Calhoun warned, because the ties that hold it together are being broken one by one. To save it, the North must grant the South an equal right in the territories acquired from Mexico, must carry out the duty to return fugitives from slavery, and must stop its attacks on slavery.

*Closely paraphrased from John C. Calhoun's last speech to the Senate, written while he was ill and read for him by Senator James Mason of Virginia, 4 March 1850. This is a summary written for practice, not Calhoun's own words.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about disagreements between North and South over slavery in the territories gained from Mexico.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is the Wilmot Proviso and says slavery should not exist in any land taken from Mexico. Source B is Calhoun and says the South should have an equal right to the land. So they disagree. Source A is from a Northerner and Source B is from a Southerner, so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share some ground. Both are about the same land, the territory won from Mexico, and both treat the federal government as the body that must decide what happens to slavery there. Both also present their demand as a matter of right: Source A makes the ban 'an express and fundamental condition' of acquiring the land, and Source B demands an 'equal right' for the South in what the whole nation had gained.

On the question itself, however, they are directly opposed. Source A would forbid slavery in every part of the new territory, with only punishment for crime as an exception. Source B would allow Southerners to take enslaved people into the same land on equal terms. Source A also speaks only of the territory, while Source B adds two more Southern demands, the return of fugitives and an end to Northern attacks on slavery, which shows that for Calhoun the territories were one grievance among several.

Context explains the clash. Source A was moved in August 1846 as an amendment to a bill asking for 2 million dollars to negotiate peace in a war that had hardly begun, by a Democrat from Pennsylvania who supported President Polk. Its purpose was to make sure that free Northern farmers, not slaveholders, would settle the new land, and it passed the House but never the Senate. It is evidence of Northern opinion, but a limited one: Wilmot said he was protecting free white labour, not attacking slavery in the South. Source B is later, from March 1850, when California had applied to join as a free state and the Senate was debating Henry Clay's compromise. It comes from the dying leader of the South, so its purpose was to make a last warning, and its tone is pessimistic because Calhoun believed the South was losing its political balance. The speech was read for him by someone else, which fits its role as a statement of principle rather than a negotiation. The four years between them explain why the Southern demand is harder: in 1846 the question was hypothetical, while by 1850 the gold rush had made California a state in all but name and the South saw a free state about to join. Source A is therefore stronger evidence of the origin of the dispute and Source B of how entrenched the Southern position had become."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the war, the gold rush, the debate of 1850) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue. A **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Territorial expansion was the main reason for increasing tension between North and South before 1860.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the Missouri crisis (1819–20); Texas and the Mexican War, the Wilmot Proviso, Free Soil; the Compromise of 1850; Kansas–Nebraska, Bleeding Kansas, Lecompton; every major crisis began with the question of slavery in new land.
- Against 1, differences of society and economy, and slavery itself: cotton, "positive good", free labour; abolitionism; the Fugitive Slave Act (a fight over slavery in the North, not the territories).
- Against 2, politics: the collapse of the Whigs, the rise of the Republicans and the Democratic split.
- Against 3, events and personalities: Brooks–Sumner, the Lincoln–Douglas debates.
- Judgement: territory was the arena but slavery was the cause; criterion: what explains why the sections could no longer compromise.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Territorial expansion was very important. The Missouri Compromise of 1820 was needed because Missouri wanted to join as a slave state. After the Mexican War of 1846–48, the United States gained California and the Southwest, and David Wilmot proposed that slavery should be banned there. The Compromise of 1850 settled it for a while, but then in 1854 the Kansas–Nebraska Act repealed the Missouri Compromise and there was fighting in Kansas. This shows that territorial expansion was the main reason for the increase in tension."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet expansion explains where the sections fought, not why they fought. Every territorial dispute was about one thing: whether slavery could spread. The land was the arena, and the underlying cause was slavery and the very different societies built on it. By 1860 about 3.95 million people were enslaved, and cotton made up almost 60 per cent of American exports, so Southern leaders believed their wealth and their social order depended on slavery's survival and growth. The North, with its free-labour ideology, believed that slavery's growth would close the West to free farmers. Neither belief depended on any one territory. The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 shows this: it concerned no territory at all, but it did more than any land question to turn ordinary Northerners against the South, by forcing them to take part in returning people to slavery.

The political system also changed in ways that expansion alone cannot explain. As late as 1852 both parties accepted the Compromise of 1850 as a 'finality'. It was the failure of the parties that took away the means of compromise. The Whigs collapsed over the Kansas–Nebraska Act, the Republican Party formed in 1854 with no Southern members, and in 1860 the Democrats split in two over a federal slave code. The first all-Northern party won 114 electoral votes in 1856, and in 1860 Lincoln was able to win the presidency with 180 electoral votes without a single Southern state. That is a result of the party system's breakdown, which was itself caused by disputes over slavery in the territories, so it connects back to expansion but cannot be reduced to it.

Overall, expansion was the main reason why slavery turned into a national political crisis, because it gave the sections a constant, practical reason to quarrel and it destroyed each compromise in turn (1820, 1850, 1854). But it was not the main reason for the tension itself. That lay in slavery and in the different societies and values it created, and the collapse of the parties made it impossible to contain. The statement is therefore best seen as true of how the quarrel was fought, but not of why it existed."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a criterion (the difference between the arena and the cause) and ranks the factors. With an opening paragraph on the case for expansion, this is Level 5 (19–20). Note that "the territory" and "slavery" are not rival causes in most answers: the best answers show how they were connected, then decide which one explains the tension.
:::

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that Northern opinion hardened against the South in the 1850s?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (a law that forced Northerners to join in returning people to slavery, a cause of anger), B (Northern opponents call the Act a violation of a pledge), C (a Northern artist presents Southerners as violent and cowardly), D (a Northern politician says the nation cannot remain half slave). Challenge or qualify: A (the law was passed by Congress, with Northern votes, and signed by a Northern-born president), B (written by a few men already known as anti-slavery), C (a cartoon by one publisher in Philadelphia), D (Lincoln says he does not expect the Union to be dissolved, and says nothing of ending slavery in the South).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: an official text, so reliable about what the law said. But as evidence of Northern *opinion* it is indirect: it is the South's demand, in law, and it shows what caused the anger rather than the anger. Its effect is shown by what followed: the rescue attempts in Boston, the escape of thousands to Canada, and the writing of *Uncle Tom's Cabin*.
- **B**: written by Free Soil and Republican-leaning members, with a clear aim of persuading the North to oppose Douglas's bill. Their use of "sacred pledge" appeals to a sense of fairness, not just to hostility to slavery. It shows the views of the most committed, but the Kansas–Nebraska Act did pass the House 113 to 100 with some Northern Democratic votes, so it does not show that all Northerners agreed.
- **C**: a cartoon is propaganda; it exaggerates for effect and was made for a Northern market. But the fact that it was published, and that Sumner was treated as a martyr, shows a large audience was ready to believe the picture. Southerners saw the attack differently, sending Brooks canes.
- **D**: a political speech to a Republican convention; it is both a statement of belief and a campaign opening. Lincoln lost the 1858 Senate race, but the speech made him known. It is strong evidence of the Republican view, and notably moderate: he said the Union would not be dissolved.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A, B and C agree that Northern anger grew in the early and middle 1850s, and each gives a different reason. In Source A the cause is the law itself, which forced citizens to take part in returning people to slavery and denied the accused a hearing; in Source B it is a broken promise, the Missouri Compromise; in Source C it is violence, the attack on Sumner. Source D shows what that anger became by 1858: a party leader saying that the nation must in the end be 'all one thing, or all the other'. Together they suggest a steady hardening, and the party results confirm it: the Whigs were gone by 1856 and the Republicans, who were not even founded until 1854, won 33.1 per cent of the vote and carried 11 free states in 1856. On balance the sources strongly support the view that Northern opinion hardened. But the evidence also needs care. Source B comes from men who were already anti-slavery, Source C comes from a single Northern publisher, and both A and B had Northern supporters: many Northern Democrats voted for the Kansas–Nebraska Act, and Buchanan won Pennsylvania in 1856, so the North was never united. Source D, too, is moderate, as Lincoln did not threaten the South's institution where it existed. The sources therefore agree to a large extent: Northern opinion hardened against the *extension* of slavery and the Slave Power, but not into a united demand to end slavery."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of the 1850s; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the Fugitive Slave Act the most important reason for growing tension between North and South in the 1850s?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Fugitive Slave Act was passed on 18 September 1850. It was part of the Compromise of 1850. It said that federal commissioners would decide whether a person was a fugitive and would get ten dollars if the person was sent back and five dollars if they were freed. It said that the accused could not speak in court or have a jury. It also said that citizens had to help catch fugitives. Many Northerners did not like it. Anthony Burns was arrested in Boston in 1854. Other things also happened like Bleeding Kansas and the Kansas–Nebraska Act."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it retells what the Act said without saying why it mattered for the question, and the last sentence merely names other events. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The Fugitive Slave Act was important because it brought slavery into the lives of Northerners who had never thought about it. Federal commissioners were paid more for returning a person than for freeing them, the accused could not testify, and citizens were commanded to help, so a Boston crowd that would have ignored a distant dispute about Kansas found itself watching soldiers march Anthony Burns to a ship in 1854. However, it did less than the Kansas–Nebraska Act to change the party system: the Act hurt the Whigs, but the Republicans were founded because of the repeal of the 36°30′ line in 1854." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name three ways in which the North and South differed in 1860.
A: Economy (North: factories, railways, free farms and cities; South: cotton plantations worked by about 3.95 million enslaved people); population (the North grew faster, mainly through immigration); values (free labour in the North, slavery defended as a "positive good" in the South).
Q: What were the three parts of the Missouri Compromise (1820)?
A: Missouri entered as a slave state and Maine as a free state; slavery was banned north of 36°30′ in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase; the balance of free and slave states in the Senate stayed equal.
Q: What did the Wilmot Proviso propose, and why did it matter even though it was never law?
A: It proposed that slavery should be banned from any territory taken from Mexico. It passed the House (83–64) but not the Senate. It mattered because members voted by section rather than party, and the question of slavery in the territories became the central issue.
Q: List the five parts of the Compromise of 1850.
A: California admitted as a free state; Texas boundary settled with 10 million dollars to Texas; Utah and New Mexico organised with popular sovereignty; the slave trade (not slavery) banned in the District of Columbia; a stronger Fugitive Slave Act.
Q: Why did the Fugitive Slave Act anger many Northerners?
A: It let federal commissioners decide cases without a jury, paid them more for returning people, banned the accused from testifying and ordered citizens to help, so Northerners were forced to take part in enforcing slavery and free Black Northerners were at risk.
Q: What did the Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854) do, and why was it controversial?
A: It created Kansas and Nebraska Territories, repealed the Missouri Compromise ban on slavery north of 36°30′ and applied popular sovereignty. It broke a 34-year settlement and opened land closed to slavery.
Q: What was "Bleeding Kansas"?
A: Violence in Kansas Territory from 1854 to 1859 between pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers, including the Sack of Lawrence (21 May 1856) and the Pottawatomie killings (24 May 1856), with about 56 people killed.
Q: Why did the Brooks–Sumner incident matter?
A: It showed in the Senate itself the violence that Kansas had shown on the Plains. The North saw it as proof that the South used force; the South hailed Brooks, and the two sides drifted further apart.
Q: How did the Lincoln–Douglas debates and the Freeport Doctrine affect the Democratic Party?
A: Douglas's answer at Freeport (27 August 1858) that settlers could keep slavery out by refusing to pass local laws angered Southern Democrats, who demanded a federal slave code, and led to the party's split in 1860.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Missouri Compromise was reached in 1820.
A: Indicative content: Missouri's request to join as a slave state in 1819 threatened the balance of eleven free and eleven slave states in the Senate; Tallmadge's amendment (restricting slavery in Missouri) passed the House but not the Senate, and Southerners feared it was a first step to abolition; Maine's request to be admitted as a free state allowed a trade (Missouri slave, Maine free); the 36°30′ line gave both sides a clear division of future territory in the Louisiana Purchase; Clay's skill as a mediator. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: the balance of Senate power made neither side willing to give way, and the trade of Maine for Missouri plus the line made a bargain possible. It ends with a judgement on the main reason, e.g. fear of losing Senate balance.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Fugitive Slave Act (1850) increased sectional tension.
A: Indicative content: its harsh provisions (no jury trial, no testimony, commissioners' fees of ten and five dollars, penalties for helping fugitives) outraged Northerners; it made ordinary citizens complicit in slavery; free Black Northerners were at risk of being seized; Northern states responded with personal liberty laws and crowds rescued fugitives (Anthony Burns case 1854); Southerners saw this resistance as a breach of the Constitution and of the Compromise, and demanded stricter enforcement. Level 4: connect the Northern reaction to the Southern response (each side's behaviour confirmed the other's suspicion) and judge which effect mattered most, e.g. turning moderates against the South.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent did differences between the economies of the North and the South cause increasing tension before 1860?
A: For: the South's wealth was tied to cotton and slavery (about 3.95 million enslaved people; cotton about 60 per cent of exports), while the North had industry, railways and immigrants; the two economies wanted different policies on tariffs, land and internal improvements; the North's faster population growth threatened Southern political balance. Against: tariff disputes faded after 1833 and rates were lowered in 1846 and 1857; the economies were also interdependent (Northern mills used Southern cotton); the disputes of the 1850s concerned slavery in the territories, not trade; Southern fears and Northern ideology (free labour, Slave Power) mattered more. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that economic difference mattered through slavery, which was both an economic and a moral and political system, so the difference mattered less as an economic argument than as the base of two different societies.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The collapse of the Whig Party was the main reason for growing sectional tension in the 1850s.' How far do you agree?
A: For: the Whigs had been a national party that held North and South together; they split over the Compromise of 1850 and collapsed after Kansas–Nebraska; their collapse left the Republicans (Northern) and the Democrats (increasingly Southern in tone); Know-Nothings split; without national parties, sectional parties could dominate. Against: the Whigs collapsed because of slavery disputes, not the other way round; the Fugitive Slave Act, Bleeding Kansas and Brooks–Sumner were more direct causes of anger; the Democrats stayed national until 1860. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "what made compromise impossible?", and judges, e.g., that the party collapse was a result of tension as well as a cause, and was an important accelerator rather than the main reason.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about attempts to settle the issue of slavery in the territories.
A: Similarities: both concern how to deal with slavery in the western lands and show it as a source of anxiety or controversy (A fears the Union will not survive the question; B offers a plan to leave it to the settlers). Differences: A (1820) is a private reaction and a warning that the Missouri settlement hides a deeper problem; B (1854) is a law, and a proposed answer that treats the matter as one of local democracy. A fears a geographical line dividing the country; B sweeps a geographical line away. Explanation: A is a retired ex-president's private letter, written in the Missouri crisis, so frank and personal; B is the official language of Congress and is designed to justify the repeal of the old line. Level 4 explains the differences through nature and purpose and judges which is better evidence, e.g. A as a warning of what would follow and B as proof that the 1820 settlement did not last.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources support the view that attempts to settle the issue of slavery in the territories were failing by 1858?
H: Order the sources in time: what was each trying to settle, and did it last?
H: Source C was written in 1857 and Source D in 1858: what does each tell you about whether the 1857 hope came true?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature, origin and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: A (1820) warns that the Missouri settlement is a temporary truce; B (1854) shows that the 1820 settlement had been repealed and replaced by a new formula; C (1857) hopes that the problem would soon be finally settled, which implies it was not yet; D (1858) shows the voters of Kansas rejecting the pro-slavery constitution by 10,226 to 138. Challenge: B and C show that politicians still tried to settle the issue by new means, and the settlements of 1820 and 1850 had in fact lasted 34 and 4 years respectively; D's figure comes from a poll that supporters of the constitution boycotted, so it measures Free-State opinion, not the whole territory. Evaluation with knowledge: A is a private letter, so frank, but written by a retired president with a stake in the issue (he owned enslaved people); B is a law's wording, so reliable about what the law said, but not about how it was received; C is a president's hope, written for his inauguration, so not evidence of what actually happened; D is a vote count in a poll with a known boycott. Level 5 judges how far: the sources agree that every attempt failed or was in trouble, but they also show that the settlements lasted a good while and that the 1858 vote reflects a boycott, so it is strong support for "failing" but not for "failed".
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far was Bleeding Kansas more significant than the Compromise of 1850 in causing a breakdown of trust between North and South?
H: Set a criterion: what does "breakdown of trust" mean? Broken promises? Violence? Loss of faith in the law?
H: Compare what changed for each side after 1850 and after 1854–56.
A: Compromise of 1850: the Fugitive Slave Act made Northerners feel forced to enforce slavery; the South resented California; but both main parties accepted the Compromise as a "finality" in 1852, so trust remained fairly stable until 1854. Bleeding Kansas and Kansas–Nebraska: repeal of 36°30′ broke a promise; settlers fought; the Sack of Lawrence and Pottawatomie killed; Brooks–Sumner; the Lecompton fraud. Level 5: e.g. 1850 started the distrust but 1854–56 destroyed the belief in compromise, because the repeal of 1854 broke a settlement and Kansas showed that popular sovereignty could not work.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Eric Foner argued that Republicans were driven by "free labour" ideology rather than concern for the enslaved. How far does the evidence of 1846–60 support this view?

A: Support: the Wilmot Proviso was justified as protecting free white labour; Free Soil slogans ("Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men"); Republicans in 1856 and 1860 opposed the extension of slavery but did not call for abolition in the states; Lincoln in 1858 denied any plan to interfere with slavery in the South and opposed equal political rights for Black Americans; economic planks (homesteads, railways) appealed to free farmers. :::recap
- **Two societies**: the South's cotton and slavery (3.95 million enslaved people in 1860; cotton almost 60 per cent of exports) against the North's factories, railways, free farms and immigrants; "free labour" against slavery defended as a "positive good".
- **Tariff**: the Tariff of Abominations (1828) and the Nullification crisis (1832–33) showed the South's willingness to threaten the Union, but the tariff faded as a cause.
- **Missouri Compromise (1820)**: Missouri slave, Maine free, slavery banned north of 36°30′ in the Louisiana Purchase.
- **Expansion**: Texas annexed (1845), the Mexican War (1846–48), the Mexican Cession; Wilmot Proviso (8 August 1846); Free Soil Party (August 1848); popular sovereignty.
- **Compromise of 1850**: California free; Utah and New Mexico by popular sovereignty; slave trade banned in DC; Fugitive Slave Act (18 September 1850), a major cause of Northern anger.
- **Kansas–Nebraska Act (30 May 1854)**: repealed the 36°30′ line; Bleeding Kansas (1854–59); Brooks beats Sumner (22 May 1856); Lecompton Constitution rejected (10,226 to 138 on 4 January 1858).
- **Parties**: the Whigs collapsed (1852–54), the Republican Party was founded (1854; Frémont 1856), the Lincoln–Douglas debates (1858; Freeport Doctrine), the Democrats split (1860).
- **Judgement**: slavery was the underlying cause; territorial expansion was the arena in which it was fought; the breakdown of the national parties removed the means of compromise.
