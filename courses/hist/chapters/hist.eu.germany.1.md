:::why
In 1815 there was no Germany on the map: there were 39 states, from the great powers Austria and Prussia to tiny principalities and free cities, tied together in a loose Confederation. Thirty-three years later, in a few weeks of March 1848, kings and princes across those states gave way to crowds and promised constitutions. Why did a system built to prevent exactly that collapse so fast? The answer teaches you how to weigh ideas (liberalism, nationalism), structures (the Confederation, the Zollverein, railways) and crises (the hunger of 1846–47) against each other, and it is the starting point for the whole story of German unification in this course.

## The German states after 1815

### The Congress of Vienna and the Confederation

For almost a thousand years the German lands had been part of the **Holy Roman Empire**, a patchwork of several hundred states under an emperor who had little real power. Napoleon destroyed it. In 1806 he forced the last emperor, Francis II of Austria, to give up the title, and he organised many western and southern states into a French-led **Confederation of the Rhine**. Prussia was beaten at Jena in 1806. Then Napoleon's defeat in Russia and at Leipzig in 1813 freed the German lands, and the **Congress of Vienna** (September 1814 to June 1815) had to decide what to put in their place.

The negotiators were conservative statesmen led by Prince Klemens von Metternich, the Austrian Foreign Minister, and they wanted three things: peace, a barrier against any new French attack, and no revolution. They rejected a united German state. Austria feared that a strong Germany would be led by Prussia, and the middle-sized states (Bavaria, Württemberg, Saxony and others) did not want to lose their independence. Instead the **Federal Act** of 8 June 1815 created the **German Confederation** (*Deutscher Bund*).

:::def German Confederation
The loose union of 39 sovereign German states (35 monarchies and 4 free cities) created at the Congress of Vienna in 1815. It had a Federal Diet (*Bundestag*) at Frankfurt where state envoys met, with Austria in the chair. It was an alliance of independent states, not a nation-state.
:::

Its main features were these:
- **Members.** 39 states, from Austria and Prussia down to tiny principalities and the free cities of Frankfurt, Hamburg, Bremen and Lübeck. Austria and Prussia belonged only for the lands that had been part of the old Holy Roman Empire; Hungary, Galicia and Prussia's eastern provinces were outside. Some members had foreign rulers: the King of Denmark ruled Holstein, the King of the Netherlands ruled Luxembourg, and until 1837 the King of Britain ruled Hanover.
- **The Diet.** A permanent congress of envoys of the state governments, meeting at Frankfurt with an Austrian as president. It was not elected, and on important questions it needed agreement from all the states, so one state could block change.
- **A federal army, but no common army.** Each state supplied a contingent in wartime. There was no standing federal army, and the great fortresses on the French frontier (Mainz, Luxembourg) were federal, but the army depended on the states' goodwill.
- **No common government.** No head of state, no federal ministers, no federal courts able to override state law, no common currency, no common tariff and no common law.
- **Article 13.** The Federal Act said that "a constitution of estates" would be introduced in every state. It set no date, no form, and no way of enforcing the promise, so rulers read it as they liked.

:::key Strengths and weaknesses of the Confederation
**Strengths.**
1. **Security.** It gave the middle and small states a guarantee against both French aggression and Prussian or Austrian domination, and it kept the peace between its members for roughly half a century.
2. **Stability.** It replaced the chaos of the Napoleonic years with a settled system that conservative rulers wanted.
3. **Flexibility for the states.** Each ruler kept his sovereignty, so Bavaria and Baden could develop their own institutions.

**Weaknesses.**
1. **Austrian control.** Austria chaired the Diet and could use it to protect its own interests and to block reform.
2. **The unanimity rule.** It was almost impossible to change anything important.
3. **No common institutions** of army, economy or law, so no economic or military unity.
4. **No voice for the people.** No elected body, no basic rights. Liberals and nationalists saw it as a "princes' alliance".
5. **Divided loyalties.** Austria and Prussia had interests outside Germany, and each distrusted the other.
:::

Notice that the Confederation was designed to prevent change. That made it strong against external danger and weak against the pressure that built up inside it. Every later cause in this chapter is a pressure that the Confederation could suppress but not satisfy.

### The Third Germany and the Prussian–Austrian rivalry

The medium-sized states, sometimes called the **Third Germany** (the Germany that was neither Austria nor Prussia), used the Confederation as protection against the two great powers. Some of them gave their people constitutions very early: Baden and Bavaria in 1818, Württemberg in 1819, Hesse-Darmstadt in 1820. Their rulers did this partly to unite territories that Napoleon had stitched together, and partly to keep the loyalty of their subjects. These southern constitutions created parliaments with two chambers and limited powers, and they turned out to be the training ground of German liberalism.

Meanwhile Austria and Prussia, the two great powers, were the Confederation's natural leaders and natural rivals. Austria was a multi-national empire that ruled Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Italians and others, and had the most to lose from nationalism. Prussia, enlarged in 1815 with the Rhineland and Westphalia, was a mainly German state whose western lands contained the coal and iron of the future Ruhr. This rivalry is the thread that runs through the whole topic.

## Metternich's system and the Carlsbad Decrees

### The aims of the system

**Metternich's system** is the name historians give to the set of policies by which Austria, with the cooperation of Prussia and the Confederation, tried to keep Europe and Germany unchanged after 1815. For Metternich the chief enemies were **liberalism** (government by constitutions and parliaments) and **nationalism** (the belief that people who share a language and culture should form a nation-state). Both were dangerous to Austria in a special way. A constitution would give Hungarians, Czechs and Italians a voice, and national feeling among Germans, Italians or Hungarians could break an empire in which Germans were a minority. Metternich was Austrian Foreign Minister from 1809 and became Chancellor in 1821. He did not have to invent the system; he had to persuade the German rulers, above all the King of Prussia, to use it.

### The student movement

The first sign of trouble came from the universities. Young men who had fought against Napoleon in 1813–15 came home to a Germany that was still divided and ruled by princes. In 1815 students at Jena founded the first **Burschenschaft**, a student association with the motto "Honour, Freedom, Fatherland" and the colours black, red and gold (taken from the uniform of a volunteer corps of the war of 1813). Others followed at many universities.

:::def Burschenschaft
A German student association founded in 1815 at Jena that supported national unity and constitutional freedom. Its black, red and gold colours later became the colours of German liberalism and nationalism.
:::

On 18 October 1817 about 500 students from many universities met at the **Wartburg** castle in Saxe-Weimar to mark the 300th anniversary of Luther's protest and the 4th anniversary of the Battle of Leipzig. There were speeches for unity and liberty, and the students burned books and symbols of reaction. Their own demands were vague, and the damage was small. But rulers saw an organised, national, youthful movement, and the burning of books suggested worse to come.

### The murder of Kotzebue and the Carlsbad Decrees

On 23 March 1819 a theology student and Burschenschaft member, **Karl Ludwig Sand**, stabbed **August von Kotzebue**, a successful playwright and journalist, in his house at Mannheim. Kotzebue had mocked the student movement and was thought to send reports on German opinion to the Russian government. Sand was executed in May 1820. Metternich seized his chance. He met the King of Prussia's ministers and then called a conference of ministers from ten German states to Carlsbad in Bohemia in August 1819. Their proposals were approved by the Federal Diet on 20 September 1819.

:::def Carlsbad Decrees
Four laws approved by the Federal Diet on 20 September 1819 to suppress liberal and nationalist movements: a **University Law** (a government curator to supervise each university, dismissal of "dangerous" professors, and a ban on the Burschenschaften), a **Press Law** (censorship before publication of newspapers and of books of fewer than twenty sheets, about 320 pages), an **Investigation Law** (a central commission at Mainz to investigate "revolutionary conspiracies"), and an **Execution Law** (federal action against a state that failed to comply).
:::

What the Decrees did in practice:
- **Universities.** Each had a government agent (a curator). Professors who spread "harmful doctrines" lost their posts and could not be hired by any other state. Ernst Moritz Arndt, a famous nationalist writer, lost his chair at Bonn, and the gymnastics leader Friedrich Ludwig Jahn was arrested. The Burschenschaften went underground.
- **Press.** Newspapers, magazines and short books were censored before printing. This was first a five-year law, and in 1824 it was made permanent.
- **Investigation.** The Mainz commission questioned suspects until 1828 and gathered files on hundreds of people across the states.
- **The Vienna Final Act (1820)** added that all state power must remain with the head of state and that the Diet could act against states whose assemblies went too far, making the Confederation a barrier to change in the constitutional states too.

The Decrees were effective in some places and weak in others. They depended on state governments to apply them, and Prussia and Austria did so far more energetically than Baden or Bavaria. Writers found ways round them: they published abroad, wrote in code, or wrote books long enough to escape pre-censorship. But they made Germany a place where opposition had to be indirect, and they drove it into secrecy, into poetry and music, and into the universities.

:::key Why Metternich issued the Carlsbad Decrees
1. **Trigger**: the murder of Kotzebue (23 March 1819) gave him a reason to act that Prussia and the middle states could not refuse.
2. **Fear of the student movement**: the Wartburg Festival and the Burschenschaften were seen as an organised national movement of the educated young.
3. **Preserving Austria**: nationalism and liberalism threatened a multi-national empire, so he needed to stop them spreading in Germany and into Austria.
4. **Opportunity**: Prussia's King Frederick William III had moved away from reform after 1815, and the king's advisers were ready to cooperate.
:::

## The influence of liberal ideas

### What liberals wanted

:::def Liberalism
In early nineteenth-century Germany, the belief in government by a written constitution and an elected assembly that controls taxation and law-making, with protection of individual rights (freedom of the press, speech, assembly and religion, and equality before the law). Most German liberals wanted a **constitutional monarchy**, not a republic, and a vote limited by property or education.
:::

The **aims of the Liberals** can be listed:
1. A **written constitution** limiting the ruler's power.
2. A **parliament** elected by the propertied and educated, with the right to approve taxes and laws.
3. **Rights**: freedom of the press, of speech and of association; trial by jury; equality before the law.
4. **Free trade** and an end to the restrictions of guilds and internal customs barriers.
5. **An end to aristocratic privilege** in the army, civil service and the law.

Notice what most liberals did not want. They did not want rule by the poor. The vote they asked for was limited by property and education, and they feared the "mob" almost as much as the princes did. This matters in 1848, when liberals and radicals broke apart.

### The emergence of the middle class

Liberalism was the politics of a social group that grew in the early nineteenth century: the **middle class** (*Bürgertum*). Historians divide it in two.
- The **educated middle class** (*Bildungsbürgertum*): civil servants, lawyers, judges, professors, teachers, doctors and clergy. In the German states, where universities were state institutions and states ran schools and the law, this group was often the most politically active.
- The **business middle class** (*Wirtschaftsbürgertum*): merchants, bankers, manufacturers and owners of workshops, whose numbers grew with trade, the Zollverein and industry.

This was a small share of the population. About three-quarters of Germans in 1815 still lived on the land, and the towns were small. But the middle classes were literate and organised: they ran newspapers, reading societies, choirs, gymnastic clubs and the town councils. They paid taxes and had no say in how they were spent, they saw nobles holding top posts in the army and diplomacy, and the Confederation's censors silenced their newspapers. That mix of rising wealth and exclusion is the classic recipe for pressure for change.

### Liberalism in the south and the west

Liberalism was strongest in the south-west, where constitutions existed. In Baden, **Karl von Rotteck** and **Karl Welcker**, two professors at Freiburg, edited the *Staatslexikon* (1834–43), a political encyclopaedia that spread liberal ideas. In the parliaments of Baden and Württemberg liberal deputies argued about budgets, press freedom and rights. Their rulers could still ignore much of what the parliaments said, but the debates were printed and read.

Rulers could also attack liberalism. In 1837 the new King of Hanover, Ernest Augustus, cancelled the liberal constitution that his predecessor had granted in 1833. Seven professors at Göttingen, including the brothers Jacob and Wilhelm Grimm (known for their fairy tales and their German dictionary), protested that they were bound by their oath to the old constitution. They were dismissed, and three were expelled from Hanover. The "Göttingen Seven" became heroes to liberals across Germany: a sign that liberal principles were spreading, but also that rulers could crush them without a fight.

### The extent of support for liberalism in Prussia

Prussia was the most important state, and in 1815 its king had promised it a constitution. King Frederick William III's decree of 22 May 1815 promised a "representation of the people", but he never created it. Instead he set up **provincial diets** in 1823, one for each province, with members chosen by estates (nobles, townspeople, peasants) that could advise but not decide. There was no Prussian parliament, no constitution and heavy censorship.

Support for liberalism in Prussia was real but narrow, and it was concentrated in particular places and groups.
- **The Rhineland**, annexed in 1815, had been influenced by French law and had a strong business class. Liberal businessmen such as **Ludolf Camphausen** and **David Hansemann** led its opposition, and in 1842–43 the *Rheinische Zeitung*, a Cologne newspaper edited for a time by Karl Marx, was closed by the government.
- **East Prussia**, where Königsberg professionals and the doctor Johann Jacoby asked in 1841 for the promised constitution.
- The **civil service** included many reformers, though most obeyed the king.

Against that stood the king, the army, the Junker nobility of the east (the landowning class) and a Protestant church that taught obedience. The peasants, who were the majority, mostly cared about land and dues, not constitutions.

The accession of **Frederick William IV** on 7 June 1840 raised hopes. He was a clever, romantic man who admired the Middle Ages, and he eased the censorship a little. But he believed in monarchy by divine right. In April 1847 he called all eight provincial diets together as a **United Diet** in Berlin, mainly because he needed approval for a loan to build a railway from Berlin to Königsberg. In his opening speech he declared that he would never allow a "written sheet of paper" to come between God and his land. The deputies, who were nobles, businessmen and a few peasants, refused the loan until the king promised regular meetings of a parliament with real powers. Hansemann said in the debate that where money was concerned, good nature came to an end. The king dissolved the Diet on 26 June 1847.

:::key Liberalism in Prussia by 1847
1. **Support was real**: the Rhineland and East Prussian middle class, much of the educated elite, and (as the United Diet showed) a majority of the deputies.
2. **But it had no institutions** to act through: no parliament, press freedom or party.
3. **The king would not give way**: Frederick William IV saw a constitution as a limit on his divinely given authority.
4. **So the demand for a constitution stayed open** and became more urgent in 1847–48, when hunger gave it mass support.
:::

## The growth of nationalist ideas

:::def Nationalism
The belief that people who share a language, history and culture should form one nation with its own government. In Germany it meant a demand for a united German state in place of 39 sovereign states.
:::

Remember the difference. **Liberals** asked "who governs, and with what rights?" **Nationalists** asked "what is Germany, and where are its borders?" Most educated Germans were both, and both pointed against the Confederation, but they were separate ideas, and in 1848–49 the order of priority between them split the revolutionaries.

### The sources of national feeling

German nationalism began as a cultural movement. Writers collected folk tales and songs (the Grimms), scholars studied the German language and medieval history, and philosophers such as Fichte had called in 1807–08 for a German national education to resist Napoleon. The wars of liberation of 1813–15 turned this into political feeling. Singing societies (*Liedertafeln*), gymnastic clubs (the *Turner* movement founded by Jahn) and shooting clubs spread the idea in towns after 1815.

### The impact of the 1830 revolutions

In July 1830 revolution in Paris overthrew King Charles X, and the news set off movements across Europe. Several German states had their own small revolutions in the autumn: in **Brunswick** the duke was driven out in September; in **Saxony**, **Hesse-Kassel** and **Hanover** unrest produced new constitutions in 1831–33. Sympathy for the Polish uprising of 1830–31 against Russia was strong too, and refugees passed through German towns. None of these movements overthrew the Confederation. But they showed that pressure could win concessions, and they revived the opposition after the years of silence.

### The Hambach Festival and the reaction, 1832

The most striking event of these years was the **Hambach Festival**. On 27 May 1832 between 20,000 and 30,000 people (estimates vary) climbed to the ruined castle of Hambach in the Bavarian Palatinate. The organisers were two journalists, **Johann Georg August Wirth** and **Philipp Jakob Siebenpfeiffer**. The crowd waved black, red and gold flags, and speakers called for national unity, popular sovereignty and the freedom of the press. Some also called for cooperation with other free peoples in Europe, and some speakers were republicans. It was the first mass political demonstration in German history, and it was not a student event: artisans, farmers, journalists and townspeople took part, and a few women.

The governments reacted at once. Bavaria arrested the organisers, and the Federal Diet passed two sets of measures.
- The **Six Articles** of 28 June 1832 restated that all state power belonged to the ruler, forbade state parliaments to refuse the money their states needed to meet their duties to the Confederation, and allowed the Diet to overrule state assemblies that went beyond these limits.
- The **Ten Articles** of 5 July 1832 banned political associations and public meetings and festivals without permission, banned the wearing of colours other than those of the state, and tightened censorship and the supervision of universities.

In April 1833 a small group of students and exiles tried to seize the Frankfurt guardhouse and so spark an uprising (the *Wachensturm*). It failed completely, and the Diet set up a central investigation office in Frankfurt. Over the next years thousands of people were questioned and many students imprisoned. In December 1835 the Diet banned the writings of a group of authors called "Young Germany", including Heinrich Heine, who then lived in exile in Paris. In 1834 the Hessian writer Georg Büchner printed a pamphlet, *The Hessian Courier*, which urged poor peasants to rise against the rich with the slogan "Peace to the cottages, war on the palaces", and its co-author Friedrich Ludwig Weidig died in prison in 1837.

:::key Reactions to the growth of nationalist ideas
1. **Repression**: the Six and Ten Articles (1832), the investigation office in Frankfurt (1833), the ban on Young Germany (1835) and trials of students.
2. **It worked in the short term**: associations were broken up and leaders arrested or exiled.
3. **It failed in the long term**: censorship made writers think in code, and the Hambach flag, black-red-gold, remained the symbol of German unity. The ideas moved from conspiracy to song and print.
:::

### Support in the universities

Universities remained the centre of nationalism. Students joined the Burschenschaften even when they were banned, and professors taught history and law in a nationalist spirit. Many leaders of 1848 had been students in these years: the Frankfurt Parliament of 1848 would be full of men who had belonged to a Burschenschaft or been prosecuted for membership. But the universities also show how limited the movement was. Only a few thousand Germans were students, and the student movement was divided between moderate liberals who wanted a constitutional Germany and radicals who wanted a republic.

### Why nationalism grew in the 1840s

After a quiet decade, national feeling surged in the 1840s for several reasons.
- **The Rhine crisis of 1840.** France's government under Adolphe Thiers, angry at being left out of a settlement of a Middle Eastern crisis, talked of taking the Rhine as France's frontier. Germans reacted with outrage, and poets wrote national songs: Nikolaus Becker's "The German Rhine" ("They shall not have it, the free German Rhine") and Max Schneckenburger's "The Watch on the Rhine". The danger was imaginary, but the reaction revealed how widely national feeling had spread.
- **Songs, clubs and festivals.** In August 1841 August Heinrich Hoffmann von Fallersleben, a professor at Breslau, wrote "The Song of the Germans" on the island of Heligoland. Its first line, "Germany, Germany above all", meant that loyalty to the whole nation should come before loyalty to any one state, and its third verse asked for "unity and justice and freedom". Choral societies and gymnastic clubs grew rapidly, and in 1842 Frederick William IV laid the foundation stone to complete Cologne Cathedral, which had stood unfinished since the Middle Ages and became a national symbol.
- **Schleswig-Holstein.** In 1846 the King of Denmark seemed about to bind the duchy of Schleswig more closely to Denmark. Germans saw this as an attack on a German territory (Holstein was a member of the Confederation), and the issue united nationalists in 1846–48.
- **The Zollverein and railways** (below) brought Germans into more contact with each other and made the Confederation look out of date.
- **A bigger press.** Newspapers and pamphlets grew in number as censorship loosened in the 1840s, particularly in Prussia.

## The economic and political impact of the Zollverein and the railways

### The Prussian customs union and the Zollverein

Before 1818 the German states were divided by customs barriers: a trader sending goods from Berlin to Cologne crossed many frontiers, with tolls at each. In 1818 Prussia passed a customs law (26 May) that ended all internal tariffs in its own scattered territories and set one moderate tariff on imports from outside. This **Prussian Customs Union** forced small states inside or between Prussian lands to negotiate. Many of them, such as Schwarzburg-Sondershausen in 1819, joined. The Prussian minister of finance, Friedrich von Motz, built the system through treaties in the 1820s, and in the same years Bavaria and Württemberg formed a rival customs union in the south. After some years of rivalry the groups merged.

:::def Zollverein
The German customs union (*Zoll* = customs, *Verein* = union), led by Prussia, that came into force on 1 January 1834. Its members abolished tariffs between them and shared one external tariff, with the revenue shared in proportion to population. Austria was not a member.
:::

The Zollverein began in 1834 with 18 states and about 23 million people. Baden joined in 1835, Frankfurt in 1836, and by 1836 most of the Third Germany was in, with Hanover, Oldenburg and the northern coast the main exceptions. Hanover and Oldenburg stayed outside until 1851–54.

The **economic impact** was substantial but not dramatic.
- It created a free-trade area with a single set of rules, weights and measures, so goods moved more cheaply and businesses could plan for a bigger market.
- It increased the income of state governments, because the tariff produced more revenue than a collection of small customs posts.
- It helped trade and industry grow, though industrialisation was driven mostly by railways, coal and iron, and by changes in technology.

The **political impact** is the debated part.
- It made **Prussia** the leader of the German economy. Prussia ran the negotiations, kept the biggest share of the union, and the smaller states depended on its goodwill.
- It **excluded Austria**. High-tariff Austria could not join a low-tariff union without hurting its industry, and Prussia did not want Austrian influence, so the Zollverein divided German trade along the line between the two powers.
- It encouraged the idea of **unity** among businessmen and professionals, who saw a larger market and wanted a common law, currency and parliament. The economist **Friedrich List** argued for a German national economy, tariff protection for young industries and a unified railway network.
- But it was **not a plan for a state**. The members were sovereign princes who wanted revenue and trade. Nobody in 1834 called the Zollverein a step to political union, and Metternich did not see it as a plot against Austria until later. Its political effect came from what Prussia and the nationalists made of it afterwards.

:::warn Do not say the Zollverein united Germany
It was an economic union of states, and Austria stayed outside. Examiners reward the careful claim: the Zollverein created economic ties and a Prussian-led bloc that made a later political union without Austria more likely. It did not make unification inevitable, and the political question was fought out by diplomacy and war. The 2027 specimen question asked exactly this ("the Zollverein's role was economic not political"), so be ready to argue both sides.
:::

### Railway development

The first German railway ran from Nuremberg to Fürth on 7 December 1835, a line of about 6 kilometres in Bavaria. The first long line, from Leipzig to Dresden, opened in 1839, and the pace then increased sharply. The table gives approximate totals of track in the German states. Historians' figures differ slightly because they count different areas.

| 1850 | 6,000 |

*Adapted from the tables of B. R. Mitchell, European Historical Statistics (rounded).*

Railway building had both economic and political effects.
- **Economic**: it created demand for coal, iron and engineering. Berlin's August Borsig built his first locomotive in 1841, and the iron and coal industries of the Ruhr and Silesia grew to supply track and engines. Railways cut transport costs, linked markets, and employed tens of thousands of navvies and workers. They also drew investment: the share boom of 1845–46 and its collapse in 1847 helped to bring on the credit crisis described below.
- **Political and social**: people and newspapers travelled farther and faster. Troops could be moved quickly, as governments discovered in 1848–49. Friedrich List had argued that railways and the Zollverein belonged together as a national system, and many liberals shared his view that the railway map was a visible picture of unity. The Prussian government's need for money to build the eastern railway was one reason it called the United Diet in 1847.

:::key The Zollverein and railways, compared
1. **Both** made the German economy more connected and pulled the Third Germany towards Prussia.
2. **The Zollverein** was a deliberate political and fiscal act that excluded Austria. Its main impact was on state finances, trade and the balance between Austria and Prussia.
3. **The railways** were a technological change that affected industry, jobs, travel and ideas. Their main impact was on the economy and on how Germans saw their country.
4. **Neither** made revolution in 1848 inevitable; they changed the conditions in which liberals, nationalists and workers organised.
:::

## Problems facing the German states in the 1840s

By the mid-1840s the political pressure from liberals and nationalists was joined by social and economic problems. This is the third strand of causation, and perhaps the one that turned discontent into mass action.

### Population growth, urbanisation and industrialisation

The population of the German states rose from about 25 million in 1815 to about 35 million by 1850, an increase of about 40 per cent. Better food in good years, vaccination and fewer wars cut the death rate while births stayed high. Land did not grow, and in many areas farms were divided among heirs into holdings too small to support a family. Villages filled with landless labourers and smallholders who worked part of the year for others. Many moved to the towns or emigrated, and emigration to the USA rose sharply in the 1840s.

**Urbanisation.** Towns grew fast. Berlin grew from about 170,000 inhabitants in 1800 to about 420,000 in 1850, more than twice as many. New arrivals crowded into cheap housing without sanitation, and had few rights in the town. Contemporaries used the word **pauperism** (*Pauperismus*) for the new mass poverty of people who had work, or had had it, but could not live on it.

:::def Urbanisation
The growth of towns and cities as people move from the countryside to find work, producing overcrowding, poverty and new social tensions.
:::

**Industrialisation and skilled workers.** Industrialisation came late to the German states and was uneven. The Ruhr, Saxony, Silesia and the Rhineland were industrialising; much of the rest was not. The group hit hardest were the **skilled workers**, the artisans (*Handwerker*) such as weavers, tailors, shoemakers, cabinet-makers and masons. Their problems were these:
- **Competition** from cheap factory goods, especially British cotton cloth and machine-spun yarn.
- **Freedom of trade** in Prussia (from 1810–11) and elsewhere meant guilds could no longer limit the number of masters. Trades became overcrowded, journeymen could no longer expect to become masters, and prices and wages fell.
- **Putting-out work**: in textiles, merchants gave spinners and weavers material to work at home and paid them by the piece, which left them dependent and at risk when the merchant cut the rate.

Artisans did not want a capitalist industrial society; many wanted guilds and protection restored. This gave them a different politics from the liberal middle class, and it is one reason why liberals and artisans were uneasy allies in 1848.

### The Silesian weavers' revolt, 1844

Linen weavers in the mountains of Silesia had been poor for a generation, and machine-spun yarn from Britain made their position worse. In early June 1844, at Peterswaldau and Langenbielau, weavers marched on the houses of the merchants who paid them, broke in, and destroyed goods and machines. Prussian soldiers fired on the crowd at Langenbielau on 5 June and eleven weavers were killed. The revolt was crushed within three days. In Berlin the government and charities set up a committee for the welfare of the working classes, but little changed.

The revolt mattered less for what it achieved than for the shock it gave to educated opinion. Poets, journalists and early socialists wrote about it. Heinrich Heine's poem "The Silesian Weavers", printed in Paris a month later, has the weavers weaving a shroud for "old Germany" and cursing God, the king and the false fatherland. It was a work of art by an exile, not a report, but it shows that educated Germans began to link the "social question" of poverty with the political question of who ruled.

### The economic crisis of 1846–47

Then came the worst crisis of the whole period. A fungus (potato blight) reached Europe in 1845 and destroyed much of the potato crop, the staple food of the poor. The grain harvests of 1845 and 1846 were also poor, and the price of rye (the bread grain) and potatoes rose far above normal: in some markets it roughly doubled. A family of the poor already spent most of its income on food; now it could not afford anything else.

This produced a chain of effects.
1. **Hunger and disease**: in places, famine; typhus spread in Upper Silesia in 1847–48.
2. **Unemployment**: people who spent everything on bread stopped buying clothes, furniture and other goods, so workshops laid off workers.
3. **Credit crisis**: the railway share boom of 1845–46 collapsed in 1847, helped by the crisis in Britain, and banks and merchants cut credit, so businesses failed.
4. **Unrest**: food riots across the states. In Berlin the "Potato Revolution" of 21–23 April 1847 saw crowds attack market stalls and bakers' shops until troops restored order. There were similar riots in Stuttgart and Ulm.
5. **Loss of confidence in the rulers**: the poor blamed hoarders and officials, and the middle class blamed an inflexible government that did nothing and refused to give them a say.

:::key Why the economic crisis of 1846–47 mattered
1. It **put hundreds of thousands of poor people in desperate need at the same moment**, so they had reason to join a revolt.
2. It **hit the middle class too** through bankruptcies, so liberals and workers had a common complaint.
3. It **discredited governments** that seemed unable to feed their people.
4. It **happened at the same time as the political crisis of 1847** (the United Diet, the Offenburg and Heppenheim meetings), which gave the discontent a political target.
:::

### The political movement in 1847

In 1847 political opinion hardened into two programmes. At **Offenburg** in Baden on 12 September radicals led by Friedrich Hecker and Gustav Struve published the "Demands of the People": freedom of the press, conscience and teaching, a people's militia, a representation of the people at the Federal Diet, a progressive income tax and the adjustment of the imbalance between capital and labour. A month later at **Heppenheim** (10 October) moderate liberals of south-west Germany, including Heinrich von Gagern, met and asked for a German parliament and an enlarged Zollverein as the basis of national unity. Notice the difference: radicals wanted a deeper change in society, liberals wanted a political framework. In 1848 the two groups would work together at first and then fall out.

By the beginning of 1848 the German states were therefore waiting. The political demands were agreed in principle, the king of Prussia had refused them, the economic crisis had hit the poor and the middle class together, and then on 24 February 1848 revolution in Paris overthrew King Louis-Philippe and proclaimed a republic. News of it reached the German states within days.

## Why did revolution come in 1848? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | The Confederation's weaknesses; Metternich's system; the growth of the middle class | Created grievances and kept them from being solved, but they had existed since 1815 |
| Medium-term | Liberalism and nationalism (1830 revolutions, Hambach, universities); the Zollverein and railways | Gave opposition ideas, symbols and economic weight; made the Confederation look out of date |
| Short-term | Population growth and artisan distress; the Silesian revolt (1844); the Rhine crisis and Schleswig-Holstein (1840, 1846); the failure of the United Diet (1847) | Added a mass of discontented people and made the political demands urgent |
| Immediate | The crisis of 1846–47 | Brought hunger, unemployment and credit failure at the same time |
| Trigger | The revolution in Paris (24 February 1848) | Showed that a regime could fall and set off the March movements |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Political and national grievances gave the revolutionaries their **aims** and their leaders, and had existed for thirty years without bringing down a government. The economic crisis of 1846–47 supplied the **mass of angry people** and the **timing**. The revolution in Paris supplied the **trigger** and the example. Without the economic crisis the liberals would probably have remained a frustrated minority, as in the 1830s. Without the political ideas the crisis would have produced riots like the Potato Revolution, not a revolution to remake Germany. A strong answer says which of these it thinks decisive and why.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **Thomas Nipperdey**, *Germany from Napoleon to Bismarck* (1983; English translation 1996), set the revolution within the long process of modernisation: society and the economy were changing faster than the political order allowed, and 1848 was the result of that gap.
- **Jonathan Sperber**, *The European Revolutions 1848–1851* (1994), treated the revolutions as a pan-European movement that combined political demands with the social and economic distress of the "hungry forties", and paid close attention to artisans and peasants, not only the educated.
- **William Carr**, *A History of Germany 1815–1990*, is a standard English survey and a good place to read the narrative.
You can use this at AS to show why causes are weighed differently, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

AQA's A Level History (7042) has a breadth study, The Quest for Political Stability: Germany, 1871–1991, which begins after unification, so the causes of the 1848 revolutions are not a separate topic there. Cambridge 9489 starts in 1815 and asks for them in full.

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Metternich issued the Carlsbad Decrees in 1819.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Metternich issued the Carlsbad Decrees because a student called Sand murdered Kotzebue in March 1819. There had also been the Wartburg Festival in 1817, where students burned books. The Decrees brought in censorship of the press and made the universities more strictly supervised. They were approved by the Federal Diet in September."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes an event. The word "because" is attached to one fact, and no sentence explains how the murder led Metternich to act. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The immediate reason was the murder of Kotzebue on 23 March 1819. Kotzebue was a famous writer who had mocked the student movement, and Sand was a member of a Burschenschaft, so the murder looked like proof that the nationalist student societies were a danger to the state. It gave Metternich a reason to act that rulers who disliked his system found hard to refuse. A second reason was that Metternich feared the movement the murder came from. Since the Wartburg Festival in 1817, where students from many universities flew black-red-gold colours and burned books, he had seen the Burschenschaften as an organised national movement. The Decrees aimed at the sources of that movement: curators supervised each university, and professors who spread dangerous ideas could be dismissed."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was Metternich's deeper concern for the Austrian Empire. Nationalism and liberalism threatened a state ruled by German-speaking Habsburgs over Hungarians, Czechs and Italians. If the German states accepted constitutions and a national idea, the example would spread to Austria's peoples. Metternich could not act inside other states' borders, so he used the Confederation, where Austria chaired the Diet, to make the same policy apply everywhere. Prussia's cooperation was vital, and Frederick William III, who had made no move towards the constitution he promised in 1815, was ready to agree.

These factors were connected. The murder was the trigger and gave Metternich the excuse; the Wartburg Festival and the Burschenschaften supplied evidence that the danger was organised; and his fear for Austria explains why he used the opportunity to make a permanent system, such as the Press Law, which was extended in 1824, rather than a short-term response to a crime. The decisive factor was therefore not the murder itself but Metternich's belief that nationalism and liberalism threatened the Habsburg Empire. The murder was the occasion that let him turn that belief into law."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the trigger, the evidence, the underlying fear), and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

While this law remains in force, no publication that appears in daily issues or in instalments, or that does not exceed twenty sheets of print, shall be printed in any German state without the previous approval of the state authorities. Each state is answerable, not only to the state attacked, but to the whole Confederation, for the writings that appear under its supervision.

*Adapted from the Press Law, one of the Carlsbad Decrees, approved by the Federal Diet at Frankfurt, 20 September 1819.*

**Source B**

All associations with political aims, and all public meetings and festivals held without the permission of the authorities, are forbidden in every state of the Confederation. No one may wear cockades, ribbons or other emblems in colours that are not those of the state to which he belongs. The governments shall watch the press and the universities more closely, and the Carlsbad laws shall be strictly applied.

*Adapted from the Ten Articles, a resolution of the Federal Diet at Frankfurt, 5 July 1832. A close paraphrase of the main clauses.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about how the German Confederation tried to control political opinion.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that publications must be approved before they are printed. Source B says that political associations and meetings are forbidden. So they are different. Both are from the Federal Diet, so they are the same kind of source and both are biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. The similarity (same body) is a surface point about the source's nature and not developed, and the comment on bias is a general assertion. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share the method of control. Both are decrees of the Federal Diet that bind every state, and both rely on the state governments to apply them: Source A makes each state 'answerable ... to the whole Confederation' for what is printed, and Source B bans associations 'in every state of the Confederation'. Both are also preventative: Source A requires approval 'previous' to printing, and Source B bans meetings 'held without the permission of the authorities'. Neither punishes a deed already done. The aim is to stop opinion forming and spreading.

The difference is in what they target. Source A controls the printed word, the means by which ideas travelled among the educated, while Source B controls public activity: associations, meetings, festivals and the 'colours' of cockades and ribbons. The ban on colours is a specific reference to the black, red and gold of the national movement. Source B also asks for the Carlsbad laws to be 'strictly applied', which suggests that they had not been.

The context explains this. Source A followed the murder of Kotzebue in March 1819 and the Wartburg Festival of 1817: the fear was of writers and students, a small educated group, so the Press Law covered newspapers and short books and the University Law supervised professors. Source B followed the Hambach Festival of 27 May 1832, when between 20,000 and 30,000 people from many walks of life gathered with national flags. The authorities now faced a public movement, not a conspiracy of the educated, and the second set of laws had to deal with crowds, clubs and symbols. The repetition of the Carlsbad laws in Source B is also a sign of how little the first set had achieved. As evidence, both documents are reliable on what the authorities feared and intended, because they are official decisions, but they show only intentions: they cannot show how far the states enforced them. Source B, by showing that the earlier laws had to be repeated and extended, is better evidence that the opposition had survived and spread."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the Kotzebue murder, Hambach) and through the nature and purpose of each source; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Economic distress was the main cause of the revolutions of 1848 in Germany.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: population growth, pauperism and artisan distress; Silesian weavers (1844); potato blight and harvest failures; the crisis of 1846–47 (prices, unemployment, credit); the Potato Revolution (April 1847); this is what put crowds on the street in March 1848.
- Against 1, political ideas: liberalism (Rotteck, Welcker, the southern constitutions, the Göttingen Seven, the United Diet) and nationalism (Hambach, the Rhine crisis, the Song of the Germans). The aims of 1848 were constitutions and a German parliament, which came from these ideas.
- Against 2, the political system: the Confederation and Metternich's system gave no outlet; the king of Prussia refused a constitution in 1847.
- Against 3, the trigger: Paris, 24 February 1848.
- Judgement: distress was needed for mass action, but it did not set the aims. Criterion: what turned hunger into revolution.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Economic distress was very important. The German states had a growing population and many artisans were poor because of competition from British factory goods. In 1844 the Silesian weavers rose in revolt and were shot down by troops, and in 1845–47 the potato blight and bad harvests doubled food prices in some places. People who spent all their income on food stopped buying other goods, so there was unemployment, and there were food riots such as the Potato Revolution in Berlin in 1847. This shows that economic distress was the main cause of the revolutions."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: food riots are not the same as revolutions that demanded constitutions. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet economic distress alone cannot explain why people demanded what they did in March 1848. Hunger had caused riots before, as in 1816–17, without bringing down any government. What was new in 1848 was a political programme that the educated had developed over thirty years. Liberals such as Rotteck and Welcker had spread the idea of a constitution through the southern parliaments and the *Staatslexikon*. The Göttingen Seven in 1837 and the United Diet in 1847 had shown that constitutional rights had support among officials, professors and businessmen. The Hambach Festival, the Rhine crisis and the Song of the Germans had spread the demand for national unity. When the radicals at Offenburg in September 1847 asked for a free press, a people's militia and a popular representation, they were drawing on that tradition and not on the price of bread.

The political system also explains why the distress became a revolution. The Confederation and Metternich's system provided no lawful outlet for grievances: there were no parliaments in Prussia and Austria, and the press was censored, so people could not influence government through the vote. When Frederick William IV dissolved the United Diet in June 1847 he told liberals that peaceful pressure would fail. Meanwhile the middle class was hit by the credit crisis of 1847 and could share a complaint with artisans, which made an alliance possible for a few weeks. Finally, the French revolution of 24 February 1848 showed that a government could fall, and it triggered the March movements.

Overall, economic distress was essential, because without the crisis of 1846–47 the liberals would probably have remained a frustrated minority, as they were in the 1830s, and because it supplied the crowds on the streets of Berlin and Vienna. But it was not the main cause. It created the opportunity and the numbers, while the political system and the ideas of liberalism and nationalism determined what the revolutionaries asked for and why rulers could not answer them. The most accurate view is that distress was a necessary condition and the political crisis the decisive one: hunger put people on the streets, but the political ideas told them what to ask for."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (what turned hunger into revolution) and distinguishes necessary from decisive causes. With an opening paragraph on the case for distress, this is Level 5 (19–20).

There is no tear in their gloomy eyes; they sit at the loom and grind their teeth. Germany, we are weaving your shroud, and into it we weave the threefold curse. We weave, we weave! A curse on the God to whom we prayed in winter's cold and hunger; we hoped and waited in vain. A curse on the king, the king of the rich, whom our misery could not move, who took our last penny from us and had us shot like dogs. We weave, we weave!

**Question.** How far do these sources agree that political ideas, rather than economic distress, were the main cause of discontent in the German states in the 1840s?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support (political ideas): B (national unity, "justice and freedom"), C (the king's refusal of a constitution shows the political question was central), D (press freedom, a militia, a representation of the people). Challenge (economic distress): A (hunger, wages and the king who "had us shot"), D (the progressive income tax and the "imbalance between capital and labour"). Qualify: A is political as well (it curses the king and Germany), D mixes both kinds of demand.

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a poem by an exiled writer in Paris, so it is a work of art for a political purpose (the newspaper was radical) and not a report from the weavers, and "shot like dogs" is poetic licence. But the revolt it refers to is well attested (troops killed eleven weavers on 5 June 1844), and it shows that the educated were beginning to link poverty with politics. Strong evidence of **educated opinion** about distress, weak evidence of what weavers thought.
- **B**: a professor's song, written to be sung by many voices, and later sung at festivals and by choral societies. It is good evidence of the **nationalism of educated Germans**, and Hoffmann was dismissed from his post in 1842 for his political poems. But it says nothing about the poor, and its wide popularity is clearest after 1848.
- **C**: the king speaking in public to the deputies, so it is a reliable guide to his own view and to the fact that a constitution was a live demand. Its purpose is to resist that demand, so it cannot tell us how widespread the demand was. The context matters: the king had called the Diet because he needed a loan for a railway, a financial problem.
- **D**: a manifesto of the **radical** wing in Baden, in the south-west where political activity was strongest, so not typical of the whole of Germany. It shows that political and social demands were joined in one programme, and that the radicals wanted to answer both.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources B and C support the view that political ideas were central. Hoffmann's song makes unity and freedom the goal in 1841, and the king's speech in 1847 shows that rulers saw the demand for a constitution as the main danger. Source D, written by radicals in Baden, lists mainly political demands such as a free press and a popular representation. Against this, Source A shows that for the poorest it was the loom and the wage that mattered, and the king's soldiers who answered them, and Source D adds a tax on income and the balance of 'capital and labour'. But the sources do not simply divide, because in A the weavers' curse falls on 'the king' and 'false' fatherland, so distress led to political anger, and in D the same programme asks for both. Evaluation supports this: A and B come from educated writers, not from the weavers or the crowds; C is the response of a ruler under pressure from liberals; D is the radical wing, not the whole of the opposition. On balance the sources agree to a limited extent. They show political ideas as the demand of the educated and organised, in the sources that were written to persuade, and economic distress as the cause of mass anger. Taken with my own knowledge, in which the distress of 1846–47 put crowds on the streets while the aims of 1848 were constitutional and national, the sources suggest the two causes were joined, with political ideas the more important for what the revolutionaries asked for and economic distress for why so many acted."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of the 1840s; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far did the Zollverein prepare the way for German unification before 1848?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Zollverein was started in 1834 by Prussia. It had 18 states and 23 million people. The states removed customs duties between them and had one tariff on imports. The money was shared out by population. Baden joined in 1835 and Frankfurt in 1836. Austria was not in it. The Zollverein was an important development in German history."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **description**: the paragraph never says how the Zollverein prepared unification, and the last sentence only repeats that it was "important". It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then qualify. "The Zollverein prepared the way for unification because it made Prussia, not Austria, the leader of the German economy. By 1834 it had 18 states and about 23 million people, and it formed a single market in which Prussia negotiated the tariff and Austria had no place. Businessmen in the member states came to see a larger economic unit as natural. However, it did not make unification certain: the princes who joined wanted revenue and trade and not a nation, and the political question was still open in 1848." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: How many states were in the German Confederation, and who presided over its Diet?
A: 39 states (35 monarchies and 4 free cities). The Diet met at Frankfurt with an Austrian as president.
Q: Give two weaknesses of the Confederation and one strength.
A: Weaknesses: no common army, economy or law; the unanimity rule and Austrian control blocked reform; no voice for the people. Strength: it protected the middle and small states against French and great-power aggression and kept the peace between members.
Q: What were the Carlsbad Decrees and why were they issued?
A: Four laws approved on 20 September 1819 (university supervision, press censorship, a central investigation commission and enforcement against states). They followed the murder of Kotzebue by the student Sand, and expressed Metternich's fear of liberalism and nationalism.
Q: What was the difference between liberalism and nationalism in the German states?
A: Liberalism sought constitutions, parliaments and rights; nationalism sought a united German state. Most educated Germans held both, but they were different aims.
Q: What happened at the Hambach Festival and what were the Six and Ten Articles?
A: On 27 May 1832 between 20,000 and 30,000 people met at Hambach castle, with black-red-gold flags, to call for unity and liberty. The Six Articles (28 June) limited state parliaments and the Ten Articles (5 July) banned political associations, meetings and unauthorised colours.
Q: Why was the Zollverein important, and why was Austria not a member?
A: It was a customs union from 1 January 1834 that ended tariffs between members and made Prussia the leader of the German economy. Austria, a high-tariff state, could not join a low-tariff union without hurting its industry, and Prussia did not want Austrian influence.
Q: Give three problems facing the German states in the 1840s.
A: Population growth and pauperism; the distress of artisans and weavers (the Silesian revolt of 1844); the potato blight and harvest failures leading to the crisis of 1846–47 (food prices, unemployment, a credit crisis). Also urbanisation, and the refusal of Frederick William IV to grant a constitution.
Q: Why did Frederick William IV call the United Diet in 1847 and what happened?
A: He needed approval for a loan to build the Berlin to Königsberg railway. The deputies refused it unless he promised regular parliaments with real powers; he refused and dissolved the Diet on 26 June 1847.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the German Confederation was unable to satisfy liberals and nationalists.
A: Indicative content: the Confederation was a union of princes, not peoples, with no elected body or basic rights; the Diet needed unanimity, so reform could be blocked by one state; Austria chaired it and opposed change; Article 13 promised constitutions but gave no date or enforcement, so Prussia and Austria did not have any; it had no army or common economy of its own, so it could not give nationalists a state. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: the Confederation was built to prevent change, so it could not respond to the demands that grew with the middle class and nationalism, and ends with a judgement about the main reason (its structure as a princes' alliance with a veto).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Federal Diet issued the Six Articles and Ten Articles in 1832.
A: Indicative content: the immediate cause was the Hambach Festival (27 May 1832), a mass demonstration of 20,000–30,000 with national flags and calls for popular sovereignty; the 1830 revolutions had produced new constitutions and liberal parliaments in several states that rulers wanted to limit; Metternich feared the contagion of events in Paris and Poland; the Carlsbad laws had not stopped the movement, so governments needed new powers against associations and meetings, not only the press. Level 4: connect the background (the 1830 revolutions and liberal parliaments) to the trigger (Hambach) and judge which mattered more.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The growth of the middle class was the main reason for the growth of liberalism in the German states before 1848.' How far do you agree?
A: For: the educated and business middle classes were the readers, voters and leaders of liberalism (Rotteck and Welcker, the southern parliaments, Camphausen and Hansemann); their wealth and literacy grew; they were excluded from power by nobles and censorship. Against: the Napoleonic reforms and the southern constitutions gave liberalism institutions; the Enlightenment and the French example spread ideas; rulers' repression (the Carlsbad Decrees, the Göttingen Seven) made liberals more determined; the economic crisis of 1846–47 hit the middle class. Level 5 (17–20) judges with a criterion, for example that the middle class was the social base of liberalism, but its growth explains the strength of liberalism only if one adds the experience of constitutions in the south and the rulers' refusal to give way.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Railways did more than the Zollverein to prepare for German unity before 1848.' How far do you agree?
A: For: railways grew from 6 km in 1835 to about 6,000 km in 1850, moved people, goods, news and troops, and created industrial demand for coal and iron; their effect was felt in everyday life across all states, including Austria; List saw them as the physical unity of the nation. Against: the Zollverein was a political and fiscal decision that built Prussian leadership, excluded Austria, and produced the first lasting institutional link between states; revenue sharing gave states a stake. Level 5 uses a criterion, for example "which made a united Germany more thinkable and more likely", and judges, for example, that the Zollverein had the clearer political effect, but the railways made it a lived reality and powered the economic growth that made Prussia strong.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the promise of constitutional government in 1815.
A: Similarities: both promise a form of representation based on estates (A "a constitution of estates", B members chosen "from the provincial estates"); neither promises an elected parliament with power; both are official documents of rulers. Differences: A is a general statement for all 39 states, B a detailed Prussian plan with a commission and a task for the assembly (advice on laws and taxes); A gives no date or process, B names a commission "at once". Explanation: A was a compromise at the Congress of Vienna among states that disagreed, so it is vague; B was issued by the king alone, in the same month, in a period when he had just called on his people to fight Napoleon. Provenance and judgement: both are promises, not actions, and neither was kept in Prussia or Austria; B is better evidence of what a ruler intended in 1815, A of how little the Confederation could compel. Level 4 explains these differences through context and judges the value of each.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources show that the German rulers were committed to constitutional government after 1815?
H: Decide which sources are promises (A, B) and which are actions (C, D).
H: Use your own knowledge: what did Prussia do after 1815, and what did the Carlsbad Decrees and the Hanoverian king do?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: A and B show that in 1815 rulers promised constitutions of estates; some states (Baden, Bavaria, Württemberg) did introduce them in 1818–20. Challenge: C shows a Confederation that supervised and censored universities and removed teachers who taught harmful ideas, so the Federal power was used against liberal opinion; D shows a king cancelling a constitution in 1837 and professors who thought their oath binding; B was not kept in Prussia, which had provincial diets only (1823) and no constitution until 1848–49. Evaluation: A and B are promises made when rulers needed popular support after the Napoleonic wars (B was issued about a month before Waterloo), so they are weak evidence of long-term intent; C is a law approved by the Diet and is strong evidence of what Metternich and his allies actually intended; D is a statement by victims of the policy, so it is strong evidence of the king's action but also of opposition. Level 5 judges "how far": the sources show a commitment in words in 1815 and in a few southern states, but not by the main rulers, and overall they show a policy of suppression; they agree only to a small extent.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Nationalism, not liberalism, was the main force for change in the German states between 1815 and 1848.' How far do you agree?
H: Set a criterion: what counts as "force for change"? Pressure on rulers? Events? Lasting results?
H: Compare the lists of events for each idea: Wartburg, Hambach, the Rhine crisis, the Song of the Germans against the southern constitutions, the Göttingen Seven and the United Diet.
A: Nationalism: Wartburg, Hambach, the Rhine crisis of 1840, the Song of the Germans, choral and gymnastic societies, the Cologne Cathedral project, Schleswig-Holstein in 1846, and the Zollverein and railways which gave it an economic base. Liberalism: the constitutions of Baden, Bavaria and Württemberg, the Staatslexikon, the Göttingen Seven, the Prussian liberals and the United Diet, Heppenheim. Challenge: the two were mixed in the same people and in the same events (Hambach demanded both); neither changed governments before 1848; the repression (Carlsbad, Six and Ten Articles) was aimed at both. Level 5 judges with the criterion, for example that liberalism produced actual institutional change (constitutions in the south) while nationalism produced the mass feeling that made a united Germany a demand; before 1848 liberalism was the stronger force for change in the states, and nationalism became the stronger force once the revolution broke out.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Thomas Nipperdey argued that German society and economy were changing faster than the political order. How far does the evidence of 1815–48 support this view?
H: List the changes in society and economy: population, towns, artisans, railways, Zollverein, the middle class.
H: Then list what stayed the same: the Confederation, the Carlsbad system, the Prussian monarchy. Did the old order bend at all?
A: Support: population grew by about 40 per cent; Berlin more than doubled; railways went from 6 km to about 6,000 km; the middle class grew in numbers and wealth; yet the Confederation, the Diet and the Carlsbad laws were unchanged, Prussia had no constitution, and the press was censored. This gap created the pressure. Challenge: political change was not absent (southern constitutions, the 1830 constitutions, Prussian provincial diets and the United Diet), and many rulers adapted (the Zollverein was a Prussian answer to economic change); the gap does not explain the timing, which needs the crisis of 1846–47 and the Paris revolution; most Germans were still on the land. A* judgement: the gap between society and politics explains why pressure existed and why it was national, but timing and success depended on crisis and chance.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Poems and songs such as Heine's "The Silesian Weavers" and Hoffmann's "Song of the Germans" are sometimes used as evidence of public opinion before 1848. How far are they reliable evidence of what ordinary Germans wanted?
H: Who wrote them, where, and for whom?
H: What do you know about how many Germans could read, sing or hear such works?
A: Strengths: both capture themes that are confirmed elsewhere (the weavers' revolt, the national feeling shown in the Rhine crisis and the festivals); both were published and spread; Hoffmann's song was sung by choral societies and Heine's poem was reprinted. Weaknesses: both were written by educated men (Heine in exile, Hoffmann a professor); they are literary works that aim at effect, not surveys; Heine writes in the voice of the weavers but not as one; the song's popularity is mainly after 1848; the poor, the peasants and many Catholics are not represented. Judgement: reliable evidence of what the educated and politically active felt, and of the images that moved them; less reliable for the majority of Germans, for whom we need other evidence such as riots, petitions and election results.

:::recap
- **Confederation**: 39 states, a Diet at Frankfurt with Austria in the chair (Federal Act, 8 June 1815); no common army, law or economy; unanimity; Article 13 promised constitutions of estates.
- **Metternich's system**: preserve the order of 1815 and stop liberalism and nationalism; Wartburg (1817), Kotzebue (23 March 1819) and the Carlsbad Decrees (20 September 1819: university, press, investigation and execution laws).
- **Liberalism**: constitutions, parliaments and rights; the middle class (educated and business); southern constitutions (1818–20); Rotteck and Welcker; the Göttingen Seven (1837); in Prussia no constitution, provincial diets (1823), the United Diet (April–June 1847).
- **Nationalism**: 1830 revolutions; Hambach (27 May 1832, 20,000–30,000 people); Six Articles (28 June) and Ten Articles (5 July 1832); Young Germany banned (1835); the 1840s: the Rhine crisis (1840), the Song of the Germans (1841), Cologne Cathedral, Schleswig-Holstein (1846).
- **Zollverein and railways**: Prussian customs law (1818); Zollverein 1 January 1834, 18 states, about 23 million people, Austria outside; first railway 7 December 1835; about 6,000 km by 1850.
- **1840s problems**: population from about 25 to 35 million; urbanisation (Berlin about 170,000 to 420,000); artisans hit by industrialisation; Silesian weavers (June 1844); potato blight and the crisis of 1846–47; Offenburg and Heppenheim (1847).
- **Judgement**: political ideas gave the revolutionaries their aims, the crisis of 1846–47 the numbers and the timing, the revolution in Paris (24 February 1848) the trigger.
