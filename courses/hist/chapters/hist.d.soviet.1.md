## How the satellite states were made, 1944–53

The Second World War ended with the Red Army in control of a belt of countries between the USSR and Germany. By 1948 every one of them had a communist-led government. This is the background to everything in this chapter, so it is worth seeing how it was done.

:::def Satellite state
A state that is formally independent but whose government, economy and foreign policy are in practice controlled by a stronger power. The term is used for the states of eastern Europe under Soviet influence after 1945: Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Albania (which broke with Moscow in 1961 but stayed a Pact member until 1968).
:::

In 1944–45 the Red Army drove the German army out of eastern Europe, and the Soviet Union decided who would govern the liberated countries. At first most of them had coalition governments that included non-communists. Over the next three or four years the communist parties, helped by Soviet officers and the secret police, took over. The Hungarian communist leader Mátyás Rákosi called his method "salami tactics": slicing off one opposing party after another until nothing was left. Elections were rigged or controlled, as in Poland in January 1947. In February 1948 the Czechoslovak communists, with the police behind them, forced the non-communist ministers out of the government in a coup, and Czechoslovakia, the last democracy in the region, became a one-party state. In 1949 the Soviet zone of Germany became the German Democratic Republic, a few months after the western zones became the Federal Republic of Germany (West Germany).

Three things then tied the new states to Moscow.

- **The party.** Each state was run by a communist party, or a party formed by a merger of communists and socialists (the Polish United Workers' Party in 1948 and the Socialist Unity Party of Germany, the SED, in 1946). The party took its orders, in the end, from Moscow. The **Cominform**, set up in September 1947, was a forum where the communist parties of the USSR and eastern Europe were told to follow the Soviet line.
- **The Soviet model.** After 1948 the satellites were made to copy the USSR: one-party rule, a state-owned economy run by five-year plans, collective farms, a powerful secret police and a cult of the leader. In Czechoslovakia, Hungary and Bulgaria in 1949–52 there were show trials of senior communists accused of being spies or "nationalists", modelled on Stalin's trials of the 1930s. Rákosi's government hanged László Rajk, a leading communist, in 1949. In the Slánský trial in Prague in November 1952, eleven of fourteen defendants were hanged, among them the former party secretary Rudolf Slánský, and most of them were of Jewish origin, which gave the charges of "Zionist" (Zionism backed a Jewish state in Palestine, and the regime claimed that Jews were secretly serving Israel and the West) conspiracy an antisemitic tone.
- **Economic ties.** The Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (**Comecon**), founded in January 1949, answered the Marshall Plan by tying the economies of the satellites to the Soviet economy.

The one state that broke away showed what was at stake. In June 1948 the Cominform expelled Yugoslavia, whose leader Josip Broz Tito refused to take orders from Stalin. Tito kept power, because the Red Army had not liberated Yugoslavia and his own partisans held the country, and Stalin could not remove him without an invasion. Stalin's response to the expulsion was to tighten his grip on the other states and to hunt for "Titoists" in them. This is the background to the show trials. It also created a precedent that mattered after 1953: a communist state could follow its own road if it kept control of its own party and army.

:::key What Stalin left behind in 1953
1. **One-party states** copied from the Soviet model, governed by communist parties that Moscow could instruct.
2. **Secret police** forces advised by Soviet officers, and show trials that had removed any leader who might be independent.
3. **Planned economies** with priority for heavy industry, tied together by Comecon.
4. **Soviet troops** stationed in several of the states, and Soviet officers in key posts. The Polish Defence Minister from 1949 was a Soviet Marshal of Polish birth, Konstantin Rokossovsky.
5. **No institution** through which the satellites could discuss their grievances with Moscow as equals. The Warsaw Pact of 1955 was meant to supply a framework.
:::

## The Warsaw Pact, 1955

### The formation of the Pact

On **14 May 1955** representatives of eight states met in Warsaw and signed the Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Mutual Assistance, better known as the **Warsaw Pact**. The signatories were the USSR, Poland, East Germany, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria and Albania. The treaty was to last twenty years, and for ten more after that unless a state denounced it a year before the end (Article 11). Its two main provisions were these.

- **Article 4** said that if one or more of the parties was attacked in Europe, each of the others would "immediately" give it assistance, "including armed force", as it considered necessary.
- **Article 5** set up a **Joint Command** of the armed forces assigned to it. In practice the Command sat in Moscow, and its commander-in-chief was always a Soviet marshal, starting with Marshal Ivan Konev. A Political Consultative Committee brought the party leaders together.

:::def Warsaw Pact
The military alliance of the USSR and seven eastern European states, signed in Warsaw on 14 May 1955, with a Joint Command of the armed forces. It lasted until 1991 and was the Soviet bloc's counterpart to NATO. Its members' forces were commanded in practice by Soviet officers in Moscow.
:::

### Why the Pact was formed

The treaty gives its own reason in the preamble: it says that the Paris agreements, which allowed West Germany to rearm and join NATO, created a danger for the peace-loving states of Europe. West Germany joined NATO on **9 May 1955**, five days before the Pact was signed. Historians who have read the Soviet and eastern European archives, notably Vojtech Mastny and Malcolm Byrne in *A Cardboard Castle? An Inside History of the Warsaw Pact, 1955–1991* (2005), have shown that the official reason was only one of several. A good answer separates them.

:::key Reasons for the formation of the Warsaw Pact
1. **Security against West Germany.** The USSR had lost over twenty million people in the war, and German rearmament in a NATO that contained the United States frightened Soviet leaders. This was the reason the treaty stated, and the fear was sincere.
2. **A legal basis for Soviet troops.** The Soviet army had stayed in Hungary and Romania to guard its supply lines to its zone in Austria (Austria, like Germany, had been divided into four occupation zones, held by the USA, the USSR, Britain and France, since 1945). On **15 May 1955**, the day after the Pact was signed, the Soviet Union signed the Austrian State Treaty, which made Austria neutral and ended the occupation. A military alliance gave the USSR a legal reason to keep troops in eastern Europe after the occupation of Austria ended. Soviet forces stayed in Poland, East Germany, Hungary and Romania (Soviet troops left Romania in 1958).
3. **Control of the satellites' armies.** The Pact tied the armed forces of the satellites into a command that Moscow dominated. Within a year East Germany, which had been rearmed only in 1956 with a National People's Army, was fitted into the same structure, with Soviet officers at the top.
4. **Legitimacy and a bargaining counter.** A multilateral treaty looked more like an alliance of equals than a set of Soviet garrisons. It also gave the USSR something to trade: it proposed that both blocs be dissolved and replaced by a system of European collective security, and Article 11 said the Pact would lapse if such a system were created. The Soviet Union hoped to use the Pact to bargain over Germany and the American presence in Europe.
:::

The reasons did not conflict. The Pact was a response to NATO and also a way of keeping the Soviet army in place and the satellites in line. The evidence from its later history decides which reason carried more weight: in its first thirteen years the Pact never fought an enemy from the West, and the one time it acted as an alliance, in August 1968, it entered the territory of a member. Mastny and Byrne conclude that for the Soviet leaders the Pact was always more important as an instrument of political control than as a defence against NATO. A critic of that view would say that for the Soviet General Staff the satellites' territory was a buffer against NATO, so that the two aims could not be separated. Treat that as a debate, with the evidence on both sides.

:::note The Pact in the crises
The Pact did not play the part in 1956 that its treaty might suggest. The Soviet attack on Hungary on 4 November 1956 was carried out by Soviet forces, helped by a government in Hungary that Moscow had installed, and the Pact did not decide it. But Nagy's announcement on 1 November that Hungary would leave the Pact made the Pact itself the issue, and it is hard to read the Soviet decision of that week without it: a state that left the alliance would take its territory out of the Soviet military system. In 1968 the Pact was used for real, when Soviet, Polish, Bulgarian and Hungarian troops entered Czechoslovakia together. Romania refused to take part, and Albania, which had broken with Moscow and stopped attending in 1961, left the Pact formally later in 1968.
:::

## The nature of government in the satellite states

### The common model

Despite their differences, the governments of the satellites had the same structure:

- **A one-party state.** The communist party ruled, and its leading role was written into the constitution or the law. Other parties, where they were allowed, were fronts: they had to join a **National Front** led by the communists.
- **Power in the Politburo.** A small committee of the party took the decisions, headed by a **First Secretary** (the title varied: General Secretary in some periods). The state's parliament and ministries carried them out.
- **Elections without a choice.** Voters were offered a single list approved by the party.
- **The *nomenklatura*.** The party decided who would be appointed to every important job, from factory manager to editor to army officer, and so it controlled the careers of the whole elite. A **nomenklatura** was the list of posts the party filled and, by extension, the privileged class that held them.
- **Soviet influence.** Soviet ambassadors, advisers and the Soviet embassy's connections with the party leaders, plus the Soviet troops and the KGB, meant that no satellite leader could ignore Moscow's wishes.

:::def Nomenklatura
In the Soviet system, the list of senior posts in the state, the economy and society that could be filled only with the approval of the Communist Party, and, by extension, the class of party-approved officials who held those posts and enjoyed privileges such as better housing and shops.
:::

Rule was by the party, then, but the way the party ruled differed from country to country. The differences explain why the crises of 1953–68 had different results.

### Czechoslovakia

Czechoslovakia was two nations in one state: the Czechs in the west, around the capital Prague, and the Slovaks in the east, a smaller and poorer people, with the government in Prague. It had been a democracy between the wars and, in 1946, the one eastern European country where the communists won a large share of a free vote (about 38 per cent). After the coup of February 1948 it was run by Gottwald, who died in March 1953, a few days after attending Stalin's funeral. His successor as party leader, Antonín Novotný, was a party official from the machine, not a reformer. He ruled until 1968, and the country stayed more Stalinist than its neighbours: statues of Stalin stayed up until 1962, and the victims of the show trials were not rehabilitated (declared innocent and honoured again) for years. The regime was not popular, but it kept order. Its economy was the most advanced in the bloc, and so its problems were the most visible when growth stalled in the early 1960s (the Third Five-Year Plan collapsed in 1962–63). Its biggest weakness was political: Novotný had no answer to the demand for change from intellectuals, from the Slovaks, and from inside the party.

### Poland

Poland was the largest satellite and the most difficult. Its communists had come to power with Soviet help, in a country whose national tradition was Catholic and anti-Russian, and whose history of partition by Russia made a Soviet-controlled government doubly hated. Bierut ran a Stalinist state in which the Soviet Marshal Rokossovsky commanded the army. But unlike the other satellites, Poland never collectivised most of its farmland (most peasants kept their land) and its Church kept real independence. The party was therefore weaker in Poland, and when it split in 1956 there was a respected communist, Władysław Gomułka, whom the Poles trusted because Stalinists had imprisoned him.

### East Germany

The GDR was in a unique position. It was the front line of the Cold War and the only satellite whose people had a ready alternative: a larger, richer German state next door, with the same language, which they could reach by taking a train to West Berlin. Walter Ulbricht, who led the SED (Socialist Unity Party) from 1950 to 1971, was a Stalinist who ran a harsh and efficient state, with Soviet troops (about 400,000 in the 1950s, a figure to treat as approximate) stationed in the country. Its government was always aware that it had to prove itself better than the Federal Republic, and that it could be abandoned in a deal over Germany. For this reason Ulbricht pressed Moscow for support more than any other satellite leader did, and he was able to hold Soviet policy to a harder line.

### Hungary

Rákosi, who called himself "Stalin's best Hungarian pupil", ran a regime of terror between 1948 and 1953, with the ÁVH (State Protection Authority) as his instrument, and with a series of show trials, internment camps and forced collectivisation. After Stalin died, Moscow summoned Rákosi and ordered him to share power: in July 1953 the reforming communist **Imre Nagy** became prime minister and began a "New Course" (cuts in forced industrialisation and collectivisation, release of prisoners, more consumer goods). Rákosi, who stayed party leader, fought Nagy and had him dismissed in April 1955 and expelled from the party in the autumn. The conflict between Rákosi's men and Nagy's followers set up the crisis of 1956.

:::key The satellite governments compared
1. **Same system, different strength.** All were one-party states under the Soviet model, but the party was weakest where it faced a national tradition and a strong Church (Poland) and strongest where it could rely on a disciplined machine (Czechoslovakia before 1968) or on Soviet troops and a threat of Western rivalry (East Germany).
2. **The leader mattered.** Rákosi and Ulbricht were hard Stalinists who resisted change; Nagy in Hungary and Gomułka in Poland were communists with a reputation for putting the nation first.
3. **Geography mattered.** East Germany and Czechoslovakia bordered the West. Hungary lay next to neutral Austria after 1955. Poland lay between the USSR and the GDR, so that the Soviet army's lines of supply ran through it.
:::

## The Communist Party and repression

The party's power rested on a mixture of control of information, force, control of belief and control of movement. Each of the four methods in the syllabus (censorship, the security forces, policy towards the Church and restrictions on emigration) was used in every satellite, with differences in harshness that mattered later.

### Censorship and propaganda

Every newspaper, publisher, radio station and film studio was owned by the party or the state. Editors were appointed from the *nomenklatura*, and a censorship office checked books, plays and broadcasts before they appeared. The aim was not only to stop criticism but to fix the official story: the party was always right, the Soviet Union was the friend of the people, and the West was a warmonger. History itself was rewritten, so that the victims of purges vanished from the books and photographs.

:::def Censorship
Control by the state of what may be published, broadcast or performed, usually by checking material before it appears and banning or cutting what the authorities dislike. In the satellite states it was part of the party's control of all means of communication.
:::

The system had two weaknesses. It could not stop foreign radio. Western stations, above all Radio Free Europe and the Voice of America, broadcast to eastern Europe from 1950 onwards, and the BBC and the American-sponsored RIAS in West Berlin reached East Germans who could hear them. The regimes jammed some broadcasts, but jamming was costly and unreliable. And the censors could not control what people said to each other, so that jokes, rumour and the memory of how things used to be were a second source of information. In Poland and Czechoslovakia writers who were forbidden to publish circulated their work in typescript, and the censors' control became weaker, not stronger, when the party lost its confidence after 1953.

### State security forces

Each satellite had a secret police copied from the Soviet model, advised by Soviet officers, and a network of informers.

:::def Stasi
The Ministry for State Security of East Germany (*Staatssicherheit*), founded in 1950 as the GDR's secret police and intelligence service. It watched the population through paid officers and unofficial collaborators, and it was called "the shield and sword of the Party".
:::

Their methods were arrest without a proper trial, forced confessions, imprisonment in labour camps and the fear of denunciation, since anyone might be an informer. After 1953 the worst of the Stalinist terror, the executions of leading communists, ended, but surveillance continued. The significance for the key question is that **repression changed in kind**: in Stalin's time it was mass terror, including against the party itself, and after 1953 it became a quieter system of watching, dismissal and imprisonment of known opponents. The satellite regimes could not rely on terror alone, and the crises of 1953–68 show what happened when their people stopped being afraid.

### Policy towards the Church

Communism was officially atheist, and communist governments saw churches as rivals for people's loyalty, but they could not treat all churches alike.

- **Poland.** About nine in ten Poles were Catholics, and the Church had survived the war with great prestige. After a short agreement in 1950, the government in 1953 took a say in church appointments by decree. When the Primate, **Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński**, refused to give way, he was arrested on 25 September 1953 and held without trial for three years. He was released on 28 October 1956 when Gomułka came to power, and an agreement of December 1956 allowed religious teaching in schools again. The Church was the one organisation the state could not destroy, and in the decades that followed it became a rival centre of authority.
- **Hungary.** The Catholic Primate, **Cardinal József Mindszenty**, was arrested in December 1948 and sentenced to life imprisonment in a show trial in February 1949. He was freed during the uprising on 30 October 1956 and when the Soviet army returned on 4 November he took refuge in the American legation (a diplomatic mission smaller than an embassy) in Budapest, where he stayed for fifteen years.
- **Czechoslovakia.** In 1949 the state took control of church finance and the appointment of clergy, closed monasteries and imprisoned many priests; Archbishop **Josef Beran** of Prague was confined from 1949, interned in remote places from 1951 and, in 1965, allowed to go to Rome only on the understanding that he would not come back.
- **East Germany.** The Protestant Church was the larger one. In 1952–53 the SED campaigned against its youth organisation, the *Junge Gemeinde*, and expelled students from school, and it stopped after the shock of June 1953. From 1954 it promoted a secular ceremony, the *Jugendweihe*, to replace confirmation.

The pattern was that the state attacked the Church hardest in the Stalinist years and then settled for restriction, because the believers did not go away. Poland is the extreme case: a state that could arrest a cardinal could not make him disappear from public life.

### Restrictions on emigration

A state that is confident that its people prefer its system does not need to stop them leaving. All the communist states sealed their western borders from the late 1940s. Czechoslovakia and Hungary fenced and mined theirs, with watchtowers, from the early 1950s. East Germany closed most of its frontier with West Germany in May 1952 and made leaving without permission a crime in December 1957 (*Republikflucht*, "flight from the republic"). Only Berlin, where the sector borders were open, remained a way out, and that explains why the Berlin crisis of 1958–61 (below) was so serious. The need to prevent departure was a sign of weakness: an estimated 2.7 million people left East Germany for West Germany between 1949 and the building of the Wall in 1961.

:::key How the party kept control, 1953–68
1. **Control of information**: censorship and propaganda, partly offset by Western radio.
2. **Security forces**: terror under Stalin, then surveillance, informers and targeted arrests.
3. **Control of institutions**: the *nomenklatura* gave the party power over every career, and the Church was restricted rather than abolished.
4. **Closed frontiers**: leaving was treated as a crime.
5. **Soviet backing**: behind all four stood the Soviet army and the knowledge that Moscow would help a satellite leader.
Each worked best when people believed the regime could not be changed, and none could make the people believe in it.
:::

## Social and economic conditions in the satellite states

### Economic centralisation

The satellites were given the Soviet system of **central planning**: the state owned industry and, after 1948, most farms; a planning office set production targets in five-year and annual plans; prices and wages were fixed by the state. The model gave priority to heavy industry (coal, steel, machinery and arms), at the cost of consumer goods and housing.

:::def Centralised economy (command economy)
An economy in which the state owns most of the means of production and a central planning body decides what is produced, in what quantities and at what prices, instead of leaving it to markets.
:::

Three things made the system worse in eastern Europe than at home.

- **It was copied without regard to local conditions.** Czechoslovakia and East Germany, already highly industrial, were forced to build more steel and heavy machinery, and Poland and Hungary were told to industrialise rapidly although they lacked the raw materials.
- **It served Soviet needs.** **Comecon** (1949) was meant to coordinate the satellites' trade with the USSR. In its early years Moscow took reparations from East Germany until the end of 1953, and bought Polish coal at prices far below those of the world market until 1954.
- **It was rigid.** A planner in Moscow or Warsaw could not know what shops needed, so there were shortages of goods that people wanted (and queues) and surpluses of goods nobody wanted. Managers who were judged on meeting targets had no reason to improve quality or to save materials. Workers faced rising "norms" (the amount of work expected for a given wage), the issue that began the rising in East Berlin in 1953.

Farmland was **collectivised**: peasants were pressed to give up their land and join collective farms. It was done fastest in Czechoslovakia, in East Germany (completed in the "socialist spring" of 1960), and, with forced methods and a pause after 1953, in Hungary. Poland, as noted above, gave up the attempt in 1956. Collectivisation was unpopular and cut output at first. In East Germany it added to the number of farmers leaving for the West in 1960–61.

### Changes in living standards

For ordinary people the effects were mixed, and they changed over time, which is the point to make in an essay.

| Period | Living standards |
|---|---|
| Late 1940s to 1953 | Hard. Heavy industry took the investment, food and goods were scarce, wages were held down and norms raised. In East Germany, food rationing continued; it was not ended until 1958. |
| 1953–56 | Concessions: the "New Course" in Hungary and East Germany shifted some investment to consumer goods and housing, though it was often reversed. |
| Late 1950s–1960s | Slow improvement from a low base: most families got jobs, free health care and education, subsidised food, rent and transport, and the first televisions, refrigerators and small cars. But the gap with Western Europe grew, since West Germany and Austria were recovering fast, and queues, housing shortages and poor quality remained. |

The **Czech** and **East German** populations had the highest living standards in the bloc, and for that reason they compared themselves with the West. The regimes tried to justify their rule with promises of material progress, which meant that any failure, such as the stagnation of Czechoslovakia's economy in 1962–63, was a political danger.

:::key Social and economic causes of discontent
1. **A system that could not deliver** the standard of living of the West next door, and that was copied from the USSR without regard to local needs.
2. **Pressure on workers** (norms, shortages) that led to strikes in 1953 and 1956.
3. **Resentment of Soviet exploitation** (reparations, cheap coal), which gave economic grievances a national target.
4. **Collectivisation** and the attack on religion, which angered the peasantry and the Church, the two largest groups outside the party.
Economic hardship alone did not cause the crises: in each case it combined with a political event (Stalin's death, the secret speech, a leadership struggle) that made protest seem possible.
:::

## The crisis of the system: Stalin's death and the East German rising, 1953

**Stalin died on 5 March 1953.** His successors, Georgy Malenkov, Lavrenty Beria and Nikita Khrushchev, were not sure of their own position and were afraid that the satellite states might be unstable. They told the leaders of several of them, Hungary and East Germany among them, to relax the pressure on their people: the "New Course". In Czechoslovakia a sharp currency reform in late May 1953 caused strikes and riots at the Škoda works in Plzeň on 1 June, which the army and police put down.

East Germany's case was the most serious. The SED's Politburo had raised work norms by 10 per cent on 28 May 1953, and on 9 June, under pressure from Moscow, it announced a "New Course" of concessions to farmers and small business but kept the higher norms. On **16 June** building workers on the Stalinallee, the showpiece boulevard of East Berlin, marched to the government offices to demand that the norms be withdrawn. On **17 June** strikes and demonstrations took place in hundreds of places across the GDR, with demands that moved from norms to free elections and the end of the regime. Soviet troops and tanks, and units of the East German armed police, put the rising down by the evening. Soviet martial law was declared in East Berlin. At least 55 people were killed, and some later estimates are higher, and several thousand were arrested.

The rising was important for four reasons:
- It was the first large revolt against communist rule in the satellite states and it showed that workers, the class in whose name the party ruled, could rebel against it.
- It showed that Moscow would use force to keep a satellite regime in power. The Soviet army, not the East German party, saved Ulbricht.
- It strengthened Ulbricht's position. Moscow had considered replacing him, but now concluded that weakening him would risk a collapse. The arrest of Beria on 26 June also helped Ulbricht to remove two rivals from the SED leadership. The Soviets ended reparations from East Germany from 1 January 1954 and gave aid.
- The West did not intervene. There were demonstrations in West Germany, and 17 June was a public holiday there until 1990, but no Western government took action. The lesson for Moscow was that its sphere was secure; the lesson for the satellite peoples was that no one would come to their aid.

## 1956: de-Stalinisation and the crises in Poland and Hungary

### The secret speech and the idea of national roads

In 1955 Khrushchev, now the leading Soviet figure, visited Belgrade and made peace with Tito, accepting that there could be different "roads to socialism". On **25 February 1956**, in a closed session at the end of the Twentieth Congress of the Soviet Communist Party, he gave the **secret speech**, which denounced Stalin's "cult of personality" and the crimes of his rule (the arrests and executions of loyal communists). It was not published in the USSR, but the Polish party made a copy available, the text reached the West within weeks, and it was soon known all over eastern Europe. The Cominform was dissolved in April 1956.

The speech had a serious effect. If Stalin had been a tyrant, then the men who had governed the satellite states in his name were open to attack as Stalinists. Rákosi's regime was almost impossible to defend, and in Poland the death of the Stalinist leader Bierut in Moscow on 12 March 1956 left the party without an obvious leader. The speech opened the door to reform and to protest and it was impossible to close again.

:::key Why 1956 was a year of crisis
1. **De-Stalinisation** made the old leaders look guilty and gave reformers within the parties a language and a cause.
2. **Moscow's uncertainty**: Khrushchev wanted loyal parties that would not need terror, but he had no plan for what to do if reform went further than he wished.
3. **Economic grievances**: low wages, shortages and high norms.
4. **National feeling**: resentment of Soviet control, strongest in Poland and Hungary.
5. **A leadership vacuum**: the Polish and Hungarian parties had no leader whom the public trusted.
:::

### Poland: Poznań and the October of 1956

On **28 June 1956** workers at the Stalin Works in Poznań, who had been refused a hearing for complaints about wages and norms, went on strike and marched through the city. Some demonstrators attacked the offices of the security police and the party; the army was called in with tanks. According to later Polish investigations more than fifty people were killed, and hundreds were wounded. The authorities at first blamed "imperialist agents" but then admitted that the workers had real grievances, and the trial of those arrested was conducted more mildly than a few years earlier.

The party leadership, headed by Edward Ochab after Bierut's death, was divided between Stalinists and reformers, and the reformers wanted Gomułka, who had been released from prison in 1954–55. **Władysław Gomułka** had been the party's leader in 1943–48, when he spoke of a "Polish road to socialism", and was removed in 1948 as a "nationalist deviationist". When the Central Committee met in the Eighth Plenum on **19 October 1956**, the Stalinists tried to prevent him from coming to power. Moscow reacted at once: on the morning of 19 October Khrushchev, Molotov, Kaganovich, Mikoyan and Marshal Konev, the Commander-in-Chief of the Warsaw Pact, flew unannounced to Warsaw, and Soviet troops in Poland and East Germany began to move towards the capital. Polish troops under the Internal Security Corps and armed workers in Warsaw's factories prepared to defend the city. Both sides negotiated through the night.

The outcome was a compromise. On **21 October** the Central Committee elected Gomułka First Secretary and removed Marshal Rokossovsky, the Soviet Marshal who served as Polish Defence Minister, from the Politburo (he was sent back to the USSR in November). Gomułka promised Khrushchev that Poland would remain in the Warsaw Pact and a socialist state with the party in command. Khrushchev accepted this, and the Soviet army halted. Gomułka had to prove that he could keep order, and he did. Over the next months he reached an agreement under which Soviet troops would remain in Poland (December 1956), released Wyszyński, allowed most collective farms to dissolve, and eased censorship. He also pressed students and workers who had hoped for more to accept limits: by 1957 the reform had halted.

:::def Polish October
The change of leadership in Poland in October 1956, when the party elected Władysław Gomułka First Secretary in the face of a threatened Soviet intervention. It brought a measure of national autonomy within the Soviet bloc without removing the party's monopoly or Poland's membership of the Warsaw Pact.
:::

### Hungary: causes

Hungary's party, like Poland's, was split by de-Stalinisation, and the Hungarian public was angrier. **Causes** can be sorted into four groups.

1. **The record of the regime.** Rákosi's terror, the show trials, forced collectivisation, the cult of personality and the ÁVH had made his government hated. Forced industrialisation had held down living standards for years.
2. **De-Stalinisation and the division in the party.** After the secret speech, writers and students in the Petőfi Circle in Budapest held public debates in the summer of 1956, with crowds of thousands, which criticised the regime. Nagy's supporters demanded his return. Moscow forced the removal of Rákosi in July 1956, and replaced him with Ernő Gerő, who was not seen as a break with the past. The reburial of the executed Rajk on **6 October 1956**, which a very large crowd attended (estimates run to 100,000 or more), was in effect a public condemnation of the regime.
3. **The Polish example.** News of Poznań and of Gomułka's return in October showed Hungarians that a communist government could be forced to change.
4. **National feeling.** The Soviet occupation, Soviet control of the economy, and the memory of the Habsburg-era struggle for independence (the revolution of 1848 was remembered as a national festival) made the movement a national one as well as a reform of communism.

### Hungary: course

- **23 October 1956.** Students at the Technical University in Budapest drew up sixteen demands. They called for the withdrawal of Soviet troops, free elections with several parties, the return of Nagy to government and the removal of Stalin's statue. A large crowd marched to the statue of the poet General Bem (a hero of 1848) and to Parliament. That evening the crowd pulled down the giant statue of Stalin. When a delegation tried to read the students' demands on the radio, the ÁVH fired on the crowd outside the radio building, and the demonstration became an armed uprising. Gerő called in Soviet troops, who entered Budapest during the night.
- **24–28 October.** Nagy was made prime minister on 24 October, but the arrival of Soviet tanks, and martial law, turned most Hungarians against the government. On 25 October shooting outside Parliament killed dozens of unarmed demonstrators, and the party replaced Gerő with **János Kádár**. Workers' councils and revolutionary committees took over towns and factories, and young fighters, using weapons taken from the army, held parts of Budapest. On 28 October Nagy agreed a ceasefire and announced that the ÁVH would be abolished.
- **29 October–1 November.** On 30 October Nagy restored a multi-party system, and on the same day a Soviet government declaration promised relations with the other socialist states on the basis of equality and said that the Soviet army units would be withdrawn from Budapest. Soviet forces began to leave the capital. Mindszenty was freed. On 31 October the Soviet leaders, who had been divided, decided to intervene after all. Historians point to several reasons: fear that Hungary would leave the bloc, pressure from other communist leaders, including China's, who had at first urged restraint, and the Suez crisis, which began on 29 October with the Israeli attack on Egypt and went on with the Anglo-French landings of 5–6 November, and which distracted the Western powers. On **1 November**, as new Soviet troops crossed the border, Nagy announced Hungary's **withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact** and its neutrality, and appealed to the United Nations. Kádár had left Budapest and, with Soviet support, formed a rival government.
- **4 November.** At about five in the morning, about 30,000 Soviet soldiers and over 1,000 tanks (estimates of the force vary) began a coordinated assault on the capital under the code name Operation Whirlwind. Nagy broadcast a brief message to the world announcing the attack; "our troops are fighting", it said. He took refuge in the Yugoslav embassy. Resistance, strongest among young fighters in working-class districts, lasted about a week, with a general strike and the workers' councils continuing into December. The government of Kádár, which had been formed in Moscow, was installed.

### Hungary: impact

- **Casualties and exodus.** About 2,500 to 3,000 Hungarians were killed (estimates vary) and about 700 Soviet soldiers; about 200,000 Hungarians, roughly 2 per cent of the population, left the country, mostly for Austria.
- **Repression in Hungary.** Nagy was lured out of the embassy under a promise of safe conduct on 22 November, then seized by Soviet forces and taken to Romania. He was tried in secret and hanged on **16 June 1958**, with Pál Maléter, his defence minister, and Miklós Gimes, a journalist. Hundreds of others were executed and many thousands imprisoned or interned.
- **Kádár's regime.** Kádár, who had himself been tortured under Rákosi, began as a hated figure, installed by Soviet tanks, but he gradually changed policy. He reversed his slogan from "he who is not with us is against us" to "he who is not against us is with us" (1961), reduced terror, granted an amnesty in 1963 and, in the 1960s, tried to win acceptance by raising living standards and allowing a measure of private economic activity. By 1968 Hungary was the most liberal state in the bloc in everyday life, while its foreign policy stayed within the Soviet line.
- **International.** The USSR's action was condemned at the United Nations, but the West did nothing. The United States had spoken of "rollback" and its radio had encouraged Hungarians, but President Eisenhower was concerned with the election and with Suez, and a war with the USSR over Hungary was not a serious possibility. Many communists in the West left their parties. The Soviet Union showed that it would use full force, if it saw the survival of communism in a satellite at risk.
- **Within the bloc.** The lesson for the other satellites was that a state could change its leaders and ease its rule, as Poland had done, but could not leave the Pact or end the party's monopoly.

:::key Why did Poland and Hungary end so differently in 1956?
1. **Where the leader stood.** Gomułka was a communist who promised loyalty to the Pact and the party's rule; Nagy, pressed by events, ended by announcing neutrality and a multi-party system, which Moscow could not accept.
2. **Who held the party and army together.** In Poland the party, with Gomułka, and the army kept control, and no fighting broke out. In Hungary the party collapsed, the ÁVH was hated and then disbanded, the army split, and armed civilians took over.
3. **The pace of events.** Khrushchev negotiated in Warsaw for little more than a day, with the Polish party still in charge. In Hungary within twelve days a demonstration had become a revolution, and the Soviet leaders had to react to fighting and to the Pact's loss.
4. **The international setting.** The Suez crisis from 29 October and Western passivity made intervention in Hungary cheap, while in Poland the Soviet army would have faced a united people, an army and a Church.
5. **Geography.** Poland lay on the line of supply to the Soviet forces in East Germany, which made it too important to lose, and also made it too large to occupy easily.
:::

## Berlin and the building of the Wall, 1958–61

### Why Berlin was a problem

After 1945 Berlin was divided into four occupation sectors, deep inside the Soviet zone. The Soviets had tried to force the Western powers out in the blockade of 1948–49, which failed after the Berlin airlift. West Berlin was a prosperous Western city with a shop-window effect on its eastern neighbour, and, unlike the rest of the East German border, the line between East and West Berlin stayed open to people on foot, by underground and by elevated railway. From the 1950s that open frontier was the main route by which East Germans left. Most of those who fled went through Berlin, and they were disproportionately young and skilled: engineers, doctors, teachers and technicians. By the summer of 1961 East Germany was at times losing about a thousand people a day, and Ulbricht warned Moscow that his state would not survive if the loss continued. A Soviet-allied state that could not keep its citizens was a standing argument for the West.

:::def Berlin Wall
The barrier of concrete walls, fences, wire and guard towers that East Germany built between 13 August 1961 and later years to seal off West Berlin from East Berlin and from East Germany. The East German government called it the "anti-fascist protection rampart".
:::

### Khrushchev's ultimatum and the crisis

On 27 November 1958 Khrushchev sent notes to the Western powers demanding that West Berlin become a demilitarised "free city" and that they negotiate within six months, after which the USSR would hand its rights to East Germany. He had several aims: to end the haemorrhage of people, to gain recognition for East Germany, to stop West German access to nuclear weapons and to show Soviet strength. The deadline passed without action, and a summit in Paris in 1960 collapsed after the shooting down of an American U-2 spy plane. At the Vienna summit of 3–4 June 1961 Khrushchev again told the new American President, John F. Kennedy, that he would sign a separate peace treaty with East Germany. On 25 July Kennedy declared that the USA would defend three essentials: the Western presence in West Berlin, free access to it and the freedom of its people.

The Wall is therefore best understood as the Soviet bloc's answer to the failure of the ultimatum. On **15 June 1961** Ulbricht told a press conference, in answer to a question, "Niemand hat die Absicht, eine Mauer zu errichten" (no one has the intention of building a wall), fifty-nine days before it was built. In the first half of August the flow of refugees rose again. At a meeting in Moscow on 3–5 August the leaders of the Warsaw Pact states agreed to allow East Germany to close the border in Berlin, and Khrushchev gave approval once the Western reaction was clear.

### The Wall

In the early hours of **Sunday 13 August 1961** East German police, soldiers and factory militia stretched barbed wire along the sector boundary and tore up the streets that crossed it, while Soviet troops stayed in the background. The crossing points were closed and, in the following days, the wire was replaced by concrete blocks. Over the years it grew into a double wall with a strip between, searchlights, watchtowers and guards who had orders to prevent escape, with weapons if necessary. At least 140 people died at the Wall between 1961 and 1989, according to the Potsdam Centre for Contemporary History and the Berlin Wall Foundation (other counts are higher). One of the first to become well known was Peter Fechter, aged 18, shot while trying to cross on 17 August 1962 and left to bleed to death in the border strip in view of Western police and press.

### Impact

- **For East Germany**, the Wall worked. The outflow of skilled workers stopped, and the economy was stabilised, so that in the 1960s the GDR's people had the highest living standards in the bloc, together with the Czechs. The state could now plan on the assumption that its people could not leave.
- **For the USSR**, it solved a problem without a war. Khrushchev dropped his deadline in October 1961. The cost was a propaganda defeat: the Wall was a monument to the failure of a state that had to shut its people in. Kennedy, who could not stop it without a war, said in West Berlin in June 1963 "Ich bin ein Berliner".
- **For the West**, the Wall did not break any of Kennedy's three essentials, and so the West did not act. There were tense moments, notably on 27–28 October 1961 when American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, but both sides stepped back.
- **Within the bloc**, the Wall was the clearest sign that the system could not keep its people by consent. It was a barrier aimed at the citizens of a satellite state, not at an enemy. It ended the open crisis over Berlin, and it stood until 9 November 1989.

## Soviet control under Khrushchev: what had changed by 1964?

By the early 1960s the Soviet bloc had a stable pattern. The new leaders of Hungary (Kádár), Poland (Gomułka), East Germany (Ulbricht) and Czechoslovakia (Novotný) were loyal communists who kept order and, in return, were allowed some room. Their relationship with Moscow was less like a master's with his servants and more like that of a boss with the managers of his branch offices: more bargaining, but a boss who would act if the business was in danger. Khrushchev's fall in October 1964 changed Moscow but not the system. Two states had moved away from the Soviet line, but only in ways that did not alter the party's power:

- **Romania** under Gheorghiu-Dej, and then Nicolae Ceaușescu from 1965, held to a one-party state, with a harsher regime than Hungary's, but followed an independent line in foreign policy, refusing to accept Comecon's plan for a division of labour that would have made it a farming country. Soviet troops had left in 1958.
- **Albania** sided with China in the Sino-Soviet split and stopped attending Pact meetings in 1961.

## The Prague Spring, 1968

### Causes

By 1967 Czechoslovakia was a stagnant state with a growing reform movement.

1. **Economic stagnation.** After the collapse of the Third Five-Year Plan in 1962–63, central planning looked as if it had failed in the most industrial state in the bloc. Economists, led by Ota Šik, proposed giving enterprises more independence and using market signals, and a "New Economic Model" was adopted in 1965 but only half carried out.
2. **De-Stalinisation that had stopped.** The victims of the show trials were rehabilitated only slowly, which kept the injustices a live issue. Writers, who had a strong voice, demanded freedom. At the Writers' Union congress in June 1967 several of them, among them Ludvík Vaculík and Milan Kundera, openly criticised the regime's control of culture, and the party punished the Union.
3. **Slovak resentment.** The Slovaks felt that Prague treated Slovakia as a poor relation, and in the 1950s the regime had imprisoned Slovak communists, among them Gustáv Husák, as "bourgeois nationalists". Alexander Dubček, the Slovak party leader, became the leader of the opposition within the party.
4. **A crisis of leadership.** In October 1967 the police broke up a student demonstration in Prague, after the electricity failed in the Strahov hostels, and the brutality shocked public opinion. In the Central Committee Novotný's opponents, including Dubček, attacked him for months. When Brezhnev visited Prague in December 1967, he decided that the dispute was an internal matter and did not save him.

On **5 January 1968** the Central Committee replaced Novotný as First Secretary with **Alexander Dubček**. In March Novotný resigned as President and General Ludvík Svoboda took his place.

### Course

- **The Action Programme (April 1968).** Dubček was a loyal communist, a Slovak who had grown up in the USSR and trained in Moscow, and he believed that the party could regain its people's trust if it reformed. The Central Committee adopted the **Action Programme** on 5 April 1968. It promised freedom of speech, the press, assembly and travel, an end to arbitrary police action, the rehabilitation of the victims of the 1950s, economic reform (with more independence for firms), and a federal system that would give Slovakia equality. It talked of "socialism with a human face". It kept the party's leading role, which it said would have to be earned and not imposed. Censorship collapsed in practice from about March, and the press, radio and television began to discuss subjects that had been forbidden for twenty years.
- **Pressure from outside.** Moscow and the more hardline satellite leaders became alarmed. At a meeting in Dresden on 23 March 1968 Brezhnev, Ulbricht, Gomułka, Kádár and Zhivkov questioned the Czechoslovak delegation closely about the speed of reform. Ulbricht and Gomułka, who feared that reform would spread to their own states, were the most anxious; Kádár, who had personal experience of 1956, tried to mediate. On 27 June the writer Ludvík Vaculík published a manifesto called "Two Thousand Words" that urged ordinary people to press the reform. It alarmed Moscow because it appeared to call for action outside the party. Warsaw Pact exercises in Czechoslovakia in June were followed by the slow departure of Soviet troops. On 15 July the five states (the USSR, Poland, East Germany, Hungary and Bulgaria) sent a letter from Warsaw telling the Czechoslovak leaders that socialism was in danger in their country and that they must take firm action to end it.
- **Negotiation.** Dubček refused to attend the Warsaw meeting but agreed to meet the Soviet Politburo at the border village of **Čierna nad Tisou** on 29 July–1 August 1968. A compromise was patched up, and on 3 August the six states issued a declaration at Bratislava which affirmed their unity on the basic principles of socialism, without any mention of Czechoslovakia. During the summer Tito, the Yugoslav leader, and Ceaușescu of Romania visited Prague and supported the reform. Some hard-line Czechoslovak communists, including Vasil Biľak, asked the Soviets to help, in a letter that was only produced in the 1990s.
- **The decision.** The Soviet Politburo was divided for months. It decided around 16–18 August, when Brezhnev was convinced that Dubček had not kept the promises he had made and that the hardliners in the party would lose control. The motives are discussed below.

### The invasion

At about 11 pm on **20 August 1968** troops of the USSR, Poland, Bulgaria and Hungary crossed the Czechoslovak border, and by the morning they held Prague, its airport and the main roads. East German troops had been ordered, at the last minute, not to cross, so as not to revive the memory of the German occupation of 1938–45. The estimates of the force vary, from about 165,000 men at first to figures of several hundred thousand later (up to about 500,000) and thousands of tanks. Dubček and other leaders were arrested by the KGB and flown to Moscow. The Czechoslovak army was ordered by the government not to resist, and the resistance was by civilians who talked to the soldiers, painted over street names and held the party's Fourteenth Congress in secret in a Prague factory. The invasion killed 137 Czechs and Slovaks, in the count most often cited, and about 500 were seriously wounded.

### Impact

- **The Moscow Protocol.** Dubček and his colleagues were forced to sign, on 26 August, a protocol that committed them to undo much of the reform, with only the veteran communist František Kriegel refusing to sign.
- **Normalisation.** On 16 October 1968 a treaty allowed the "temporary" stationing of Soviet troops, which stayed until 1991. In April 1969, after anti-Soviet riots that followed a victory by Czechoslovakia over the USSR in an ice hockey match, Dubček was replaced by **Gustáv Husák**, who purged the party of hundreds of thousands of reformers and restored censorship. This was called "normalisation". The only reform that survived was the federalisation of the state, which took effect on 1 January 1969.
- **Emigration.** Many thousands of Czechs and Slovaks left the country (about 70,000 at once, and about 300,000 over the next two decades, by one estimate).
- **International.** The West protested but did not intervene, as in 1956, and the American government was preoccupied with Vietnam. Romania refused to join and Albania left the Pact. Several Western communist parties condemned the invasion. China, which was in conflict with the USSR, condemned the invasion as aggression.

### Why did the USSR invade? Assessing the reasons

This is the question behind many essays. Sort the reasons into groups.

:::key Reasons for the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia, 1968
1. **Fear of contagion.** The Czechoslovak press, which was read in the Soviet Union and the satellites, showed that criticism of the party was possible. Soviet leaders feared that the example would affect Ukraine, where a national movement existed and which bordered Czechoslovakia, and Poland, where students had demonstrated in March 1968.
2. **Strategic position.** Czechoslovakia bordered West Germany. Soviet generals regarded it as the western front of the Warsaw Pact, and they were nervous about the idea that Dubček would pursue an independent foreign policy.
3. **The loss of party control.** The Action Programme, the end of censorship and the "Two Thousand Words" suggested that the party was no longer in control of events, and that a multi-party system might come. For leaders such as Brezhnev, the party's monopoly was the basis of the Soviet bloc.
4. **Pressure from the hardliners.** Ulbricht, Gomułka and Zhivkov pushed Brezhnev, and the Czechoslovak hardliners asked for Soviet help. Brezhnev also needed to carry his own Politburo, in which several members, including the KGB chairman Yuri Andropov and the Defence Minister, Marshal Grechko, urged firm action.
5. **The lessons of 1956.** Hungary showed that a reform movement could go beyond its leader's intentions; Dubček's assurances that he would stay in the Pact, as Nagy's had been at the start, did not reassure Moscow.
6. **The failure of other methods.** Negotiation at Čierna and Bratislava, economic pressure and the military exercises had not made Dubček change his course, in the opinion of Brezhnev.
:::

Historians who have used the Soviet archives, among them Mark Kramer, stress that the Soviet leaders did not act quickly or lightly, and that Brezhnev, who is often portrayed as the leader of the hawks, held back until August and came to the decision when he feared that the party would blame him for losing Czechoslovakia. A good answer uses that point: the invasion was a decision of a divided and anxious leadership more than of a calculating one, and the argument that carried the day was about control of the party.

## Soviet reactions to dissent

Soviet policy in the crises had three tools, which it used in different combinations. This is the core of the key question.

:::key Three kinds of Soviet reaction to dissent
1. **Political change.** Remove the discredited leader and allow reform within limits: Rákosi was removed in July 1956 on Moscow's advice and Gomułka was accepted in October 1956. This was the method Khrushchev preferred when it could work, and it succeeded in Poland.
2. **Repression.** Arrests, trials and executions in the satellite state, usually carried out by its own government: the secret trial and hanging of Nagy in 1958, the reprisals of Kádár's government in 1957–58, Husák's purge of the Czechoslovak party in 1969–70. Soviet security officers and advisers took part.
3. **Military action.** The use of Soviet troops, alone or with the satellites' forces: East Berlin in 1953, Budapest in 1956, Czechoslovakia in 1968, and the threat in Poland in 1956. The Berlin Wall was a form of internal military action: a closure of the border by the East German forces, with Soviet troops in support.
The three were linked. Moscow used political change first and force when the first failed or seemed too late, and after force it relied on repression in the satellite state to restore stability.
:::

### The Brezhnev Doctrine, 1968

After the invasion the Soviet Union needed to justify its action, which was a breach of the principle of the equality of states that Moscow had proclaimed in October 1956. The justification became known in the West as the **Brezhnev Doctrine**. It was set out in an article by Sergei Kovalev in *Pravda* on 26 September 1968 ("Sovereignty and the International Obligations of Socialist Countries") and stated by Brezhnev himself at the Polish party congress in Warsaw on 12 November 1968. Its argument was that the socialist states formed a community, and that the defence of socialism in any one of them was the concern of all.

> *This means that each Communist party is responsible not only to its own people, but also to all the socialist countries, to the entire Communist movement … The sovereignty of each socialist country cannot be opposed to the interests of the world of socialism, of the world revolutionary movement.*
>
> *Adapted from S. Kovalev, "Sovereignty and the International Obligations of Socialist Countries", Pravda, September 1968, in the translation by Novosti, Soviet press agency (the Fordham Internet Modern History Sourcebook dates it 25 September; most sources give 26 September).*

:::def Brezhnev Doctrine
The principle, justified in 1968 after the invasion of Czechoslovakia, that the Soviet Union and its allies had the right and duty to intervene in any socialist state where socialism was threatened, because the sovereignty of each socialist country was limited by the common interests of the socialist community.
:::

The doctrine did not change Soviet practice: the Hungarian and Czechoslovak interventions had already followed the same logic. What it did was to give the practice a theory and to warn the satellite leaders that reform movements would be crushed. Western writers called it a doctrine of "limited sovereignty". It remained the stated policy until Gorbachev renounced it in the late 1980s (covered in [The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (4)](#hist.d.soviet.4)).

## How did the relationship develop? Weighing the evidence

Putting the period together, three phases can be seen.

1. **1953–56: control through terror gives way to control through loyalty.** The death of Stalin and the secret speech weakened the old methods and the old leaders. The Soviet reaction to the East German rising, Poznań and the Polish October shows a Moscow that was willing to compromise if the party and the Pact were safe.
2. **1956–61: the limits are drawn.** Hungary showed that leaving the Pact and ending the party's monopoly were not allowed. Berlin showed that the Soviet bloc would use physical barriers when its people tried to leave.
3. **1961–68: consolidation, and a challenge from within the system.** The satellite governments, with Soviet backing, found ways to rule with less terror and more consumer goods. The Prague Spring tested the limit again, this time from a reforming communist party, and the invasion and the doctrine made the limit explicit.

:::key A judgement you can defend
Soviet control rested on three supports: the party monopoly in each state, the Soviet army and the Pact, and the willingness to use force. It was never based on the consent of the peoples. Between 1953 and 1968 the first support was shaken three times (1953, 1956, 1968) and in each case the third was used to restore it, in 1956 and 1968 with great force. The relationship therefore developed from the crude dominance of the Stalin period into a more flexible system, in which satellite leaders had some room, but the line between permitted reform and forbidden change was drawn by Moscow and enforced by tanks. The "development" was thus real in method and limited in substance.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
- **Anne Applebaum**, *Iron Curtain: The Crushing of Eastern Europe, 1944–1956* (2012), argues that Sovietisation was planned and deliberate, a systematic export of the Soviet model that was resisted but made secure by the secret police.
- **Vojtech Mastny and Malcolm Byrne** (eds), *A Cardboard Castle? An Inside History of the Warsaw Pact, 1955–1991* (2005), use the Pact's archives to show that it was an instrument of Soviet control more than a defence against NATO, and a poor military alliance.
- **Mark Kramer**, in "The Soviet Union and the 1956 Crises in Hungary and Poland: Reassessments and New Findings" (*Journal of Contemporary History*, 1998), shows, from Soviet archives, that the Presidium (the name of the Politburo from 1952 to 1966) was divided and hesitant in October 1956 and decided to intervene in Hungary only on 31 October.
- **Vladislav Zubok**, *A Failed Empire: The Soviet Union in the Cold War from Stalin to Gorbachev* (2007), stresses that the Soviet leaders saw their control of eastern Europe as the reward of the Second World War and the basis of their status as a world power, so that they felt unable to let any state leave.
Use these to show you know that the question is debated. Paper 4 rewards your own judgement, backed by evidence, more than a list of names.

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 7.1
1. **Define your criteria first.** For "the greatest threat to Soviet control", criteria could be *the threat to the party's monopoly*, *the threat to the Warsaw Pact*, *the size of the Soviet response* and *the long-term effect*. For "reasons for the invasion", use *necessity* (could Moscow have lived without invading?) and *urgency* (what made August 1968 the moment?).
2. **Compare crises, not describe them.** Every paragraph should rank one crisis against another: Hungary against Czechoslovakia, Poland against Hungary.
3. **Use exact evidence**: 14 May 1955, 17 June 1953, 23 October–4 November 1956, 13 August 1961, 20–21 August 1968, 137 killed in Czechoslovakia, about 200,000 refugees from Hungary, about 2.7 million emigrants from East Germany.
4. **Keep the dates in view.** "Up to 1968" ends with the invasion and the doctrine; do not go on into the 1970s.
5. **Separate Soviet control from the satellites' consent.** A common mistake is to treat the compliance of the satellite governments as popular support.
6. **Judge.** Say which was the greatest threat, the most important reason or the biggest change, and what would have been different without it.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, with a total out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'The Hungarian uprising of 1956 was the greatest threat to Soviet control before 1968.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement partly substantiated; a range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criteria: (1) did it threaten the party's monopoly in the state? (2) did it threaten the Warsaw Pact and the Soviet military position? (3) how much force did Moscow need? (4) what was the long-term effect on Soviet control?
- For the view: Hungary is the only crisis in which a satellite government itself broke with the system: multi-party system (30 October), withdrawal from the Pact and neutrality (1 November), appeal to the UN; the ÁVH abolished; the army split; about 30,000 Soviet troops and over 1,000 tanks; about 700 Soviet dead.
- Against 1: East Germany 1953 (nationwide, a rising of workers against the regime in hundreds of places, but over in a day or two; no break with the Pact) and Berlin 1958–61 (about 2.7 million emigrants; a threat to the GDR's survival, which Moscow regarded as its keystone in Europe).
- Against 2: Poland 1956 (the largest satellite, on the line of supply to East Germany; Khrushchev's flight on 19 October; a more important prize than Hungary, but the party stayed in control).
- Judgement: Hungary was the most direct challenge, because it was the only one that broke with the system, but not the greatest in its long-term effect: Poland and Berlin did more to shape later Soviet policy.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Hungarian uprising was a big threat to Soviet control. It started on 23 October 1956 when students marched in Budapest, and the people pulled down the statue of Stalin. Imre Nagy became prime minister and said that Hungary would leave the Warsaw Pact. The Soviet army then invaded on 4 November with tanks and killed many Hungarians. About 200,000 people left the country. So the Hungarian uprising was the greatest threat to the Soviet Union because it was the most violent."

*Commentary.* The facts are correct and the paragraph is relevant, but it **narrates** the uprising and then asserts a judgement ("because it was the most violent") that is not argued. Violence is not the same as a threat to control. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 3 (about 8), accurate but without exact dates or comparisons. To move up: say what the uprising threatened and test it against other crises.

**A Level 4 paragraph (explains one side, but without a comparison).** "Hungary was the greatest threat because it challenged the two things on which Soviet control rested, the party's monopoly and the Warsaw Pact. On 30 October Nagy restored a multi-party system and on 1 November he declared Hungary neutral and announced that it was leaving the Pact. The ÁVH had been abolished on 28 October, and the Hungarian army had split. The Soviet leaders, who had decided on 30 October to withdraw their troops from Budapest and to talk of equality between socialist states, reversed their decision on 31 October because they feared that if Hungary left, other states would follow. It then took about 30,000 troops and over 1,000 tanks and 700 Soviet dead to crush the revolution. No other crisis before 1968 needed this scale of force."

*Commentary.* A criterion is stated implicitly (the party's monopoly and the Pact), the evidence is precise and the explanation is clear, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). But the paragraph never compares Hungary with the other crises, and the claim that "no other crisis needed this scale of force" is left unproven. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs comparison with 1953, Poland and Berlin, and a judgement.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"A threat to Soviet control can be measured in four ways: how far it endangered the party's monopoly in a satellite, how far it endangered the Warsaw Pact and the Soviet military position, how much force Moscow needed to restore control, and how much it changed Soviet control in the long term. On the first three tests Hungary in 1956 was the greatest threat before 1968; on the fourth it was not, and so the view is true of the severity of the challenge but not of its lasting importance.

Hungary was unique in that a satellite government itself left the Soviet system. After the shooting at the radio building on 23 October the party collapsed. Nagy abolished the ÁVH on 28 October, restored a multi-party system on 30 October, and on 1 November announced that Hungary was neutral, that it was withdrawing from the Warsaw Pact and that he was appealing to the United Nations. These were not demands by a crowd but acts of a government, and they struck at both supports of Soviet control at once. The effort needed to restore it shows the scale of the threat: the Soviet army had withdrawn from Budapest on 30 October, and four days later about 30,000 troops and over 1,000 tanks returned, and about 700 Soviet soldiers died, a far larger operation than in 1953. The decision of 31 October to reverse the withdrawal, taken after a Presidium that was divided, shows that Moscow saw the loss of Hungary as intolerable.

The East German rising of 1953 was a different kind of threat. It was larger in the sense that it spread across the country, with strikes and demonstrations in hundreds of places, and it was a rising of industrial workers, the class in whose name the party ruled. But it was over in about two days, it was directed against the regime and the norms and not against the Soviet system, and no East German institution broke with Moscow: the party and the police stayed loyal, and Soviet tanks, which had no difficulty, saved Ulbricht. The Berlin crisis of 1958–61 threatened the existence of East Germany, whose population, some 2.7 million of whom left between 1949 and 1961, was voting with its feet, and Moscow thought East Germany so important that Khrushchev risked a confrontation with the West over it. But this was a threat from the West and from emigration, not from an uprising, and Moscow chose the time and the method of ending it: the Wall of 13 August 1961 cost it prestige but no loss of control. Poland in 1956 had the potential to be a more serious loss than Hungary, since it was larger and since it lay across the Soviet supply lines to East Germany, and Khrushchev's flight to Warsaw on 19 October shows the importance he gave it. Yet the threat did not develop because Gomułka, a loyal communist, promised to keep Poland in the Pact and the party in control.

On the fourth test the verdict is different. The crushing of Hungary strengthened Soviet control for twelve years: it taught the satellites the limits, and Kádár's regime became a model of reform within limits. Poland and Berlin had longer effects on the way the empire worked. The Polish October ended collectivisation, gave the Church new freedom and began the pattern of a satellite that could bargain with Moscow, and the Wall made East Germany stable and settled the German question for a generation.

Overall, Hungary was the greatest threat to Soviet control before 1968 in its immediate danger, because it was the only crisis in which a satellite state tried to leave both the party system and the Pact and in which Moscow needed a full invasion to prevent it. It was not the most important in its consequences, since Poland and Berlin shaped the later relationship more. The view is therefore largely right, so long as 'greatest' means the most direct challenge and not the most lasting."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with **four criteria** and uses them in turn, and each paragraph compares Hungary with another crisis using precise evidence (30 and 31 October, 1 November, 28 October, about 700 Soviet dead, 2.7 million emigrants, 19 October). The judgement is qualified by the difference between severity and lasting importance. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13), precise and chosen to prove points; one or two figures could be given with their dates for the top mark. Total about 27/30.
:::

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the reasons for the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968.
**What the levels want.** An "Assess the reasons" question needs a range of reasons, a criterion for ranking them, and a judgement on which mattered most and how they connect. At Level 5 each reason is explained with precise evidence and compared with the others.

**Plan.**
- Criterion: which reason, if absent, would probably have prevented the invasion (necessity), and which explains why it happened in August 1968 (timing)?
- Reasons: (1) the party's loss of control: the Action Programme, the end of censorship, "Two Thousand Words" (27 June); (2) the strategic position and the cohesion of the Warsaw Pact (West German border; Albania and Romania already out of line); (3) fear of contagion in Ukraine and Poland (students in March 1968); (4) pressure from Ulbricht, Gomułka and Zhivkov and the Czechoslovak hardliners' letter; (5) the failure of negotiation at Čierna (29 July–1 August) and Bratislava (3 August).
- Against over-simple readings: Dubček never proposed leaving the Pact or ending the party's rule; Brezhnev held back until August.
- Judgement: loss of party control and the unity of the bloc were the main reasons; pressure from the hardliners and the failure of talks explain the timing.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"Two kinds of reason have to be separated: those that made a Soviet intervention likely, which are about what the Prague Spring threatened, and those that made it happen in August 1968, which are about the course of events. The reasons of the first kind were the more important, because without them nothing would have been decided. The most important was the loss of party control, and next the unity of the bloc.

Moscow's essential interest was that every satellite remained under a communist party that held a monopoly of power. After Dubček replaced Novotný on 5 January 1968, that monopoly looked less secure each month. Censorship ended in practice from March; the Action Programme of 5 April promised freedom of speech, assembly and travel and the rehabilitation of the victims of the show trials; and on 27 June Ludvík Vaculík's 'Two Thousand Words' urged ordinary citizens to push the reform forward on their own, which suggested to Moscow that the initiative was passing from the party to the public. The Soviet leaders did not need Dubček to announce a multi-party system: they feared a process that the party was no longer controlling, and Hungary in 1956, where a reformer had been overtaken by events, was their model of what would follow.

The second reason was the cohesion of the Warsaw Pact and the strategic importance of Czechoslovakia. The country bordered West Germany, and Soviet generals regarded it as the Pact's western front. By the summer of 1968 Romania was pursuing an independent foreign policy and Albania had not attended Pact meetings since 1961, and Ceaușescu and Tito visited Prague to show support for Dubček. A reforming Czechoslovakia in alliance with them would have weakened the Pact's discipline. Dubček had said that Czechoslovakia would stay in the Pact, and Moscow did not claim that it would leave, but the Soviet generals' fear was of an erosion of the bloc, not of a sudden break.

Fear of contagion belongs to the same category. The Czechoslovak press was widely read, and the Polish students' demonstrations of March 1968 and the Ukrainian border made Moscow anxious that the example could spread to Poland and to the Soviet Union itself. This explains why Gomułka and Ulbricht, who ruled states that would have been the first to be affected, were the most urgent in demanding action at Dresden on 23 March and in the Warsaw Letter of 15 July.

The reasons of the second kind explain why the decision came in August. The five-power pressure and the hardliners' appeal, in a letter signed by Vasil Biľak and others that the Soviet government revealed only in the 1990s, gave Brezhnev both a political cover and a claim to have been invited. The failure of the talks at Čierna nad Tisou (29 July–1 August) and Bratislava (3 August) to produce a change of course in Prague convinced the Soviet leaders that Dubček would not or could not restore control. Historians such as Mark Kramer have shown that Brezhnev, who is often seen as the leader of the hawks, held back until the summer, and that the Politburo decided only around 16–18 August. The invasion was thus the result of a leadership that feared losing control more than it wanted to use force.

These reasons are connected. The loss of party control mattered because the Pact and the bloc depended on it; the pressure from the satellites mattered because Brezhnev needed their backing and because their fear of contagion matched his; and the failure of talks mattered because it showed that persuasion had failed. If the first had been absent, as it was in Poland in 1956 after Gomułka had taken control, there would have been no invasion, whatever the others did. That is why I judge that fear of losing the party's control was the main reason, with the unity of the bloc second, and the hardliners' pressure and the failed talks as the factors that fixed the date. Its confirmation is in the Brezhnev Doctrine: the justification offered after the invasion was that the defence of socialism in one state was the duty of all, and not a defence against a Western threat."

*Commentary.* The answer opens with a **criterion and a distinction** (what made intervention likely against what fixed the date), gives each reason precise evidence (5 January, 5 April, 27 June, 23 March, 15 July, 29 July–1 August, 3 August, 16–18 August), shows how the reasons connect and ends with a ranked judgement tested by a counterfactual (Poland 1956). Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13). Total about 27/30.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Evaluate the view that the building of the Berlin Wall in 1961 showed that Soviet control of East Germany was weak." [30]

A student wrote:

"On 13 August 1961 East Germany closed the border in Berlin and built the Berlin Wall. Many people had been leaving East Germany for the West, about 2.7 million between 1949 and 1961, and Ulbricht said that nobody intended to build a wall two months before. The Wall was made of concrete and had guards with guns, and 140 people died trying to cross it. Kennedy gave a speech in 1963 in which he said 'Ich bin ein Berliner'. The Wall stood until 1989 when it was knocked down. This shows that the Soviet Union was weak because it needed a wall."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are accurate, but the paragraph **tells the story of the Wall** and then ends with an assertion ("this shows that the Soviet Union was weak because it needed a wall"). It does not say what "weak" means, it gives no evidence for the other side (the Wall was built by Moscow's choice and at its timing, without a Western response), and details such as the 1963 speech and the fall of the Wall in 1989 are not used to make a point.

**The fix.** State a criterion, give both sides and weigh them. "The Wall showed weakness in one sense and strength in another, and the difference depends on what is meant by 'control'. It showed that the regime could not keep its people by consent: about 2.7 million had left between 1949 and 1961, and in the summer of 1961 the flow reached a thousand a day at times, so that closing the border was an admission that East Germany could not compete with West Berlin. But as an act of control the Wall succeeded: the Western powers, whose three essentials of 25 July did not include free movement between the sectors, did nothing, the emigration stopped, and Khrushchev ended his ultimatum without losing anything in Germany. The Wall therefore shows the weakness of the GDR's appeal and the strength of Soviet power to settle the question on its own terms." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement, and the paragraph states what would change the view.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ "The Berlin Wall was built because Soviet control of East Germany was weak." Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content: for: 2.7 million had left; a thousand a day in summer 1961; the economy was losing skilled workers; the Wall was an admission that the GDR could not keep its people. Against: the Wall was built to solve a specific problem after the ultimatum of 1958 failed; Soviet troops remained in the GDR and the Pact approved it; Moscow chose the time and the Western powers did not react; Ulbricht's own pressure on Moscow was a cause. A Level 5 answer separates the weakness of the GDR regime from the weakness of Soviet control, and judges that the Wall showed the first, not the second.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which living standards in the satellite states improved between 1953 and 1968.
H: Choose criteria: access to food and consumer goods, housing, services (health and education), and comparison with the West.
H: Remember that the states differed: Czechoslovakia and East Germany against Poland and Hungary.
H: Use the dates: the New Course of 1953, the end of rationing in East Germany (1958), the stagnation in Czechoslovakia (1962–63) and the Hungarian reforms of the 1960s.
A: Indicative content: improvement: full employment, free health care and education, subsidised food and housing, first television sets and cars, the end of rationing in East Germany in 1958, the "New Course" concessions of 1953, and Kádár's compromise in the 1960s. Limits: shortages and queues, a housing shortage, poor quality and a growing gap with West Germany and Austria; economic stagnation in Czechoslovakia in 1962–63; high norms in 1953 and 1956. A Level 5 answer weighs the improvement in absolute terms against the failure in relative terms and notes that discontent depended on comparison with the West, so that rising living standards did not buy loyalty.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ "Soviet control of the satellite states depended more on force than on the support of the satellite governments." Evaluate this view.
H: Set criteria for "control": what kept each state inside the bloc in normal times, and what restored control in a crisis?
H: Compare crises where force was used (East Germany 1953, Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968) with ones where it was not (Poland 1956).
H: Consider the role of the Pact, the Soviet troops stationed in the states and the nomenklatura.
A: Indicative content: for force: 1953, 1956 and 1968, the threat in Poland in October 1956, the Soviet troops stationed in the satellites and the Brezhnev Doctrine. For the governments: in normal times the party leaders in each state wanted to stay in power and relied on Moscow; Gomułka, Kádár and Husák worked the system; the nomenklatura gave a class an interest in it; the secret police and censorship were run by national officials. A Level 5 answer judges that in a crisis force mattered most, as the last resort, but that it was the satellite parties that provided the daily control and then re-established it after each crisis (Kádár in 1956, Husák in 1969), and Soviet force was needed only when the party failed.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the Prague Spring failed because of the mistakes of Dubček.
H: What would "success" have meant for Dubček: reform within the party and the Pact, or something beyond?
H: Compare what Dubček did with what Gomułka did in 1956, and what Nagy did.
H: Consider the other causes: Soviet fear of contagion, pressure from the hardliners and the lack of Western help.
A: Indicative content: for: Dubček could not control the press and the public mood; he refused to attend the Warsaw meeting; he did not satisfy Moscow after Čierna and Bratislava; he did not remove the hardliners; he did not prepare any resistance. Against: he stayed within the party's leading role and the Pact; Gomułka's success in 1956 depended on Moscow's trust, which Brezhnev did not have in Dubček; the key factors were the Soviet leaders' fears of losing party control and of contagion, the pressure of Ulbricht and Gomułka, and the unwillingness of the West to act. A Level 5 answer judges that Dubček's mistakes were less important than the fact that Moscow could not accept any reform that weakened the party's control, so that his actions determined the timing, but not the outcome.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ "Between 1953 and 1968 the Soviet Union's methods of control changed more than its aims." Evaluate this view.
H: Identify the aims first: what did Moscow always want from the satellites?
H: Then list the methods in 1953 (terror, show trials, tanks), 1956 (compromise and tanks), 1961 (a wall), 1968 (tanks and a doctrine).
A: Aims were constant: loyal communist governments under the party's monopoly, the military position in Europe and the Pact, and security from West Germany. Methods changed: from mass terror and show trials under Stalin to the replacement of discredited leaders (Rákosi, Novotný's fall in 1968), concessions to national feeling (Gomułka, Kádár's reforms), the Warsaw Pact's institutions, and a physical barrier (the Wall). But the use of troops was constant (1953, 1956, 1968). A* judgement: the aims did not change, and the methods changed in style (the terror ended) but not in their final reliance on force, so the view is true of the early methods but overstates change in the last resort.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the significance of Khrushchev's secret speech of February 1956 for Soviet control of eastern Europe.
H: Think about the short term (1956) and the long term (to 1968).
H: Compare its effect in Poland and Hungary with its effect in East Germany and Czechoslovakia.
A: Significance: it discredited Stalinist leaders (Bierut's death, Rákosi's removal), encouraged debate (the Petőfi Circle, Polish writers), raised hopes of reform and made Poznań and the Polish October and the Hungarian uprising possible; it helped to create the movements that Moscow then had to suppress. Limits: it did not cause the crises by itself (economic grievances, national feeling and leadership struggles); in East Germany and Czechoslovakia the regimes took little notice; Khrushchev's aim was a loyal reform, and Moscow quickly returned to control. A* judgement: the speech was a necessary condition of the 1956 crises (they could not have taken their form without it) but not a sufficient one, and it taught Moscow how much de-Stalinisation its empire could stand.
:::

:::recap
- **Pattern**: after 1945 the Red Army and Moscow-backed communist parties built one-party satellite states on the Soviet model; Comecon (1949) tied their economies together.
- **Warsaw Pact** (14 May 1955): eight states, a Joint Command in Moscow, twenty years; reasons: West Germany in NATO (9 May 1955), a legal basis for Soviet troops after the Austrian State Treaty (15 May 1955), control of the satellites' armies, a bargaining counter.
- **Government**: one party and the *nomenklatura*; Czechoslovakia (Gottwald, Novotný), Poland (Bierut, Gomułka), East Germany (Ulbricht), Hungary (Rákosi, Nagy, Kádár).
- **Control**: censorship (partly offset by Western radio), secret police (Stasi 1950, ÁVH, UB/SB, StB), restricted churches (Wyszyński 1953–56, Mindszenty 1948–), closed borders; living standards low under Stalin and slowly rising after 1953.
- **1953**: Stalin dies (5 March); East German rising on 16–17 June, ended by Soviet tanks, at least 55 dead.
- **1956**: secret speech (25 February); Poznań (28 June); Polish October (Gomułka 21 October) accepted by Moscow; Hungarian uprising 23 October–4 November, Nagy's withdrawal from the Pact (1 November), Operation Whirlwind (4 November), about 2,500–3,000 Hungarians and about 700 Soviet soldiers killed, about 200,000 refugees, Nagy hanged 16 June 1958.
- **Berlin**: about 2.7 million left East Germany 1949–61; ultimatum 27 November 1958; Wall 13 August 1961.
- **Prague Spring**: Dubček 5 January 1968, Action Programme 5 April, "Two Thousand Words" 27 June, Čierna 29 July–1 August, invasion 20–21 August (USSR, Poland, Bulgaria, Hungary), 137 Czechs and Slovaks killed, Moscow Protocol 26 August, Husák 17 April 1969.
- **Soviet reactions**: political change, repression, military action; Brezhnev Doctrine (Pravda 26 September, Warsaw 12 November 1968).
- **Judgement**: control rested on the party monopoly, the Soviet army and the Pact, and the will to use force, never on consent.
:::
