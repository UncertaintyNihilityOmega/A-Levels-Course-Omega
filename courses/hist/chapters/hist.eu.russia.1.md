## Russia in 1881: an empire and its people

Russia was the largest state in the world. The 1897 census, the first and only one under the Tsars, counted **125.6 million people**. About 44 per cent were Great Russians; the rest were Ukrainians, Poles, Belarusians, Jews, Finns, Baltic peoples, Tatars, Georgians, Armenians and dozens of others. That diversity matters for this chapter: a ruler who defines the empire as Russian and Orthodox is, by that act, creating minorities.

Three more facts frame everything that follows.
- **A peasant country.** About four in five people were peasants. Only **13.4 per cent** lived in towns (16.8 million people in 1897) and only about **21 per cent** could read. The great cities were still few: St Petersburg had about 1.26 million people and Moscow about 1.04 million in 1897.
- **A late-developing economy.** Serfdom had been abolished only in 1861. Russia had fewer railways, less coal and less iron per head than Britain, Germany or France, and her armies and her great-power position depended on an economy that lagged behind her rivals.
- **A state with a small government.** Russia was ruled by a few thousand officials over a huge area. The state could not be everywhere, so it relied on the nobility, the Church, the army and the police to hold the empire together.

:::def Autocracy
A system of government in which the monarch holds unlimited power. In Russia the Tsar was called *samoderzhets* ("self-ruler"). The Fundamental Laws of the empire described the emperor as an autocratic and unlimited monarch whose authority God himself commanded his subjects to obey, and no elected body limited his power.
:::

## The assassination and Alexander III's answer

### Alexander II and the "Tsar Liberator"

Alexander II (Tsar from 1855) had earned the name "Tsar Liberator". After defeat in the Crimean War (1853–56) he decided Russia must modernise or fall behind. He freed the serfs in 1861; created elected local councils, the **zemstvos**, in 1864; reformed the courts in 1864 so that trials were open and juries could sit; and introduced universal conscription in 1874. These **Great Reforms** were the largest change in Russia since Peter the Great. But they raised hopes they did not satisfy. The peasants paid heavy "redemption payments" for their freedom and often had too little land; the educated young wanted a voice in government; and the Tsar himself would not share his power.

Some young radicals, the **Populists** (*narodniki*), believed that the peasants, organised in their village communes, could build a socialist Russia without capitalism. In the "going to the people" of 1874 hundreds of students went into the villages to preach this; most peasants distrusted them and the police arrested many. A few turned to terror. In 1879 the **People's Will** (*Narodnaya Volya*) was formed with one aim: to kill the Tsar and so shake the state. After several attempts, on **1 March 1881** (13 March New Style) a bomb thrown by Ignaty Grinevitsky killed Alexander II on a St Petersburg embankment, after an earlier bomb had failed to stop his carriage. The Tsar died within hours. He was 62.

The story has an irony that every student should know. On that very morning Alexander II had, according to his minister Mikhail Loris-Melikov, agreed in principle to a cautious plan to bring elected representatives into a consultative commission. The People's Will killed the one Tsar who was moving, slowly, towards sharing power. Five of the conspirators, including Sofia Perovskaya, the first woman in Russia to be executed for a political crime, were hanged on 3 April 1881.

:::def People's Will
(*Narodnaya Volya*) A small secret revolutionary group, formed in 1879 from the Populist movement, that used terror to try to destroy the autocracy. It killed Alexander II on 1 March 1881 and was then broken up by the police.
:::

### The Manifesto of 29 April 1881

The new Tsar, **Alexander III** (aged 36), was a huge man, conservative, devout and suspicious of liberals. He had been shocked by his father's death and was advised by his old tutor, **Konstantin Pobedonostsev**, the Over-Procurator of the Holy Synod, who believed that parliaments and elections were lies. After weeks of debate in the Council of Ministers, in which Loris-Melikov and other ministers who favoured limited reform lost, the Tsar issued the Manifesto of 29 April 1881, drafted with Pobedonostsev's help. It declared that he would rule with "faith in the strength and truth of the autocratic power", which he was called to "affirm and safeguard" against any encroachment (an adapted wording; see the source in the worked example). Loris-Melikov and the other reformist ministers resigned.

This is the turning point of the period. After 1881 the regime chose to defend the autocracy in its existing form rather than adapt it. The rest of the chapter explains what that meant in practice and what it cost.

## How Russia was ruled in the period 1881–94

### The Tsar and the structure of autocracy

The Tsar was supreme. He made the law, appointed and dismissed every minister, commanded the army and was head of the Orthodox Church. Nobody could vote him out or force him to consult them. His personality therefore mattered enormously: Alexander III was hard-working, honest in his private life and determined, though he left much of the detail to ministers.

### The central institutions

- **The State Council.** Set up in 1810, it discussed draft laws, but its members were all appointed by the Tsar and it could only advise. The Tsar could overrule the majority and sometimes did.
- **The Committee of Ministers and the ministries.** There was no prime minister or cabinet. Each minister answered to the Tsar alone, so ministries often competed or contradicted each other. The Committee of Ministers met to discuss business, but it was not a government in the western sense. The most powerful ministries were **Interior** (the police, local government and the censorship), **Finance** (taxes, tariffs, railways and foreign loans), **War** and **Education**.
- **The Senate.** The highest court, which also checked that laws were properly issued. It was staffed by appointees and had no power to refuse the Tsar.
- **The Imperial Chancellery and the police.** The old political police (the Third Section) had been replaced in 1880 by a Department of Police inside the Interior Ministry. In 1881 it set up local branches called *okhrannye otdeleniya* (security sections), the **Okhrana**, in St Petersburg, Moscow and Warsaw. They used informers and agents, watched suspects, opened letters and arrested people.

:::def Okhrana
The security sections of the tsarist political police, first set up in 1881 in the Department of Police of the Ministry of the Interior. They watched revolutionaries and suspected opponents with informers, agents and the opening of letters.
:::

### The army

Russia had the largest army in Europe. Conscription had been made universal in 1874, though many were exempt, and the term of service was shorter than the old 25 years. The officers came mostly from the nobility and were loyal to the Tsar. The army protected the empire against foreign enemies, but it also stood behind the police. Under the 1881 Statute governors could call on troops to deal with disorder, and in 1905 the army's loyalty would decide whether the regime survived. Russia's relative economic weakness showed in the army's poor equipment, which also explains why the government worried about industrialisation.

### The Church

The Orthodox Church was a department of state. Since Peter the Great it had been governed by the **Holy Synod**, whose lay head was the **Over-Procurator**, a government official who sat beside the Tsar. From 1880 to 1905 he was Pobedonostsev. About 70 per cent of the people were Orthodox. The Church ran many village schools, taught obedience to the Tsar as a religious duty and blessed the regime in its ceremonies. In return the state protected Orthodoxy and persecuted other faiths.

The ruling slogan of the age was **"Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality"**. It had been coined in 1833 by an earlier minister, Count Uvarov, and Alexander III and Pobedonostsev took it as their programme: loyalty to the Church, to the unlimited Tsar and to the Russian nation.

### Local government

Russia had 50 provinces in European Russia, each ruled by a **governor** appointed by the Tsar and answerable to the Interior Ministry. The governor commanded the police and could enforce emergency rules. Below the governors there were the **zemstvos**, elected councils at province and district level created in 1864. They ran schools, hospitals, roads and famine relief, but they were dominated by nobles and could not touch national politics. Peasant villages were run by the **commune** (*mir* or *obshchina*), an assembly of heads of household that shared out land, tax payments and often kept the land in common, redistributing strips among families from time to time.

:::key What kind of autocracy was it?
1. **Unlimited in law**: the Tsar had no parliament, constitution or independent judiciary to check him.
2. **Limited in practice**: a small bureaucracy could not govern 125 million people; the state relied on the nobility, the police, the army and the Church, and on the passive habits of the peasants.
3. **Fragmented**: ministers reported separately and competed. Without a prime minister the Tsar had to coordinate everything himself.
4. **Dependent on the Tsar's character**: a strong Tsar such as Alexander III held it together; a weak one would expose the lack of any other centre of decision.
:::

## Reform and repression: Alexander III's policies

### The aims

Alexander III's policy is often called **counter-reform**: undoing or limiting the liberal reforms of his father. It had two aims. The first was to make the autocracy safe, by tightening control of the police, the press, the universities and local government. The second was to unify the empire around Russian and Orthodox values. The policies came from a small group around the Tsar: Pobedonostsev; **Dmitry Tolstoy**, Minister of the Interior from 1882 to 1889 (a strong supporter of the nobility's role in the countryside); **Ivan Delyanov**, Minister of Education from 1882; and **Mikhail Katkov**, the conservative newspaper editor in Moscow, whose writing influenced them.

### Repression: the police and the law

- **The State Order Statute (14 August 1881).** The "Statute on Measures for the Protection of State Order and Public Tranquillity" was introduced as a temporary measure. It allowed governors to declare areas in a state of "reinforced" or "extraordinary" protection. They could then arrest suspects, exile them without trial for up to five years, close schools, factories and newspapers, ban meetings and send political cases to military courts. It was renewed every three years until 1917, so the "temporary" law lasted for the rest of the Tsarist era.
- **The press.** Provisional Press Rules of 1882 allowed the Interior Ministry to close newspapers without a trial and to forbid editors to work. Censorship also covered books and plays.
- **Exile and prison.** Thousands of political suspects were sent to remote provinces or Siberia. The "exile" system also helped the revolutionaries: exiles met each other, wrote and read, and Marxist ideas (those of Karl Marx, who taught that workers would overthrow their employers; see "The growth of Marxism" below) spread.
- **The Okhrana** (above) infiltrated student groups and revolutionary circles. After the arrests of 1881–82, the People's Will was broken as a mass organisation.

### Counter-reforms in education and the countryside

- **Universities.** The University Statute of 23 August 1884 ended the autonomy granted in 1863. Rectors and deans were now appointed by the ministry, and student societies were restricted. In 1887 an education circular (nicknamed the "cooks' children" circular after the groups it named) told gymnasium directors (heads of secondary schools) to discourage the children of servants, coachmen, cooks and small shopkeepers from entering secondary schools, to protect the "upper" classes from social mixing. Jewish students faced quotas from 1887: 10 per cent inside the Pale, 5 per cent outside it and 3 per cent in Moscow and St Petersburg.
- **Land captains (12 July 1889).** The government created **land captains** (*zemskie nachalniki*), local nobles appointed by the governor and responsible for supervising the peasants. They had both administrative and judicial power: they could overrule the peasant assembly, fine and have peasants arrested for short periods. They replaced the justices of the peace elected under Alexander II. The measure was designed to put the nobility, whose estates were in decline, back in control of the villages.
- **Zemstvos (12 June 1890).** A new statute increased noble representation in the zemstvos and gave governors the right to confirm their members and officers; peasants' representatives were now chosen by the governor from lists nominated by village assemblies. A 1892 law restricted town councils in a similar way. Liberals in the zemstvos, who had hoped to use them as a base for political reform, were silenced.

:::def Counter-reform
The policy of Alexander III's government (1881–94) of limiting or reversing the reforms of Alexander II, strengthening the police, nobility, Church and central control in place of the self-government and legal freedoms that his father had begun.
:::

### Russification

The government wanted a single Russian empire, which meant pressing the non-Russian peoples towards the Russian language, religion and institutions. This policy was called **Russification**.

:::def Russification
The policy of imposing Russian language, institutions and Orthodox religion on the non-Russian peoples of the empire, especially after 1881, in order to unify the empire and reduce the risk of national movements.
:::

- **Poles.** After the failed Polish rising of 1863 the Poles were already closely watched. In the 1880s Russian was pressed further into the schools, courts and government offices of Poland.
- **Baltic provinces.** The German-speaking nobility of Estonia, Livonia and Courland lost power: Russian became the language of administration and the courts in the 1880s, and the German university at Dorpat became a Russian one (renamed Yuryev in 1893).
- **Finland.** The grand duchy kept its own laws, but its autonomy began to be eroded: its postal service was brought under Russian control in 1890. The attack on Finnish autonomy became much more serious under Nicholas II.
- **Ukrainians and Belarusians.** The government did not recognise a separate Ukrainian nation, treating the Ukrainians as "Little Russians", and restrictions on printing and teaching in Ukrainian stayed in force.
- **Muslims and other faiths.** Catholics, Lutherans, Muslims and the Old Believers (who had split from the Church in the seventeenth century) were pressed to conform to Orthodoxy or faced discrimination in office-holding, education and church building.

### The Jews: pogroms and the May Laws

Russia contained the largest Jewish population in the world, most of it confined to the **Pale of Settlement**, a western region in which the law required Jews to live. After the assassination, rumours blamed Jews for the murder, and in **April 1881** a wave of pogroms (violent attacks on Jewish communities) began in the south-west, at Elizavetgrad, Kiev and Odessa and in many smaller towns and villages, continuing into 1882. The authorities were slow to protect the victims, and local officials sometimes stood aside. The government's response was to restrict the Jews, not their attackers. The **Temporary Rules of 3 May 1882**, the "May Laws", forbade Jews, inside the Pale, to settle anew outside towns and boroughs; stopped them acquiring or renting land outside towns; and forbade them to trade on Sundays and on the chief Christian holidays. "Temporary" again became permanent: the rules were not repealed until 1917. In 1887 came the school quotas, and in 1891–92 thousands of Jews were expelled from Moscow. Roughly two million Jews left the empire between 1881 and 1914, mostly for the United States.

:::warn Do not call every policy "Russification" or every violent act "government policy"
The pogroms of 1881–82 were not ordered by the central government, which was slow to stop them. The May Laws were government policy. Use precise wording: "the pogroms, which the authorities failed to stop, were followed by laws that restricted Jews further". Also separate Russification (making non-Russians more Russian) from antisemitism and from religious persecution, which overlapped but were different policies.
:::

### Reform and repression: how to assess it

Not everything Alexander III did was repressive. The same reign saw reform of a kind: the abolition of the poll tax, the Peasant Land Bank, early factory laws and a drive to industrialise (below). But these measures were meant to strengthen the state and the peasants' loyalty, not to share power. The distinction is useful in an essay: **social and economic reform without political reform**.

:::key Why the regime chose repression after 1881
1. **Fear**: the assassination and the later plot of 1887 convinced the Tsar and his advisers that concessions invited terror.
2. **Ideology**: Pobedonostsev and Katkov argued that autocracy, the Church and the Russian people were the empire's only sound foundation, and that liberal reforms had caused disorder.
3. **Weakness of the state**: with a small bureaucracy, a huge area and a poor peasantry, the regime used emergency powers and the police as its tools.
4. **Diversity of the empire**: fear that national movements could break up the state led to Russification.
:::

## The development of the Russian economy, 1881–94

### The problem and the aim

By 1881 the Russian state faced a plain fact: to remain a great power it needed railways, factories, steel and a stable currency, and all of these cost money. The war of 1877–78 against Turkey had been paid for by borrowing and printing paper money, and the budget was in deficit. Three finance ministers in turn tried to solve the problem; each used the state to push the economy forward from above.

### Bunge, 1881–86

**Nikolai Bunge** was an economist and former professor, Finance Minister from 1881 to 1886. He believed that Russia's prosperity depended on relieving the peasantry, who were most of the taxpayers and consumers. His policies:
- **Redemption payments** (the annual payments the peasants made for the land they received in 1861) were reduced from 1881 and made compulsory from 1883, so every peasant household was free of the old obligations to its lord.
- **The poll tax**, which fell only on the lower classes, was cut and then abolished in 1886 (the decree was passed in 1883). The government made up part of the loss with new taxes on inheritances and on income from capital.
- **The Peasant Land Bank (1882, opened 1883)** gave peasants loans to buy land from the nobility on easier terms, and a **Noble Land Bank** (1885) helped the gentry.
- **Factory laws.** An 1882 law forbade the employment of children under twelve in factories, limited the hours of those aged twelve to fifteen, and created a **factory inspectorate**. In 1885 night work by women and children in textile mills was banned. A law of 1886, after the Morozov strike, regulated fines and required written wage books.
- **Tariffs** were raised gradually to protect new industries.

Bunge's policies were relatively gentle, but his tax cuts opened a budget deficit and his critics, including Katkov, said he was soft on the peasants and the workers. In 1886 he lost office.

### Vyshnegradsky, 1887–92

**Ivan Vyshnegradsky**, an engineer and a former professor of mechanics who had also been a successful businessman, was Finance Minister from January 1887 to August 1892. His method was almost the opposite: to balance the budget by squeezing the people.
- **Higher indirect taxes** on goods that everyone used, such as sugar, kerosene and vodka, which fell hard on the poor.
- **High tariffs**, in 1887 and especially in 1891, to protect Russian industry against foreign competition.
- **Grain exports.** The state needed gold and a favourable balance of trade to support the rouble and to repay foreign debts, and Russia's main export was grain. The government encouraged peasants to sell more, partly by taxing them. A saying attributed to Vyshnegradsky summed up the policy: "We may not eat enough, but we will export" (the exact wording is reported differently in different accounts). It was a policy that left very little in reserve when the harvest failed.
- **Foreign loans.** From 1888 French banks lent heavily to Russia, which was starting to approach France in a political alliance (agreed 1891–94). This opened the Russian economy to foreign capital.
By 1889 the budget was balanced and the state had gold reserves. It was an achievement paid for by the peasants.

### Witte begins: railways and the Trans-Siberian

**Sergei Witte**, who had managed private railways, became Director of Railway Affairs in the Finance Ministry in 1889, Minister of Communications in February 1892 and Finance Minister in August 1892. Under Alexander III the state was already building and buying railways, which it saw as vital for moving troops and grain. In 1891 work began on the **Trans-Siberian Railway** to link European Russia with the Pacific, a state project of enormous scale. Witte's main policies, including the gold standard of 1897, belong to the next chapter. What matters here is that by 1894 the state had taken the lead in industrialisation.

The two charts show the long-run change. Coal output grew almost fivefold between 1880 and 1900 (from about 3.3 to about 15.9 million tons), and the length of railway line more than doubled (from about 22,900 to about 53,200 kilometres). Each chart shows only three years, twenty years apart, so it cannot show when within those twenty years the growth came. Other series show that most of the spectacular growth came in the 1890s, under Witte; in the 1880s and early 1890s growth was real but modest, and the economy was still overwhelmingly agricultural. Published figures for pig iron differ widely from one source to another, so this chapter does not chart it. Growth was concentrated in a few regions: the Moscow and Ivanovo textile district, St Petersburg, the Polish towns such as Łódź, the coal and iron of the Donets basin and the Urals, and the oil of Baku.

### Economic problems: taxation and the peasants

Growth came at the peasants' expense. Peasants paid redemption payments until 1907, direct taxes, local rates and the indirect taxes on tea, sugar, kerosene and vodka. They also paid the landowners and the Church. Their holdings were too small to feed growing families: the population was growing quickly and the commune redistributed land among more mouths. Many peasants fell into arrears on taxes. Agricultural techniques were poor: strips were scattered, ploughs were often wooden, and yields were low compared with western Europe. A law of 1893 made it harder to leave the commune or to sell or mortgage allotment land, which locked the peasants into the system that the government still saw as the guarantor of order.

### The famine of 1891–92

The summer of 1891 brought drought and an early frost to the Volga and the black-earth provinces. The harvest failed over a huge region. There was no reserve; the peasants had been taxed and had already sold their grain. The government did not ban grain exports until mid-August 1891, giving merchants time to ship stocks abroad, and then the ban came too late to reassure the markets. Merchants held back grain, as the writer Leo Tolstoy reported from the district where he organised relief.

- **Scale.** The famine began along the Volga and spread through many provinces. The government gave aid to 27 provinces, totalling about 172 million roubles.
- **Response.** The authorities at first played down the disaster (the press was told to say "poor harvest", not "famine"). On 17 November 1891 the government appealed for voluntary relief, and the future Nicholas II chaired a relief committee. The zemstvos and private volunteers, among them Tolstoy, who set up soup kitchens in Ryazan province, did much of the work. Foreign donations also arrived.
- **Deaths.** Estimates of deaths from famine-related disease start at about 375,000 and are sometimes put as high as half a million. Some historians argue that most of the extra deaths in 1892 came from the cholera epidemic, which killed some 300,000 people in Russia that year. Historians therefore give a range, and you should too.
- **Consequences.** The famine showed the weakness of Vyshnegradsky's policy: he resigned in 1892, ill. It also pushed educated Russians to criticise the government: many believed that the state had failed its people while society, through the zemstvos and volunteers, had saved them. Historians often trace a revival of opposition, including Marxism and liberalism, to this anger, though some argue the effect has been exaggerated.

:::key How successful was the economy by 1894?
1. **Successes**: balanced budget and gold reserves; high tariffs protected young industries; railways, coal and textile output grew; Bunge's land bank and abolition of the poll tax gave some relief; foreign capital began to flow in.
2. **Failures**: the peasants' poverty and arrears; reliance on grain exports and indirect taxes; no reserve against bad harvests; low yields; the famine and cholera of 1891–92.
3. **Judgement**: by 1894 the state had begun a successful drive to build industry, but it rested on a poor and vulnerable countryside, which is why the next regime faced both a booming industry and a restless peasantry.
:::

## Social change, 1881–94

### The nobility

The nobility was the first of the legal **estates** (*sosloviia*) and supplied the officers, officials and governors. But it was in economic decline: many nobles had sold or mortgaged their land since emancipation and moved into state service or the professions. The government's counter-reforms (land captains, the Noble Land Bank, noble control of the zemstvos) were meant to rescue the nobility as the dependable local agents of the state.

### The peasants

About four in five Russians were peasants. They were legally free but remained a separate estate, with their own courts and a dependence on the commune. Their lives were hard: poverty, land hunger, high taxes, periodic famine and low literacy. Peasant life created a deep gulf between the villages and the educated classes in the towns. The peasantry's anger focused on landlords, tax collectors and rent, but there was no national peasant movement in this period; protests were local, against a particular landlord or tax.

### Urban growth and the working class

Towns grew as peasants moved to work in factories, mines and railways. By 1897 the urban share was 13.4 per cent, with St Petersburg and Moscow the two giants. Most workers were still peasants by origin and kept ties to their villages; they often returned home for the harvest. Conditions were poor: long hours (11 or 12 a day in many mills), low pay, fines, overcrowded barracks and insecurity. Official statistics suggest that the factories under the inspectorate employed about 1.5 million workers in the 1890s. They were few compared with the peasantry, but they were concentrated in big mills in a few cities, close to the centres of government, and they were a new kind of social force.

### The middle class

Russia's **middle class** (the bourgeoisie) was small. It included merchants and manufacturers, such as the Morozov, Ryabushinsky and Tretyakov families of Moscow, professionals (doctors, lawyers, engineers, teachers), and the clerks and technicians of banks and railways. Many merchants were conservative: they depended on the state for contracts and protection, and some were Old Believers. In the zemstvos, a "third element" of doctors, teachers and statisticians (employed by the zemstvos, besides the nobles and peasants who were their members) worked in the villages and developed a liberal outlook. The **intelligentsia** (educated people, such as writers and students, who were critical of the regime) was a larger group than the middle class and more radical. Only about one person in five could read, so the educated were a small minority.

:::key Social change by 1894
1. A **small middle class** and a **growing working class**, but still a country of peasants: social change was real but its scale was small.
2. A **declining nobility** whom the regime tried to prop up.
3. **Urban growth** concentrated in a few cities and industrial regions.
4. The new groups (workers, professionals, intelligentsia) had no way of taking part in politics: that is why they mattered for opposition.
:::

## The role of opposition, 1881–94

### How strong was opposition?

At first sight opposition was weak. The People's Will was smashed; the press was censored; the universities were controlled; the countryside was quiet. But the regime did not feel safe, and the opposition was growing in ways that would matter later.

### Terrorism after 1881

The terrorists did not disappear. In March 1887 a small group of students, the "Terrorist Faction of the People's Will", tried to kill Alexander III on the sixth anniversary of his father's death. The police arrested them before they could act. Five were hanged on 8 May 1887, including **Alexander Ulyanov**, a gifted young scientist and the elder brother of **Vladimir Ulyanov**, who was 17 and later called himself Lenin. Many historians stress this execution as one of the events that shaped Lenin's hatred of the regime, though they warn against giving it too much weight: Lenin was drawn to Marxism a year or two later, and he rejected terrorism as a method.

### Peasants

There was no organised peasant movement. Peasants protested against rents, taxes and landlords, refused to pay arrears or attacked tax collectors, and in 1892 some "cholera riots" broke out when villagers blamed doctors and officials for the epidemic. But these were scattered protests, not a revolution. The authorities, with their land captains and troops, usually put them down. The peasants were more likely to hope for justice from the Tsar than to attack him, a belief which the events of 1905 would shake: in the revolution of that year (see [Russia (2)](#hist.eu.russia.2)), troops fired on a peaceful march of workers who came to petition the Tsar ("Bloody Sunday").

### Urban workers

Strikes were illegal, but strikes happened. The famous one was at the **Morozov textile mill** at Orekhovo-Zuyevo near Moscow in **January 1885**, where about 8,000 workers struck against fines and wage cuts; the troops were called in and many were arrested. The strike was a shock to the government, which responded in 1886 with the law on fines and wage-books (the law protected the workers a little, but strikes remained illegal). Strikes remained small in these years: according to official statistics, there were about 30,000 to 60,000 strikers a year in 1895–1900, a small number out of a total of about 1.5 million factory workers. Workers' protest was only starting.

### The growth of Marxism

Marxism arrived in Russia as a challenge to Populism. **Karl Marx**'s *Capital* was published in Russian as early as 1872 (the censors let it through because they thought it too dull and technical for anyone to be dangerous). In 1883, in Geneva, a group of exiled former Populists led by **Georgy Plekhanov**, with Pavel Axelrod and Vera Zasulich, founded the **Emancipation of Labour** group, the first Russian Marxist organisation. They argued that Russia was already becoming capitalist, that the peasant commune would not save it, and that the revolutionary class would be the **industrial working class**, not the peasants. Their books and pamphlets were smuggled into Russia. Plekhanov's *Our Differences* (1885) set out the argument against the Populists.

In Russia itself the first Marxist study circles formed in St Petersburg, Moscow and Kazan in the late 1880s and 1890s. The young Lenin joined a circle in Samara in 1889 and came to St Petersburg in 1893, where he made contact with workers and Marxist intellectuals. Marxism was still the faith of a few hundred people at the end of Alexander III's reign. But it gave revolutionaries a new theory (the growing working class), and it matched the facts as industrialisation spread.

:::def Marxism
The set of ideas developed by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels that explains history as a struggle between classes. It predicted that capitalism would create an industrial working class (the proletariat) that would overthrow the middle class (the bourgeoisie) and build socialism. In Russia it appealed to revolutionaries who saw the factories growing.
:::

:::def Populism
A Russian movement of the 1860s–80s whose followers (*narodniki*) believed that Russia could reach socialism through the peasant commune, without going through capitalism. Some Populists turned to terrorism, including the People's Will.
:::

### Liberals and the educated

Liberals in the zemstvos and universities wanted a constitution, but they were few and cautious, and the counter-reforms silenced them. They did not form a party until after 1900. Writers such as Tolstoy and Chekhov criticised the state in indirect ways. The famine increased their anger. It showed that the educated could organise relief while the government could not.

### National opposition

Russification pushed non-Russians into resistance. Polish, Finnish, Georgian and Jewish groups kept their own culture alive, mostly in secret. They did not yet threaten the state, but they gave later revolutionaries an audience.

:::key Opposition to 1894: weak but growing
1. **Terrorism** was defeated by the police but remained a model for the Socialist Revolutionaries (the peasant-based revolutionary party founded in 1901; see [Russia (2)](#hist.eu.russia.2)) after 1901.
2. **Peasants** were angry but disorganised and loyal to the Tsar in principle.
3. **Workers** had struck at Morozov in 1885 and were few, but they were the future.
4. **Marxism** was tiny but gave a plan for the future.
5. **Liberals** were silenced but not satisfied.
Most of the evidence says: no immediate danger in 1894; much to come after 1900.
:::

## Why was the regime under pressure? Weighing the challenges

An essay on this key question asks you to rank the challenges and judge how serious they were. Sort them first.

| Type | Challenge | How serious? |
|---|---|---|
| Political | Pressure for a voice in government (liberals, zemstvos) | Low in 1894: silenced by counter-reforms |
| Revolutionary | The People's Will; later Marxists | Terror defeated; Marxism tiny |
| Economic | Poverty of the peasantry, famine, dependence on grain exports and foreign loans | Serious: a long-term weakness |
| Social | A new working class and middle class with no political rights | Growing: the long-term danger |
| National | Russification and the Jews | Created resentment; later helped opposition |
| Structural | An autocracy that relied on one man and a small bureaucracy | The deep weakness |

:::key A judgement you can defend
In 1881–94 the regime was **not in immediate danger**: it had the army, the police, the Church and the peasants' passivity. The challenges that mattered were **long-term**: a poor countryside, a state that modernised the economy without sharing power, and the growth of the very classes (workers, professionals) that the political system excluded. So the best answer to "how serious were the challenges?" is: **limited in the short term, deep in the long term**. The regime met the threat of 1881 with success but stored up the problems of 1905.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **Hugh Seton-Watson**, *The Russian Empire 1801–1917* (1967), a standard narrative history, shows the contrast between political reaction and economic change under Alexander III.
- **Richard Pipes**, *Russia under the Old Regime* (1974), argues that the Russian state was "patrimonial": the Tsar treated the country as his property, and the state dominated a weak society. On this view the police and the bureaucracy gave the regime great strength.
- **Orlando Figes**, *A People's Tragedy* (1996), stresses the gap between the autocracy and a changing society, and the way the 1891–92 famine alienated educated Russians.
- **Geoffrey Hosking**, *Russia: People and Empire* (1997), argues that the Russian state built an empire but failed to build a nation, so that the pressure of ruling many peoples weakened it.
Use this at AS to show that the same facts can be weighed differently, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Alexander III followed a policy of repression after 1881.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Alexander II had been killed by the People's Will in 1881. Alexander III did not like reform. He had Pobedonostsev as his adviser and he issued the Manifesto and the State Order Statute. He also used the Okhrana. So there was a lot of repression after 1881."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it is a list of what happened. The word "so" claims a link without explaining it. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The first reason was fear after the assassination. The People's Will had killed Alexander II on 1 March 1881 and had tried again, and a further plot followed in 1887, so the new Tsar and his advisers believed that revolutionaries were still dangerous and that a strong police response was needed. This led to the State Order Statute of 14 August 1881, which let governors arrest and exile suspects without trial. A second reason was the influence of Pobedonostsev. As the Tsar's old tutor and Over-Procurator of the Holy Synod, he believed that liberal ideas such as parliament and the free press were false and dangerous, and he helped to draft the Manifesto of 29 April 1881 that proclaimed the autocracy."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add a third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was the weakness of the state itself. Russia had 125 million people and only a small bureaucracy, so officials could not control the empire by ordinary law. Emergency powers, such as the State Order Statute, gave governors a way to act at once, and the Okhrana and the land captains of 1889 put reliable agents in the localities. The empire's many peoples added to the problem: fear that national movements might break the empire apart led to Russification and to restrictions on Poles, Finns and Jews.

These factors were linked. The assassination supplied the fear; Pobedonostsev supplied the ideas that made repression seem right rather than merely necessary; and the state's weakness made emergency powers the practical method. The decisive factor was Pobedonostsev's influence, because the fear of 1881 alone could have been met by a mix of reform and police action, as Loris-Melikov had planned. The Tsar chose repression because his adviser convinced him that the autocracy could survive only if it refused to share power. The assassination was the trigger, but the ideology set the direction of policy for thirteen years."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the assassination as trigger, the ideology as direction, weakness as method), and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

In the midst of Our great sorrow, the voice of God commands Us to stand courageously at the helm of government, with faith in the strength and truth of the autocratic power, which We are called to affirm and safeguard for the good of the people from any encroachment upon it.

*Adapted from the Manifesto of Tsar Alexander III, 29 April 1881, issued after the assassination of his father. The wording is adapted from English translations.*

**Source B**

Among the falsest of political principles is the principle of the sovereignty of the people. Speechmaking serves for the glory of orators, for the increase of their popularity, and the making of their careers.

*Adapted from Konstantin Pobedonostsev, Reflections of a Russian Statesman, a book of essays first published in Russian in 1896 (English translation by R. C. Long, 1898). Pobedonostsev was the Over-Procurator of the Holy Synod.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the ideas on which the Tsarist regime based its rule.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says the Tsar has faith in the autocratic power. Source B says the sovereignty of the people is false. So they are similar because both support the Tsar. They are both from the government so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One similarity, supported, but the comparison is thin: no difference, and "biased" is an assertion without context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share a central idea: that power should stay with the ruler and not pass to the people. Source A has the Tsar commit himself to 'the autocratic power' and to guard it from 'any encroachment', and Source B calls the principle of 'the sovereignty of the people' one of the 'falsest' in politics. Both therefore reject any limit on the Tsar by an elected body, and both treat this as a matter of truth, not just convenience: A speaks of 'faith' and 'truth', B of what is 'false'.

They differ in how they justify this. Source A rests the autocracy on God ('the voice of God commands Us') and on duty ('for the good of the people'), so it is an appeal to religion and loyalty. Source B is an argument against an alternative: it says that popular sovereignty is a falsehood and that parliaments serve the vanity of speakers. A is a statement of intention by a ruler; B is a reasoned attack on democracy by an adviser.

The context and provenance explain both the agreement and the difference. Source A was issued in April 1881, about two months after the assassination, in a crisis. The Tsar was choosing between the limited reform plan of Loris-Melikov and the autocracy; the Manifesto was a public announcement to all his subjects that he had chosen the second, and it was drafted with Pobedonostsev's help. That explains the religious and emotional language, for it was meant to reassure supporters and warn opponents. Source B was published in 1896, after fifteen years of Pobedonostsev's influence, and it is a reflection by the man who shaped the policy. By then no crisis demanded reassurance, so Pobedonostsev could set out the theory: his target was the western parliament, which he saw as a danger for Russia. The two sources agree because Pobedonostsev stood behind both. But Source B is better evidence of the ideas behind the regime, because it explains them, while Source A is better evidence of what the regime publicly claimed at a moment of fear. Neither shows what most Russians thought, since both were written by a ruler and his adviser."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the crisis of 1881, the later book) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: How far did the Russian economy develop successfully between 1881 and 1894?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Success: balanced budget and gold reserves under Vyshnegradsky; tariffs (1887, 1891); railways and the Trans-Siberian (1891); Bunge's Peasant Land Bank (1883) and end of the poll tax (1886); French loans from 1888; coal output about 3.3 million tons (1880) to about 15.9 million (1900), with the quickest growth in the 1890s.

- Failure 2, vulnerability: the policy of exporting grain; the famine of 1891–92 (aid to 27 provinces, about 172 million roubles; deaths in the hundreds of thousands).
- Failure 3, dependence: foreign capital and a small industrial base; most people still worked on the land.
- Judgement: successful as state-led building of an industrial base and a sound budget, unsuccessful as a development of the whole economy, since agriculture, where most Russians lived, was neglected or squeezed. Criterion: whether the development was **sound and sustainable**.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The Russian economy developed successfully. Bunge set up the Peasant Land Bank in 1883 and ended the poll tax in 1886, which helped the peasants. Vyshnegradsky balanced the budget by 1889 and built up gold reserves. He raised tariffs in 1887 and 1891 to protect Russian industry, and the state began the Trans-Siberian Railway in 1891. French banks started to lend money to Russia. So the economy was successful in this period."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than judges. The policies are described as if they were results. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with the famine merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the economy developed unevenly, and the success was bought at the peasants' expense. Vyshnegradsky's budget was balanced through high indirect taxes on sugar, kerosene and vodka and through pressing the peasants to sell grain abroad, so that the gold reserves were built on the poverty of the countryside. When the harvest failed on the Volga in 1891 there was no reserve: the government banned exports only in mid-August, and the famine that followed led to the relief effort in 27 provinces costing about 172 million roubles and to deaths from disease that historians estimate in the hundreds of thousands. Vyshnegradsky resigned in 1892. In the same years the peasants' arrears grew and the 1893 law bound them more tightly to the commune, so that the great majority of Russians, who farmed, saw few of the benefits of state-led industrial growth.

Industrial development itself was real but narrow. Coal rose from about 3.3 million tons in 1880 to about 15.9 million in 1900, and railway line from about 22,900 to about 53,200 kilometres, but most of that increase came in the 1890s under Witte and not in the years to 1894, and it depended on the state, on tariffs and on French loans. Industry was concentrated in a few regions and employed about 1.5 million factory workers in a population of 125 million. A reasonable view is that the foundations were laid, not that the economy had been transformed.

Overall, the economy developed successfully in what the state valued most: a balanced budget, protected industry and the beginning of a railway network to carry troops and grain. But it was not successful as a development of Russia's whole economy, since agriculture, where most people lived, was neglected and then hit by famine. The criterion that matters is whether the growth was sound and sustainable, and by that test the answer is only to a limited extent: Bunge's relief of the peasants was reversed by Vyshnegradsky's squeeze, and the structure was left fragile."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (sound and sustainable) and distinguishes the state's aims from the people's welfare. With an opening paragraph on the case for success, this is Level 5 (18–20). Note how it separates Bunge's policies from Vyshnegradsky's: strong answers do not treat "the government" as one thing.

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: written by a moral critic who had organised relief, not a neutral observer, and in the month when the crisis was at its height. It is first-hand evidence about the grain market in one district. It does not show the whole famine, and Tolstoy's own wish to shame the rich shapes what he picks out. It is strong evidence of how the market worked in one district and for the state's failure to prepare, weak evidence about the whole empire.
- **B**: figures for output and length of line from respected year-books and economic histories. They are good evidence of growth, but both series start in 1860, run to 1900 (beyond the question) and are for the whole period; they tell us nothing specific about 1881–94, when growth was slower than in the 1890s, and they do not show who gained.
- **C**: statistics collected by the government, quoted by a revolutionary in 1913. Lenin's purpose was to show how strikes had grown after 1905, so he chose the figures to contrast with the quiet years. Lenin also said the official figures covered only factories under the inspectorate. The numbers show little worker protest in 1895–1900, though they come after the period in question, so they are weak evidence for 1881–94 itself.
- **D**: the text of a law, reliable evidence of what the regime ordered. But it shows the intention, not how far the law was obeyed, and it concerns one minority. It supports the picture of a regime ready to use the law against groups it distrusted, which can be read as strength or as insecurity.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "The sources are strongest in showing the regime's strength in the form of control and growth. Source D shows a government able to pass and keep for thirty years laws restricting a large minority, and Source B shows that the state was beginning to build the railways and coal output that a great power needed. Source C adds that in the later 1890s the workers were not a threat: strikes involved 30,000 to 60,000 people a year in a factory workforce of about 1.5 million. Against this, Source A shows the limits of the regime's capacity: it could not feed its people when the harvest failed, and the grain market, which the policy of exporting had encouraged, worked against the hungry. Source D can also be read as the action of a government made fearful by the assassination and looking for scapegoats, since it followed the pogroms of 1881. On balance the sources support the view that the regime was strong in the sense of being secure from immediate danger, because its police and laws controlled society and its workers were quiet. But Sources A and B qualify it: the famine, and the growth that would create the working class of Source C's later figures, were weaknesses in the regime's long-term position. Strong in 1894 against its enemies, but weak in its ability to look after its people and to meet the challenge that its own policies were creating: the sources support the view to a large extent for the short term, and only partly for the long term."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1881–94; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify, and uses own knowledge (the pogroms of 1881, the policy of exporting grain, the strikes of 1905). The judgement answers "how far" directly, with a distinction between short term and long term. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Russification strengthened the Tsarist regime." How far do you agree? [20]

A student wrote:

"Russification was the policy of making the non-Russian people of the empire more Russian. Alexander III made Russian the language of schools and courts in Poland and the Baltic provinces. In Finland he took over the post office. The Jews were forced to live in the Pale and the May Laws of 1882 made things worse for them. Some people left Russia. Pobedonostsev supported Russification. So Russification strengthened the Tsarist regime because it made the empire more Russian and the Tsar was more powerful."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative and assertion**: it describes Russification and then states the answer without evidence that the regime became stronger. It never considers the other side, and it mixes Russification with antisemitism. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, test it, and weigh. "Russification strengthened the regime only in the short term and only in some respects. It gave the government a single language of administration in the western provinces, and it pleased Russian nationalists such as Katkov, who were the regime's loyal supporters. But it also created resentment among Poles, Finns, Balts and Jews, and in the longer term these groups supplied recruits to revolutionary parties and nationalist movements. The Jewish emigration of about two million between 1881 and 1914 also shows a policy that drove away part of the population. A fair judgement is that Russification strengthened the regime's control of the western borderlands in the 1880s but weakened its long-term support, since it turned minorities into opponents." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give three reasons why Alexander III rejected political reform after his father's assassination.
A: Fear of further terror (the People's Will, the plot of 1887); the influence of Pobedonostsev and other conservatives such as Katkov, who thought liberal reforms had caused disorder; the belief that only a strong autocracy could hold together a vast empire of many peoples.
Q: What were the State Order Statute of 1881 and the land captains of 1889?
A: The Statute let governors declare emergency rule and arrest and exile without trial; it was "temporary" but renewed until 1917. Land captains were local nobles, appointed by the governor, who supervised the peasants with administrative and judicial powers.
Q: Name three groups affected by Russification and give an example of its effects.
A: Poles (Russian pressed further into schools, courts and offices), the Baltic Germans (Russian made the language of administration; Dorpat University Russified), Finns (the post office under Russian control from 1890), Ukrainians (restrictions on printing in Ukrainian), Jews and other faiths (the May Laws, school quotas).
Q: What was the difference between Bunge's and Vyshnegradsky's economic policies?
A: Bunge tried to relieve the peasants (Peasant Land Bank, abolition of the poll tax) and introduced factory laws; Vyshnegradsky balanced the budget with high indirect taxes and pushed grain exports, which left no reserve against a bad harvest.
Q: Why did the famine of 1891–92 damage the regime's reputation?
A: The government had pressed grain exports, did not act in time, played down the crisis and left much of the relief to the zemstvos and volunteers; many educated Russians concluded that society could do what the state could not.
Q: What did Plekhanov's Emancipation of Labour group argue?
A: That Russia was becoming capitalist, that the peasant commune would not save it, and that the industrial working class, not the peasants, would carry out the revolution.
Q: Why was the Morozov strike of 1885 significant?
A: It was a large strike (about 8,000 workers) against fines and low pay that showed that workers could organise; it led to the factory law of 1886 on fines and wage-books.
Q: Give one reason why opposition was weak in 1881–94 and one reason why it was growing.
A: Weak: the Okhrana, censorship and exile had broken the People's Will and silenced liberals, and the peasants were scattered and loyal in principle. Growing: industrialisation created a working class, the famine angered educated Russians and Marxist ideas were spreading.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Pobedonostsev had so much influence on Alexander III.
A: Indicative content: he had been the Tsar's tutor and was trusted personally; he was Over-Procurator of the Holy Synod from 1880 and so spoke for the Church; his views fitted the Tsar's own conservatism and fear after the assassination; he had helped draft the Manifesto of April 1881 and won the argument against Loris-Melikov in the Council of Ministers; other conservative voices (Katkov, Dmitry Tolstoy) agreed with him; and ministers who disagreed, such as Loris-Melikov, resigned. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: the personal bond gave him access, the timing (the assassination) gave his ideas force, and the allies around the Tsar kept him in power until 1905. It ends with a judgement, e.g. that the crisis of 1881 mattered more than his character.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the government introduced Russification after 1881.
A: Indicative content: the empire was multinational and less than half the population was Great Russian; fear of nationalism after the Polish rising of 1863; the ideology of Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality; the wish to make administration and schooling uniform; the influence of Pobedonostsev and Katkov; distrust of Jews after the assassination and the pogroms; the belief that a single religion and language would produce loyal subjects. Level 4 connects the background (diversity and fear of disintegration) to the ideology that justified it and the trigger of 1881, and judges which mattered most.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Russification strengthened the Tsarist regime.' How far do you agree?
A: For: it unified administration and schooling in the western provinces; it pleased Russian nationalists; it made it easier to control the borderlands by police and courts; opposition from Poles, Finns and Balts did not break the regime before 1905. Against: it created resentment in the minorities (Poles, Finns, Balts, Jews), who later supplied revolutionary and nationalist recruits; about two million Jews left the empire between 1881 and 1914; Finnish and Polish resistance made the borderlands harder to govern; many Russian peasants were untouched by it. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion (control, loyalty or stability) and judges, e.g. that it increased control in the short term but weakened loyalty in the long term, so the answer is only to a limited extent.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How successful were the economic policies of Bunge?
A: Success: ended the poll tax (1886); cut and then made compulsory the redemption payments; opened the Peasant Land Bank in 1883; created the factory inspectorate and limited child labour; protected industry by tariffs. Limits: the tax cuts widened the deficit; the Land Bank helped richer peasants; the factory laws were poorly enforced; conservative critics (Katkov) brought his fall in 1886 and Vyshnegradsky reversed the emphasis on relieving the peasants. Level 5 judges against a clear criterion, e.g. whether the reforms achieved his aim of relieving the peasants while strengthening the state: partly, since his work was cut short and the long-term burden on the peasants remained.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the Tsarist regime's attitude to society.
A: Similarities: both express the regime's view that order, faith and loyalty come before political freedom; both distrust the "sedition" or "despotism" of speech and print; both come from the Tsar's circle. Differences: A is a public appeal to subjects to serve the Tsar and the state and to restore morality; B is a reasoned attack on speechmaking and the press as selfish and dangerous. A was issued in the crisis after the assassination (April 1881) and aims to reassure and mobilise; B was published in 1896 after fifteen years of rule and is a reflection by the man who shaped the policy. Provenance: A is a state document in the Tsar's name, drafted with Pobedonostsev; B is a book by Pobedonostsev himself, so both reflect his views. Level 4 explains the differences through context and judges which is better evidence of the regime's attitude: B for the ideas, A for the regime's public claims.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources A–D in the worked example above ("Paper 1 (b) [25]"). How far do these sources support the view that the main challenge to the Tsarist regime in 1881–94 came from economic problems?
H: Decide which sources are about the economy (A famine, B growth, C workers) and which are about politics and control (D).
H: Ask what each economic source shows: a failure (A), a success (B), or something not yet happening (C).
H: Use your own knowledge of the opposition (Marxism, the 1887 plot, the zemstvos) to judge whether the economic problems were the main challenge.
A: Support: A (famine shows the weakness of the economic policy and a hungry peasantry); B (growth created a new working class, the later danger); C (strikes later show workers' grievances). Challenge: D (the regime's main response was political control, the fear of revolutionaries and of minorities); C (strikes were small before 1900, so workers were not yet a challenge); B (growth was a strength, not a problem). Evaluation with knowledge: A is Tolstoy's report from one district; B is long-run and does not cover 1881–94 closely; C is Lenin's selection; D shows policy, not effect. Own knowledge: the plot of 1887, Russification, the silencing of the zemstvos. Level 5 judges how far: the economic problems, above all the peasantry's poverty and the famine, were the most serious long-term challenge, but in 1881–94 the regime itself felt most threatened by revolutionaries and national movements, so the sources support the view only to a limited extent.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The famine of 1891–92 was the main reason for the growth of opposition to the regime by 1894.' How far do you agree?
H: Set a criterion: what counts as "growth of opposition"? Numbers, organisation, ideas, or willingness to act?
H: Compare the famine with other causes: the counter-reforms, Russification, industrialisation and the example of Marxism.
A: For: the famine showed the state's failure and led educated Russians to organise relief themselves, which gave zemstvo liberals, writers such as Tolstoy and young Marxists a grievance; Plekhanov's group and Marxist circles used the argument that the old system was failing. Against: the Emancipation of Labour group was founded in 1883, and the Morozov strike was in 1885, both before the famine; counter-reforms, censorship and Russification created grievances among students, liberals and minorities; industrialisation was creating a working class, the long-term base of opposition; the famine's effect on opposition may be exaggerated since the regime recovered quickly and strikes were small until after 1900. Level 5 judges, e.g., that the famine was a catalyst for opposition among the educated but not its main cause, which was the regime's own policies and the social change it was promoting.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Richard Pipes argued that the Russian state was "patrimonial", dominating a weak society. How far does the evidence of 1881–94 support this view?
H: Match the idea to the facts: the Tsar's power, the police, the land captains and the Church.
H: Then ask what the famine and the zemstvo relief show about society's strength.
A: Support: the Tsar's unlimited power; the Okhrana, censorship and the State Order Statute; the land captains put the nobility under the governor's control; the zemstvos and universities were brought under ministers; the Church was a department of state; the opposition was smashed in 1881–82; the economy was driven from above by the Finance Ministry. Challenge: society was not simply passive: the zemstvos and volunteers (and Tolstoy) organised famine relief when the state failed; the intelligentsia and workers were forming new groups (Marxist circles, Morozov strike); the state itself relied on the nobility and Church to rule and was short of officials. A* judgement: the regime's formal power matched Pipes's picture, but its practical reach was thin, and 1891–92 showed an active society, so "patrimonial" describes the form of rule better than its real strength.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ The census of 1897 is sometimes used as evidence of the condition of Russia in the 1880s and 1890s. How far is a census of 1897 reliable evidence for 1881–94?
H: What did the census count (population, towns, literacy, religion, language), and when?
H: What does the date of the census mean for using it as evidence for the earlier period? Who ran it, and what might people fear?
A: Strengths: the only census of the Tsarist era, covering the whole empire, collected by the state with a standard form; it gives population (125.6 million), the urban share (13.4 per cent), literacy (about 21 per cent), language and religion; these shape the story of a mainly peasant, low-literacy empire. Weaknesses: taken in 1897, three years after the period ends, so it records the effect of the 1890s boom and not of 1881–94 specifically; the enumerators were mostly temporary staff, and some people may have feared that the count meant more tax or conscription; categories (language, estate, religion) reflect the state's view of the empire; it counts people, not their grievances. Judgement: reliable for the scale and shape of the society (the contrast between peasants and towns), less reliable for change within 1881–94, so best used together with other evidence such as the famine reports and strike statistics.

:::recap
- **Autocracy**: the Tsar unlimited by law; State Council (advisory), ministers (each reporting alone), Senate, army, police and Okhrana (1881), Church (Holy Synod, Pobedonostsev), governors, zemstvos, commune. About 125.6 million people in 1897, 13.4 per cent urban, about 21 per cent literate.
- **1881**: Alexander II killed on 1 March (Old Style) by the People's Will; Manifesto of 29 April proclaims the autocracy; Loris-Melikov resigns; State Order Statute of 14 August.
- **Counter-reforms**: press rules 1882; University Statute 1884; "cooks' children" circular 1887; land captains 1889; Zemstvo Statute 1890; city statute 1892.
- **Russification and the Jews**: Poles, Baltic provinces, Finland, Ukrainians; pogroms from April 1881; May Laws 3 May 1882; school quotas 1887; about two million Jews emigrated 1881–1914.
- **Economy**: Bunge (1881–86): Peasant Land Bank, poll tax abolished 1886, factory laws; Vyshnegradsky (1887–92): high taxes and tariffs, grain exports, French loans; Witte from 1892; Trans-Siberian from 1891. Coal about 3.3 to 15.9 million tons 1880–1900; railways 22,865 to 53,234 km.
- **Famine of 1891–92**: failed harvest on the Volga; exports banned too late in August 1891; aid to 27 provinces (about 172 million roubles); deaths from at least about 375,000, plus cholera in 1892; reputation of the government damaged.
- **Society**: declining nobility, peasants about four in five, a growing working class (about 1.5 million in factories), a small middle class and an intelligentsia.
- **Opposition**: People's Will broken; plot of 1887 (Alexander Ulyanov hanged 8 May); Morozov strike January 1885; Emancipation of Labour 1883; Marxist circles; liberals silenced.
- **Judgement**: not in immediate danger in 1894, but the poverty of the countryside, the exclusion of new social groups and the reliance on one man were deep weaknesses.
