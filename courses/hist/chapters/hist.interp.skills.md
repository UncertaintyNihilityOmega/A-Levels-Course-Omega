:::why
Every history book is an argument. Two historians can read the same documents and write opposite books, and neither need be careless or dishonest. Paper 3 tests whether you can see this: you are handed one unseen paragraph of a historian's writing and asked what it shows about what they think and how they came to think it. Nobody can learn that skill by memorising facts alone. It is a method, and this chapter teaches the method on a neutral topic, the French Revolution, so that you meet the three Paper 3 topics (the First World War, the Holocaust and the Cold War) already knowing what to do.
:::

Q: What is the difference between a primary source and a secondary source? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))

## The paper in one page

Paper 3 is the shortest and most unusual paper in the course. You answer one question, about one extract, in 75 minutes, so each minute is worth about half a mark. It rewards a calm, structured answer more than any other paper.

| Feature | What the paper does |
|---|---|
| Choice | One topic, studied for the whole course; one section of the paper |
| Material | One extract, at most 600 words, from a historian's writing; author not named |
| Question | "What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of … to explain your answer. [40]" |
| Marks | 40: Table A (AO4) out of 20, plus Table B (AO1) out of 20 |
| Time | 1 hour 15 minutes |
| Not asked | To evaluate whether the historian is right; to compare the extract with other historians; to name the author |

### What the question means

**Interpretation** is what the historian *concludes*: their claims about what happened and why, and who or what mattered most. **Approach** is what the historian *brings*: the questions they ask, what interests them, and how their beliefs and ideology shape what they look for and what they leave out. The syllabus says you need not separate the two, because they are linked: a historian who asks "who started the war?" will tend to look at diplomats and decision-makers, and so will reach conclusions about intentions.

:::def Historical interpretation
A historian's account of the past that explains why something happened and what mattered most. It is built from evidence but is not itself a fact: it is an argument that other historians can accept, reject or refine.
:::

:::def Approach (of a historian)
What a historian brings to the study of the past: the questions they ask, what interests them, the methods and kinds of evidence they prefer, and the beliefs or ideology that shape what they look for. An approach and an interpretation are linked: the questions asked help decide the answers found.
:::

Notice what the question does **not** ask. It does not say "How convincing is this extract?" (that is the question in some other exams, including AQA's: see the board box below). It does not say "Compare this with another view." It says what can you *learn* about *the historian*. A student who spends two paragraphs arguing that the historian is wrong has answered a different question.

:::warn Three common ways to answer the wrong question
1. **Evaluating.** "The historian is biased and ignores the evidence of ..." The mark scheme rewards explaining the interpretation, not grading it. Knowledge can show where the claim is strong or limited, but only as part of explaining it.
2. **Summarising.** Walking through the extract line by line ("The first paragraph says … the second paragraph says …"). Summary is Level 2 at best, because it does not say what the extract adds up to.
3. **Guessing the author.** The paper never asks, and the mark scheme gives nothing for a name. Time spent on "this sounds like Fischer" is lost.
:::

## Why historians disagree

The syllabus gives four reasons. Learn them: they are also the toolkit for explaining an approach.

:::key Four reasons why historians disagree
1. **The fragmentary nature of the evidence.** The past has left only part of its record. Meetings were never minuted, papers were burned or lost, and many people (peasants, soldiers, victims) left little in writing. Historians fill the gaps by inference, and different inferences give different histories.
2. **Selection and interpretation of the evidence.** Nobody can use all the evidence. Each historian chooses what to stress, what to treat as typical and what to explain away, and then decides what the chosen evidence means.
3. **The passage of time.** New evidence appears (archives open, documents are published), and new historians answer the arguments of old ones. A book is partly a reply to earlier books.
4. **The time and place in which the historian works.** A historian writes in a particular country, at a particular moment, with the worries and questions of that world: a war just lost or won, a regime in power, a debate in the newspapers.
:::

These four are connected, not separate boxes. Here is a short illustration of each, using the French Revolution.

**Fragmentary evidence.** We know the Parisian crowd stormed the Bastille on 14 July 1789, but we do not have the records of the thousands who took part: most of what survives was written by officials, journalists and a few participants, sometimes years later. Historians can agree on the events and still differ on who the crowd were and what they wanted.

**Selection and interpretation.** The *cahiers de doléances* of 1789 are a huge archive. A historian who wants to show that the revolution had deep social causes can quote the complaints about seigneurial dues; one who wants to show that the aims of 1789 were political can quote the demands for consent to taxation and regular meetings of the Estates-General. Both are quoting real cahiers.

**The passage of time.** In 1939 Georges Lefebvre wrote his famous account using the evidence then known and the questions then asked. In 1964 Alfred Cobban re-examined who the deputies of 1789 actually were. Cobban was answering Lefebvre's generation, and in turn he was answered by those who came after him, such as François Furet in 1978. No book stands alone.

**Time and place.** Lefebvre wrote in France in the 1930s, in the age of the Popular Front and of Marxist ideas about social classes, and he became a historian of the people "from below". Cobban, writing in Britain in the 1950s and 1960s, was sceptical of grand theories that claimed to explain a whole revolution by one force. Furet, in France in the 1970s, wrote when the intellectual prestige of Marxism was falling. Each question was shaped by its decade.

### How approaches differ

Reasons are why historians differ. **Approaches** are the *ways* in which they differ, and Paper 3 asks you to see them in the text. Three are most useful.

- **Different questions.** One historian asks "Who was to blame?" and looks for a culprit. Another asks "How did it come about?" and looks at a chain of decisions. A third asks "Why did people at the time believe what they believed?" and looks at ideas and assumptions. The extract's question shapes its conclusion.
- **Ideology and beliefs.** A historian who believes that economic classes drive history will look for class interests. A historian who believes in the importance of individual decisions will look for decision-makers. A nationalist historian may look for ways to defend or condemn a nation. Beliefs do not make a historian dishonest, but they steer attention.
- **The link between approach and interpretation.** What a historian looks for decides what they find. This is the heart of the Paper 3 question: you are asked to see that the interpretation (the conclusion) and the approach (the lens) fit together.

:::note Three famous historians on the nature of history (briefly)
- **E.H. Carr**, *What Is History?* (1961), argued that facts do not speak for themselves: the historian chooses and arranges them, so history is "a continuous process of interaction between the historian and his facts, an unending dialogue between the present and the past". Carr's lesson for Paper 3: always ask what the historian is selecting.
- **Geoffrey Elton**, *The Practice of History* (1967), answered that historians must let the evidence in the sources control their conclusions, and he distrusted theory and the claim that history serves the present. Elton's lesson: an approach that stays close to the documents is itself an approach.
- **Richard Evans**, *In Defence of History* (1997), defended the possibility of reasonably objective history against the argument that it is only narrative and rhetoric. Evans's lesson: knowing that historians have approaches does not mean that all accounts are equally good.
You are not examined on these three. Use them to understand why Paper 3 treats every historian as someone with a standpoint.
:::

:::key Reasons for disagreement, in a sentence each
The evidence is **incomplete**; historians **select** from it; **time** brings new evidence and new questions; and **place** (country, decade, politics) gives each historian different worries. Together these explain why two honest historians can write opposite books.
:::

### Step 1: read twice

Read the extract once at normal speed for the gist: what is it about, and what does the writer seem to think? Then read it again with a pencil. Mark:
- **Claims:** sentences that say what happened or why ("The war was not planned but …").
- **Words of judgement:** *only*, *merely*, *above all*, *largely*, *rather than*, *the real cause*, *myth*. They show what the historian thinks matters.
- **Contrast words:** *but*, *however*, *although*, *it has often been said*. A historian who writes "it has often been said that …" is telling you what the extract argues **against**, and that is half of the interpretation.
- **Names, groups and places** the historian puts at the centre (kings, classes, nations, ministers, ideas).

### Step 2: find the main interpretation

Write one sentence. The specimen mark scheme shows that a main interpretation usually has **two linked aspects**. Often these are a claim about *who or what* mattered most, and a claim about *how* or *why* that produced the outcome; or a claim about the cause and a claim about what the cause rules out. For example: "The Revolution was made by lawyers and officials of the old order, not by a rising capitalist class, so it cannot be explained as the victory of capitalism over feudalism." That is one sentence with two aspects (who made it; what it was not).

Check your sentence against the extract's **opening and closing** sentences, which usually state the claim; and against the contrast words, which tell you what the claim is opposed to.

### Step 3: test every paragraph

Now test each paragraph against your sentence. Ask: "Is this paragraph part of the main message, a supporting step, or a side point?"
- A **sub-message** is a smaller claim inside the extract that supports the main interpretation or adds a detail ("peasants already owned much land").
- Level 4 and Level 3 answers go wrong here. The mark scheme describes them: they identify elements without explaining them, "often treating less important messages as equally or more important" (Level 4), or they explain only sub-messages, or argue "a different interpretation in each paragraph" (Level 3).
- A good test: if you deleted the paragraph, would the main message change? If not, it is a sub-message.

### Step 4: explain with knowledge

Half of the marks (Table B, AO1) are for knowledge, and the knowledge is not separate from the analysis: the specimen mark scheme describes knowledge "used to support the analysis of the interpretation". Section "Using your knowledge" below shows how. In short: for each claim you identify, say **what it means**, then show **what you know** that explains, tests or places it.

:::steps
1. Read the extract twice, marking claims, words of judgement and contrast words.
2. Write one sentence for the main interpretation, with its two aspects.
3. Test each paragraph: main message, supporting step or sub-message?
4. Plan paragraphs by *aspect of the interpretation*, not by paragraph of the extract.
5. For each aspect, explain it with the extract's own words and add precise knowledge.
6. Finish with the approach: what questions the historian asks, what they stress and what they leave out, and how these fit the interpretation.
:::

:::exam Time plan for 75 minutes
About 10 to 12 minutes to read, mark and plan; about 55 minutes to write; the last few minutes to check that every paragraph says something about the **whole** extract and uses at least one precise fact. This is a suggested split, not a rule. The paper has one question, so there is no time to lose on a bad start: do not begin writing until you have your one-sentence main interpretation.
:::

## The levels: what each mark band looks like

The Paper 3 mark scheme has two tables, each out of 20. These are the Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the columns slowly: they describe a *ladder* of understanding.

| Level | Marks | Table A (AO4): the interpretation and approach |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 18–20 | Detailed, accurate use of the extract; complete understanding of the interpretation and of the approach(es); explains **all elements** of the interpretation |
| 5 | 15–17 | Detailed, accurate use; sound understanding; engages with elements of the interpretation **without explaining it as a whole** (may cover less important sub-messages) |
| 4 | 12–14 | Uses the extract but partial understanding; identifies elements without adequately explaining them, often treating less important messages as equally or more important |
| 3 | 9–11 | Understands that the extract contains interpretations but explains **only sub-messages**; or uses part of the extract to argue for an interpretation the whole does not support, or a different interpretation in each paragraph |
| 2 | 5–8 | Summarises the main points; explanations of the extract as an interpretation lack validity |
| 1 | 1–4 | References to some aspects of the extract; fragments of relevant material |

| Level | Marks | Table B (AO1): knowledge |
|---|---|---|
| 6 | 18–20 | Detailed and accurate knowledge that is entirely relevant |
| 5 | 15–17 | Detailed and mostly accurate knowledge, mainly relevant |
| 4 | 12–14 | Mostly relevant and accurate knowledge |
| 3 | 9–11 | Generally accurate and relevant knowledge |
| 2 | 5–8 | Some accurate and relevant knowledge |
| 1 | 1–4 | Limited knowledge |

The specimen mark scheme says that "best fit" is used: an answer that convincingly meets a level scores at the top of its band, one that adequately meets it in the middle, and one that just meets it at the bottom. The two tables are marked separately and added, so a total is the sum of the two: for example Level 6 in both gives 36 to 40, and Level 5 in both gives 30 to 34.

:::key What moves an answer up
- **Level 2 to 3:** stop summarising; say what the extract *argues*. Level 3 still explains only sub-messages.
- **Level 3 to 4:** read the extract as **one** argument. Do not give a different interpretation to each paragraph.
- **Level 4 to 5:** rank the messages. Say which are main and which are supporting, and explain the main ones with detail.
- **Level 5 to 6:** explain the interpretation **as a whole**: all its elements (usually both aspects), how they fit together, and the approach that lies behind them.
- **Knowledge, all levels:** from limited facts (Level 1) to detailed, accurate, entirely relevant knowledge (Level 6). Relevant means each fact is used to explain or test a claim in the extract.
:::

### Labels: only when the extract earns them

Historians' schools have names (intentionalist and structuralist; traditionalist, revisionist and post-revisionist; revisionist and anti-revisionist). The specimen mark scheme credits such labels **only when they are appropriate to the extract and supported from it**. Using a label correctly can help you show you understand how an approach fits a school; using one that the extract does not support is an error, not a bonus. A label is a shortcut for a sentence you must still write: "This extract is revisionist **because** it argues against the long-accepted view that …". If you cannot write the *because*, leave the label out.

## Using your knowledge

Table B is half the marks, and Paper 3 is a rare history exam in which you may not simply "show what you know". Your knowledge must be **tied to the extract**. There are three jobs for a fact.

:::key Three jobs for knowledge in a Paper 3 answer
1. **Explain a claim.** The extract says something; you show what it refers to. ("The historian says the deputies were mostly lawyers: Sieyès's pamphlet and the Third Estate's deputies, many of them lawyers, are what this refers to.")
2. **Test a claim.** You check the claim against what you know, and say where it holds and where it is stretched. ("This is well supported for the Third Estate's deputies, but the peasants' rising in the Great Fear shows that dues still mattered.")
3. **Show what the approach stresses or omits.** What the historian leaves out reveals the approach. ("The extract says nothing about the harvest crisis of 1788–89, which suggests that the historian is more interested in who made the Revolution than in what drove people onto the streets.")
:::

Compare two paragraphs about the same sentence of an extract.

**Weak (knowledge as narrative).** "In 1789 the Estates-General met on 5 May. The Third Estate called itself the National Assembly on 17 June and swore the Tennis Court Oath on 20 June. The Bastille fell on 14 July."

**Strong (knowledge as explanation).** "The historian describes the deputies as men of the law and of office. This is accurate for the Third Estate: lawyers dominated its deputies in 1789, and Sieyès, a clergyman, wrote the pamphlet that gave them their programme. By treating this as the key fact, the historian is claiming that the Revolution's leaders came from the old order's own institutions."

The weak paragraph is accurate but answers no question. The strong paragraph uses only the facts it needs, ties each to the extract, and ends by saying what the claim means. At Table B Level 6, every sentence of knowledge does this.

:::eg Table B: three versions of one knowledge paragraph
Take one sentence of Practice extract C (it is printed in full in the next section): "Most peasants already owned some land, and the dues they paid were burdens on property rather than relics of serfdom." Here are three ways to use knowledge on it.

**A Level 3 paragraph (Table B: "generally accurate and relevant", 9–11).** "Peasants in France paid a lot of taxes. They paid the tithe to the Church and the taille to the king, and they were very poor. The French Revolution began in 1789 and the peasants joined in by attacking the nobles."

*Commentary.* The facts are accurate but general, and none is tied to the claim about land and dues. The reader is left to guess what the knowledge is for.

**A Level 4 paragraph (Table B: "mostly relevant and accurate", 12–14).** "The historian says peasants owned land. Some did: a minority owned enough to prosper, though many rented, sharecropped or worked for wages. The dues the historian mentions were seigneurial dues, paid in money, in a share of crops and in labour, with fees for using the lord's mill and oven. The historian is right that these were attached to land."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with some range. But it never says what the knowledge **does** to the claim: does it support it, or limit it?

**A Level 6 paragraph (Table B: "detailed and accurate knowledge that is entirely relevant", 18–20).** "The claim fits the way the law treated dues. In the August Decrees of 4–11 August 1789 the Assembly abolished personal servitude without compensation but declared dues attached to land redeemable, that is, treated them as property that had to be bought out. But the peasants of the Great Fear (about 20 July to 6 August 1789) did not see them as ordinary property: they burned the *terriers*, the records of the dues, and most refused to redeem them until the dues were abolished without compensation in 1793. So the historian's sentence is accurate about the legal form and leaves out how peasants felt, which shows that the historian is interested in structures of property more than in popular anger."

*Commentary.* Every fact is precise (dates, the legal distinction, the terriers, 1793), each is used to test the sentence, and the paragraph ends by saying what the choice shows about the approach. That is "entirely relevant".
:::

## A practice extract and a full worked answer

Real Paper 3 extracts are 500 to 600 words from a real historian's text. You will meet real ones when you work on past papers (the Cambridge past-paper page is in the links below). For learning the method, this course gives you **practice extracts**: short summaries, written for this course, of the arguments of named historians. They are clearly labelled, they are not the historians' own words, and they are shorter than a real extract so that you can see the whole argument at once.

:::eg Paper 3 [40]: What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the French Revolution to explain your answer.
**Practice extract C** (about 200 words)

For a long time the French Revolution was explained as the victory of a rising capitalist middle class over a feudal nobility. That explanation rests on a social label, not on evidence. When we look at who sat in the National Assembly in 1789 and who held power in the years that followed, we find lawyers, royal officials and landowners, not merchants and manufacturers. These were men of the old order's own institutions, closer to a declining group than to a rising class.

Nor was France ruled by a feudal system that capitalism had to destroy. Most peasants already owned some land, and the dues they paid were burdens on property rather than relics of serfdom. The Revolution then made the peasant landowner more secure, and the result was a countryside that clung to small farms. It is hard to argue that this speeded the growth of industry. If anything, it held it back.

The historian who wants to understand 1789 must therefore stop asking which class interest the Revolution served, and ask instead what particular men, with particular careers and fears, actually did.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Alfred Cobban, The Social Interpretation of the French Revolution (1964). The words are not Cobban's.*

**Plan (read, find, test).**
- Gist: the extract attacks an old explanation of the Revolution.
- Words of contrast: "for a long time … explained as"; "not on evidence"; "not merchants … but lawyers"; "Nor was"; "If anything"; "must therefore stop asking … and ask instead".
- **Main interpretation (two aspects):** (1) the Revolution's leaders were lawyers, officials and landowners tied to the old order, not a rising capitalist class; (2) the Revolution neither overthrew feudalism nor advanced capitalism, so the "social interpretation" (the Revolution as the victory of a class) fails.
- Sub-messages: peasants already owned land; dues were burdens on property; the Revolution entrenched the small peasant farm; capitalism may have been held back.
- Approach: tests a big theory against evidence about who the actors were; sceptical of class labels; interested in individuals' careers and fears more than in social forces; writes in reply to an earlier explanation.

**What the levels want.** Table A: Level 3 (9–11) explains only sub-messages or argues a different interpretation; Level 5 (15–17) explains elements with detail but not the whole; Level 6 (18–20) explains all elements and the approach as one argument. Table B: from "generally accurate and relevant" (Level 3, 9–11) to "detailed and accurate knowledge that is entirely relevant" (Level 6, 18–20).

**A Level 3 answer (about 20 of 40).** 
"The extract says that the French Revolution was not made by a rising middle class. It says that the National Assembly contained lawyers. It also says that peasants owned land and that the Revolution made them more secure. So the historian is saying that the peasants gained a lot from the Revolution and that this was the most important result. The historian also says the Revolution held back industry. In the French Revolution there was the Estates-General in 1789, the Tennis Court Oath, the storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 and the August Decrees. The historian therefore thinks that the Revolution was a big success for the peasants."

*Commentary.* The answer picks up sub-messages (peasant landowners, security, industry) and builds a **different interpretation** from the real one ("a success for the peasants"). The extract's attention to peasants is a supporting point; the main claim is about the leaders and the failure of the class explanation. The knowledge is generally accurate but is a list of events that is not connected to any claim: **Level 3** in Table A (10) and Table B (10), about 20 out of 40.

**A Level 5 answer (about 32 of 40).** 
"The main message of the extract is that the Revolution's leaders were not a rising capitalist class. The historian says that 'for a long time' it was explained as the victory of a capitalist middle class, and says this rests on a label, not on evidence. The evidence is the membership of the National Assembly: lawyers, officials and landowners, 'men of the old order's own institutions'. This fits what we know. The Third Estate's deputies in 1789 were dominated by lawyers, and the bourgeoisie's complaint before the Revolution was less poverty than exclusion: the Ségur Ordinance of 1781 closed army commissions to those without four generations of nobility, and top posts in Church and government were closing to commoners. Sieyès, a clergyman, wrote the pamphlet of January 1789 that told the Third Estate it should be 'something'. The historian is right that such men were products of the old order, and the extract uses this to make them 'closer to a declining group than to a rising class'.

The extract also argues that France was not feudal. It says most peasants already owned land and the dues were burdens on property. This is partly accurate: many peasants owned a little land, and the August Decrees of 1789 made dues attached to land redeemable rather than abolishing them, which fits the idea that they were attached to property. But the cahiers and the Great Fear in the summer of 1789 show that peasants resented seigneurial dues enough to burn the records of them, so the claim that feudalism was no real burden is something the historian plays down. The historian also says the Revolution held back industry, which is a further point."

*Commentary.* The first paragraph explains one aspect of the interpretation fully, with precise knowledge used to explain and test it (the Ségur Ordinance, the lawyers, Sieyès, the August Decrees). The second paragraph deals well with the feudalism claim but treats it as another point, not as the other half of one argument; the closing sentence on industry is a loose sub-message. There is **no explanation of the approach** and no statement of how the two claims fit together. This is **Level 5** in Table A (16): "sound understanding; engages with elements without explaining the interpretation as a whole". Knowledge is detailed and mostly accurate (Table B 16). About 32 of 40.

**A Level 6 answer (about 37 of 40).** 
"The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. Its main interpretation is that the French Revolution cannot be explained as the victory of a capitalist class over feudalism, because neither half of that explanation survives a look at the evidence: the people who made the Revolution were lawyers, officials and landowners of the old order, and the old order was not a feudal system waiting to be destroyed by capitalism, so the Revolution neither expressed a rising class nor advanced capitalism.

The first aspect concerns who made the Revolution. The historian says the older view 'rests on a social label, not on evidence', and offers as evidence the people who sat in the National Assembly and held power. This is consistent with what is known. Lawyers dominated the Third Estate's deputies in 1789; the bourgeoisie's grievance was exclusion (the Ségur Ordinance of 22 May 1781 barred commoners from army commissions) rather than poverty; and Sieyès, in *What is the Third Estate?*, spoke for a group who wanted a share of political power rather than a different economic order. The historian chooses these facts because they are the ones that damage a class theory, and says nothing of merchants or manufacturers, who were few among the deputies.

The second aspect concerns what the Revolution was and did. The historian says peasants already owned land and the dues were 'burdens on property rather than relics of serfdom', and concludes that the Revolution made the peasant owner more secure and so held industry back. This is the part of the argument that most needs testing against knowledge. The August Decrees of 4–11 August 1789 abolished personal servitude without compensation but made dues on land redeemable, which suggests that dues were treated as property, as the extract says. Yet the Great Fear shows that peasants experienced them as feudal, and the redemption terms kept the countryside restless until dues were abolished without compensation in 1793. The extract's claim fits the law but plays down what peasants felt, and that is a choice: the historian is concerned with structures of property and with who held office, not with the hunger and anger that drove crowds and peasants to act in 1788–89.

That choice is the approach. The historian is interested in testing a large theory against the details of who the revolutionaries were; the final paragraph says historians should 'stop asking which class interest the Revolution served' and ask what 'particular men, with particular careers and fears, actually did'. This is the language of someone sceptical of grand explanations, who prefers detailed evidence about individuals to social forces. It also fits the interpretation: if you ask what individuals did, you will find lawyers and officials rather than a class, and you will find landowners rather than a feudal order. Because the extract is a reply to an earlier explanation ('for a long time … explained') it can fairly be called a **revisionist** account: it sets out to overturn an established view with evidence, and the label is supported by the extract's own opening."

*Commentary.* The opening sentence states the **whole** interpretation and its two aspects. Each aspect is explained with the extract's own words and tested with precise, relevant knowledge (the Ségur Ordinance with its date, the August Decrees, the Great Fear, 1793). The approach is explained in the historian's own terms, shown to be linked to the interpretation, and the label "revisionist" is used **because** the extract supports it. **Level 6** in Table A (19) and Level 6 in Table B (18): about 37 of 40. About 600 words is enough in 55 minutes.
:::

### What the three answers teach

:::key What separates Level 3, Level 5 and Level 6
- **Level 3** takes sub-messages and builds a different message from them ("a success for the peasants"). Its knowledge lists events.
- **Level 5** explains one aspect of the real interpretation well, or both aspects as separate points, but never says how they form one argument, and does not explain the approach.
- **Level 6** states the whole interpretation in a sentence, explains **every** element, links them, then explains the approach and shows how it fits. Every fact has a job.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: the same Practice extract C. A student wrote this as the second paragraph of an answer:

"The historian also says that peasants owned land. This was true because peasants in France did own some land before 1789. The historian says that the Revolution held back industry. The Revolution did hold back industry because there was a war from 1792 and the economy was damaged. So the historian is correct that the Revolution was bad for industry. The historian therefore shows a negative view of the Revolution."

**The level.** Table A **Level 3** (9–11): it explains sub-messages and from them creates a new interpretation ("a negative view of the Revolution") that the whole extract does not support: Practice extract C does not say the Revolution was bad, it says that the class explanation fails. The knowledge (the war after 1792) is accurate but tested against the wrong claim: the extract says the Revolution made peasants secure, so the **result** was slow industrial growth, not that war harmed industry. Table B Level 3.

**The fix.** First, say how the sub-message supports the main interpretation: "The historian argues that peasants already owned land to show that France was not a feudal society that capitalism had to overthrow; this is why the point matters." Then test it: "The August Decrees' redemption terms support the idea that dues were property; but the Great Fear shows peasants felt them as burdens." Finally, ask what the choice reveals about the approach (a concern with property and who held office rather than popular anger). Now the paragraph explains and tests a claim that is part of the whole.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What does Paper 3 give you, and how long and how many marks is it?
A: One unseen extract of at most 600 words from a historian's writing, with the author not named; 1 hour 15 minutes; one question marked out of 40 (Table A for AO4 out of 20, Table B for AO1 out of 20). You answer one question from one section only.
Q: What is the difference between an interpretation and an approach?
A: An interpretation is the historian's conclusion: what they claim about what happened and why. An approach is what they bring: the questions they ask, their interests, their beliefs and the evidence they prefer. The two are linked because the questions asked help decide the answers found.
Q: Name the four reasons the syllabus gives for historians differing.
A: The fragmentary nature of the evidence; selection and interpretation of the evidence; the passage of time (new evidence and new interpretations by other historians); and the time and place in which the historians work.
Q: What is a sub-message, and why is it a trap?
A: A smaller claim inside the extract that supports the main interpretation or adds detail. Answers at Levels 3 and 4 explain sub-messages as if they were the main message, so the whole interpretation is missed.
Q: Why must you never name the historian who wrote the extract?
A: The author is not identified and candidates are never asked to identify them; the mark scheme gives nothing for a name, and a wrong guess makes you read the extract through a stereotype instead of its own words.
Q: Give three jobs for knowledge in a Paper 3 answer.
A: Explain a claim (show what it refers to); test a claim (say where it holds and where it is stretched); show what the approach stresses or leaves out.
Q: When may you use a label such as "revisionist"?
A: Only when it is appropriate to the extract and you can support it from the extract with a "because" (for example, the extract sets out to overturn an established view). Unsupported labels earn nothing.
Q: How do the two tables combine into a mark out of 40?
A: Table A (AO4) is out of 20 and Table B (AO1) is out of 20; the two marks are added. Level 6 in both gives 36 to 40; Level 5 in both gives 30 to 34.
:::

:::note Sources for exam practice questions
The three practice extracts below are summaries of the arguments of named historians, written for practice in the course's own words. They are **not** the historians' text, and real Paper 3 extracts are longer (up to 600 words). Use them as you would a real extract.

**Practice extract 1** (about 170 words)

The year 1789 was not one revolution but several, each with its own cause. First came the revolt of the aristocracy, which broke the king's power to govern without them and opened the way. The bourgeoisie, a class that had grown rich in trade, in the professions and in office, then claimed the political power that matched its wealth, and it was the bourgeoisie that gained most from what followed. But it could not have won alone. The people of the towns rose because they were hungry and afraid of a plot to starve them, and the peasants rose against dues that had burdened their families for centuries. The bourgeois leaders did not start these risings, but they used them, and then made laws to end the privileges of birth while leaving property secure. The Revolution was therefore the end of the old feudal order and the victory of a new class. To understand it we must look at the economy and at the classes, and we must listen to the crowd as well as the deputies.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Georges Lefebvre, Quatre-vingt-neuf (1939; in English The Coming of the French Revolution, 1947). The words are not Lefebvre's.*

**Practice extract 2** (about 170 words)

The Old Regime was not a doomed system waiting for a revolution. France was wealthy and growing, and its society was less rigid than the old stories suggest: nobles and wealthy commoners shared tastes, ideas and even interests. What destroyed the monarchy was a crisis of its own making. The state's debts, swollen by the war in America, forced the Crown to seek reform, and the reforms failed, because a king and his ministers could not persuade the privileged to accept them. When the privileged, led by the parlements and the Notables, fought back and demanded the Estates-General, the king lost control of events. From then on the question was no longer one of money but of who held political power. The Revolution grew out of this political collapse, and out of the choices that individuals made inside it, more than out of a long conflict of classes. A historian who wants to explain 1789 must begin with the crisis of the state.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of William Doyle, Origins of the French Revolution (1980). The words are not Doyle's.*

**Practice extract 3** (about 160 words)

The old story of the Revolution as a conflict of classes has served as a catechism more than as an explanation. It treats ideas and words as a mask on the real interests beneath. But in 1789 the language itself was the revolution: the nation, the people, the general will, the idea that all power came from below. The revolutionaries did not merely express a class; they invented a new politics in which anyone who spoke for the people could claim absolute authority, and anyone who doubted it could be called an enemy. This is why the Revolution moved so fast from reform to terror. The logic of its political culture, not the plans of any group, pushed it on. We should therefore read what the revolutionaries said, how they said it and what they feared, and treat their words as the subject of the history, not as a screen.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of François Furet, Penser la Révolution française (1978; in English Interpreting the French Revolution, 1981). The words are not Furet's.*

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Read Practice extract 1 (in the box above). In one sentence state the main interpretation, with its two aspects, then name two sub-messages.
A: Main interpretation (two aspects): the Revolution was a series of linked revolutions (aristocratic, bourgeois, popular and peasant) each with its own cause, **and** its chief result was the victory of the bourgeois class over the old feudal order. Sub-messages (any two): the aristocratic revolt came first and opened the way; the townspeople rose from hunger and fear of a plot; the peasants rose against old dues; the bourgeois leaders used the risings but did not start them; the laws ended birth privilege but kept property safe. A top-level answer ranks these: the first two are the whole argument and the rest are steps in it.
Q: [10 marks] ★ Read Practice extract 2. Explain what the historian's **approach** is, using the extract. Do not use a label unless the extract supports it.
A: The historian asks what destroyed the monarchy, and looks first at the state: its debts, its failed reforms and the political quarrel with the privileged, not at classes. The historian is replying to "the old stories" that the Old Regime was doomed and rigid, and stresses the contingency of choices ("the choices that individuals made inside it"). Approach: political and financial, interested in decisions and crises, and sceptical of long-term class explanations. The label "revisionist" is supported only by "less rigid than the old stories suggest" and "more than out of a long conflict of classes"; a good answer says why (the extract sets out to correct an older view) and does not guess an ideology that the extract does not show.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Practice extract 3 argues that the language of 1789 was itself the revolution. Name **three** facts from 1789 that you could use to explain or test this, and say what each does.
A: Any three, each linked: (1) Sieyès's *What is the Third Estate?* (January 1789) with its question about the Third Estate's place "in the political order", which explains how the vocabulary of the nation drove events; (2) the Third Estate's declaration of 17 June 1789 that it was the National Assembly, which fits the idea that a claim to speak for the nation was revolutionary; (3) Article 3 and Article 6 of the Declaration of the Rights of Man (26 August 1789: sovereignty in the nation; law as the general will), which show the vocabulary written into law; to **test** the claim: the hunger of 1788–89 and the Great Fear show that material grievances also drove the crowds, which the extract leaves out. The top-level skill is saying whether each fact explains, tests or exposes what is omitted.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Use the four reasons for disagreement to explain why Practice extracts 1 and 2 reach different conclusions about the origins of the Revolution.
A: Selection and interpretation: Extract 1 chooses the economy, classes and popular risings; Extract 2 chooses the state's finances and the privileged orders' resistance. Both use real evidence. Passage of time: extract 2 answers older accounts such as extract 1's ("the old stories"), using later research on the Old Regime. Time and place: Lefebvre wrote in 1930s France, a decade of class politics; Doyle wrote in Britain in 1980, after the rise of revisionism. Fragmentary evidence: neither can know the thoughts of the crowd or of the king, so both infer motives. A top-level answer says how each reason *produced* a difference, not only that it exists, and notes that the two extracts are not necessarily incompatible: one explains why a crisis occurred, the other who benefited.
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract 1. What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the French Revolution to explain your answer.
H: Write the main interpretation in one sentence with two aspects (the Revolution as several revolutions; the bourgeoisie as the main beneficiary).
H: List the knowledge you would use to explain each aspect: the aristocratic revolt of 1787–88, the hunger of 1788–89, the Great Fear and the August Decrees.
H: Then test the claim that the bourgeoisie gained most: who were the deputies, and what did the August Decrees do to dues?
A: Level 6 explains both aspects as one argument. Aspect 1: the Revolution as a combination of separate revolts, each from its own social group, so that no single actor made it (knowledge: the Notables and Parlement of Paris in 1787–88, Vizille, the Day of the Tiles; the hunger of 1788–89 and the Réveillon riots; the Great Fear and the peasants burning the *terriers*). Aspect 2: the bourgeoisie as chief gainer and the end of the feudal order (knowledge: the Third Estate's deputies, mostly lawyers and officials; the Ségur Ordinance of 1781; the August Decrees, which abolished personal servitude and the tithe and made most dues redeemable; the Declaration's protection of property in Article 17). Tests: the August Decrees did not give the peasants all they wanted (dues were redeemable until 1793), which fits an extract that sees the bourgeois leaders as protecting property; but the "class" of the deputies is more complicated, since liberal nobles and parish priests played a part. Approach: social and economic, focused on classes and on "the crowd as well as the deputies"; the closing sentence shows an interest in history from below. The label "social interpretation" or "class-based" is supported by the extract's language of class; a political label ("Marxist") is better left out or explained by the extract's reference to a class winning over a "feudal order". A weaker answer treats the crowd paragraph as the main message (Level 3) or explains the aristocratic revolt without linking it to the bourgeois victory (Level 4/5).
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract 2. What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the French Revolution to explain your answer.
H: The main interpretation has two linked aspects: what did not cause the Revolution, and what did.
H: Which of the extract's claims can you test with the 1788 budget, the parlements and the 1614 ruling?
H: What does the extract leave out that other historians would stress?
A: Main interpretation: the Old Regime was not doomed, and the Revolution came from the political and financial collapse of the state and the choices made inside it. Knowledge to explain: the 1788 budget (629 million livres spending, 503 million revenue, a deficit of 126 million, 318 million on interest); Calonne's land tax and the Notables in 1787; the parlements' demand for the Estates-General; the aristocratic revolt of 1787–88; the king's decision on 8 August 1788; the dispute over voting by head, the 1614 ruling of 25 September 1788. To test "wealthy and growing, less rigid": France was Europe's second most populous state with about 28 million people (so power and wealth were real), but the Ségur Ordinance of 1781, the 1614 ruling and the bread price (8 to 14½ sous in Paris from August 1788 to February 1789) show strains the extract does not mention. Approach: political-institutional, interested in decisions, crisis management and contingency; sceptical of "the old stories". "Revisionist" can be supported from the extract's own words on the old stories; no ideology should be named. Level 6 explains both aspects together; Level 5 treats the finance narrative as the whole.
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract 3. What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the French Revolution to explain your answer.
H: Two aspects: the class explanation fails because it treats ideas as a mask; the Revolution's language carried its own logic towards terror.
H: What does "the language itself was the revolution" mean for the evidence the historian will use?
H: Which events of 1789 would you use, and what does the extract leave out?
A: Main interpretation: the Revolution is not best explained as a conflict of classes, because the ideas and words of the revolutionaries (the nation, the general will, popular sovereignty) were not a mask for class interest but the driving force, giving a political culture whose logic led from reform towards terror. Knowledge: Sieyès's pamphlet; the declaration of the National Assembly on 17 June; the Tennis Court Oath; the Declaration of the Rights of Man (Article 3: sovereignty in the nation; Article 6: law as the expression of the general will); the later Terror (see [France (3)](#hist.eu.france.3)). Tests: the Third Estate's and the crowd's actions in 1789 were fed by hunger and fear of a plot (the Great Fear), which suggests material causes also mattered. Approach: intellectual and cultural, reads what revolutionaries said as the subject of history, attacks a "catechism" (the extract's own word). Level 6 links the two aspects (ideas drive the logic) and explains the approach; Level 5 explains the attack on the class explanation but not the claim about the logic of political culture.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ A student writes: "Practice extract 1 is a Marxist interpretation." Is this label supported by the extract? Explain how you would decide.
A: The extract speaks of classes, the economy and a "feudal order" replaced by a "new class", which resembles the vocabulary of class analysis, so a label such as "social" or "class-based" is supported. "Marxist" names an ideology and a theory of history; the extract does not mention Marx, historical stages or class struggle, so a safer statement is that its approach is *close to* a Marxist-style social analysis. The mark scheme credits a label only when it is appropriate to the extract and supported from it: write the label and the *because*.

:::recap
- **Paper 3**: A Level only; 1 hour 15 minutes; 40 marks; one question from one section (topic); one unseen extract of at most 600 words, author not named. Table A (AO4) 20 + Table B (AO1) 20.
- **The question**: what can you learn about the **interpretation** (the historian's claims and conclusions) and **approach** (what they bring: questions, interests, ideology and beliefs); use the extract and knowledge. Not "evaluate", "compare" or "name the author".
- **Why historians disagree**: fragmentary evidence; selection and interpretation; the passage of time; the time and place in which they work.
- **Method**: read twice; find the main interpretation (usually two linked aspects); test each paragraph (main message or sub-message); explain each aspect with precise knowledge; end with the approach.
- **Levels (Table A)**: 6 explains all elements and the approach; 5 explains elements but not the whole; 4 identifies without explaining; 3 explains only sub-messages or a different interpretation; 2 summarises; 1 refers to aspects.
- **Knowledge (Table B)**: used to explain, test or place a claim; detailed, accurate and relevant at the top.
- **Labels**: credited only when supported from the extract.
