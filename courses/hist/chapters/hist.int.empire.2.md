:::why
Between 1880 and 1914 the great powers of Europe divided a continent among themselves without going to war over it. That is remarkable, and it needs explaining. Yet the same years saw their relations grow steadily worse, and by 1914 Europe was split into two armed camps. This chapter asks how far the competition for colonies was the cause: what the powers did when their claims collided in Africa, how they patched up some quarrels and made others worse, and why colonial deals produced the alliances that went to war in 1914. It is also a lesson in weighing a cause that was important, but not the only one.

## The Berlin Conference, 1884–85

### Why it was held: the purpose

By 1884 the powers were already quarrelling over the Congo and the Niger, the two great river highways into the interior. Three things came together.

**The Congo dispute.** Leopold II's International Association of the Congo, working through Stanley, had been making treaties with chiefs since 1879. The French explorer Pierre Savorgnan de Brazza had made a treaty with the Teke king Makoko in 1880 and claimed the north bank for France. Portugal claimed the mouth of the river on the strength of its old coastal forts. In **February 1884** Britain and Portugal signed a treaty by which Britain would recognise Portugal's claim, in return for rights of trade and free navigation. France, Germany and Leopold all protested that this would hand the key to the Congo to a weak state under British influence. Britain, facing a storm of opposition, let the treaty drop in June 1884. The powers now needed rules to prevent the Congo from becoming a cause of war.

**Pressures on Bismarck.** Chancellor Otto von Bismarck had said for years that he did not want colonies, because they cost money and could cause quarrels. By 1884 he had changed course, for several reasons that historians weigh differently:
- *Merchants and colonial societies.* Hamburg and Bremen firms trading in West Africa (such as Woermann), and the colonial associations that had formed since 1882, wanted protection. In April 1884 Bismarck put the claims of the Bremen merchant Adolf Lüderitz in South West Africa under German protection, and in July 1884 Gustav Nachtigal raised the German flag in Togo and Cameroon. Bismarck's way was cheap: the state would protect the flag, and merchants and chartered companies would pay.
- *Elections.* A Reichstag election was due in October 1884, and colonial protection was a popular cause. Some historians see this as "social imperialism": using empire to win support at home.
- *Diplomacy.* Britain's occupation of Egypt in 1882 had angered France. Bismarck saw a chance to work with France against British dominance and to keep France's attention away from the lost provinces of Alsace-Lorraine. He had also seen that German traders needed free access to the Congo and Niger, which a British, French or Portuguese monopoly would shut. As he is reported to have said in 1888, his "map of Africa" lay in Europe: colonies mattered for what they did to relations between the powers.
Bismarck therefore invited the powers, working with the French Prime Minister Jules Ferry, to meet in Berlin. The conference opened on **15 November 1884** and the General Act was signed on **26 February 1885**, 103 days later.

**The intentions of different powers.** Fourteen states attended: Germany, Austria-Hungary, Belgium, Denmark, France, Britain, Italy, the Netherlands, Portugal, Russia, Spain, Sweden-Norway, the Ottoman Empire and the United States. No African ruler was invited. Their aims differed:

| Power | What it wanted at Berlin |
|---|---|
| Germany (Bismarck) | Free trade for German merchants; no British monopoly on the Niger or Congo; good relations with France; a lead in setting the rules |
| Britain | Free trade and navigation (its merchants dominated the Niger); to stop Portugal and France closing the rivers; to prevent any great rival controlling the Congo; its position in Egypt, which was also under dispute |
| France | To confirm Brazza's claim north of the Congo; to limit British influence; to keep a first option to buy the Congo if Leopold failed |
| Leopold II (the International Association) | Recognition as a sovereign state, which he secured by promising free trade and by winning support from the United States (April 1884), France and Germany |
| Portugal | To keep its claim to the mouth of the Congo, which it largely lost |
| The United States | Free trade; it had recognised Leopold's Association in April 1884 but did not ratify the Act later |

:::key Why the Berlin Conference was held
1. **To settle the Congo dispute** before it led to war: the Anglo-Portuguese treaty of February 1884 had united France, Germany and Leopold against Britain.
2. **Bismarck's reasons:** pressure from merchants and colonial societies; the election of October 1884; and his wish to work with France against Britain while keeping Germany at the centre of European diplomacy.
3. **Free trade and navigation:** every power with traders wanted the Congo and Niger kept open.
4. **Rules for new claims:** the powers feared that unregulated grabbing would cause quarrels.
Most important: the aim was to *prevent* conflict among the powers, not to start the partition of Africa.
:::

### What it decided: the outcome

The **General Act of the Berlin Conference** (26 February 1885) had these main parts:

- **Free trade in the Congo basin** (Articles 1–5): "The trade of all nations shall enjoy complete freedom" in the whole basin of the Congo, a region that reached from the Atlantic to the Indian Ocean coast. No import duties were to be charged for twenty years.
- **Free navigation** on the Congo (Articles 13–25) and the Niger (Articles 26–33) for the ships of all nations.
- **End of slavery** (Articles 6 and 9): the powers in the Congo basin "bind themselves to watch over the preservation of the native tribes, and to care for the improvement of the conditions of their moral and material well-being, and to help in suppressing slavery, and especially the Slave Trade" (Article 6), and declared that the territories "may not serve as a market or means of transit for the trade in slaves" (Article 9). Missionaries and explorers were to be protected.
- **Effective occupation** (Articles 34 and 35). The chapter heading refers to the conditions in which new occupations on the coast "may be held to be effective". A power taking land on the African coast, or declaring a protectorate, had to notify the others (Article 34), and the signatories recognised "the obligation to insure the establishment of authority in the regions occupied by them on the coasts of the African Continent sufficient to protect existing rights, and, as the case may be, freedom of trade and of transit" (Article 35).
- **Recognition of King Leopold's Congo claim.** The conference recognised the flag of the International Association of the Congo and its territories. The name Congo Free State was announced in August 1885, and Leopold became its sovereign ruler.

:::def Effective occupation
The principle, set out in Articles 34 and 35 of the General Act of Berlin (1885), that a claim to African territory counts only if the claimant has established enough real authority to protect existing rights, and has notified the other powers. In the Act it applied to claims on the *coasts*.
:::

:::def General Act of the Berlin Conference
The treaty signed on 26 February 1885 by fourteen states, which set rules for trade and navigation on the Congo and Niger, condemned the slave trade, and set conditions for new claims on the African coast. It was signed by European states, the Ottoman Empire and the United States, and no African ruler took part.
:::

### How much did Berlin matter?

A common mistake is to say "Berlin divided up Africa". It did not. The conference did not draw a map, and most borders were fixed in later treaties between pairs of powers (Britain and Germany in 1890, France and Britain in 1898–99, and so on). Article 35 applied only to the coasts, which left the interior open to the "hinterland" claims that powers made by sending expeditions and signing treaties with chiefs. Even so, Berlin mattered in four ways:
1. It gave a **legal code for claiming land** and so removed some causes of quarrel. The result was that the powers could compete without fighting.
2. It **legitimised Leopold's Congo**. The Free State's promises of free trade and care for Africans were ignored: by the early 1890s Leopold had given himself a monopoly of rubber and ivory (see [chapter 1](#hist.int.empire.1)). The Act had no way of enforcing its own terms.
3. It **raised the stakes**. The rule that claims must be backed by occupation made speed important, and powers hurried to put flags and officials on the ground. In the next fifteen years most of the continent was claimed.
4. It showed that Africans' interests were secondary. The humanitarian clauses were used later as a moral cover.

:::warn Do not write "Bismarck called the conference to carve up Africa"
Bismarck and Ferry called it to settle the rules for the Congo and Niger and to prevent conflict. The "carve-up" came later, in many separate agreements, and the Act itself created almost no new colonies. A precise sentence earns credit: "Berlin recognised Leopold's claim and set a rule for claims on the coast, which encouraged a rush to occupy the interior."
:::

## Rivalries between colonial powers

The Berlin rules kept the peace in the 1880s. In the 1890s and 1900s the places of friction moved: first to the Nile and southern Africa, then to Morocco, and in every case another force joined the colonial motive, the German wish for world power.

### Britain and Germany: Weltpolitik and naval rivalry

In 1890 relations between Britain and Germany were friendly. They settled East African boundaries in the **Heligoland-Zanzibar treaty** of 1 July 1890 (Britain took the protectorate over Zanzibar, Germany took Heligoland). Over the next twenty-four years the two became rivals.

:::def Weltpolitik
"World policy": the German policy, adopted after about 1897 under Kaiser Wilhelm II and his advisers Bernhard von Bülow and Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, of making Germany a world power through colonies, trade and a large navy, rather than only a continental power.
:::

**The turning point of the 1890s.** After Bismarck was dismissed in 1890, Wilhelm II wanted Germany to have "its place in the sun". On **6 December 1897** Bernhard von Bülow, the Foreign Secretary (Chancellor from 1900), told the Reichstag, in a debate on Germany's seizure of the Chinese port of Kiaochow: "we do not want to put anyone in our shadow, but we also demand our place in the sun." The phrase became the slogan of Weltpolitik. At the same time Tirpitz, the Secretary of State of the Navy Office from June 1897, planned a battle fleet.

**The Kruger telegram, January 1896.** The first British–German clash came over South Africa. Cecil Rhodes's friend Dr Leander Starr Jameson led about 600 men from the Cape into the Transvaal on 29 December 1895, hoping to spark a rising against President Paul Kruger's Boer government; he was captured within days. On **3 January 1896** Wilhelm II sent Kruger a telegram congratulating him on restoring peace "without appealing to friendly powers" for help, which British opinion read as a hint that Germany would support the Boers against Britain. The British press was furious, crowds attacked German property in London, and the government assembled a "flying squadron" of warships. In fact Germany had no plan to help the Transvaal, and Wilhelm's telegram was an impulsive gesture; the Chancellor had pressed him to tone it down. But it left lasting bitterness.

**The Boer War, 1899–1902.** Britain went to war with the Transvaal and the Orange Free State on **11 October 1899**, and after hard fighting won, by the Treaty of Vereeniging (31 May 1902). The war showed how isolated Britain was. Most European opinion, and especially German opinion, was pro-Boer. Germany stayed neutral and refused the Boers' appeals, but Britain had been shown that it had no friends. Meanwhile Britain and Germany had made a secret agreement in 1898 about what to do if Portugal's African colonies collapsed. Tension over South Africa was a cause of the change in British policy that followed (see below).

**The naval race.** The real danger was the fleet. Germany's **First Navy Law** (28 March 1898) provided for 19 battleships, and the **Second Navy Law** (14 June 1900) doubled the programme to 38. Tirpitz's "risk theory" was that a German fleet strong enough to make any attack on it costly would force Britain to be friendly. The British saw it differently: a navy built close to home was aimed at them. They had always kept a navy as large as the next two combined (the "two-power standard"), and the new German fleet threatened it. In 1906 Britain launched HMS *Dreadnought* (10 February), a battleship so far ahead of all others that it made the existing ones obsolete. Germany followed, and both built dreadnoughts as fast as they could afford them. Tension over the fleet grew, though it was not itself colonial: the Germans said they needed a fleet to protect their trade and colonies, and the British said the real danger was to the British Isles.

:::key Why Britain and Germany became rivals
1. **Weltpolitik:** Germany, a latecomer with few colonies, demanded a share of world power. Britain, with the largest empire, felt challenged.
2. **South Africa:** the Jameson Raid, the Kruger telegram and the Boer War poisoned opinion on both sides.
3. **The naval race:** the German Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 and the Dreadnought of 1906.
4. **Mutual suspicion:** each saw the other's actions as aggressive (see the Crowe Memorandum below).
Only the first two were colonial in origin. Careful answers say that colonial rivalry fed an antagonism whose main cause was Germany's growing power and fleet.
:::

### Britain and France: the Sudan and the Fashoda Incident

France and Britain had been colonial rivals for two centuries, and Egypt in 1882 made it worse. In the 1890s France wanted to link its colonies from the Atlantic across the Sahel to the Red Sea, and to establish a French presence on the upper Nile, which would challenge Britain in Egypt. Britain wanted a continuous line from the Cape to Cairo, and was committed to controlling the Nile, on which Egypt depended. The two lines crossed on the Nile.

In 1896 Captain Jean-Baptiste Marchand left Libreville with about 120 African riflemen and a dozen French officers and NCOs, and spent two years crossing central Africa. On **10 July 1898** he raised the French flag at **Fashoda**, on the White Nile. On **2 September 1898** Kitchener's Anglo-Egyptian army destroyed the Mahdists at Omdurman. Kitchener then took five gunboats up the Nile and reached Fashoda on **19 September**. The two commanders behaved politely, and each referred the matter to his government. Britain's foreign secretary, Lord Salisbury, insisted that the whole Nile valley was a British sphere and that the French must go. France's foreign minister, **Théophile Delcassé**, who had come to office in June 1898, knew that the French navy could not match the Royal Navy and that France was divided by the Dreyfus affair. He was also coming to think that Germany, not Britain, was France's greater danger. On 3 November 1898 the French government ordered the withdrawal, and Marchand left Fashoda in December. By the Anglo-French Convention of 21 March 1899, France accepted a frontier at the watershed between the Nile and the Congo, which kept it out of the Nile valley.

Fashoda brought the two countries close to war, and left French public opinion hostile to Britain. But the crisis also *ended* one quarrel: it settled the Sudan, and Delcassé drew the conclusion that an agreement with Britain would be better than a rivalry. Most historians see the settlement as a first step to the Entente Cordiale five years later.

### France and Germany: Morocco

Morocco was the last independent state in north-west Africa, and France, which held neighbouring Algeria and Tunisia, wanted to extend its influence. Germany had trading interests there and wanted to prevent France from gaining a closed zone. Under the Entente Cordiale of 1904 Britain agreed to leave France a free hand in Morocco (see below). Germany had not been consulted.

- **The First Moroccan Crisis (1905).** On **31 March 1905** the Kaiser landed at Tangier and declared his support for the independence of the Sultan, Abdelaziz. It was a challenge to France, and perhaps a test of the new Anglo-French friendship. Germany demanded an international conference. Delcassé fell from office in June 1905. The **Algeciras Conference** (16 January–7 April 1906, 13 states) confirmed the independence of the Sultan and the "open door" for trade, but gave France and Spain the police in the Moroccan ports and left France with the leading role in Moroccan finance. Britain, Russia, Italy, Spain and the United States all backed France; only Austria-Hungary supported Germany. The crisis had the opposite effect to what Germany wanted: it strengthened the Entente, and British and French officers began secret military talks.
- **The Second Moroccan Crisis, or Agadir Crisis (1911).** In spring 1911 a rebellion against the Sultan led France to send troops to Fez. The German Foreign Secretary, Alfred von Kiderlen-Wächter, decided to demand compensation. On **1 July 1911** he sent the gunboat *Panther* to the Moroccan port of Agadir, supposedly to protect German firms. Germany's real aim was to force France to give up land in the French Congo. The British government, afraid that Germany wanted a naval base on the Atlantic coast, was alarmed. On **21 July 1911** the Chancellor of the Exchequer, David Lloyd George, gave a speech at the Mansion House in London. He said that if peace could only be kept by letting Britain be treated "as if she were of no account in the Cabinet of nations", then "peace at that price would be a humiliation intolerable for a great country like ours to endure". The German government took it as a warning. After four months of negotiation France and Germany signed a convention on **4 November 1911** (see below).

## Attempts to resolve tensions after 1900

### The end of "splendid isolation"

:::def Splendid isolation
The phrase for Britain's nineteenth-century policy of avoiding permanent alliances with European powers, relying on its navy and empire. It ended in the years 1902–07, when Britain made agreements with Japan, France and Russia.
:::

By 1900 Britain's isolation looked dangerous. It was hated by much of Europe over the Boer War, it had a vast empire to defend, and its rivals (France in Africa, Russia in Asia, Germany at sea) were all challenging it. The first step was the **Anglo-Japanese Alliance** of **30 January 1902**. Each power promised to help if the other were attacked by two powers in East Asia. It protected Britain's interests in China against Russia and let Britain move its warships home. It was the first British peacetime alliance for nearly a century.

### The Anglo-French Entente, 1904

Britain also made an agreement with its old rival. After Fashoda the way was clear, and King Edward VII's visit to Paris in May 1903 helped. The foreign ministers, Delcassé and Lord Lansdowne, negotiated the **Entente Cordiale**, signed on **8 April 1904**. It was not an alliance: it was a set of agreements that settled colonial disputes. In Article 1 Britain declared that it had "no intention of altering the political status of Egypt", and France that it would "not obstruct the action of Great Britain in that country". In Article 2 France declared that it had no intention of altering the political status of Morocco, and Britain accepted that France, as a neighbour, should "preserve order in that country, and to provide assistance". There were also deals on Newfoundland fisheries, West Africa, Siam and Madagascar. Secret articles dealt with what would happen if either country's position changed.

The Entente was a **colonial settlement**: Egypt for Britain, Morocco for France. But its effect was larger. It removed an old quarrel, made Britain and France friends and, after the Moroccan crises, close partners.

### The Algeciras Conference, 1906

The Algeciras Conference, called by Germany to challenge France in Morocco, ended in a settlement that favoured France and showed how isolated Germany was (see above). Its Act, signed on 7 April 1906, promised the independence of the Sultan and equal trade rights, but gave French and Spanish officers the police in the ports and made the French the leading partner in a new State Bank of Morocco. It did *not* end German resentment.

### The Anglo-Russian Convention, 1907

On **31 August 1907** Britain and Russia settled their quarrels in Persia (divided into spheres of influence), Afghanistan and Tibet. Russia had been weakened by defeat by Japan (1904–05) and revolution (1905), and needed peace in Asia; Britain wanted to remove a long-standing threat to India. With the Franco-Russian alliance (1894) and the Entente, Britain was now linked to both of France's partners. The three powers became known as the **Triple Entente**. Germans called it "encirclement".

### The convention of November 1911

After the *Panther* sailed to Agadir, the French and German governments bargained for four months. By the **convention of 4 November 1911** Germany recognised France's freedom of action in Morocco; France made Morocco a protectorate (by the Treaty of Fez, 30 March 1912). In return, France gave Germany about 275,000 square kilometres of the French Congo, called "New Cameroon" (*Neukamerun*), which connected Germany's Cameroon to the Congo River, and Germany gave France a small area. Germans were disappointed: they had wanted more, and public opinion thought the government had been humiliated. The agreement settled the immediate dispute but left bad feeling in both countries.

:::key Did the attempts to resolve tension work?
**Yes, in part:** they ended particular quarrels (Egypt and Morocco, Persia, the Sudan, Morocco again in 1911), and they showed that colonial rivalries could be settled by bargaining.
**No, in the long run:** the settlements divided Europe into two blocs; Germany felt excluded and its fleet and army grew; each Moroccan crisis made the Entente stronger and Germany angrier; and the colonial deals did not touch the larger rivalries (the naval race, Alsace-Lorraine, the Balkans). A judgement worth defending: colonial agreements removed old causes of conflict between Britain and France but created a new alignment against Germany.
:::

## The situation in 1914

### The extent of European empires

By 1914 the world had been divided. Figures from the German statistician Hübner, printed in Lenin's *Imperialism* (1916), give a picture, though they are rough, and modern scholars give different totals:

|---|---|---|---|
| Britain | 22.5 | 33.5 | 393.5 |
| France | 0.9 | 10.6 | 55.5 |
| Russia | 17.0 | 17.4 | 33.2 |

Britain's empire, with the home islands, covered about a quarter of the world's land and a little over a quarter of its people. France's colonies were twelve times as large in 1914 as in 1876. Germany, a nation of 65 million people, had acquired 2.9 million square kilometres in thirty years, much of it in Africa and the Pacific, but most of these colonies cost the German state more than they earned. ### The influence of empire on alliances

Colonial deals helped to make the alliance system of 1914:
- The **Triple Alliance** (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy, 1882) came from European concerns, not colonial ones; so did the **Franco-Russian alliance** (1894), made because France needed an ally against Germany.
- The **Entente Cordiale** (1904) and the **Anglo-Russian Convention** (1907) were colonial settlements. They brought Britain close to France and Russia, and so linked Britain to the continental alliance against Germany.
- The **Moroccan crises** pushed Britain and France together: military and naval talks followed (a secret Anglo-French naval agreement in 1912, in which the Royal Navy would guard the Channel and the French fleet would concentrate in the Mediterranean).

| Link | Date | Between | Origin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Triple Alliance | 1882 | Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy | European (Italy's quarrel with France over Tunisia in 1881 was colonial) |
| Franco-Russian alliance | 1894 | France, Russia | European (France needed an ally against Germany) |
| Entente Cordiale | 1904 | Britain, France | Colonial settlement, then strengthened by Morocco |
| Anglo-Russian Convention | 1907 | Britain, Russia | Colonial settlement (Persia, Afghanistan, Tibet) |

So the colonial scramble did not *cause* the alliances, but it affected which powers were in which. Colonial rivalry between Britain and France in 1898 would have been a normal reason for them to be on opposite sides in 1914; colonial settlement turned them into partners. And Germany, which had few colonies and felt shut out of the world, believed that Britain, France and Russia were united against it.

Two points temper this. First, by 1914 the colonial quarrels between Britain and Germany were largely settled: in 1913–14 the two governments were close to agreements on the Portuguese colonies and the Baghdad Railway. Second, war came in 1914 from a crisis in the Balkans, not Africa, and the colonies were drawn in afterwards.

### Contemporary views and verdicts

**Contemporary supporters of empire** saw it as a duty and a sign of greatness (Kipling in Britain, Ferry in France, Bülow and the Pan-German League in Germany: in 1890 a letter from a Dr Hugenberg, printed in the League's programme, said "he who seizes these territories quickest and holds fast the most tenaciously will possess them"). **Critics** blamed it for international tension. The radical economist **J.A. Hobson** argued in 1902 that imperialism served a few investors and risked war; the journalist **E.D. Morel** attacked the Congo; **Norman Angell**'s *The Great Illusion* (1909) argued that war between industrial powers could not pay. In the Foreign Office, **Eyre Crowe**'s famous memorandum of 1 January 1907 asked whether Germany was aiming at a political hegemony, and argued that British policy must therefore take account of the German fleet.

**Verdicts of historians** differ.
- **Lenin** in 1916 said that the war was an imperialist war to redivide the world among the great powers.
- **Robinson and Gallagher** (1961) saw British policy as guided by the defence of strategic routes, especially the Nile and Suez, so that quarrels such as Fashoda were defensive, not greedy.
- **Paul Kennedy**, *The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism, 1860–1914* (1980), argued that the antagonism had many causes, among which the colonial disputes mattered less than the naval race and Germany's wider challenge to Britain's position.
- **Christopher Clark**, *The Sleepwalkers* (2012), argues that the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911 mattered because of what they taught leaders: that they could be pushed to the edge of war and survive, and that the powers' faith in diplomacy was wearing away.

:::key How far was colonial rivalry the main cause of tension between the powers?
**For:** it created every crisis from 1896 to 1911 (the Kruger telegram, Fashoda, Morocco twice); it made old rivals (Britain and France) hostile until 1898 and then drove the colonial deals that formed the Entente; it fed German resentment and the demand for "a place in the sun".
**Against:** the most serious tension (the Anglo-German naval race, Germany's power in Europe, Alsace-Lorraine, the Balkans) was not colonial; the colonial disputes were mostly settled by negotiation and, between Britain and Germany, nearly settled by 1914; and the crises of 1905 and 1911 were about Germany's wish to test the Entente as much as about Morocco.
**A judgement worth defending:** colonial rivalry was the *occasion* for most crises and the means by which the blocs formed, but not the deepest cause of the tension: that lay in the shifting balance of power in Europe.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the Berlin Conference was held in 1884–85.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The Berlin Conference was held because the European powers wanted to share out Africa. Bismarck wanted colonies for Germany. Leopold II wanted the Congo. Britain and Portugal had a treaty about the Congo. The conference was held in Berlin from 1884 to 1885 and the powers agreed rules for the Congo."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes. "Share out Africa" is a common mistake (the conference did not divide Africa), and none of the facts is explained as a reason. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was the dispute over the Congo. In February 1884 Britain and Portugal signed a treaty that would have recognised Portuguese control of the mouth of the Congo. France, Germany and Leopold II's International Association all protested, because it would have given a weak country, backed by Britain, the key to the whole river. Britain dropped the treaty in June, but the powers now needed agreed rules to stop the Congo from becoming a cause of war. A second reason was Bismarck. By 1884 he had changed from opposing colonies to protecting German merchants' claims in South West Africa, Togo and Cameroon, partly because of pressure from merchants and colonial societies and partly for the election of October 1884. He wanted free trade for German firms on the Congo and Niger, and he saw a chance to work with France's Jules Ferry against British influence."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was Leopold II's search for recognition. Since 1879 Stanley had been making treaties for him on the Congo, but he was a private man with no state. In 1884 he persuaded the United States (April) and France (with a first option to buy) to recognise his Association, and he promised free trade, which made him acceptable to Germany and Britain. The conference was his chance to obtain legal recognition from all the powers.

These reasons were linked. The Anglo-Portuguese treaty showed that the Congo was a danger to peace; Bismarck had the position and the motive to call a conference, because Germany had no past claims there and wanted free trade and good relations with France; and Leopold's claim gave the powers a way to settle the dispute without giving the river to a rival, because a small, weak Association was safer for everyone than a rival's colony. The decisive reason was the Congo dispute, since it was the immediate threat to peace, and the other factors determined who called the conference and who benefited. The conference was held to prevent a quarrel among the powers, not to partition Africa."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the dispute created the need; Bismarck supplied the initiative; Leopold's claim supplied the solution), with a supported conclusion. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

... we do not want to put anyone in our shadow, but we also demand our place in the sun. ... A division of our cruisers was dispatched to and occupied the port of Kiaochow ... we have only benevolent and kind intentions toward China ...

*Extracts from a speech by Bernhard von Bülow, German Foreign Secretary, to the Reichstag, 6 December 1897, in the English translation by Adam Blauhut in German History in Documents and Images (from the Reichstag's official record). Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Source B**

... we have conquered for ourselves a place in the sun. ... In spite of the fact that we have no such fleet as we should have, we have conquered for ourselves a place in the sun. ... this place in the sun shall remain our undisputed possession ... for our future lies upon the water ...

*Extracts from a speech by Kaiser Wilhelm II to the North German Regatta Association, 1901, in C. Gauss, The German Kaiser as Shown in His Public Utterances (1915). Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about German aims in world affairs around 1900.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Both sources talk about a place in the sun, so Germany wanted colonies. Source A is a speech by Bülow and Source B is a speech by the Kaiser. Source B mentions a fleet but Source A does not, so they are different. Both are speeches so they might be biased."

*Commentary.* One similarity and one difference, both supported. The comment on bias is generic. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"Both sources use the same slogan and show a Germany that demanded a share of world power. Source A, from 1897, says 'we also demand our place in the sun', and Source B, from 1901, says Germany has 'conquered for ourselves a place in the sun' and that it 'shall remain our undisputed possession'. In both, the aim is to win status as a world power through overseas expansion, and in both the setting is a recent German gain: the occupation of Kiaochow in A, and the 'conquest' in B.

The differences are in tone and in the means. Bülow is reassuring: Germany 'does not want to put anyone in our shadow', its cruisers merely 'occupied' Kiaochow, and its intentions towards China are 'benevolent and kind'. The Kaiser is boastful: he speaks of having 'conquered' the place and of an 'undisputed possession', and he adds that Germany still lacks the fleet 'it should have' and that 'our future lies upon the water'. Bülow speaks of ports and trade; the Kaiser links expansion to sea power.

These differences are explained by purpose and audience. Bülow was a Foreign Secretary speaking to the Reichstag, only three weeks after the seizure of Kiaochow (in November 1897), defending a risky act to deputies, some of whom were doubtful about colonies and costs, and trying not to alarm other powers; the reassurances were meant to calm Britain, Russia and Japan as much as the Reichstag. In 1897 Tirpitz had just become head of the Navy Office and the First Navy Law was not passed until March 1898, so Bülow could not yet openly talk of the fleet. The Kaiser, speaking in 1901 to a body of yachtsmen and sailing enthusiasts, had a naval audience and his own passion for the navy; the Second Navy Law had been passed in 1900, so a boast about the future fleet suited his purpose. The Kaiser was also known for speaking on impulse, which made his speeches less guarded than a minister's.

As evidence, then, they are complementary. Source A is better evidence of how the German government presented its policy to the world, carefully and defensively, and Source B is better evidence of the Kaiser's own ambitions and the naval aims behind them. Together they show that Germany's aim of world power was consistent between 1897 and 1901, while the way it was expressed depended on who was speaking and to whom."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the Kiaochow seizure, the Navy Laws) and through each author's role and audience; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Colonial rivalry was the main cause of tension between the Great Powers before 1914.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the crises of 1896 (Kruger telegram), 1898 (Fashoda), 1905–06 and 1911 (Morocco) were all colonial; they poisoned Anglo-French relations until 1904 and Anglo-German relations after 1896; German resentment of "encirclement" followed from the Entente, itself a colonial deal.
- Against 1, the naval race: the Navy Laws (1898, 1900) and Dreadnought (1906) were about power at sea, not territory (Crowe, 1907).
- Against 2, Europe: Alsace-Lorraine, the Balkans and the alliance systems, which existed from 1882 and 1894 (not colonial).
- Against 3, settlement: colonial disputes were settled by negotiation (Fashoda 1899, Entente 1904, Anglo-Russian 1907, Agadir 1911), and Britain and Germany were close to agreement in 1914.
- Judgement: colonial rivalry was the occasion of the crises and shaped the blocs, but the deeper, more dangerous tension was the power rivalry in Europe. Criterion: which tensions pointed to a European war?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Colonial rivalry was a main cause of tension. In 1896 the Kaiser sent the Kruger telegram after the Jameson Raid, which angered Britain. In 1898 Britain and France nearly went to war at Fashoda. In 1905 and 1911 France and Germany fought over Morocco, and each time Britain supported France. These crises made the powers distrust each other and led them to form the Entente and the alliances. This shows that colonial rivalry was the main cause of tension."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than argues: it never explains why colonial crises matter more than other tensions. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the most dangerous tension was not colonial. The antagonism between Britain and Germany came above all from the naval race, which began with the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 and was renewed by the Dreadnought in 1906. The Foreign Office official Eyre Crowe made the point in his memorandum of January 1907: he asked whether Germany was aiming at political hegemony, and he discussed the German fleet, not German colonies. The colonial disputes between the two countries were by then smaller: they had settled East Africa in 1890 and had made an arrangement about the Portuguese colonies in 1898, and by 1914 they were near to settling the Portuguese colonies and the Baghdad Railway again. Behind the naval race was a wider shift: a united Germany with the largest army in Europe and a growing economy was challenging a European order that had Britain at its head. Alsace-Lorraine and the Balkans, where Austria-Hungary and Russia clashed, were also not colonial, and the Triple Alliance (1882) and Franco-Russian alliance (1894) both pre-dated the Entente.

Colonial rivalry did, however, shape how the tension was expressed. Each crisis of 1896–1911 was colonial in form, and the Entente, which turned Britain and France from rivals into partners, was a colonial bargain (Egypt for Morocco). The Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911 were not about Morocco itself but about whether Germany could break up the Entente, and each time they tightened it instead. Colonial deals also showed that quarrels could be settled: after 1898 Fashoda was forgotten within six years.

Overall, colonial rivalry was a main *occasion* for tension and the means by which the blocs formed, but it was not the main *cause*. If the question is which tensions made a European war likely, the answer lies in Germany's challenge to the balance of power, and the naval race in particular, with colonial crises as the arenas in which it was acted out. The statement is therefore partly true: it explains why the crises happened where they did, but not why they mattered so much."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (which tensions pointed to a European war) and separates occasion from cause. With an opening paragraph on the case for colonial rivalry, this is Level 5 (18–19).

- **A**: part of a treaty signed by fourteen states, drafted to *avoid* quarrels over African claims. It shows that in 1885 colonial rivalry was seen as something to be managed and that the powers could cooperate. It is weaker evidence for 1896–1911, when the rules were stretched or ignored.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "The sources are most useful in showing the *limits* of the view. B and C support it: B shows how a colonial incident in South Africa damaged Anglo-German relations in 1896, and C how the Agadir crisis made a British minister speak of a threat of war in 1911. They support the view only partly."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1885–1911; the answer explains where sources support and where they challenge; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

Question: "How far was the Fashoda Incident of 1898 a turning point in relations between Britain and France?" [20]

A student wrote:

"In 1898 Captain Marchand set out from Libreville with about a hundred and twenty men and spent two years crossing Africa. He reached Fashoda on 10 July 1898 and raised the French flag. **The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "Fashoda was a turning point because it settled the one quarrel that most threatened a war between Britain and France, and it made the French foreign minister, Delcassé, conclude that an agreement with Britain was better than a rivalry. Against: it did not divide Africa or draw borders (they were fixed in later bilateral treaties); the rush began before it (Egypt 1882, Lüderitz 1884, the Congo treaties from 1879); the causes were the motives (economic, strategic, national) and the means (technology, medicine) explained in chapter 1; Article 35 covered only the coasts. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "what made the partition happen when and as it did", and judges that Berlin was an accelerator and a set of rules, not the cause.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the naval race the main reason for the decline in Anglo-German relations between 1898 and 1914?
A: For: the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 and the Dreadnought of 1906 made Britain see Germany as a threat; Crowe's memorandum (1907); British concentration of the fleet in home waters; the naval talks of 1912 failed. Against: Weltpolitik and the demand for "a place in the sun"; South Africa (Jameson Raid, Kruger telegram, the Boer War); German resentment at the Entente and "encirclement"; the Moroccan crises of 1905 and 1911; trade rivalry. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. "which issue Britain could not settle by negotiation", and judges that the fleet was the issue that most affected British security, while the colonial quarrels were settled by 1914.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources B and C (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the part played by rivalry in the extension of empires.
A: Similarities: both present competition as a spur to action (B: seize "quickest"; C: no other power's troops could be allowed in Egypt). Differences: B is a call for German expansion that treats colonies as a prize in a race, aggressive in tone and written as propaganda by a nationalist society; C is a retrospective, reflective explanation of why Britain acted in 1882, defensive in tone (the aim was to stop others, not to gain) and aware of the diplomatic cost with France. Provenance: B is an 1890 letter in the programme of a pressure group wanting to shape opinion; C was written in 1908 by the official who ran Egypt, defending his government's decision, so he stresses necessity. Level 4 explains the differences through these contexts and judges which is better evidence of how rivalry worked (C shows a government reacting to rivals; B shows a lobby demanding expansion).
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). 'Fear of rivals was the main reason for the extension of European empires.' How far do these sources support this view?
H: Decide what each source says about *why* (A: markets and rivals; B: a race to seize land; C: keeping others out of Egypt; D: an agreement dividing spheres).
H: Evaluate each source by nature and purpose (A is an argument for expansion by a soldier-administrator; D is a treaty).
H: Use your own knowledge: where did fear of rivals decide events? (Egypt 1882; Berlin; Fashoda; Morocco.) Where did other motives?
A: Support: B (the race), C (keeping other troops out of Egypt), A (Germany's spending is a spur: "Germany has spent over a million sterling"). Challenge: A also gives economic reasons (markets, hostile tariffs); D shows rivalry being *settled* by agreement, and mixes it with a deal on Egypt that serves British strategic interests. Evaluation: A is a soldier and later administrator writing to persuade the British public in 1893, so it selects arguments; B is a pressure-group propaganda piece, weak evidence of what governments did, though it shows the mood; C is retrospective and justifies a decision; D is a treaty, strong evidence of what the two governments agreed in 1904. Level 5 judges: the sources show that fear of rivals was a major motive, especially for strategic decisions (Egypt), but A shows the economic motive was also stated, and D shows rivalry could be resolved; the support is substantial but not complete.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far did the Entente Cordiale remove the causes of tension between Britain and France?
H: What exactly did the 1904 agreements settle, and what did they leave open?
H: Compare relations before (Fashoda, 1898) and after (1905–14).
A: Removed: the Egypt/Morocco question (the main colonial dispute); Newfoundland, West Africa, Siam and Madagascar; the Sudan was settled in 1899. Not removed or new: the Entente was not an alliance, and its meaning depended on the Moroccan crises; British distrust of France remained among some Liberals; the Entente made Britain dependent on Germany's behaviour. Level 5: judges that it removed the colonial causes of tension but that the lasting reason for friendship was the shared fear of Germany.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'By 1914 the empires of Europe had made war between the powers more likely.' How far do you agree?
H: Define "made war more likely": through rivalries, alliances, or attitudes.
H: Weigh the colonial crises against the settlements.
A: For: colonial crises created hostility (Britain and Germany, France and Germany), blocs formed from colonial deals, German "encirclement", the Moroccan crises; empires gave a sense of power and status. Against: war came from the Balkans; empires gave powers a reason to avoid war because they had much to lose; colonial disputes were being settled (1911, 1913–14); the powers cooperated against the Boxers (1900) and at Berlin. Level 5 judges that empires shaped the alignment and mood, but war was caused by European problems.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Christopher Clark argues that the Moroccan crises were important less for what they achieved than for what they taught leaders. How far does the evidence support this view?
H: List what Britain, France and Germany *did* in 1905–06 and 1911, and what each learned.
H: Compare the short-term outcomes (Algeciras, the convention) with the effects on attitudes (German humiliation, British commitment).
A: Support: little changed in Morocco in 1905–06 (the Act confirmed French influence) and the 1911 convention gave Germany only a strip of the Congo; but the crises showed Germany that bluff could fail and that Britain would stand by France; they taught French leaders that Britain would support them and German leaders that they were isolated; they brought military talks and the naval agreement; the public mood in Germany turned to resentment. Challenge: the outcomes did matter, since France got Morocco and Germany got Neukamerun; the Entente's strengthening was a concrete result; leaders' attitudes were shaped by many other events (the naval race, the Bosnian Crisis of 1908–09). Judgement: the crises' lasting effects came from the lessons drawn (as Clark says), but the lessons had real material consequences, so the two cannot be separated.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ The Crowe Memorandum is often used as evidence of why Britain moved closer to France and Russia. How far does it explain British policy?
H: Who wrote it, for whom, and with what authority?
H: What did it argue, and what were the policy choices at the time (1907)?
A: Strengths: written by a senior Foreign Office official for the Foreign Secretary, Grey, who found it "most valuable"; it sets out a clear argument that Germany's aims, including a large fleet, could threaten British security and that the Entente should be kept; it reflects the view in the Foreign Office. Weaknesses: it was one memorandum from one department; Crowe was a known sceptic of Germany; policy was decided by Grey and the Cabinet, where the Liberals were divided and many opposed commitments; policy had already moved (the Entente of 1904, military talks in 1906) before January 1907; it frames German aims as a question and reasons from suspicion rather than evidence of intent. Judgement: it is good evidence of how the Foreign Office thought and of the arguments that influenced Grey, but weak evidence of the whole policy, which also reflected the naval race, Cabinet politics and the colonial settlements.

:::recap
- **Berlin Conference** (15 November 1884 – 26 February 1885, 14 states, no African rulers): called by Bismarck and Ferry after the Anglo-Portuguese treaty of February 1884; free trade in the Congo basin, free navigation of the Congo and Niger, a ban on the slave trade, effective occupation on the coasts (Articles 34–35), recognition of Leopold's Association. It did not divide Africa.
- **Britain and Germany:** the Kruger telegram (3 January 1896), Weltpolitik ("a place in the sun", 6 December 1897), the Navy Laws (1898, 1900), the Boer War (1899–1902), the Dreadnought (1906).
- **Britain and France:** Fashoda (Marchand 10 July 1898; Kitchener 19 September); France withdrew; convention of 21 March 1899.
- **France and Germany:** Morocco: Tangier (31 March 1905), Algeciras (16 January – 7 April 1906), Agadir (*Panther*, 1 July 1911), Mansion House speech (21 July).
- **Settlements:** Anglo-Japanese Alliance (30 January 1902), Entente Cordiale (8 April 1904), Anglo-Russian Convention (31 August 1907), Franco-German convention (4 November 1911: Morocco for France; about 275,000 km² of the French Congo for Germany).
- **1914:** Britain's empire about a quarter of the world; France's colonies 10.6 million km²; Germany's 2.9; colonial deals helped form the Triple Entente against the Triple Alliance.
- **Judgement:** colonial rivalry was the occasion of the crises and the means by which the blocs formed, but the deepest tension was Germany's challenge to the balance of power, above all at sea.
