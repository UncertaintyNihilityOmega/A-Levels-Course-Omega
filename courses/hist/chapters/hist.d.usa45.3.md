## What "change" means: four tests

The question asks *how far* the years were ones of change. To answer, you need a way of measuring change. This chapter uses four tests:
1. **Scale:** how many Americans were affected?
2. **Depth:** did people's lives, jobs, beliefs or relationships really alter, or only the objects they owned?
3. **Permanence:** did the change last beyond 1961, or was it a passing fashion?
4. **Continuity:** what stayed the same, or went backwards, for particular groups such as women, Black Americans, rural families and the poor?

The key point is that the 1950s can look like the most conservative decade of the century (the housewife in the suburban kitchen, the grey flannel suit, Eisenhower's golf) and also like one of the most changed (a nation of homeowners, drivers and television viewers, with a youth music that outraged its parents). Both pictures are true. Your job is to say which of them matters more for each area, and why.

## Consumerism and urbanisation

### Why demand boomed

:::def Consumerism
A way of life, and an economic system, in which the buying of goods and services by ordinary households drives production and gives people their status and their sense of well-being. In 1950s America it meant mass ownership of cars, appliances and televisions, backed by advertising and credit.
:::

In 1945 many experts expected a post-war slump, as had followed the First World War. About 12 million men and women were in the forces at the end of the war and were about to return to civilian life, and wartime orders were ending. Instead demand surged, for five connected reasons.
- **Wartime savings and pent-up demand.** During the war there had been well-paid work but little to buy: car factories had built tanks, new houses had been rationed by building controls, and many goods were scarce. Families emerged with savings and a long list of things they wanted.
- **Rising incomes.** Union contracts and a strong labour market pushed up wages (see the section on prosperity below).
- **The baby boom.** The number of births jumped after 1945 and stayed high, at about four million a year through the later 1950s. Every new baby needed a bigger house, furniture, food, clothes and, later, schools. Between about 1946 and 1964 some 76 million Americans were born.
- **Credit and cheap mortgages.** The GI Bill and the Federal Housing Administration (FHA) made it possible to buy a house with little or no deposit; shops and banks offered instalment credit for cars and appliances.
- **Government spending.** Defence orders, and from 1956 the highway programme, sustained jobs and incomes (see below).

The result was a long boom, interrupted only by short recessions in 1948–49, 1953–54 and 1957–58. Unemployment was mostly between about 3 and 7 per cent; it passed 7 per cent in the 1957–58 recession.

In current dollars, gross national product (GNP, the value of all goods and services produced) more than doubled between 1945 and 1960, from about 228 billion to about 546 billion, and rose by about four-fifths between 1950 and 1960. Prices also rose, so the real increase was smaller, and 1945 was inflated by wartime production. ### Cars, televisions and appliances: the economic impact

The consumer boom rested on a few big products.
- **Cars.** Passenger cars registered rose from about 25.8 million in 1945 to about 59.5 million in 1959 (US Federal Highway Administration), more than double. Car-making was the largest single industry, with supplier firms, steel, rubber, glass, oil, garages, motels and roadside restaurants depending on it. The first McDonald's franchise opened in 1955, and Holiday Inn began in 1952, both for travellers by road.
- **Television.** About 9 per cent of homes had a set in 1950; by 1960 the figure was close to nine in ten, with sources differing slightly on both numbers.

Television created a new industry (sets, networks, advertising, programme-making) and soon changed politics, sport and family life.
- **Appliances.** Refrigerators, washing machines, dryers, vacuum cleaners and, later, dishwashers spread from the better-off to ordinary families. Frozen foods and supermarkets changed shopping; "TV dinners" appeared in 1953.

:::def Service sector
The part of the economy that provides services rather than goods: shops, banks, insurance, education, health, entertainment, advertising and government. It grew faster than farming or manufacturing in the 1950s, and by the middle of the decade white-collar workers (clerks, managers, professionals) outnumbered manual workers for the first time.
:::

*Domestic market and services.* The boom was mainly a boom in the **home market**: most of what American firms made, they sold to Americans. This made the economy less dependent on exports than in the 1920s. It also created jobs outside the factories: in shops, offices, schools, hospitals and advertising agencies. Between them these changed the typical worker from a man in a factory to a person in an office or a shop, and, as we shall see, increasingly a woman.

*Technology.* Wartime research fed civilian life: the transistor (1947) led to cheap portable radios from 1954; the first commercial computers, such as UNIVAC (1951), began to be used by government and big firms; synthetic fibres and plastics filled the shops; air conditioning made hot regions habitable; Jonas Salk's polio vaccine (announced 12 April 1955) made a feared disease preventable; and passenger jets arrived at the end of the decade. Mechanisation also changed farms: tractors, combine harvesters and chemicals meant that fewer farmers grew more food, driving people off the land.

### Credit and debt

Americans had once been told to save and avoid debt. In the 1950s borrowing became normal.
- **Instalment buying** let a family take home a car, a refrigerator or a television and pay over months or years.
- The **Diners Club** card, launched in New York in February 1950, began as a charge card for restaurant meals, paid off in full each month. It was the start of the credit card. Carte Blanche and the American Express card followed in 1958, and BankAmericard, which let customers carry a balance, was launched in 1958 as well.
- **Mortgages** were the biggest debt: FHA and veterans' loans meant that a family could buy a house with a small deposit, and a mortgage lasting thirty years.

According to a study by the National Bureau of Economic Research, total consumer credit tripled during the 1950s. This helps to explain why demand grew faster than wages in some years. It also made households more exposed to a recession: in 1957–58, when unemployment rose, families with large payments were hurt. But debt in the 1950s was low by later standards, and most borrowers kept up their payments; the historian's point is about the *attitude* that changed: spending now and paying later became a normal and respectable way to live.

### The social effects of consumer culture

:::def Consumer culture
The set of values and habits that treat buying and owning goods as normal, desirable and a measure of success, spread by advertising and television. It changes how people see themselves and their neighbours as well as how they spend.
:::

Consumer culture changed how Americans lived and what they thought a good life was.
- **A shared lifestyle.** The same brands, television programmes and kinds of house spread across the country. Regional differences narrowed.
- **Advertising.** Television carried advertising into every home. Writers worried about its power: Vance Packard's *The Hidden Persuaders* (1957) argued that advertisers used psychology to manipulate buyers.
- **Consumption as citizenship.** Politicians treated mass buying as a patriotic act and as a weapon in the Cold War. In the "Kitchen Debate" of 24 July 1959, at the American National Exhibition in Moscow, Vice-President Richard Nixon stood in a model American home and argued to Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev that American capitalism gave ordinary people choice and comfort. The historian **Lizabeth Cohen**, in *A Consumers' Republic* (2003), argued that after 1945 leaders came to see mass consumption as the way to deliver both prosperity and a fairer society, and that the "purchaser as citizen" became a central ideal. She also argued that this ideal was never fully shared, because the new shopping centres and suburbs were often closed to Black Americans and to the poor.
- **Criticism.** The economist **John Kenneth Galbraith**, in *The Affluent Society* (1958), argued that the USA was rich in private goods but neglected public ones such as schools, parks and transport, an imbalance he called one of private wealth and public poverty. Writers on conformity argued that the pressure to own the right things and live the same way made people more alike and less independent.

### Urban growth: the suburbs

:::def Suburbanisation
The growth of residential areas on the edge of cities, whose residents travel to the city or to local offices and shops for work. In the USA after 1945 it was driven by the car, cheap mortgages, new roads and the baby boom.
:::

The most visible change was where Americans lived. There had been a severe housing shortage after the war: during the Depression and the war, few homes had been built, and returning veterans married and started families. The government and builders met it by building out, not up.
- **Levittown.** William Levitt and his firm Levitt and Sons built a planned community on former potato fields at Hempstead on Long Island, New York, starting in 1947. They used mass-production methods, such as teams working in sequence on standard houses, to build quickly and cheaply. By about 1951 they had built some 17,000 houses. A basic house cost $7,990, with a small deposit for veterans.
- **How it was paid for.** The **GI Bill** (1944) guaranteed home loans for veterans; the Veterans Administration backed nearly 2.4 million of them between 1944 and 1952. The FHA insured mortgages for others. Between 1940 and 1960 the proportion of American households that owned their homes rose from about 44 per cent to about 62 per cent.
- **Roads.** The car made the suburbs possible, and the government built roads for it. The **Federal-Aid Highway Act** of 29 June 1956 authorised about 41,000 miles of Interstate highway, with a Highway Trust Fund paid for by fuel taxes and the federal government meeting 90 per cent of the cost.
- **Shopping.** Shopping centres moved to the suburbs; the first enclosed mall, Southdale in Minnesota, opened in 1956.

The census shows the effect. In 1950 about 23 per cent of Americans lived in suburbs (in metropolitan areas but outside the central cities); by 1960 about 31 per cent did, and the suburban ring had grown by about half in the decade, while the central cities grew by only about a tenth, much of it by taking in surrounding land. The census has no single definition of "suburb", so the exact figures depend on how it is measured, but the direction is clear.

The suburbs were also **segregated**. Levitt's early leases barred Black buyers, and the FHA, as other lenders, marked Black and mixed neighbourhoods as poor risks ("redlining") and so denied them mortgages. In *Shelley v. Kraemer* (3 May 1948) the Supreme Court ruled that courts could not enforce racial covenants, but builders, banks and neighbours still found ways to keep suburbs white. The historian Ira Katznelson, in *When Affirmative Action Was White* (2005), argued that the GI Bill's benefits were administered so that Black veterans, especially in the South, gained far less from them than white ones.

### The rural-urban divide, the Sun Belt and the inner cities

*Leaving the land.* Mechanisation and low prices cut the number of farm workers. Many left the countryside, and more than a million African Americans left the South in each of the 1940s and 1950s, heading to Northern and Western cities (Census estimates). Rural areas, especially in the South and Appalachia, remained poorer, with worse schools and fewer hospitals, than the suburbs; this divide is part of the poverty story below.

*The South and West.* Defence contracts, cheap land, oil, air conditioning and sunshine drew people and money to California, Texas and Florida, a region later called the Sun Belt. California's population grew from about 10.6 million in 1950 to about 15.7 million in 1960, a rise of nearly half, helped by aircraft and electronics firms working for the Pentagon.

*The inner cities.* As white, better-off families moved out, the older cities of the North and Midwest lost people and tax income. Detroit's population fell from about 1.85 million in 1950 to about 1.67 million in 1960; St Louis fell from about 857,000 to about 750,000. Factories followed workers to the suburbs, leaving jobs far from the Black families who were arriving in the cities and were kept out of the suburbs. The result was the problem that came to be called the **inner-city** crisis: ageing housing, falling tax revenue, poorer schools and rising segregation. The Housing Acts of 1949 and 1954 paid for slum clearance and public housing, but in many cities clearance destroyed more homes than were built in their place, and the high-rise projects that replaced them, such as Pruitt-Igoe in St Louis (opened 1954), became symbols of the problems of the poor.

:::key Judging consumerism and urbanisation
**Evidence of great change:** GNP up from about 228 to 546 billion dollars in current prices (1945–60); passenger cars from 25.8 million to 59.5 million (1945–59); television from about 9 per cent of homes to close to nine in ten; consumer credit tripled in the 1950s; home ownership from about 44 to about 62 per cent (1940–60); suburbs from about 23 to about 31 per cent of the population (1950–60); the Interstate Highway Act of 1956.
**Evidence of limits and continuity:** the boom built on wartime industry and the New Deal; the suburbs were closed to most Black Americans; inner cities and rural areas lost out; Galbraith and Cohen show that private wealth hid public neglect.
**Judgement to defend:** the 1950s saw a **real and lasting transformation of how most Americans lived and shopped**, with limits for those left out.
:::

## The changing role and status of women

### The wartime background and the return to "normal"

During the Second World War about six million women took jobs, many in factories making aircraft, ships and weapons, and women's labour-force participation rose from roughly 28 per cent in 1940 to more than a third by 1945. Government posters used the image of "Rosie the Riveter". When the war ended, many women lost their jobs as men returned, and employers, unions and the government assumed that women would go home. Magazines, films and advertising praised the woman who made a home and brought up children.

The **historian Elaine Tyler May**, in *Homeward Bound: American Families in the Cold War Era* (1988), argued that after 1945 the Cold War made the family a symbol of security. The "domestic containment" she described was the idea that, as the USA contained Communism abroad, the nuclear family (a breadwinner, a homemaker and children, in a suburban house) would contain the dangers of the atomic age at home. The age at marriage fell, to around 20 for women and 23 for men, and the number of children rose: at its peak in 1957 an American woman could expect to have about 3.7 children, against about two in the 1930s.

### The impact of economic growth and demand for labour

Yet the same boom that praised homemakers needed their work.
- **Women's work grew.** The proportion of women aged 16 and over who were in the labour force rose from about 33.9 per cent in 1950 to about 37.7 per cent in 1960 (Bureau of Labor Statistics), and women made up about 29.6 per cent of workers in 1950 and about 33.4 per cent in 1960. **Married women** were the fastest-growing group, many of them working part-time to pay for the new house, car or children's schooling.
- **Where women worked.** The growing service sector needed clerks, typists, shop assistants, teachers and nurses, jobs seen as "women's work". The labour market was divided, with few women in managerial or professional posts, and a woman typically earned about 60 cents for every dollar a man earned. The Equal Pay Act did not come until 1963.
- **Two pictures at once.** The result was a contradiction: the ideal was the full-time homemaker, but millions of women, especially working-class and Black women, always had to work. The "return to the home" was therefore strongest for the middle-class white women who were pictured in the advertisements.

### The impact of more education

The GI Bill brought veterans into colleges; they filled the campuses in the late 1940s and women's share of places and degrees fell. Total college enrolment rose from about 2.3 million in 1950 to about 3.6 million in 1960, and by 1960 about a third of bachelor's degrees went to women (65 per cent went to men). Many women who went to college did so partly to find a husband (the joke was that they were taking an "MRS degree"), but college gave a growing group of educated women expectations that the roles on offer did not satisfy. The expansion of high schools and the National Defense Education Act of 1958, which funded science, mathematics and language teaching after the shock of Sputnik, increased girls' schooling as well as boys'.

### Suburban culture and the "feminine mystique"

Suburban life gave many women new comforts: labour-saving appliances, a house with a garden, a car. But it also isolated some of them. Husbands commuted to work, the new suburbs often had few shops or services nearby, and wives were expected to be mother, cook, cleaner and chauffeur. The journalist **Betty Friedan** interviewed women from her own college class in 1957 and later wrote *The Feminine Mystique* (1963), which gave the problem a name.

:::note Evidence: a feminist's later account of the 1950s
"The problem lay buried, unspoken, for many years in the minds of American women."

*From Betty Friedan, The Feminine Mystique, 1963, chapter 1, "The Problem That Has No Name". It was written after the period and describes the 1950s in hindsight, mostly the experience of educated middle-class white women. The opening sentence is quoted exactly.*
:::

Historians now qualify Friedan's picture. In an influential article, "Beyond the Feminine Mystique" (*Journal of American History*, 1993), **Joanne Meyerowitz** argued that postwar magazines and books carried many messages, including praise for working women and for achievement outside the home, so that the culture was less uniform than Friedan suggested.

### The role of feminism

Organised feminism was weak in the 1950s, and the decade is often called a low point between the suffrage campaign of 1920 and the movement of the later 1960s. But the roots were there.
- The **National Woman's Party** kept up the campaign for an Equal Rights Amendment, which passed the Senate with a limiting rider in 1950 and 1953 but did not become law.
- Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (published in French in 1949, and in English in 1953) and Alfred Kinsey's study of women's sexual behaviour (1953) challenged ideas about women's nature.
- Women's organisations, trade-union women and the Women's Bureau of the Department of Labor pressed for equal pay and opportunity.
- **The pill**, approved in the USA on 9 May 1960, gave women, for the first time, a reliable way to control when to have children.
- On 14 December 1961 President Kennedy set up the **President's Commission on the Status of Women**, chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt until her death in 1962. Its report in 1963 documented discrimination in pay and jobs.

:::key Judging the change in women's role
**Evidence of change:** wartime work; a growing share of women at work (33.9 to 37.7 per cent of women, 1950–60) and especially of married women; the expansion of clerical and service jobs; more women in college; the pill (1960); the first federal commission (1961).
**Evidence of continuity:** the ideal of the full-time homemaker; early marriage and a baby boom (about 3.7 children per woman in 1957); jobs divided by sex and pay about 60 per cent of men's; the Equal Rights Amendment stalled; organised feminism weak.
**Judgement to defend:** women's **lives** changed more than their **status**: the economy drew them into paid work, but the culture, the law and the pay structure kept them in a subordinate position, and the change in attitudes came after 1961.
:::

## The development of a youth culture

### Why a youth culture emerged

:::def Youth culture
A set of tastes in music, dress, language and leisure that belongs to young people as a separate group, distinct from that of their parents, and is aimed at them by the market. The word "teenager" came into common use in the USA in the 1940s and 1950s.
:::

Young people had always had their own pastimes. What was new in the 1950s was a mass market of teenagers with money, free time and their own media, made possible by several causes.
- **The wartime impact on family life.** During the war many fathers were away and many mothers were at work, so older children had more independence and less supervision. Fears of "juvenile delinquency" rose in the 1940s. After the war, family life was re-established, but the sense that teenagers formed their own world remained.
- **Working parents in the 1950s.** More mothers took paid jobs, and families were smaller in space but larger in numbers of children, so older children spent time away from adults, with friends, at school or in the car.
- **Prosperity and consumerism.** Teenagers had pocket money and part-time jobs, and manufacturers and advertisers noticed. Records, clothes, magazines, hot-rod cars, cosmetics and snacks were all marketed to them. Specialist market researchers began to measure teenage spending. A magazine for girls, *Seventeen*, had appeared in 1944, and the first generation of the baby boom began to reach their teens in 1959.
- **Longer schooling.** More young people stayed at high school than ever before, which kept them together with others their own age.
- **Technology.** The 45 rpm single record (introduced in 1949), cheap record players, and from 1954 the pocket **transistor radio** let young people choose and play their own music, away from the family set.

### The role of television and film

:::def Teenpics
Low-budget films of the 1950s made for a young audience, often about rebellious or troubled teenagers. They helped to create the idea of the teenager as a figure of both fascination and fear.
:::

- **Television.** Television spread teenage fashions and music nationwide at once. *American Bandstand*, a show on which teenagers danced to records, was broadcast nationally by the ABC network from 5 August 1957 and made the host Dick Clark a national figure. Television also gave young viewers programmes, such as the Mickey Mouse Club (1955), made for their age group.
- **Films.** *The Wild One* (1953), starring Marlon Brando as a motorcycle gang leader, and *Rebel Without a Cause* (1955), starring James Dean as a troubled middle-class teenager, put rebellion on the screen. *Blackboard Jungle* (March 1955) was set in a rough inner-city school, and used Bill Haley's "Rock Around the Clock" over the opening titles. The song had been recorded in 1954, but it reached number one in the charts in the summer of 1955. Dean's death in a car crash on 30 September 1955, a month before *Rebel* was released, made him a lasting symbol.

### The role of popular music

**Rock and roll** was the centre of youth culture. It grew from rhythm and blues, the music of Black Americans, mixed with country music. A Cleveland disc jockey, Alan Freed, helped to spread the term "rock and roll" on the radio in the early 1950s and organised mixed-race concerts. Black performers such as Chuck Berry and Little Richard were among the creators of the style, but white audiences first heard it through white performers: Bill Haley and the Comets, and above all **Elvis Presley**. Presley made his first records for the Sun label in Memphis in 1954, signed with the major label RCA in November 1955, and sold millions; his first appearance on *The Ed Sullivan Show* was on 9 September 1956. Pat Boone's smooth versions of songs first recorded by Black artists such as Little Richard reached white audiences who might otherwise have missed the originals. Buddy Holly was killed in an air crash on 3 February 1959.

The music was important for three reasons. It was **mass-marketed** to the young; it crossed the lines of race, at least on the dance floor, in a society that was largely segregated; and it was **a statement of difference** from parents, with its beat, its volume, and the movements of its stars.

### The reaction: moral panic and the Beats

Adults reacted with alarm. Senator Estes Kefauver's Senate subcommittee investigated juvenile delinquency in 1953–54; Dr Fredric Wertham's *Seduction of the Innocent* (1954) blamed comic books, and the industry set up a **Comics Code** in 1954. Churches, parents' groups and some local authorities tried to ban rock and roll, and some critics saw it as a threat to morals and race relations. In practice most teenagers were conformist: they dated, went to school, and shared many of their parents' goals. Their rebellion was usually one of style rather than politics. A smaller group, the **Beats**, writers such as Allen Ginsberg (*Howl*, 1956) and Jack Kerouac (*On the Road*, 1957), rejected consumer society openly and influenced the counter-culture that followed in the 1960s.

:::key Judging the youth culture
**Reasons it developed (ranked as the judgement below ranks them):** *prosperity and marketing* (young people had money, and firms went after it); *media* (television, film, radio and records spread the style nationwide); *demography and schooling* (a large cohort kept together at school); *wartime and family change* (more independence, working mothers); *the music* itself, mixing Black and white traditions.
**Limits:** most teenagers conformed; the culture was largely commercial and often dominated by white adults who made money from it; its political effect was small until the 1960s.
**Judgement to defend:** the youth culture was a **real new social force**, created chiefly by **consumerism and the media working on a large and prosperous cohort**, though it was more a change in taste than in values until the 1960s.
:::

## Prosperity and poverty in the 1950s

### Reasons for greater prosperity

Why did the boom happen? Five causes, which you should be able to rank.
- **The GI Bill (1944).** About 7.8 million of the 16 million Second World War veterans used its education or training benefits by 1956, among them about 2.2 million who went to college, and nearly 2.4 million used its home loans by 1952. It produced a generation of graduates and homeowners.
- **Defence spending.** Spending on defence rose from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to over 50 billion in 1953, during the Korean War and after NSC-68, and stayed at roughly 40 to 50 billion a year for the rest of the decade. It supported aircraft, electronics, shipbuilding and research, especially in California, Texas and the South. Eisenhower's farewell address of 17 January 1961 warned of "the military-industrial complex" (see [Post-war USA (2)](#hist.d.usa45.2)).
- **Rising demand.** The baby boom, savings and credit, as above, kept factories busy.
- **Technology.** New products and methods raised output per worker and created whole industries, from television to electronics and plastics.
- **Infrastructure spending.** The Interstate Highways (1956) and the Hill-Burton hospital programme, as well as school building to cope with the baby boom, created jobs and made trade and travel easier.

Wages rose, and the government did not try to undo the economy's safety net: Eisenhower extended social security in 1954 and 1956 and raised the minimum wage to $1 an hour (from 1956). The 1950 contract between the United Auto Workers and General Motors, a five-year agreement which gave wage rises linked to the cost of living, pensions and health insurance, set a pattern for other industries.

### Reasons for continuing poverty

Not everyone shared in this. Using the Census Bureau's retrospective measure, about 22.4 per cent of Americans, some 39 million people, were poor in 1959. The reasons for continuing poverty were as follows.
- **Unionised and non-unionised industries.** About a third of non-farm workers were in unions at the peak (1954). Workers in the big unionised industries, such as autos, steel and mining, won high wages, pensions and job security, while those in small firms, agriculture, retail and the service sector, mostly without unions, earned far less. The **Taft-Hartley Act** (1947) weakened unions by allowing states to pass "right-to-work" laws, which spread chiefly in the South and Southwest, where unions never gained a hold. The **AFL-CIO merger** of December 1955 did not change this.
- **Rural poverty.** Mechanisation had reduced demand for farm labour, and farm families in the South, Southwest and Appalachia, especially sharecroppers and small tenant farmers, lived in poverty, often without electricity, running water or decent schools. Mexican farm workers, including those brought in under the *Bracero* programme (the 1942 agreement that brought Mexican contract labourers to US farms, ended in 1964), were among the worst paid in the country.
- **Inner-city decline.** As described above, as middle-class families and factories left for the suburbs, the cities were left with a poorer population, falling revenues and run-down housing.
- **Unskilled workers.** Automation cut the number of unskilled jobs in industry, and the recession of 1957–58, when unemployment rose above 7 per cent, hit them hardest.
- **Discrimination.** African Americans, Mexican Americans, Puerto Ricans and Native Americans were kept out of better-paid jobs, unions, schools and housing. Black unemployment was roughly twice the white rate, and in 1959 about 55 per cent of Black Americans were poor. Women were paid about 60 per cent of men's wages. The elderly, too, were badly off, as more than a third of those over 65 were poor in 1959. For Native Americans, the **termination policy** of 1953 sought to end the special federal relationship with tribes and to move people to cities; it increased hardship and was later abandoned.

### Discovering poverty

Poverty was largely **hidden** in the 1950s, because the poor were often out of sight, in rural areas, the inner city or Appalachia. Galbraith's *The Affluent Society* (1958) drew attention to it, and Michael Harrington's *The Other America* (1962), which estimated that between 40 and 50 million Americans lived in poverty, made it a public issue, influencing Kennedy and Johnson's War on Poverty. Harrington's book appeared after our period, so use it as later evidence of what the 1950s had concealed.

:::key Judging prosperity and poverty
**Prosperity:** GI Bill (7.8 million used education or training; about 2.4 million home loans by 1952); defence spending (14 billion in 1950, over 50 billion in 1953); demand and the baby boom; technology; infrastructure (the 1956 Highway Act).
**Poverty:** about 22.4 per cent poor in 1959 (about 39 million); non-union and low-paid work; right-to-work states; rural South and Southwest; inner cities; the unskilled; women paid about 60 per cent of men's pay; Black poverty about 55 per cent.
**Judgement to defend:** the prosperity was **real but unequal**: the majority were richer, but poverty **persisted for a large minority**, and the same forces (suburbanisation, mechanisation, discrimination) that enriched many left others behind.
:::

## How far were the years 1945–61 ones of change?

| Area | Scale | Depth | Permanence | Continuity |
|---|---|---|---|---|

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **James Patterson**, *Grand Expectations: The United States, 1945–1974* (1996), stresses the rising expectations of the age: prosperity, consumer goods and a belief that life would keep improving, together with the unevenness of the gains.
- **Lizabeth Cohen**, *A Consumers' Republic* (2003), argues that mass consumption became the model for the good society, but that it was built on segregation by race and class.
- **Elaine Tyler May**, *Homeward Bound* (1988), reads the family-centred, suburban 1950s as a response to the fears of the Cold War, and so as conservative as well as new.
- **Joanne Meyerowitz** (1993) argues that the culture of the 1950s was more varied than the image of the conformist housewife suggests.
Use these to show that you know there is a debate, but your marks come from your own argument backed by evidence.
:::

The most defensible overall verdict is that the 1950s saw **far-reaching economic and material change** (the consumer boom, the suburbs, television, credit) and **narrower social change** (women's work grew but women's status hardly moved; youth culture changed tastes, not politics; poverty and segregation persisted). The decade was therefore both an end and a beginning: it consolidated trends that began in the 1930s and the war, and it set up the questions, about women, race, youth and poverty, that exploded in the 1960s.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'The 1950s were a decade of conformity rather than change.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** Criteria: (1) scale (how many affected); (2) depth (lives or only objects); (3) continuity for the groups left out. Support for "conformity": suburban sameness and domestic ideal for women; the consumer pressure to buy; the Red Scare and loyalty (see [Post-war USA (1)](#hist.d.usa45.1)); most teenagers conformist. Challenge: the consumer boom, suburbs, television, credit, the Highway Act; women's work; rock and roll; the Beats; the poor. Judgement: conformity in culture and politics coexisted with great material change; the view is only partly valid.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The 1950s were a time of conformity. Many Americans moved to the suburbs, such as Levittown, where the houses were all the same. Women were expected to stay at home and look after their children. Men wore grey suits and worked for large companies. Television programmes showed happy families. However, there was also change. The economy grew, and many people bought cars and televisions. Rock and roll became popular. So the 1950s were partly conformity and partly change."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with a balance of sorts, so Table B Level 3. But the paragraph is a **list of facts on each side**, with no criteria and no weighing: the final sentence is a "bit of both" that a Level 3 answer often settles for. Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and weigh.** "The claim that the 1950s were conformist has some force if we judge change by *cultural attitudes*. Levittown's near-identical houses, the expectation that married women would be homemakers (the average age at marriage fell to about 20 for women and 23 for men, and the birth rate rose to about 3.7 children per woman in 1957) and the pressure to live like one's neighbours all suggest a society that valued sameness. But if we judge change by *how people actually lived*, the decade looks different. By 1960 about 62 per cent of households owned their homes, compared with about 44 per cent in 1940; passenger cars more than doubled between 1945 and 1959; and close to nine in ten homes had a television. These were changes of scale, and they were lasting."

*Commentary.* Now there are two criteria (cultural attitudes and actual lives) and knowledge is used to test each. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. It still needs the groups left out, a third criterion and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The claim that the 1950s were a decade of conformity rather than change is partly valid, but it confuses two things. Conformity describes the *style* of much of American life; change describes its *substance*. To judge the question I will use three criteria: how many Americans were affected, how deep the change went, and who was left out. On the first two criteria change outweighs conformity. On the third, conformity and continuity look much stronger, and this is where the view is most defensible.

On scale and depth, the decade changed the ordinary American's life in ways that lasted. Between 1945 and 1960 gross national product rose from about 228 billion dollars to about 546 billion in current dollars, and the way families lived altered with it. Passenger cars registered rose from about 25.8 million in 1945 to about 59.5 million in 1959; television reached about 9 per cent of homes in 1950 and close to nine in ten in 1960; home ownership rose from about 44 per cent in 1940 to about 62 per cent in 1960, helped by the GI Bill, which backed nearly 2.4 million home loans by 1952. The suburbs, from Levittown (begun in 1947) to the roads funded by the Highway Act of 29 June 1956, moved about a third of Americans out of the old city centres by 1960. Credit, from the Diners Club card of 1950 to instalment buying, tripled in the decade and made spending on credit normal. The historian Lizabeth Cohen called the result a 'consumers' republic' in which buying was almost a civic duty. This was not a continuation of the 1930s; it was a new economy and a new landscape. The young also changed: in the rock and roll of Elvis Presley, whose first appearance on *The Ed Sullivan Show* was on 9 September 1956, and in films such as *Rebel Without a Cause*, a youth culture emerged that set teenagers apart from their parents.

The conformity argument is strongest in three areas. First, the *domestic ideal*. Elaine Tyler May argued that Cold War fear made the suburban family a symbol of security, and the figures fit: early marriage and about 3.7 children per woman at the peak in 1957. Yet this is also where the case needs qualifying. In 1960 about 37.7 per cent of women were in the labour force, up from 33.9 per cent in 1950, and married women were the fastest-growing group, so the ideal of the homemaker described what advertisements showed rather than what many women did. Second, *politics and culture*. The Red Scare made dissent dangerous, and the Beats' rejection of consumer society was a small minority view. Third, *inequality*. The suburbs were closed to most Black Americans, who were kept out by covenants, redlining and Levitt's own early rules, and about 22.4 per cent of Americans, some 39 million, were poor in 1959. For these Americans the decade brought little change, and for the Black families who moved to the inner cities it brought a new kind of hardship as industry and tax revenue left. The more affluent society became, the more sharply the continuing poverty stood out, as Galbraith argued in 1958.

Overall, the statement is therefore only partly justified. In its way of life, the USA changed more in the 1950s than in almost any earlier decade, and this change was lasting. Conformity is a fair description of the *culture* that accompanied it, and of the limits of change for women, Black Americans and the poor. But conformity was a response to change, not an alternative to it: Americans conformed to a new, richer, suburban way of life. The decade is best seen as one of material revolution within a conservative culture, and the tensions between the two produced the social upheavals of the 1960s."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): three explicit criteria; the argument is balanced and the groups left out are weighed against the evidence for change; a named historian is used to support rather than decorate; the conclusion is qualified and sustained (change in substance, conformity in style). Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise figures and dates (228 to 546 billion; 25.8 to 59.5 million cars; 44 to 62 per cent; 29 June 1956; 9 September 1956; 33.9 to 37.7 per cent; 22.4 per cent), selected for the argument and not recited. Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 750 words, 35 to 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the reasons for the development of a youth culture in the 1950s. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. An "Assess the reasons" answer must weigh the causes against each other and judge which mattered most, using criteria such as how widespread and how necessary each cause was.

**Plan.** Causes: (1) consumerism and marketing (money, products aimed at the young); (2) media (television, film, radio, records); (3) music (rock and roll); (4) family and wartime change; (5) demography and schooling. Criteria: was the cause necessary (without it, no youth culture), and did it reach most young people? Judgement: consumerism and the media were the decisive causes; the music gave the culture its character.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "Teenagers had a lot of money in the 1950s because the economy was booming. They bought records and clothes. There were films about teenagers and Elvis Presley was very popular on television. All these things caused the youth culture."

*Commentary.* True but undeveloped: the causes are named, not explained, and there is no weighing. No dates, titles or figures. Table A Level 2 (4–6), Table B Level 1–2.

**Moving to Level 4: explain how each cause worked and compare.** "The most important cause was consumerism. Teenagers had pocket money and part-time jobs, and in an economy where gross national product rose from about 301 billion dollars in 1950 to about 546 billion in 1960, firms looked for new markets. Records, clothes and magazines such as *Seventeen* were aimed at them, and technology such as the 45 rpm single and, from 1954, the transistor radio let them listen privately. The media were the second cause: without television, a show like *American Bandstand* (national from 5 August 1957) could not have spread a style across the country in a few months. The music was a third cause, but it depended on the first two: rock and roll became a youth culture because there was a market and a medium for it."

*Commentary.* The causes are explained and ranked, and the dependence between them is argued. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs a counter-weight (the family and wartime background), a clear criterion and a judgement that tests the ranking.

**A Level 5 conclusion.**

"Overall, the youth culture was the product of several causes working together, but they were not equal. The *necessary* conditions were prosperity and a large cohort of young people with money and leisure, which explains why teenagers became a market in the 1950s and not in the Depression. The *transmitting* cause was the media: television, the cinema and radio, which made a regional taste national within months. The *shaping* cause was the music, which gave the culture its sound and its look. The wartime disruption of family life, with fathers absent and mothers at work, explains the anxiety about juvenile delinquency, but it was the marketing and the media that turned that anxiety into a culture. So I judge consumerism and the media to be the most important reasons, because without them the other causes would have produced a smaller, local and less lasting phenomenon. This also explains why the culture was commercial and why, for most teenagers, rebellion remained one of style."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies a clear scheme (necessary, transmitting and shaping causes), makes a judgement that is argued rather than asserted, and links the answer back to the character of the culture. With paragraphs on the family and on the limits of the youth culture, this is Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess how far women's lives changed in the years 1945–61." [30]

A student wrote:

"Women's lives changed a lot in the 1950s. During the war women had worked in factories. After the war many went back to being housewives, and there was a baby boom. Magazines told women to stay at home. Betty Friedan wrote a book about this. More women went to college. In 1960 the pill came out. So women's lives changed a great deal."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the paragraph lists points, and the final sentence asserts "changed a great deal" although the evidence mostly shows women being pushed back to the home. There are no criteria, no distinction between lives and status, and Friedan's book is used as evidence of the 1950s without saying that it appeared in 1963, after the period. Table B Level 2–3 (5–7): relevant, but no figures, and an unnoticed contradiction between the first and last sentences.

**The fix.** Use the same facts to prove an argument. "Women's lives changed more than their status. By 1960 about 37.7 per cent of women were in the labour force, up from 33.9 per cent in 1950, and married women were the fastest-growing group, so the economy, which needed clerks, teachers and nurses, drew women into paid work. But the ideal of the full-time homemaker was strengthened, not weakened: the age at marriage fell to about 20 and the birth rate rose to about 3.7 children per woman in 1957. Women were paid about 60 per cent of men's wages and had little access to the professions, and organised feminism was weak until Betty Friedan's *Feminine Mystique* (1963), the pill (1960) and the President's Commission on the Status of Women (1961) began to change attitudes." Now every fact is evidence for a judgement, and the dates are right.

Q: Why did demand for consumer goods rise so fast after 1945?
A: Wartime savings and pent-up demand (little had been available to buy during the war); rising incomes and strong unions; the baby boom (about four million births a year through the later 1950s); cheap credit and mortgages (the GI Bill and the FHA); and government spending on defence and, from 1956, roads.
Q: What did the Diners Club card of 1950 start, and what happened to consumer credit in the 1950s?
A: It began as a charge card for restaurant meals in February 1950, the start of the credit card. According to the National Bureau of Economic Research, total consumer credit tripled in the 1950s; instalment buying and long mortgages made debt a normal part of life.
Q: What was Levittown, and how were houses like it paid for?
A: A planned community begun in 1947 on Long Island by Levitt and Sons, which mass-produced about 17,000 houses by about 1951, the basic one at $7,990. They were bought with GI Bill and FHA loans (nearly 2.4 million veterans' home loans by 1952). Levitt's early leases excluded Black buyers.
Q: Give three causes and three effects of suburbanisation.
A: Causes: the car and the Highway Act of 29 June 1956; cheap mortgages (GI Bill, FHA); the housing shortage and baby boom. Effects: about 23 per cent of Americans in suburbs in 1950 and about 31 per cent in 1960; segregation, because Black Americans were kept out; inner-city decline (Detroit fell from about 1.85 million to about 1.67 million, 1950–60).
Q: What was "domestic containment"?
A: Elaine Tyler May's term for the Cold War idea that the suburban nuclear family would contain the dangers of the atomic age at home, as foreign policy contained Communism abroad. It helps explain early marriage and a high birth rate.
Q: How did women's employment change between 1950 and 1960?
A: The share of women in the labour force rose from about 33.9 to about 37.7 per cent, and married women were the fastest-growing group. Most worked in clerical, service, teaching and nursing jobs and earned about 60 per cent of men's wages.
Q: Why did a youth culture develop in the 1950s?
A: Teenagers had money and leisure in a rich economy; firms and media aimed products at them; television, film, radio and records (the 45 rpm single, the transistor radio from 1954) spread a common style; wartime and working parents left them more independent; and rock and roll gave them their own music.

Adults reacted with the Senate inquiry into juvenile delinquency (1953–54) and the Comics Code (1954).

Q: Give four reasons why poverty continued, and one figure.
A: Non-unionised, low-paid work (and right-to-work laws after Taft-Hartley); rural poverty in the South and Southwest; inner-city decline; unskilled workers hit by automation; and discrimination in pay against women and minorities. About 22.4 per cent of Americans (some 39 million) were poor in 1959.

A: Support: Levitt's early leases and the FHA's redlining kept Black families out of the suburbs; *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948) did not end the practice; the GI Bill's benefits were far smaller for Black veterans in the South (Katznelson); Black unemployment about twice the white rate and about 55 per cent poverty in 1959; inner-city decline. Challenge: the boom raised incomes for many Black families, and over a million moved north and west for better jobs; the Supreme Court acted against covenants; the moral pressure of the Cold War helped the case for civil rights (see [Post-war USA (2)](#hist.d.usa45.2)). A* judgement says that segregation by race and class was built into the way the new consumer society was financed and located, and that consumerism increased both the stake in equality and the sense of injustice.
:::

:::recap
- **Tests of change**: scale, depth, permanence, continuity.
- **Consumer boom**: GNP about 228 billion dollars (1945) to about 546 billion (1960), current prices; cars 25.8 million (1945) to 59.5 million (1959); TV about 9 per cent of homes (1950) to close to nine in ten (1960); credit tripled in the 1950s; Diners Club (February 1950); Packard (1957); Galbraith (1958); Cohen (2003).
- **Suburbs**: Levittown (1947); GI Bill (22 June 1944; nearly 2.4 million home loans by 1952); home ownership about 44 to about 62 per cent (1940–60); Highway Act (29 June 1956); suburbs about 23 to about 31 per cent of the population (1950–60); segregation (*Shelley v. Kraemer*, 3 May 1948); inner-city decline (Detroit about 1.85 to about 1.67 million); Sun Belt (California about 10.6 to about 15.7 million).
- **Women**: wartime work, then domestic ideal (May's "domestic containment"); labour-force share about 33.9 to about 37.7 per cent (1950–60); pay about 60 per cent of men's; Friedan (1963) and Meyerowitz (1993); the pill (9 May 1960); Commission on the Status of Women (14 December 1961).
- **Youth culture**: money and marketing, media (television, film, radio, 45 rpm, transistor radio from 1954), rock and roll (Haley 1955, Elvis 1956), *Rebel Without a Cause* (1955), *American Bandstand* (national 5 August 1957); moral panic (Senate inquiry 1953–54, Comics Code 1954); the Beats.
- **Prosperity**: GI Bill (7.8 million used education or training by 1956), defence (about 14 billion dollars in 1950, over 50 billion in 1953), demand, technology, infrastructure.
- **Poverty**: about 22.4 per cent (some 39 million) poor in 1959; non-union work and right-to-work laws; rural South and Southwest; inner cities; unskilled; discrimination (Black poverty about 55 per cent; women's pay about 60 per cent of men's); Harrington (1962).
- **Judgement**: far-reaching material change, narrower social change; prosperity real but unequal.
:::
