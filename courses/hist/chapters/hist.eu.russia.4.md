## Bolshevik policies and the first months of power

### Sovnarkom and the first decrees

On 26 October 1917 the Second Congress of Soviets elected the Council of People's Commissars, **Sovnarkom**, with Lenin as chairman, and adopted the first decrees. Over the next weeks Sovnarkom issued many more. In its own eyes it was a temporary government, ruling until a Constituent Assembly or a new Congress of Soviets could decide on a constitution. Four of its decrees are named by the syllabus.

- **The Decree on Peace (26 October).** It proposed to all warring nations an immediate peace without annexations or indemnities, and it appealed over the heads of governments to the workers of Europe. It led to talks with Germany (see Brest-Litovsk below).
- **The Decree on Land (26 October).** It declared that "landed proprietorship is abolished forthwith without any compensation" and put the estates of the nobles, the Crown, the monasteries and the Church at the disposal of the peasants' committees and Soviets. It legalised what the peasants had already been doing, and it won their acceptance of the new regime for the moment.
- **The Decree on Workers' Control (14 November OS).** It gave elected committees of workers the right to supervise production, hiring, stocks and accounts in every factory. In practice the factory committees often took over management, which led to confusion, falling output and angry owners. In December a Supreme Council of the National Economy (Vesenkha) was set up to direct the economy from above, and banks were nationalised.
- **The Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia (2 November OS).** It promised equality and sovereignty to all the peoples of the old Empire, and their right to self-determination, up to the right to secede. In the months that followed, Finland, Poland and the Baltic states and Ukraine took their independence or autonomy, though the Bolsheviks later fought to recover much of the empire.

Other early decrees ended class privileges and ranks, introduced an eight-hour day, separated Church and state (January 1918) and gave civil marriage and divorce legal force. The press decree of 27 October (OS) banned newspapers that called for resistance to the new government. It was presented as a temporary measure for a time of crisis, but it was never repealed.

:::def Sovnarkom
The Council of People's Commissars, the government of Soviet Russia from 26 October 1917, with Lenin as chairman. Its members were called commissars, not ministers, as a break with the old order. Its decrees had the force of law, without waiting for an elected parliament.
:::

### The first months: a government without a state

Sovnarkom's decrees were easier to write than to enforce. Civil servants went on strike, and the state bank and ministries refused to work with the new commissars. The railwaymen's union, the Vikzhel, demanded a government of all the socialist parties. Outside Petrograd and Moscow, local soviets accepted the new government within weeks (Lenin called it a "triumphal march of Soviet power"), but they differed widely from each other and often acted on their own. In December 1917 the **Left SRs** joined Sovnarkom, which gave the Bolsheviks peasant support and a coalition. They stayed in the government until they walked out in March 1918, over Brest-Litovsk.

## The establishment of a dictatorship

### The Cheka

On 7 December 1917 (OS; 20 December NS) Sovnarkom set up the All-Russian Extraordinary Commission for Combating Counter-Revolution and Sabotage, known from its Russian initials as the **Cheka**, under **Felix Dzerzhinsky**, a Polish revolutionary who had spent eleven years in Tsarist prisons and exile. At first it was a small body of a few dozen people, set up to deal with the strike of civil servants and with sabotage, and with a power to investigate and arrest. It grew quickly. Within months it had local branches across the country, its own armed units and the power to try and shoot suspects without a court. It was answerable to the party, not to the law.

:::def Cheka
The Bolshevik political police, set up on 7 December 1917 (OS) under Dzerzhinsky. Its job was to find and destroy "counter-revolution and sabotage" by arrest, imprisonment and execution without trial. It was reorganised as the GPU in 1922 and was the ancestor of the later Soviet secret police.
:::

It was used first against the Bolsheviks' rivals on the right, such as the Kadets, who had been declared "enemies of the people" in November 1917. In the summer of 1918, when Civil War and rebellion threatened the regime, it was used against the left as well (the Mensheviks, the SRs and the anarchists) and then, on a huge scale, in the **Red Terror**. After an attempt on Lenin's life on 30 August 1918 (he was shot and wounded by Fanny Kaplan, an SR) and the murder of the Petrograd Cheka chief Moisei Uritsky on the same day, a decree of 5 September 1918 authorised the arrest of "class enemies" as hostages and the shooting of those involved in White plots. Historians' estimates of the number killed by the Cheka in the Civil War range from tens of thousands to well over a hundred thousand, depending on what is counted. The point for this topic is not the number, but the principle: the Bolsheviks used terror as an open instrument of government.

### The closure of the Constituent Assembly

The Bolsheviks had attacked the Provisional Government for delaying the Constituent Assembly. After October, they let the elections go ahead on the date set by the old government, 12 November 1917 (OS; 25 November NS), because to cancel them would have been an admission that their own power did not come from the people.

The result was a disaster for them. About 40 per cent of the votes went to the **Socialist Revolutionaries** (SRs), mostly from the peasants, and about 24 per cent to the Bolsheviks, whose votes came from the cities, the northern army and the Baltic Fleet. The Kadets and the Mensheviks won a small share. Of about 700 deputies, the SRs had around 380 and the Bolsheviks about 170 (counts differ slightly). The Bolsheviks had won Petrograd and Moscow, and lost Russia.

On 28 November (OS) Sovnarkom declared the Kadets "enemies of the people" and arrested some of their leaders. In December Lenin wrote his **Theses on the Constituent Assembly**, in which he said that a republic of Soviets was "a higher form of democracy than the usual bourgeois republic with a Constituent Assembly". He also noted that the SRs had put up a united list in mid-October but had split in November, so that the voters had not been able to choose between the Right and the Left SRs.

The Assembly met in the Tauride Palace in Petrograd on **5 January 1918** (OS; 18 January NS). The SRs elected their leader **Viktor Chernov** as chairman, by 244 votes to about 150 (accounts give 151 or 153) against the Left SR Maria Spiridonova. The Bolsheviks proposed that the Assembly should recognise the Soviet government and its decrees, and when the deputies refused, the Bolsheviks and then the Left SRs walked out. The remaining deputies went on debating through the night. In the early hours of 6 January, the guards, who were sailors, told them to go home because the guard was tired, and the next day Sovnarkom issued a decree dissolving the Assembly. A demonstration of supporters of the Assembly the day before had been fired on by Red Guards and soldiers, killing several people.

:::key Why the Bolsheviks closed the Constituent Assembly
1. **They did not win it**: they had about a quarter of the votes and about a quarter of the seats, and the SRs had a majority.
2. **It would not accept Soviet power or the decrees**: it refused on 5 January to recognise the decrees on peace and land, and the Soviet government's authority.
3. **They claimed a higher democracy**: Lenin's Theses said that Soviets, which could recall their deputies, were a higher form of democracy, and that the lists had been drawn up before the split of the SRs.
4. **It would have given a rival centre of legitimacy** to the Bolsheviks' enemies, at a moment when the Civil War was about to begin.
:::

The closure was a decisive step to a one-party state. It removed the only body that had been elected by the whole country, and it showed that the Bolsheviks would not accept being outvoted. It also pushed the SRs into opposition, and many of them went to the Volga and Siberia and formed governments of their own, which fought the Bolsheviks in the Civil War. The Soviets were not the equal of the Assembly, since by the summer of 1918 the Bolsheviks had banned or excluded the Mensheviks and the SRs from them, and the first Soviet **Constitution**, adopted in July 1918, denied the vote to employers of hired labour, private traders, priests, former police officers and members of the old royal family and gave town workers more weight than peasants.

## The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk

### Why the Bolsheviks needed peace

The Bolsheviks had won power by promising peace. The army was disintegrating: soldiers were leaving the front in thousands to claim land at home. Lenin knew that the Bolsheviks could not fight. An armistice was signed with Germany on 15 December 1917 (NS) and peace talks began at Brest-Litovsk, the German headquarters on the eastern front.

### The negotiations

The German terms were harsh: Russia was to give up Poland and the Baltic lands. The Bolsheviks disagreed about what to do.
- **Lenin** argued that Russia must accept any terms to get a breathing space, because the revolution could not survive another war and the revolution in Germany would come soon to cancel the treaty.
- **Bukharin and the "Left Communists"** wanted a "revolutionary war" against Germany, in the hope of inspiring revolutions elsewhere.
- **Trotsky**, who led the Soviet delegation, took a middle line: "neither war nor peace". He declared on 10 February 1918 that Russia would stop fighting, but would not sign.

Germany answered by attacking on 18 February, and moved forward rapidly against a collapsing army, capturing a large area in a week. Lenin threatened to resign. The Central Committee, by a narrow majority, voted on 23 February to accept the German terms, which were now harder. The treaty was signed on **3 March 1918**, and ratified by a Congress of Soviets on 15 March.

### The terms

By the treaty Russia gave up Poland, Lithuania and Courland, and recognised the independence of Finland and Ukraine (in effect under German influence), withdrew from Estonia and Livonia, and gave Kars, Ardahan and Batum in the Caucasus to Turkey. The size of the losses depends on how they are counted. A Petrograd newspaper in 1918 put the loss at about 32 per cent of the population (56 million people); later accounts give about a third of the population, a large share of the coal and iron, and a very large part of Russia's industrial enterprises. Use "about a third of the population and most of the coal and iron" rather than an exact percentage.

### The effects

- **Short term.** The Left SRs left Sovnarkom in protest, and in July 1918 they rose against the Bolsheviks in Moscow after murdering the German ambassador, Count Mirbach. The rising was crushed in two days, and the Bolsheviks were left to rule alone. Within the party there was a deep split, and in March the party changed its name to the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks). The capital moved to Moscow, farther from the Germans, in March 1918.
- **Long term.** The treaty gave Lenin what he wanted, an end to the war with Germany and time to build an army. But it turned many Russians, particularly officers, against the Bolsheviks, who appeared to have sold their country, and it helped to start the Civil War. After Germany's defeat in November 1918, the Soviet government annulled the treaty, though it could not recover all the lost lands.

:::def Treaty of Brest-Litovsk
The peace treaty of 3 March 1918 between Soviet Russia and the Central Powers, by which Russia left the First World War and gave up Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland, Ukraine and parts of the Caucasus. It was annulled after Germany's defeat in November 1918.
:::

## The Civil War, 1918–21

### The causes and the sides

The Civil War began in the spring and summer of 1918 and ended, in its main phase, in 1920. It was not a simple fight between two armies. It had many causes: the closure of the Constituent Assembly, which set the Bolsheviks against the SRs; Brest-Litovsk, which turned officers and patriots against them; their policies on the land and on grain, which turned peasants against them; and the Allies' fear of Bolshevism and wish to keep Russia in the war.

The sides were:
- **The Reds**: the Bolsheviks, who held the centre of the country and the two capitals, with the Red Army.
- **The Whites**: a loose alliance of the Bolsheviks' enemies on the right and centre, including former Tsarist officers, Cossacks, landowners, liberals and SRs. They were never united.
- **The Greens**: peasant armies that fought the Reds and the Whites alike (such as Nestor Makhno's anarchists in Ukraine), because they wanted to be left alone with their land.
- **Foreign powers**: Britain, France, the United States and Japan, and other countries, which sent troops and supplies to different regions.

### The main events

**1918.** In May the **Czech Legion**, about 40,000 Czech and Slovak former prisoners of war who were crossing Siberia to leave Russia by Vladivostok, rose when Trotsky ordered them to be disarmed, and took control of towns along the Trans-Siberian Railway. Behind them an SR government formed at Samara (the Komuch), and an anti-Bolshevik government at Omsk. In the south Generals Alekseev and Kornilov, and then **Anton Denikin**, built a Volunteer Army. Britain landed troops at Murmansk in March and at Archangel in August; Japanese, American and British forces landed at Vladivostok. On 16–17 July the Tsar and his family were shot at Yekaterinburg by the local Bolsheviks, as White and Czech forces approached. The order came from the Ural Soviet, and historians debate how far Moscow approved it. In November 1918 **Admiral Alexander Kolchak** seized power at Omsk and declared himself "Supreme Ruler" of the Whites.

**1919.** The year of the Whites' greatest threats. In the spring Kolchak advanced from Siberia towards the Volga, but he was driven back by the Red Army by summer and lost Omsk in November. In the south Denikin's army took Ukraine and, in October, Orel, about 380 kilometres from Moscow. In the same month General **Nikolai Yudenich** advanced from Estonia to the outskirts of Petrograd. All three were beaten back by the end of the year. Kolchak was handed over to the Bolsheviks and shot at Irkutsk in February 1920.

**1920.** Denikin resigned in April and was replaced by **Pyotr Wrangel**, who held the Crimea until November 1920, when his army and thousands of civilians were evacuated by sea. In April 1920 Poland, under Marshal Piłsudski, attacked Ukraine and took Kiev; a Red counter-attack reached the outskirts of Warsaw in August, but was beaten back. The war ended with the Treaty of Riga in March 1921, which gave Poland large areas of Ukraine and Belorussia. The last foreign troops, the Japanese, left Vladivostok in 1922.

### Why the Reds won

The syllabus asks about the strengths of the Bolsheviks and the weaknesses of the Whites. They are two sides of one explanation.

**Strengths of the Bolsheviks**
- **Leadership.** Lenin led the party with a ruthless clarity about what mattered; **Trotsky**, Commissar for War from March 1918, built the Red Army from nothing. He brought back former Tsarist officers ("military specialists", tens of thousands of them by the end of the war) and made sure they were watched by political commissars; he enforced discipline, including the death penalty, and travelled in an armoured train to the fronts. The Red Army grew to over five million men on paper by 1920 (many never reached the front), by conscription from the spring of 1918.
- **Popular support.** The Reds were not popular with everyone, but the peasants feared a White victory more: it would bring back the landlords and the land would be taken back. The Decree on Land was the Reds' best weapon. The Reds also took the part of Russian patriotism, since they could say that the Whites were backed by foreign armies.
- **Geographical factors.** The Reds held the centre of Russia, with Moscow and Petrograd, the main railway junctions and most of the arms factories and the population (about 60 million in the area they held). They could move troops from one front to another by rail along short interior lines, while the Whites were spread around the rim of the country, thousands of kilometres apart, and could not co-ordinate.
- **Unity and organisation.** There was one party, one government and one army command, and every decision came from the centre. Propaganda (posters, agit-trains, newspapers) spread a simple message: the Whites were landlords and the servants of foreigners. The Cheka and the Terror made opposition dangerous.
- **War Communism.** The state controlled grain and industry and used both for the war. Under **War Communism** (see below) the Reds took grain by force from the villages and industry was nationalised to supply the army. It was brutal and damaging, but it kept the army and the cities fed and armed for the duration of the war.

**Weaknesses of the Whites**
- **Leadership.** The Whites had able generals but no single leader. Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich and Wrangel acted separately, and they quarrelled with each other and with their political allies. They were united only in their hostility to the Bolsheviks, and had no political programme apart from "Russia, one and indivisible" and a vague promise of a Constituent Assembly later. Kolchak's rule was military and authoritarian.
- **Use of conscription.** The Whites forced peasants into their armies and requisitioned horses and food, just as the Reds did, but with less organisation. Conscripts deserted in large numbers, sometimes to the Reds.
- **Issues of supply.** The Whites controlled poor, thinly populated regions with few factories and railways. They relied on supplies from Britain and France, which arrived irregularly and were often wasted or sold on by corrupt officers.
- **Foreign intervention.** The Allies' help was half-hearted. They sent weapons and a few troops but no large forces. After the First World War ended in November 1918, their soldiers were tired and their governments did not want another war; most troops were withdrawn in 1919. The intervention also allowed the Reds to call the Whites traitors, and it did not help them to win over the peasants.
- **Association with the landlords.** Denikin and Kolchak did not clearly accept the peasants' seizure of the land, and White officers often took revenge on villages. Their refusal to grant autonomy to Ukrainians, Cossacks and other nationalities lost them potential allies. Their armies carried out terror too, including pogroms against Jews, especially in Ukraine in 1919.

:::key Why the Bolsheviks won the Civil War
1. **Central position**: Moscow, Petrograd, the railways and the arms factories, and shorter lines, against White armies on the edge that could not join up.
2. **A unified command and a disciplined army** under Trotsky, using military specialists watched by commissars, and conscription.
3. **The peasants' fear of the Whites** and the land they had gained from the Reds.
4. **War Communism and the Terror**, which fed and controlled the army and the cities, and silenced opposition.
5. **White disunity and mistakes**: no single leader, no policy on land or nationalities, poor supply, and links with foreign powers and with the landlords.
Each White army was beaten separately, in 1919 and 1920, and none of them was ever able to fight at the same time as another.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **Evan Mawdsley**, *The Russian Civil War* (1987), is the standard one-volume military history. He stresses the Reds' central position and organisation, and the Whites' divisions.
- **Orlando Figes**, in *A People's Tragedy* (1996), stresses that the peasants, hating the Whites more than the Reds, were decisive.
- **Richard Pipes**, in *Russia under the Bolshevik Regime* (1994), stresses the dictatorship that the Bolsheviks built, and the part played by terror and by their will to hold power.
You can use this at AS to show that the Reds' victory had many causes, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.

### The costs

Estimates of the dead in the Civil War range widely, from about 7 million to 12 million or more, if famine and disease are counted with deaths in battle and terror, and the figures are uncertain. Both sides used terror, and the pogroms against Jews in Ukraine in 1919 alone killed tens of thousands. About a million or more people left Russia. Cities lost population as people went back to the villages to find food: Petrograd's population fell by more than half between 1917 and 1920.

## War Communism

**War Communism** was the economic system of the Civil War, from 1918 to 1921. It was not planned in advance. It grew out of the war, and the Bolsheviks' wish to control the economy, and many Bolsheviks believed in it as the way to socialism.

:::def War Communism
The system of economic control used by the Bolsheviks during the Civil War (1918–21): the nationalisation of industry, the forced requisition of grain from the peasants, the rationing of food, the ban on private trade and the direction of labour, all to supply the Red Army and the cities.
:::

- **Industry.** A decree of 28 June 1918 nationalised large industry, and by the end of 1920 even small workshops had been taken over. The state directed production through Vesenkha and sent managers to run the factories, and workers' control was ended.
- **Grain.** In May and June 1918 the government declared a state monopoly of grain and sent detachments to the villages to seize it, with the help of committees of poor peasants. From January 1919 this was formalised as *prodrazverstka*, the requisitioning of fixed quotas of grain. Peasants who hid grain were treated as enemies of the people, and some were shot.
- **Rationing and labour.** Food was rationed in the cities by class and in return for work; private trade was banned and the black market flourished. In 1920 workers were conscripted for labour, and Trotsky argued for the "militarisation of labour". Money lost nearly all its value.

**Effects.** War Communism helped the Reds to win, because it kept the army supplied. But the damage was very great.
- **Industry** collapsed. Large-scale industrial output fell to about a fifth of its 1913 level by 1920–21.
- **Agriculture** suffered because peasants sowed less when the state took what they produced. The grain harvest fell from about 80 million tonnes in 1913 to less than 50 million in 1920 (the figures commonly cited, for example by the historian David Christian).
- **Famine.** A drought in 1921, on top of requisitioning and war, caused a terrible famine in the Volga region and Ukraine in 1921–22. Estimates of the dead run from about 3 million to about 5 million. The American Relief Administration, led by Herbert Hoover, fed millions of people from the summer of 1921.
- **Unrest.** Peasants rose in Tambov province and in Siberia against the requisitions in 1920–21, and in February 1921 strikes broke out in Petrograd. The Red Army had won the war, but the Bolsheviks were losing the peace.

## The Kronstadt Mutiny, 1921

### Causes

**Kronstadt** is a naval fortress on an island in the Gulf of Finland, about 30 kilometres from Petrograd. Its sailors had been among the Bolsheviks' strongest supporters in 1917, and had taken part in the July Days and in October. By 1921 its crews included many recruits from the peasant villages, who heard from their families how the requisitions were ruining the countryside.

The immediate cause was the winter of 1920–21: food rations were cut, fuel ran out and the Petrograd factories closed, with strikes in February 1921. Kronstadt sent a delegation to look at the strikes, and its report made the sailors indignant. The deeper causes were the same as those of the strikes and the peasant risings: War Communism, hunger, and the loss of the freedom that the Revolution had promised. By 1921 the sailors thought the soviets had no real power, because they had been taken over by the Communist Party and its officials.

### Events

| Date (1921) | What happened |
|---|---|
| 28 February | Crews of the battleships *Petropavlovsk* and *Sevastopol* pass a resolution of fifteen demands |
| 1 March | A mass meeting of about 15,000 on Anchor Square adopts it; the head of state, Mikhail Kalinin, who is present, is shouted down |
| 2 March | A Provisional Revolutionary Committee, chaired by the sailor Stepan Petrichenko, takes over the fortress; the government announces that the rising is a White Guard plot |
| 7 March | The Red Army, under Mikhail Tukhachevsky, begins to bombard the fortress |
| 8 March | The Tenth Party Congress opens in Moscow |
| 16–18 March | The Red Army storms the fortress across the ice; Kronstadt falls on 18 March |

The sailors did not ask for the return of the Tsar or of the Whites. Their demands, in the **Petropavlovsk resolution**, were for the soviets to be re-elected by secret ballot with free campaigning, for freedom of speech and of the press for workers and peasants and for left socialist parties, for the release of socialist political prisoners, for the end of the Communist Party's special political departments, for equal rations, for the roadblock detachments (which seized food from peasants) to be abolished, and for peasants to be free to work their own land and keep livestock without hired labour.

:::eg A closer look: the Petropavlovsk resolution
The resolution was adopted on the battleship *Petropavlovsk* on 28 February 1921 and signed by the chairman, Petrichenko, and the secretary, Perepelkin. Its first demand was "immediately to hold new elections by secret ballot", because the existing soviets did not express the will of the workers and peasants. The second was "to give freedom of speech and press to workers and peasants, to anarchist and left socialist parties". The seventh was "to abolish all political departments, since no party should be given special privileges". *Adapted from the Petropavlovsk Resolution, 28 February 1921, in the translation in Paul Avrich, The Anarchists in the Russian Revolution (1973).*

**What it shows.** The sailors wanted soviet democracy, not the end of the Revolution; most of the demands were about political freedom, with some about the economy. Their slogan was "free soviets", and they turned the Bolsheviks' own cry of 1917, "All power to the Soviets", against them; the phrase "Soviets without Communists", often attached to the rising, is a later simplification.

**What it does not show.** How many sailors supported it, or how the sailors of 1921 differed from those of 1917. The text was published by the rebels themselves, in their newspaper, and survives in translations; the Bolshevik government's version described them as counter-revolutionaries led by a former Tsarist general, Kozlovsky, who was in the fortress as a military specialist; the rising was not his plot.
:::

### Impact

- **Military and political.** The rising was crushed with great force. The numbers are uncertain: Soviet figures give over a thousand rebels killed and many more wounded and captured, and the Red Army also lost heavily; about 8,000 people escaped across the ice to Finland; and the Cheka shot a large number of prisoners in the following months (about 2,100 sentenced to death by the summer, in one official count). The Bolsheviks presented the victory as the defence of the Revolution, though the rebels had been among its early supporters.
- **Policy.** It came in the middle of the Tenth Party Congress, which Lenin had already planned to use to change the policy on grain. Some 300 delegates left the Congress to take part in the assault, and ten of them died. Lenin drew two lessons from Kronstadt: tighten discipline in the party and satisfy the middle peasants as far as possible. On 9 March he told the Congress: "the petty-bourgeois counter-revolution is a greater danger than Denikin". The Kronstadt rising pushed the Congress to accept two policies within days: the end of requisitioning in favour of the **New Economic Policy**, and a ban on factions inside the party (see below).

:::def Kronstadt Mutiny
The rising of the sailors and garrison of the Kronstadt naval fortress in March 1921 against the Bolshevik government, demanding free soviet elections and an end to War Communism. It was crushed by the Red Army on 17–18 March, and it helped to convince the Bolsheviks to introduce the New Economic Policy.
:::

## The New Economic Policy

### Introduction

At the Tenth Party Congress (8–16 March 1921) Lenin proposed that the requisitioning of grain be replaced by a **tax in kind**: the peasants would give the state a fixed share of their harvest, and be free to sell the rest. The Congress approved on 15 March and a decree of 21 March made it law. Leon Trotsky had proposed something similar in early 1920 and had been turned down; the discussion had begun in *Pravda* weeks before Kronstadt, and risings such as Tambov were another reason. But Kronstadt made the decision urgent. Lenin explained the point to the delegates: it was the peasant's job as a proprietor to produce, and the state would take a minimum tax. He also described the change as the correction of the system of relations between the working class and the peasantry.

The tax in kind was the start of the **New Economic Policy** (NEP), which in the next months grew to include:
- **Free trade** in grain and other goods on the open market; the return of private shops and markets (the traders were known as Nepmen).
- **Small private business**: small factories were returned to their owners or leased, and artisans could trade.
- **State control of the "commanding heights"**: large industry, banks, transport and foreign trade stayed in state hands, but were run on commercial lines in trusts that had to make a profit.
- **Foreign concessions**: some foreign firms were invited to invest.
- **A stable currency**: a new gold-backed rouble, the *chervonets*, appeared in 1922, and a currency reform was completed in 1924. The tax in kind was replaced with a money tax in 1924.

:::def New Economic Policy (NEP)
The policy introduced in March 1921, which ended grain requisitioning and allowed peasants to sell their surplus; allowed private trade and small business; and kept the "commanding heights" of the economy (large industry, banks, foreign trade) in state hands. Lenin described it as a temporary retreat.
:::

### Impact

- **Recovery.** Agriculture recovered quickly after the famine, and by the mid-1920s farm and industrial output were close to pre-war levels. The towns revived, and shops filled.
- **Problems.** The prices of industrial goods were high compared to those of farm goods, so peasants could not afford to buy them: the "scissors crisis" of 1923. Unemployment was high in the cities, and the Nepmen and richer peasants (kulaks) became hated by many workers and by party members who felt that the Revolution was being betrayed.
- **A retreat.** Lenin called NEP a "retreat" in economics, and he said that the "commanding heights" were still in the party's hands. Many Communists supported it with reluctance.

:::warn NEP was a retreat in economics, not in politics
The Tenth Congress that approved the tax in kind also banned factions in the party. Its resolution "On Party Unity" ordered the dissolution of groups formed on the basis of platforms, and another condemned the "Workers' Opposition" (led by Alexandra Kollontai and Alexander Shlyapnikov) as an anarchist and syndicalist deviation. Other parties were already banned or suppressed. In 1922 the leaders of the SRs were tried in a show trial, and the Cheka was renamed the GPU. So the economy became freer in 1921 while politics became tighter. Do not write that NEP was a move to democracy.
:::

## Where were the Bolsheviks by 1924?

By 1924, Bolshevik power had survived and had changed.
- **A one-party state.** The Communist Party ran Russia through a hierarchy of its own, with the Soviets under its control. Other parties were banned; the press was controlled; the Cheka's successor, the GPU, watched the population. The party's own debate was restricted by the ban on factions of 1921.
- **The Soviet Union.** On 30 December 1922 the Russian republic, Ukraine, Belorussia and the Transcaucasian republic signed a treaty of union to form the **Union of Soviet Socialist Republics**. A constitution was adopted in January 1924. The Bolsheviks had recovered most of the old Empire, under a structure that gave the republics a form of autonomy under party control.
- **A recovering economy.** NEP gave the country a few years of peace and recovery, though the argument over whether it should last was just beginning.
- **A leadership in crisis.** Lenin suffered a stroke in May 1922 and further strokes in December 1922 and March 1923. In notes he dictated, which the party leaders did not publish, he criticised leading Bolsheviks and suggested that Stalin, who had been General Secretary of the party since April 1922, should be removed. He died on **21 January 1924**, aged 53. The struggle to succeed him, between Trotsky, Stalin and others, was the beginning of the next chapter of Soviet history.

:::key How the Bolsheviks consolidated their power to 1924
1. **Decrees** that won support: peace, land, workers' control and the rights of the peoples.
2. **Dictatorship**: the Cheka, the closure of the Constituent Assembly, the Terror and the ban on other parties and on factions.
3. **Brest-Litovsk**: a humiliating treaty that saved the regime by ending the war with Germany.
4. **Victory in the Civil War**: central position, Trotsky's Red Army, organisation and the peasants' fear of the Whites.
5. **War Communism** to win the war, and the **NEP** to save the regime after Kronstadt: flexible in economics, uncompromising in politics.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the Bolsheviks closed the Constituent Assembly in January 1918.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The Constituent Assembly met on 5 January 1918. The Bolsheviks did not have a majority because the SRs had won more votes. The Assembly would not agree to the Bolshevik decrees. So the Bolsheviks sent the guards to close it the next day. It was closed because Lenin did not like it."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes an event. "Lenin did not like it" is a general assertion, not an explanation. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was the election result. The elections of November 1917 gave the SRs about 40 per cent of the votes and about 380 of the 700 seats, and the Bolsheviks about 24 per cent and 170 seats. The Bolsheviks had won in the cities but the peasants, who were most of the electorate, had voted for the SRs. The Assembly therefore could not be controlled, and it could have formed a government in opposition to Sovnarkom. A second reason was the Assembly's refusal, on 5 January, to recognise Soviet power or the decrees on peace and land, and it elected Chernov, an SR, as its chairman. This showed that it would not accept the Bolsheviks' revolution, and the Bolsheviks and Left SRs walked out."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was ideological. Lenin's Theses of December 1917 said that a republic of Soviets was 'a higher form of democracy than the usual bourgeois republic with a Constituent Assembly', and that the SR lists, drawn up before the party split in November, no longer represented the voters. This gave the Bolsheviks an argument that the Soviet power was more democratic than the Assembly, and an excuse for not accepting the vote.

These factors were linked. The election result made it impossible for the Bolsheviks to govern through the Assembly; the Assembly's behaviour made it a rival government; and Lenin's argument turned the closure from a naked use of force into a claim that a higher democracy was being defended. The most important reason was the election result, because it was the failure to win that turned the Assembly from a promise into a threat. The Bolsheviks had attacked the Provisional Government for postponing it, and they let the elections go ahead, but they could not accept the result without giving up power. Closing the Assembly was therefore not a surprise, but the product of a choice they had made in October: to hold power as a party, not to share it."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the result made the Assembly a threat, its behaviour made it a rival, the ideology justified the closure), and a supported conclusion about the main reason. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The weakness of the Whites was the main reason for the Bolshevik victory in the Civil War.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: no single leader (Kolchak, Denikin, Yudenich, Wrangel acting separately); the Whites' policy on land and nationalities; conscription and desertion; supply from Britain and France; halfhearted intervention.
- Against 1, Red strengths: central position and railways; Trotsky and the Red Army; commissars; unity; the peasants' support because of the Decree on Land.
- Against 2, War Communism and the Terror: kept the army fed and silenced opposition.
- Judgement: the Whites' weaknesses gave the Reds the chance, but the Reds' strengths made use of it. Criterion: which would have mattered if the other had been different?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The weakness of the Whites was the main reason for the Bolshevik victory. The Whites were led by different generals, Kolchak in Siberia, Denikin in the south and Yudenich in the north-west, who never worked together and were thousands of kilometres apart. Their armies were made up of conscripts who deserted, and they did not have enough supplies. They also had no policy on the land, so the peasants did not support them. The Allies sent only a little help and then left. Because the Whites made so many mistakes, the Reds were able to win, so their weakness was the main reason."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: the Whites' mistakes do not by themselves explain why the Reds could exploit them. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the Whites' weakness gave the Reds an opportunity that the Reds had to be able to use. The Bolsheviks held the centre: Moscow, Petrograd, the railway junctions and the arms factories, and a population of about 60 million (a commonly cited estimate). This allowed them to use short interior lines to move troops from one front to another, so that in 1919 they beat Kolchak in the spring and summer, Denikin and Yudenich in the autumn, each in turn. Trotsky, as Commissar for War, built an army of over five million men on paper by conscription, brought in tens of thousands of former Tsarist officers under the watch of commissars, and enforced discipline, including the death penalty. The Red Army therefore had a unity of command that the Whites never had. The Decree on Land mattered too: the peasants did not love the Bolsheviks, but when Denikin's armies came back with the landlords' interests and requisitions of their own, they preferred the Reds.

War Communism and the Terror also contributed. Grain requisitioning and the nationalisation of industry kept the Red Army supplied, and the Cheka silenced opposition behind the lines. These measures caused great damage, as the famine of 1921–22 showed, but they were effective for the purpose of winning. The Whites, who held poor, thin regions, had no equivalent.

Overall, the Whites' weakness was a necessary condition, because a united and well-supplied White army with a policy on land might well have won in 1919, when Denikin was at Orel and Yudenich was at the gates of Petrograd. But it was not the main reason. The decisive factor was the Reds' organisation: their central position, their army and their control of the state, which turned White mistakes into defeat. The weakness of the Whites explains why the Reds could win, and the strengths of the Reds explain why they did. I therefore agree only to a limited extent."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (what turned the opportunity into victory) and distinguishes necessary from decisive causes. With an opening paragraph on the Whites' weaknesses, this is Level 5 (18–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

A republic of Soviets is a higher form of democracy than the usual bourgeois republic with a Constituent Assembly. The Socialist-Revolutionaries came out with united election lists for the Constituent Assembly in the middle of October 1917, but they split in November 1917, after the elections and before the Assembly met. Therefore the lists no longer express the real will of the people.

*Adapted from Lenin, Theses on the Constituent Assembly, written 11 or 12 December 1917 and published in Pravda on 26 December 1917.*

**Source B**

At present the soviets do not express the will of the workers and peasants. We therefore demand that new elections to the soviets be held immediately by secret ballot, with freedom of campaigning beforehand for all workers and peasants. Freedom of speech and press must be given to workers and peasants, and to anarchist and left socialist parties.

*Adapted from the Petropavlovsk resolution, adopted by the crews of the battleships at Kronstadt, 28 February 1921, in the translation in Paul Avrich, The Anarchists in the Russian Revolution (1973).*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to democracy and the soviets in Soviet Russia.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says a republic of Soviets is more democratic than a Constituent Assembly. Source B says the soviets do not express the will of the workers and peasants. So they disagree. Source A is by Lenin and Source B is by the sailors, so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on bias is a general assertion with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14 marks).**

"The sources share some common ground. Both appeal to the will of the people as the test of a legitimate government: Source A says the lists 'no longer express the real will of the people', and Source B says the soviets 'do not express the will of the workers and peasants'. Both also accept that the soviets are the proper form of government, since neither asks for a return to a Constituent Assembly or the Provisional Government.

They differ over what is wrong, and who is to blame. Source A defends the Soviet system against the Assembly: the Soviet republic is 'a higher form of democracy', and the fault is in the election lists, drawn up before the SRs split. Source B attacks the Soviet system as it is, saying that it is the soviets that do not represent the people, and demands new elections by secret ballot and freedom of speech and press for socialist parties other than the Communists. In Source A, democracy is a claim made in order to justify the closure of an elected assembly; in Source B it is a demand for the right to elect and to speak.

The context explains the difference. Source A was written in December 1917, six weeks after the Bolsheviks had come to power, to justify their plan to disperse the Assembly, where they had won only about a quarter of the seats. Its purpose was to give the party a theoretical reason not to accept the vote, and it was published in Pravda to persuade party members and workers. Source B was written by sailors in February 1921, after three years of Civil War and War Communism, when the soviets were controlled by the Communist Party and the rights promised in 1917 had gone. Its authors had been among the Bolsheviks' strongest supporters in 1917. They were using the party's own language of soviet democracy against it, which is why the demand was dangerous to the regime. For the question, Source A is better evidence of the Bolsheviks' attitude to democracy at the start of their rule, since it comes from the party's leader and was acted upon. Source B is better evidence of the failure of that promise, because it is a protest from inside the revolutionary camp, though as a document written by one side of a dispute it does not tell us how far all sailors, or the people, agreed."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (December 1917, February 1921) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

Comrades and citizens! The Communist Party, which rules the country, has become separated from the masses. The Provisional Committee is deeply concerned that not a single drop of blood should be spilled. It calls on workers and soldiers to keep order and to prepare fair elections to a new Soviet.

*Adapted from the first issue of the Kronstadt Izvestiya, newspaper of the Provisional Revolutionary Committee, 3 March 1921.*

**Source B**

The Petropavlovsk resolution demands the immediate removal of the roadblock detachments, equal rations for all working people, the abolition of the Communist fighting detachments in the army and the Communist guards in the factories, and full freedom for peasants to act on their land and keep cattle, provided they work without hired labour.

*A summary of demands 8 to 11 of the Petropavlovsk resolution, 28 February 1921, from the translation in Paul Avrich, The Anarchists in the Russian Revolution (1973).*

**Source C**

The petty-bourgeois counter-revolution is a greater danger than Denikin. We are passing through a period of grave danger. The political conclusion to be drawn from the present situation is that the Party must be united.

*Adapted from Lenin, summing-up speech to the Tenth Congress of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks), 9 March 1921.*

**Source D**

The surplus appropriation system was a compulsory state monopoly, driven by the needs of war, not by economic principle. The peasant needs an incentive: it is your job as a proprietor to produce, and the state will take a minimum tax. We are correcting the relations between the proletariat and the peasantry.

*Adapted from Lenin, report on the substitution of a tax in kind for the surplus appropriation system, Tenth Congress of the Russian Communist Party (Bolsheviks), 15 March 1921.*

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that the Kronstadt rising forced the Bolsheviks to change their policies?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: B (the economic demands of the sailors, including freedom for the peasants, are the ones that the Bolsheviks gave in D), C (Lenin treats the situation as a danger that requires change), D (the Bolsheviks end requisitioning six days after C). Challenge or qualify: C (Lenin's answer is political unity, not freedom: the Bolsheviks tightened their grip as they loosened the economy); A (the sailors' main demand is political, new elections, which the Bolsheviks refused); D (does not mention Kronstadt, and the tax in kind was already being discussed); and the dates (the Congress began on 8 March, the day after the bombardment started).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a rebel newspaper, written to win support in the town and to reassure the Bolsheviks that there would be no bloodshed. It is strong evidence of what the rebels said they wanted and of their political demands, weaker evidence of what the whole garrison thought; and it comes from before the assault, when the rebels were hoping for support from Petrograd.
- **B**: a summary of four of the fifteen demands, so it is selective, and the selection is of the economic demands. In the full text most demands are political. It is strong evidence that peasant grievances reached the sailors, but it must not be taken to show that the rising was mainly about economics.
- **C**: Lenin speaking to the party's own Congress, in the middle of the crisis, when Kronstadt was still unbeaten. It is a reliable guide to how the leader read the situation and what he wanted the party to do, but it is a speech to persuade, and its words "the petty-bourgeois counter-revolution" describe the rising as an enemy, not a warning. It supports unity more than change.
- **D**: a policy statement to the same Congress, a week later. It is strong evidence of the content of the change, and of Lenin's reasons, but it says nothing of Kronstadt, and Lenin gives the needs of the peasants, not the sailors' revolt, as the cause.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources B, C and D support the view that Kronstadt forced the Bolsheviks to change. Source B shows that the sailors demanded an end to the roadblock detachments and freedom for the peasants on their land, and Source D shows that within a week Lenin gave the peasants a tax in place of requisitioning. Source C, spoken on 9 March while the fortress still held out, shows Lenin calling the situation 'a period of grave danger'. But the sources also qualify the view. Source D does not mention Kronstadt, and Lenin presents the tax as a response to the peasants' need for an incentive; the discussion had begun in Pravda weeks before the rising, and there had been peasant revolts at Tambov and in Siberia. Source A shows that the main demand of the rebels was political, new elections, and to that the Bolsheviks gave no ground: in Source C Lenin's conclusion is that the Party must be united, and the Congress banned factions. The sources therefore support the view only in part: Kronstadt forced the Bolsheviks to speed up and to confirm a change in economic policy, but it made them tighten their political control. They changed what they could afford to change and refused what could have cost them power."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1921; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far did the Kronstadt mutiny force Lenin to change policy?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Kronstadt sailors rose against the Bolsheviks on 28 February 1921 and passed the Petropavlovsk resolution. On 1 March there was a mass meeting of about 15,000 people, and Kalinin was shouted down. Petrograd was put under martial law and Tukhachevsky began to attack on 7 March. The Tenth Party Congress was held on 8 March, and on 15 March Lenin introduced the tax in kind. The Red Army stormed Kronstadt on 17 March across the ice and it fell on 18 March. About 8,000 people escaped to Finland. This led to the New Economic Policy."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative**: it tells the story of the rising in order, and the last sentence only asserts the link. It never explains why Kronstadt mattered to Lenin or considers any other cause of the NEP. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "Kronstadt was important because it showed that discontent had reached the Bolsheviks' own supporters. The sailors had been among the party's strongest allies in 1917, and their demands, for free elections and for peasants to farm their own land, matched those of the strikers in Petrograd and the peasant rebels in Tambov. Lenin said on 9 March that 'the petty-bourgeois counter-revolution is a greater danger than Denikin', and within a week the Tenth Congress had replaced requisitioning with the tax in kind. However, it was not the only cause: the tax had been discussed in Pravda before the rising and Trotsky had proposed it in 1920, and the famine and the peasant risings had already shown that requisitioning could not go on. Kronstadt did not cause the change but it ended the argument." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name the four decrees or declarations of Sovnarkom in 1917 that the syllabus lists, and say what each did.
A: The Decree on Peace (26 October) proposed peace without annexations; the Decree on Land (26 October) abolished landed proprietorship without compensation; the Decree on Workers' Control (14 November OS) gave factory committees the right to supervise production; the Declaration of the Rights of the Peoples of Russia (2 November OS) promised equality and self-determination to the peoples of the Empire.
Q: What was the Cheka, who led it and when was it set up?
A: The Bolshevik political police, set up on 7 December 1917 (OS) under Felix Dzerzhinsky, with the power to arrest, imprison and shoot "counter-revolutionaries" without trial; it carried out the Red Terror from September 1918.
Q: How many votes and seats did the Bolsheviks win in the Constituent Assembly elections, and what happened to the Assembly?
A: About 24 per cent of the votes and about 170 of about 700 seats; the SRs won about 40 per cent and a majority of the seats. The Assembly met on 5 January 1918 (OS) for one night and was dispersed by the guards, and Sovnarkom dissolved it.
Q: What were the main terms of the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, and what were its effects?
A: Russia gave up Poland and the Baltic lands, recognised Finland and Ukraine and gave up Kars, Ardahan and Batum; it lost about a third of its population and most of its coal and iron. The Left SRs left the government, and the treaty added to the causes of the Civil War, but it gave Lenin a breathing space and ended the war with Germany.
Q: Give three weaknesses of the Whites.
A: No single leader and no common policy; conscription and desertion; poor supply and dependence on foreign help; half-hearted foreign intervention that allowed the Reds to call them traitors; association with the landlords and a refusal to grant autonomy to the nationalities.
Q: What was War Communism, and why did it help to win the Civil War but cause trouble afterwards?
A: Nationalisation of industry, requisitioning of grain, rationing and the ban on private trade. It supplied the Red Army and the cities, but it ruined industry and farming, caused the famine of 1921–22 and led to peasant risings and strikes.
Q: What were the Kronstadt sailors' main demands?
A: New elections to the soviets by secret ballot, freedom of speech and press for workers, peasants and left socialist parties, the release of socialist political prisoners, the end of the roadblock detachments and political departments, equal rations and freedom for the peasants to work their own land.
Q: What was the NEP, and why is it called a retreat in economics but not in politics?
A: From 1921 requisitioning was replaced by a tax in kind and private trade and small business were allowed, while the state kept the "commanding heights". At the same Congress, factions in the party were banned, so political control became tighter.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Bolsheviks signed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in March 1918.
A: Indicative content: the army had disintegrated and could not fight (soldiers leaving to claim land); the Bolsheviks had come to power on a promise of peace; the German advance of 18 February took a large area in a week and threatened Petrograd; Lenin argued that the revolution needed a breathing space and that the treaty would be annulled when revolution came in Germany; the party was split (Bukharin's revolutionary war; Trotsky's "neither war nor peace") and Lenin's threat to resign won a narrow majority. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: military weakness left no choice, the promise of peace made it politically necessary, Lenin's argument made it acceptable to the party; and judges which was most important (e.g. the state of the army).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Bolsheviks introduced War Communism.
A: Indicative content: the Civil War began in 1918 and the army and cities needed grain and weapons; the peasants would not sell grain at fixed prices when money was worthless; the Bolsheviks needed to control industry and trade to supply the front; ideology (many Bolsheviks believed nationalisation was the road to socialism); the loss of Ukraine and the grain areas after Brest-Litovsk. Level 4: connect the emergency and the ideology, and judge which was more important (e.g. that it began as an emergency but became a belief, so it was kept after the war was nearly won).
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far did the Kronstadt mutiny force Lenin to change policy?
A: For: the rising came at the opening of the Tenth Congress; Lenin said the lesson was to unite the party and satisfy the peasants; requisitioning ended within two weeks; the demands (peasant freedom, end of roadblocks) were met in part. Against: the tax in kind was already under discussion (Pravda, Trotsky's proposal of 1920); the famine, peasant revolts at Tambov and strikes in Petrograd were also pressures; the political demands were refused and politics tightened. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that Kronstadt decided the timing and removed opposition within the party to the economic change, but did not cause the change on its own.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was foreign intervention responsible for the Bolshevik victory in the Civil War?
A: For: the Whites relied on foreign supplies which were wasted; intervention allowed the Reds to claim to defend the motherland and to call the Whites traitors; the intervention was half-hearted and was withdrawn, leaving the Whites alone. Against: the Reds' central position, organisation and Trotsky's army; the peasants' fear of the Whites; War Communism and the Terror; White disunity. Level 5 judges that intervention was a minor factor in itself (a small, short effort) but it helped the Reds' propaganda; the main reasons lay in the Reds' organisation and the Whites' weakness.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The Bolsheviks used terror more than popular support to stay in power between 1917 and 1924.' How far do you agree?
H: List where terror was used: the Cheka, the Red Terror, hostages, the closure of the Assembly, Kronstadt.
H: Then list popular support: the decrees, the peasants' fear of the Whites, the Red Army's strength, the NEP.
A: Terror: the Cheka from December 1917; the Red Terror from September 1918; the dispersal of the Assembly and the repression of other parties; the crushing of Kronstadt and Tambov; the show trial of the SRs in 1922. Support: the Decree on Land and the peasants' acceptance; support from workers in the cities, particularly in 1917–18; patriotism in the war against foreign armies and the Poles; the concession of the NEP, which brought recovery. Level 5 judges, e.g., that support was decisive in the Civil War (the peasants chose the Reds over the Whites) but terror kept the regime in power afterwards, when support wore thin, so the balance changed over time.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about Bolshevik policy towards the peasants.
A: Similarities: both treat the peasants as people whom the state must satisfy and give them a right to the produce of their land (A: land to the peasants' committees and ordinary peasants' land left alone; B: "the peasant needs an incentive … the state will take a minimum tax"). Differences: A is about the land (who owns it) and gives it without payment; B is about the produce (what the state takes), and is a retreat from requisitioning; A is a revolutionary promise, B is an admission of failure. Explanation: A was adopted on 26 October 1917, when the Bolsheviks needed peasant support to survive and borrowed the SR programme; B was said in March 1921, after three years of requisitioning, famine and the peasant risings, to save the regime. Provenance: A is a decree, an official act, passed hurriedly; B is a speech by Lenin to his party, which explains the policy to delegates who feared it. Level 4 explains the differences through this context and judges which is better evidence of policy (A of the original promise, B of the practice and its failure).
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How successful was the Bolshevik government in solving its economic problems between 1918 and 1924?
H: Separate the three periods: 1918–21 (War Communism), 1921–24 (NEP).
H: Define "success": did the policy feed people, restore output, and keep the regime in power?
A: Success: War Communism fed the army and kept the regime alive in the Civil War; NEP ended the famine, restored agriculture and industry to near pre-war levels by the mid-1920s, and stabilised the currency (chervonets 1922, reform 1924). Failure: War Communism collapsed output (industry about a fifth of 1913), caused the famine and the risings; NEP produced the scissors crisis, unemployment, and resentment of the Nepmen and kulaks; it was seen by many as a retreat. Level 5 judges, e.g., that the policies were successful in what they were meant to do (survive the war, then recover), but at a very high human cost in the first period, and left unresolved the question of how to industrialise.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Historians have explained the Bolshevik victory in the Civil War mainly by the strengths of the Reds, or mainly by the weaknesses of the Whites. How far does the evidence of 1918–21 support one view more than the other?
H: List the strengths of the Reds and the weaknesses of the Whites, and ask which would have mattered if the other had been different.
H: Remember that the peasants did not choose the Reds out of love: ask what they feared.
A: Reds: the central position, railways and factories; the Red Army under Trotsky; the Decree on Land; War Communism and the Terror. Whites: separate armies, no common policy, conscription, supply, the half-hearted intervention, association with landlords and pogroms. A* judgement: the Whites' weaknesses were mostly the result of their position: they were on the edge, with no central territory, and so their disunity was not a mistake that a better leader could have corrected. This makes the Reds' geographical and organisational advantage the underlying cause, and the Whites' weakness a consequence of it. Moreover, a victory won by the central position and the peasants' fear of the landlords owed less to anyone's skill than to the circumstances of the war.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Trotsky's *My Life* (1930) is sometimes used as evidence for the building of the Red Army. How far is it reliable evidence of the reasons for the Reds' victory in the Civil War?
H: Who wrote it, when and why? Where was Trotsky in 1930?
H: What could he know first-hand, and what might he exaggerate or leave out?
A: Strengths: written by the Commissar for War, who built the army and went to the fronts; first-hand detail on the use of military specialists, commissars and the armoured train; the facts it gives (the Red Army's methods and discipline) agree with other evidence. Weaknesses: written in exile in 1929–30, after Stalin had defeated and expelled him, to defend his reputation and to show his own part in the Revolution; it exaggerates his personal role and plays down Lenin's and the party's, and the work of others such as Stalin, Tukhachevsky and Frunze; it does not discuss the costs of the Terror or the harshness of the discipline; it was a book for a western public. Judgement: reliable on how the army was organised and on Trotsky's methods, but unreliable on the weight of his own part in the victory and on the human cost; it should be checked against other evidence, such as the orders and records of the Red Army and the accounts of others.

:::recap
- **First decrees**: Sovnarkom (26 October 1917); Decrees on Peace and Land, Workers' Control (14 November OS), Rights of the Peoples (2 November OS); Left SRs in the government December 1917–March 1918.
- **Dictatorship**: the Cheka (7 December 1917 OS, Dzerzhinsky); the Constituent Assembly (elections 12 November OS: SRs about 40 per cent, Bolsheviks about 24 per cent) met on 5 January 1918 and was dispersed; the Red Terror from 5 September 1918; the constitution of July 1918.
- **Brest-Litovsk** (3 March 1918): Russia left the war and lost Poland, the Baltic lands, Finland, Ukraine and Caucasus districts, about a third of its population and most of its coal and iron; Left SRs left; capital to Moscow.
- **Civil War** (1918–21): Czech Legion (May 1918), Allied landings, Kolchak (Siberia), Denikin (south), Yudenich (north-west), Wrangel (Crimea), the war with Poland (1920); the royal family killed in July 1918.
- **Why the Reds won**: central position, Trotsky's Red Army, unity and organisation, the peasants' fear of the landlords, War Communism and the Terror; **why the Whites lost**: no single leader or policy, conscription, supply, half-hearted intervention, link with landlords.
- **War Communism** (1918–21): nationalised industry, grain requisitioning, rationing; industry about a fifth of 1913; famine of 1921–22 (about 3 to 5 million dead).
- **Kronstadt** (28 February–18 March 1921): Petropavlovsk resolution; crushed by Tukhachevsky; about 8,000 fled to Finland.
- **NEP** (March 1921): tax in kind, free trade, small business, state "commanding heights"; recovery by the mid-1920s; the ban on factions the same month.
- **By 1924**: one-party state; USSR formed 30 December 1922; Lenin dead on 21 January 1924.
