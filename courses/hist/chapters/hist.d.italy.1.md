## Italy after the First World War

Italy in 1919 was a young country. It had been united only in 1861 and Rome had been added in 1870. It was a **liberal** kingdom, that is, a constitutional monarchy with a parliament and a wide range of freedoms, but its political life was narrow and its society sharply divided. The north (Piedmont, Lombardy, Liguria) had factories and a growing working class. The south (the *Mezzogiorno*) was poor, agricultural and held by large landowners. About half the workforce still worked on the land. The Catholic Church had refused to recognise the Italian state since its capture of Rome in 1870, and the Pope had told Catholics not to stand for parliament; the ban was relaxed by 1913 and ended in 1919 with the founding of a Catholic party. Politicians were used to doing deals in parliament, a habit known as *trasformismo* (changing sides to make a majority), which made voters think of them as self-seeking.

:::def Liberal Italy
The parliamentary monarchy of the Kingdom of Italy from unification to 1922, ruled by a small group of Liberal politicians (Giolitti, Salandra and others) who formed shifting coalitions, with a king who appointed prime ministers and commanded the army.
:::

### The impact of the war

Italy entered the First World War in May 1915, almost ten months after it began, under the secret **Treaty of London**. Public opinion was divided: most Socialists, most Catholics and the Liberal leader Giolitti wanted to stay neutral, and the decision was pushed through by the king, the prime minister Salandra and noisy nationalist demonstrations (the "radiant days of May"). The war that followed was the harshest that Italy had known:
- **Deaths and wounds.** About 650,000 Italian soldiers died, and about 950,000 were wounded or permanently disabled. Most fighting was a mountain war against Austria-Hungary along the Isonzo river. The defeat at **Caporetto** (October–November 1917) cost 300,000 prisoners and produced a national shock, though the army recovered and won the final battle at Vittorio Veneto in October 1918.
- **Money.** The war cost far more than the state could raise in taxes. The government borrowed at home and abroad, and the public debt was about five times larger at the end than at the beginning. To cover costs it printed money, so prices rose: the cost of living in 1920 was roughly four times the level of 1914. People on fixed incomes (clerks, teachers, pensioners, small savers) saw their position collapse, while some industrialists who supplied the war (steel and engineering, such as Ansaldo and Fiat) made fortunes.
- **Society.** More than five million men had served, and many returned to find no work, high prices and a government that spoke of sacrifice but gave little help. Young officers who had learned to lead men in battle found no outlet for their energy. The elite storm troops, the *arditi*, and many veterans came to despise the civilian politicians who, they said, had not understood the war.

:::key The legacy of the war in Italy
1. **A bitter victory.** About 650,000 dead, a huge debt and a wartime promise unkept.
2. **An economic shock.** Inflation, unemployment as the war industries closed, and a squeezed middle class.
3. **A militarised society.** Millions of ex-soldiers, many angry and some trained to fight, and a cult of the war among nationalists.
4. **A divided country.** The war had divided Italians before it began (interventionists against neutralists), and the divisions were not healed by victory.
:::

### The "mutilated victory"

At the Paris Peace Conference the Italian prime minister **Vittorio Orlando** and his foreign minister **Sidney Sonnino** demanded all that the Treaty of London had promised and, in addition, the Adriatic port of **Fiume** (now Rijeka), where many of the people were Italian speakers. President Wilson refused. He thought the secret treaty was the old diplomacy, that its Dalmatian coast was mostly Slav, and that Fiume was needed by the new Yugoslav state as a port. Britain and France, which had signed the Treaty of London, did not press Wilson hard. Orlando left the conference on 24 April 1919 in protest, returned in May to find that decisions had gone on without him, and fell from office in June.

By the treaty of **Saint-Germain** (September 1919) Italy gained the Trentino, South Tyrol (up to the Brenner Pass, with some 200,000 German speakers), Trieste and Istria. It did not receive most of Dalmatia, or Fiume, or the colonies it had been promised in Africa and Asia Minor. By any normal measure this was a large gain. But the Italian press and nationalists, led by D'Annunzio, said that Italy had been robbed of what its dead had earned: a **"mutilated victory"** (*vittoria mutilata*).

:::def Mutilated victory
The phrase of nationalists, first used by D'Annunzio in 1918, for the claim that Italy had won the war but lost the peace, because the Paris settlement gave it less than the Treaty of London had promised and denied it Fiume and Dalmatia.
:::

The phrase mattered for two reasons. First, it turned a diplomatic disappointment into an insult to the nation's honour and to the sacrifice of the dead, which discredited the liberal governments that had made the peace. Second, it gave a cause to the ex-servicemen and nationalists who would join the Fascists. Be careful with the claim, though: many Italians did not care about Dalmatia, and the Socialists and Catholics, who together won more than half the votes in 1919, rejected the nationalists' view. The mutilated victory was a weapon for a minority, which became a majority view only because other things went wrong.

### The seizure of Fiume

Fiume had been in Austria-Hungary. An Italian-speaking group in the city wanted union with Italy, and Italian troops stayed in the city while the Allies argued. On **12 September 1919** the poet and war hero **Gabriele D'Annunzio** marched into Fiume at the head of about 2,500 men, many of them mutinous soldiers and *arditi*, and declared its annexation to Italy. Prime minister **Francesco Nitti** did not dare to use force against a national hero, and the army officers sent against him often joined him. D'Annunzio ruled the city for fifteen months. He gave speeches from the balcony, with the crowd answering his questions with a roar ("Eia, eia, alalà!"), and his supporters wore black shirts and used the stiff-armed Roman salute. In September 1920 his regime published the *Carta del Carnaro*, a constitution that mixed corporations of workers and employers with a cult of the leader.

Giolitti, who became prime minister again in June 1920, ended the affair by diplomacy and force. The **Treaty of Rapallo** (12 November 1920) with Yugoslavia made Fiume a free state, with Italy keeping Istria and the town of Zara. D'Annunzio refused to leave, and on "Bloody Christmas" (24–29 December 1920) the Italian navy fired on the city and the army moved in; he surrendered, and left in January 1921.

Fiume taught Italy and Mussolini three things: that a government could be defied by volunteers in uniform; that the army and police would not always act against nationalists, so that violence in a nationalist cause could go unpunished; and that rituals (the salute, the black shirt, the balcony speech, the rhythmic chants) could move crowds. Fascism would take much of its style from D'Annunzio. But D'Annunzio had no real organisation and no political programme, and the liberal state defeated him. Mussolini made his own movement better organised.

### Economic challenges: unemployment and inflation

Peacetime brought a sharp downturn. Armies were demobilised, war contracts ended, and engineering firms laid off workers. Unemployment rose to hundreds of thousands, and to about half a million in 1921, as a world slump hit exports. Inflation kept eroding wages even when firms were hiring. The lira, which had been worth about five to the dollar before the war, lost most of its value against the dollar by 1920. The government's budget was far out of balance: it still spent heavily on bread subsidies and war pensions. The banks suffered too: the Banca Italiana di Sconto, a major bank linked to the steel firm Ansaldo, collapsed in December 1921.

Two groups were hit hardest in ways that matter for what followed. **Workers** demanded higher wages and shorter hours, and in 1919–20 there were thousands of strikes. **The middle classes** (the "white-collar" clerks, small shopkeepers, teachers, minor officials, ex-officers) had no strong union or party to defend them. Their savings and salaries were shrinking, they feared the Socialists, and they felt abandoned by the state. They would give Fascism much of its early support.

### The Biennio Rosso, 1919–20

The two years 1919–20 are called the ***Biennio Rosso*** ("the two red years"). Inspired by the Russian Revolution, and by real hardship, the working class took militant action:
- **Strikes** spread through industry and agriculture, and the number of trade unionists soared (the Socialist union confederation CGL had about two million members by 1920, and the Catholic unions another million or so).
- **Landless labourers** in the Po valley, led by socialist and Catholic "leagues", forced landowners to hire them on better terms, and in the south peasants occupied uncultivated estates.
- In **September 1920**, after employers locked out metalworkers in Milan, about **400,000 workers** occupied factories in Milan, Turin and other northern cities for almost a month. They hoisted red flags, ran the plants and tried to make products. Revolutionaries such as Antonio Gramsci in Turin hoped it would be the start of an Italian soviet revolution.

The occupation ended without a revolution. Giolitti refused to send in troops, saying the occupation would tire itself out, and he arranged a settlement that promised workers' control of industry (which never came). The Socialist party, divided between revolutionaries and moderates, did not know what to do with the occupation, and in January 1921 its left wing broke away to found the **Communist Party of Italy** at Livorno. But the damage to the Left had a second effect. Landowners, industrialists and many in the middle class had been truly frightened. They believed that the state was too weak to protect them, and that the Socialists were a revolutionary danger. In practice the revolution was never close: the workers had no plan to seize power, and by 1921 unemployment and employers' counter-attacks had put the unions on the defensive. But what matters for the rise of Fascism is the fear, which was real.

:::warn Fear, not revolution
Do not write that Italy "was on the verge of communist revolution" in 1920. Historians agree that the Left was divided and had no plan to take power. The point is that many property-owners *believed* the danger was real, and that belief explains why they paid for, and tolerated, Fascist violence.
:::

### Government instability and unpopularity

Parliament had no effective majority. The electoral law of **1912** gave the vote to almost all men (those over 30, and those over 21 who had done military service), which raised the electorate from about three million to over eight million, and in **1919** a new law introduced **proportional representation**, with large constituencies and party lists. The old Liberal groups were now outvoted. In the **November 1919 election** the Italian Socialist Party (PSI) won 156 of 508 seats and the new Catholic **Italian People's Party** (PPI, the *Popolari*, founded by the priest **Luigi Sturzo** in January 1919) won 100. Neither would work with the other or with the Liberals, so every government rested on a shifting alliance of Liberals and groups from the centre.

Between 1919 and Mussolini's appointment in October 1922 there were five governments under four prime ministers: **Nitti** (June 1919–June 1920), **Giolitti** (June 1920–July 1921), **Bonomi** (July 1921–February 1922) and **Facta** (February–October 1922, in two ministries). None lasted a year. Each depended on the Popolari, the Socialists' abstention or Liberal factions, and each failed to deal with strikes, inflation and the violence in the countryside. The Liberals were old men whose reputations were tied to the pre-war system, and no one of them could unite the others. People saw parliament as slow, corrupt and powerless, so that "a strong government" had a wide appeal.

:::key Why liberal Italy was unstable by 1922
1. **No majority.** Proportional representation produced two large parties (PSI and PPI) that would not co-operate, and a Liberal centre too fragmented to rule alone.
2. **No solution to the economic crisis.** Inflation, unemployment and strikes, and a budget deficit.
3. **The mutilated victory.** Governments were blamed for a peace that nationalists called a defeat, and for failing to control D'Annunzio.
4. **Weak response to violence.** Prefects, police and army often let Fascist violence go unpunished, so the state seemed unwilling to defend itself.
5. **Fear of the Left.** The Biennio Rosso persuaded the propertied classes that parliament could not protect them.
:::

## The rise of Fascism, 1919–22

### Benito Mussolini

**Benito Mussolini** was born on 29 July 1883 in Predappio, in Romagna, the son of a blacksmith who was a socialist and a mother who was a schoolteacher. He trained as a teacher himself, worked in Switzerland and in the Trentino, was jailed for his political activity, and became a leading figure on the revolutionary wing of the Socialist Party. In 1912 he became editor of the party newspaper *Avanti!*. When the war began he first called for neutrality and then, in autumn 1914, argued that Italy should fight. The party expelled him in November 1914, and he founded his own paper, ***Il Popolo d'Italia***, financed in part by industrialists and by foreign money. He served at the front and was wounded in February 1917 when a trench-mortar bomb exploded during practice, and returned to Milan as a nationalist who wanted a "new Italy" built by the men who had fought.

His strengths as a politician were real: he was a gifted journalist and speaker, he knew how to dramatise events, and he was ruthlessly flexible, ready to change his programme to suit circumstances. What he believed beyond power is debated. His enemies said he had no beliefs. His admirers said he was a revolutionary reshaping Italy. A fair reading is that his ideas were nationalism, hostility to socialism and liberal democracy, the glorification of violence and war, the cult of a leader, and the aim of a strong state; and that his programme was whatever served the movement.

### The Fasci di Combattimento and the San Sepolcro programme

On **23 March 1919** Mussolini gathered about a hundred men, among them ex-soldiers, *arditi*, nationalists, Futurists and former syndicalists, at a hall in Piazza San Sepolcro in Milan and founded the **Fasci Italiani di Combattimento** ("Italian fighting leagues"). The word *fascio* (bundle) came from the Roman *fasces*, the bundle of rods tied round an axe carried before magistrates, a symbol of strength through unity. The group's programme, published in June 1919, was a mixture: it was patriotic, demanding Fiume and Dalmatia, and it was also radical. It called for a republic, a vote for women, a minimum wage and the eight-hour day, workers' participation in running industry, a heavy tax on capital, confiscation of war profits and of church property, and the abolition of the Senate.

:::def Fascism (in Italy, to 1922)
The movement founded by Mussolini in 1919: nationalist, anti-socialist and anti-liberal, led by an all-powerful leader, glorifying the nation and the war, and using paramilitary violence. Its aims at the start were vague and shifted according to what would win support.
:::

The programme had no success. In the **November 1919 election** the Fascist list in Milan, headed by Mussolini and by the conductor Arturo Toscanini, won fewer than 5,000 votes and no seat. Voters wanted either a socialist or a Catholic party, and a movement that tried to be both nationalist and radical, with only a few thousand members, looked finished. Mussolini was ready to drop republicanism and anti-clericalism, and the movement turned to violence.

### Squadrismo

From autumn 1920 the Fascists became a force through **squadrismo**. Armed groups (*squadre d'azione*, "action squads", dressed in black shirts) carried out "punitive expeditions" against Socialist and Catholic organisations, trade union halls, cooperatives, newspapers and local councils, beating officials and forcing opponents to drink castor oil or to resign. The first large attacks were on the Slovene cultural centre in Trieste in July 1920 and the shooting at the town hall in Bologna in November 1920. The method was then developed in the Po valley and Tuscany, especially around Ferrara, Bologna, Cremona and Florence, where local leaders called **ras** (after Ethiopian chiefs) such as **Italo Balbo** in Ferrara, **Roberto Farinacci** in Cremona and **Dino Grandi** in Bologna built a following of their own.

:::def Squadrismo
The campaign of violence carried out from 1920 by Fascist armed squads against socialists, trade unions, cooperatives and some Catholic organisations, mostly in northern and central Italy. Its leaders were local bosses called *ras*.
:::

The squads grew because they met the needs of different groups. For **landowners and farmers** in the Po valley, who were threatened by the leagues of labourers, the squads smashed the unions and put workers back under control, and landowners paid. For **ex-officers and veterans**, jobless and restless, the squads gave status, adventure and a wage. For **students and the lower-middle class**, they offered a nationalist crusade against "Bolshevism". The police and army, who often sympathised or at least saw the squads as an ally against the Left, gave them weapons and transport or did nothing. Magistrates seldom punished the squads. Giolitti, who saw the Fascists as a useful counterweight to the Socialists and expected to absorb them, did not suppress them. The result was hundreds of killings in 1921 alone, and by mid-1922 squads were taking over entire towns by force.

Membership jumped from some 20,000 at the end of 1920 to over 200,000 a year later. The movement was strongest in the countryside and small towns of the north and centre (Emilia-Romagna, Tuscany, Lombardy, Veneto), and much weaker in industrial Turin and in the south, where there was no strong labour movement to fight.

### Politics: from movement to party

Mussolini's aim was to turn his squads into a political force. In the **May 1921 election** Giolitti included the Fascists in a **National Bloc** with Liberals and Nationalists, hoping to tame them. About 35 Fascists were elected, including Mussolini. In **August 1921** Mussolini, worried that the violence made the movement unpopular and could provoke a crackdown, signed a **Pact of Pacification** with the Socialists. The *ras* rejected the pact with fury, and Mussolini had to give way: he resigned from the movement's executive committee, then won it back at the Rome congress of **November 1921** by turning the movement into the **National Fascist Party (PNF)**, with a more conservative programme (respect for property, the Church and the monarchy; the state to withdraw from the economy). The lesson for Mussolini was that he could not control the *ras* without their cooperation, and the *ras* needed his political cover. The pair needed each other.

The result was a **dual policy**: legal and violent at once.

:::def Dual policy
Mussolini's strategy of 1921–22: to take part in parliamentary politics and negotiate with Liberal leaders (so appearing respectable and reassuring the elites), while keeping the squads active (so threatening the state with violence and making himself indispensable as the only man who could control them).
:::

### Why the elites let it happen

In 1922 the state still had the power to crush the Fascists. The army was a loyal institution commanded by the king, and its generals included the war heroes Diaz and Badoglio; the Fascists were lightly armed. The reason it did not act lay in the attitudes of the people who mattered:
- **The political class.** Giolitti, Salandra, Orlando and Facta, the old Liberals, did not see Fascism as a threat to the state. They thought they could "constitutionalise" it by bringing it into government (**Giolitti's** bet in 1921) and use it to weaken the Socialists. Several of them were in secret talks with Mussolini during 1922 about a coalition.
- **The landowners and industrialists.** The landowners of the Po valley, who paid for squads, wanted order in the countryside. Industrial leaders in Confindustria and the banks wanted a government that would suppress strikes and cut taxes. They did not all love Fascism, but they preferred it to the Left.
- **The Church.** The Vatican distrusted the Popolari, who were independent of the hierarchy, and welcomed an anti-socialist who promised to respect religion. Pope Pius XI, elected in February 1922, did not condemn the Fascists.
- **The army and police.** Many officers sympathised openly and gave aid. Others obeyed orders not to interfere.
- **The Socialists and the Left.** Divided between reformists, maximalists and (from 1921) Communists, they refused to join Liberals and Popolari in a government that might have stood against Fascism, and in August 1922 a general strike called to defend democracy ("legalitarian" strike) failed within three days.

The **general strike of 1–3 August 1922** was a decisive moment. The Alleanza del Lavoro called it to protest at Fascist violence. The Fascists announced that they would break it if the government did not, and their squads seized the town hall of Milan and attacked Socialist strongholds in Livorno, Ancona and elsewhere. The government did not stop them. The squads had defeated the Left in front of the whole country, which made Fascism the country's de facto law-and-order force, and made Mussolini's demand to join the government seem realistic.

:::exam Linking long-term and short-term causes
Questions on 1922 often ask for "reasons". Sort them into **conditions** (the mutilated victory, economic crisis, the Biennio Rosso, liberal weakness: long-term, and not by themselves a reason why *Mussolini* rather than someone else gained power), **Fascist action** (squadrismo, the dual policy, the March on Rome), and **decisions by others** (the elites' tolerance, and above all the king's choice of 28–29 October). A top-level answer says which type of cause was decisive and why.
:::

## Mussolini becomes prime minister, 1922

### The March on Rome

By October 1922 the Fascists were strong enough to claim office. At the party congress in **Naples** on 24 October 1922, with 40,000 blackshirts in the city, Mussolini told the crowd "Our programme is simple: we want to rule Italy", and the crowd roared "To Rome!" The Fascist leaders then created a **quadrumvirate** to direct the operation (**Italo Balbo**, **Emilio De Bono**, **Cesare Maria De Vecchi** and **Michele Bianchi**). Mussolini stayed in Milan, near the Swiss border and in touch with newspaper offices, ready to negotiate.

On the night of **27–28 October** Fascist squads seized telegraph offices, railway stations and prefectures in towns across the north and centre, and about 25,000 to 30,000 blackshirts, many armed with nothing more than sticks and old muskets and soaked by rain, began to gather in three groups at Santa Marinella, Tivoli and Monterotondo, some 20 to 60 kilometres from Rome. The regular garrison in Rome numbered about 28,000 men under General Pugliese, with orders to defend the capital and with better arms, and could have dispersed the Fascists at once.

Prime minister **Luigi Facta**, who had hesitated for weeks, finally resolved to act. On the evening of 27 October his cabinet decided to declare a **state of siege** (martial law), which only the king could sign. The king, **Victor Emmanuel III**, was in Rome. After first seeming to agree, on the morning of **28 October** he refused to sign. Facta's government resigned, and the March on Rome continued without opposition.

:::key Why the king refused to sign the state of siege
1. **Fear of civil war.** If the army fired on Fascists, there might be a bloody conflict, and the king did not want blood on his hands.
2. **Doubt about the army.** Some senior officers, and the king's cousin the Duke of Aosta, seemed sympathetic to the Fascists. The king feared that troops might not obey, or that the army might split.
3. **Belief that Mussolini could be tamed.** Salandra, Giolitti and other Liberal leaders advised that he could be brought into a coalition and made constitutional, which would solve the crisis without bloodshed.
4. **Fear of losing the throne.** There were rumours in Fascist circles that the Duke of Aosta could replace him if he resisted, and he wanted a government that would protect order.
5. **His own character.** Victor Emmanuel was shy and cautious, preferred the safest course in a crisis, and put the monarchy before the liberal system.
:::

Mussolini's bluff worked because the king had decided to avoid a showdown. On **29 October** the king telegraphed Mussolini inviting him to form a government. Mussolini travelled overnight in a sleeping car from Milan and arrived in Rome on the morning of the **30th**. He met the king, still in his black shirt. On the **31st** he took office at the age of **39**, and the blackshirts paraded through the capital. Fascists afterwards called it the "March on Rome" and celebrated it as a revolution, with the seizure of power as the first day of a new calendar (they dated the new era from 28 October 1922). It was a myth: the blackshirts did not conquer Rome, and Mussolini became prime minister by the king's appointment in a legal manner.

:::warn The March on Rome was a bluff that worked
Do not describe a military seizure. The Fascists could not have defeated the army, and the march was a show of force that threatened violence. What decided it was the king's refusal to sign the martial law decree, and the political class's willingness to treat Mussolini as a partner. Mussolini arrived in Rome by train *after* he had been invited.
:::

### The role of the king and the elites

The king's decision was the hinge of the whole story. If he had signed, the army would very likely have scattered the Fascists, and Mussolini's career might have ended in failure. Historians therefore ask how far Fascism's success was due to Fascism itself and how far to the mistakes of others. The **elites** (the king, the Liberal politicians, the army command, the industrialists, the Church) did not all favour Fascism, but each decided that it was the least bad option. They wanted three things: an end to strikes and disorder, protection from the Left, and a government with a majority. Mussolini offered all three. They also made the mistake of underestimating him: they assumed that, once in office, he would obey the rules of parliament and that they could replace him whenever they wished. Liberal leaders such as Giolitti and Salandra gave Mussolini's government their support at first, expecting to steer him.

### The nature of the National Government

Mussolini's first cabinet, formed on 31 October 1922, was a **coalition**, not a Fascist government. Only a few ministers were Fascists. The rest included Liberals, Popolari, Nationalists, and the two war heroes General Armando Diaz (War) and Admiral Paolo Thaon di Revel (Navy). The philosopher **Giovanni Gentile** was Minister of Education. Mussolini kept the posts of prime minister, foreign minister and minister of the interior himself. On 16 November 1922 he addressed the Chamber with a threat: he could have turned "this drab, silent hall" into "a bivouac for my squads" (translated), but he had chosen to govern with parliament, for now. The Chamber gave him a vote of confidence by 306 votes to 116, and later granted him special powers to issue decrees on finance and administration for a year. Many Liberals and Popolari voted for him.

It is a mistake to say Mussolini was powerless in 1922. His position was weaker than that of a dictator, since he depended on the king for office and on the other parties for votes, and Fascists were a minority in the Chamber. But he held the police and the interior ministry, he had armed squads waiting to be used, and he could threaten to resign and let the squads loose. He used the next four years to turn a coalition into a dictatorship by stages.

### The Fascist Grand Council and the Militia

Mussolini's first aim was to bring the Fascist movement under his control and to place it above the state. In **December 1922** he set up the **Fascist Grand Council**, a body of party leaders that met in secret, with himself as chairman, and which would draw up policy and advise on key appointments; it was a party organ with no basis in the constitution, and it gave Mussolini a place to run the government with party men. In **January 1923** he turned the squads into the **Voluntary Militia for National Security** (*Milizia Volontaria per la Sicurezza Nazionale*, **MVSN**). The Militia was paid by the state, wore the black shirt as a uniform and answered to Mussolini, not to the army command. It had the double effect of bringing the independent *ras* under discipline and giving the regime an armed force of its own, outside the army, which could be used against opponents. In February 1923 the Nationalist Association (with its own "Blueshirts"), which shared Fascist beliefs, merged into the PNF, which added educated, conservative supporters and gave the party links with the army and diplomats.

:::def Fascist Grand Council
A body of leading Fascists, set up in December 1922 and chaired by Mussolini, which became the main policy-making body of the party and later (from 1928) a formal organ of the state. It had power to propose a successor to Mussolini.
:::

:::def Militia (MVSN)
The Voluntary Militia for National Security, created in January 1923, in which the Fascist squads were merged into a state-paid force that answered to Mussolini. It was used to police rallies and to suppress opposition, and later served abroad.
:::

Mussolini also tried to win the support of the Church and the elites. In 1923 the government introduced compulsory religious teaching in primary schools (the **Gentile reform**, which also set up a school system favouring the elite), ordered crucifixes back into classrooms, and helped the Catholic Banco di Roma when it ran into trouble. The Vatican withdrew its support from Sturzo's Popolari, and in **July 1923** Sturzo resigned as party secretary and left Italy in 1924. Meanwhile, the local violence went on: in August 1923 a Catholic priest, Don Giovanni Minzoni, who had set up Catholic youth groups in competition with the Fascists, was beaten to death in Argenta by squadristi.

### The Acerbo Law, 1923

Mussolini had a government that rested on a Chamber in which Fascists were a small minority, and he wanted a permanent majority. His undersecretary, **Giacomo Acerbo**, drafted a new electoral law, passed by the Chamber on **21 July 1923** and by the Senate later in the year. Under the **Acerbo Law** the list that won the most votes, as long as it won at least **25 per cent**, would get **two-thirds of the seats** in the Chamber (the remaining third being shared in proportion among the other lists). It was passed legally. Most of the old Liberal leaders, Salandra and Orlando among them, backed it, and several saw it as a way of making a stable government and diluting the Fascists in a larger bloc; many deputies voted under Fascist pressure, and the Popolari split, with some of their deputies voting for it. The law was a legal measure that destroyed the proportional system and was designed to deliver a rigged majority.

### The 1924 election

The election of **6 April 1924** was held under the new law. The government's "great list" (*Listone*) joined Fascists to Liberals (Salandra and Orlando among them), Nationalists and some right-wing Catholics. It was carried out with heavy intimidation: squads beat opposition candidates and supporters, closed down meetings, seized ballot papers or forced voters to show their votes, while prefects and police looked away. In some areas voters were threatened with castor oil and beatings, and some opposition candidates were prevented from standing. The result: the *Listone* won **60.1 per cent** of the votes and 355 seats, and a second government list in some regions won a further 4.9 per cent and 19 seats, a total of about **65 per cent** and **374 of the 535 seats**. The opposition parties, the Popolari, two socialist parties, the Communists and some Liberals and Republicans, shared the remaining 161 seats. About a third of voters still voted against the government, despite the violence, but in the Chamber the Fascists and their allies had a majority of any size.

### The murder of Matteotti, 1924

On **30 May 1924** the Chamber met to validate the results. **Giacomo Matteotti**, the 39-year-old general secretary of the Unitary Socialist Party (PSU), stood up and for over an hour, under constant interruption from Fascist deputies, listed in detail the violence, the threats and the electoral fraud, and demanded that the elections in many districts be annulled. Fascist deputies threatened him. On **10 June 1924** he was seized in a street in Rome beside the river Tiber, forced into a car and stabbed to death by a gang of Fascist thugs with links to the press office of Mussolini's government, led by **Amerigo Dumini**. His body was found hidden in a wood north of Rome on **16 August**.

:::def The Matteotti crisis
The political crisis of June 1924–January 1925 that followed the kidnap and murder of the Socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti by Fascist agents. It shook Mussolini's government and the public's confidence in it, and ended when Mussolini took responsibility on 3 January 1925 and moved openly to dictatorship.
:::

The public was horrified. There were demonstrations and strikes, newspapers (even some pro-government ones) attacked the government, and the car's number plate and a witness led police quickly to Dumini and other men with close links to the government. Some of Mussolini's close men resigned, including the police chief De Bono and the head of the press office, **Cesare Rossi**, and Mussolini dismissed several officials. The party itself was shaken, and many Fascists feared that the regime would fall. The opposition deputies, more than a hundred of them, responded by the **Aventine Secession** (named after the hill on which the Roman plebeians had once withdrawn): they walked out of the Chamber, hoping that moral pressure would force the king to dismiss Mussolini.

The Aventine Secession failed. The opposition would not call a general strike, the Socialists and Communists would not work together, and the Popolari and Liberals did not want to destroy the constitutional order. Most important, **Victor Emmanuel III** did nothing. He could have dismissed Mussolini, and Senate and army commanders urged him to, but he said he would rely on parliament; and parliament was no longer sitting. The Senate gave Mussolini a vote of confidence in June 1924. The opposition, having left the Chamber, had no way to act on its moral victory.

The crisis was the greatest threat to Mussolini's survival, and on **31 December 1924** the party's hard-liners forced his hand. A delegation of militia consuls (local militia commanders) visited him and told him to crush the opposition, or else the Militia would do it for him. Historians disagree about how far this was a real threat and how far a piece of stagecraft that Mussolini welcomed, but he followed their advice. On **3 January 1925** he spoke in the Chamber:

> "I declare here, before this assembly and before the whole Italian people, that I assume, I alone, the political, moral and historical responsibility for everything that has happened." *(Translated from Mussolini's speech to the Chamber of Deputies, 3 January 1925.)*

He challenged his opponents to impeach him, knowing that the Chamber would not. Within days police closed political clubs, dissolved "subversive" associations, searched homes and arrested hundreds, and the prefects were told to stamp out opposition. The Matteotti murder had shaken the regime, but the resistance of the opposition had been too weak to use the opportunity.

:::key Why Mussolini survived the Matteotti crisis
1. **The king did nothing.** He had the legal power to dismiss him and chose not to.
2. **The opposition was divided and passive.** The Aventine deputies abandoned the Chamber, the only place where they had power, and would not appeal to the streets.
3. **The elites preferred Mussolini to chaos.** Industrialists, the Church, the army leaders and most Liberals feared the Left more than they disliked a Fascist government.
4. **Mussolini held the police and the Militia.** He used his control of the interior ministry to arrest, censor and intimidate, and the hard-line Fascists gave him the push he needed to go on the offensive.
5. **Nobody could replace him.** There was no alternative coalition that could govern.
:::

### Dictatorship by law, 1925–26

Between 1925 and 1926 Mussolini passed a series of laws, called by Fascists the ***leggi fascistissime*** ("the most Fascist laws"), that made a dictatorship out of the existing state, and he used the state's own legal machinery to do it.
- **The press.** Laws of December 1925 and later made editors answerable to the government, required journalists to join an official register, and allowed the confiscation of papers. Opposition papers closed or changed ownership. The *Corriere della Sera* of Milan, a leading liberal paper, passed into Fascist-friendly hands at the end of 1925.
- **The Head of Government.** The law of **24 December 1925** made Mussolini "Head of the Government, Prime Minister, Secretary of State", responsible only to the king and not to parliament. No bill could be debated without his consent. Ministers answered to him. A law of **31 January 1926** let the government issue decrees with the force of law.
- **The unions.** The Palazzo Vidoni agreement of October 1925 between the employers' federation (Confindustria) and the Fascist unions gave the Fascist unions the sole right to bargain; a law of April 1926 banned strikes and set up state-controlled syndicates (see [the economy chapter](#hist.d.italy.3)).
- **Local government.** Elected mayors were replaced by appointed ***podestà*** (see the next chapter), and prefects were given sweeping powers.
- **The party.** In 1925 Mussolini made the hard-liner **Roberto Farinacci** party secretary, to put discipline into the party and attack the opposition, and in 1926 replaced him with the more orderly **Augusto Turati**, who purged the *ras* and brought the party under central control. The party now answered to the Duce.

A series of attempts on Mussolini's life gave the excuse for the final measures: Tito Zaniboni's plot (November 1925), the wounding of Mussolini by the Irishwoman Violet Gibson (7 April 1926), and two further attempts in the autumn (Gino Lucetti, September; and **Anteo Zamboni**, a boy of fifteen who was lynched by the crowd at Bologna on 31 October 1926). In **November 1926** exceptional decrees:
- **dissolved all parties except the PNF** and closed opposition newspapers;
- withdrew the seats of 120 deputies, including the Aventine group and the Communists;
- brought back the **death penalty** for crimes against the king, the queen, the heir and the head of government;
- set up the **Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State**, staffed by army officers and militia, to try political offences;
- introduced **confino** (internal exile to remote villages and islands) for anyone judged dangerous, by administrative order and without trial.

From the end of 1926 there was one legal party, a government that answered to the king and not to parliament, no free press, and a political police. The liberal state was dead by law, with the king's signature on every document.

:::note Mussolini's words: "everything within the state"
On 28 October 1925, at La Scala in Milan, Mussolini put the idea of the new state into a formula: "everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state" (translated). The word he used for the aim, *totalitario*, was first used by the anti-Fascists as a criticism, and he turned it into a boast. In practice the regime never achieved the total control the slogan describes. The next chapter looks at what it did and did not control.
:::

## Extent of fascist control by 1926

By the end of 1926 Italy was a one-party state in law. Was it also one in fact? Control was extensive but not complete.

### Levels of support

Fascism's support came from several groups, but not from all of them in the same way:
- **The middle classes.** Clerks, shopkeepers, teachers, small property owners, students and ex-officers were the movement's core members. They had been hurt by inflation and feared the Left, and they liked a party that promised order and national pride.
- **Landowners and farmers.** In the Po valley and Tuscany they had hired squads, and they gained when unions were smashed.
- **Industrialists and bankers.** They backed Mussolini once he showed he would suppress strikes and ease the burden on business (his finance minister, Alberto De Stefani, abolished the inheritance tax in 1923), and the Vidoni agreement showed they were content.
- **The Church and many Catholics.** Ready to accept a government that restored religion to schools and promised to settle the dispute with the papacy (the Lateran Pacts of 1929).
- **The army and the king.** The army leaders were pleased with a government that praised the army, left its internal affairs to the generals and kept the Left out. The king, as the Matteotti crisis showed, preferred Mussolini to a risky change.
- **Ordinary people.** Evidence is limited: with no free press or elections, it is hard to measure. Many Italians were passive or indifferent, tired of disorder, and some welcomed Mussolini as a strong leader. The scale of support seems to have grown in later years, and the next chapter discusses it.

### Opposition and dissent

Opposition was real but divided and increasingly dangerous.

| Group | What it did | Why it failed or was crushed |
|---|---|---|
| Socialists (PSI, PSU) | Voted against the government; Matteotti | Split since 1921 and 1922; no plan for resistance; leaders arrested or exiled |
| Communists (PCd'I) | Organised underground; **Gramsci** wrote and was arrested in November 1926 | Small; led by Bordiga and then Gramsci and Togliatti; targeted by the Special Tribunal |
| Popolari (PPI) | Opposed the Acerbo Law; Sturzo | Denied support by the Vatican; Sturzo forced into exile |
| Liberals | Giovanni Amendola, Piero Gobetti, Benedetto Croce (the *Manifesto of the Anti-Fascist Intellectuals*, May 1925) | Amendola was beaten twice and died in 1926 of his injuries, and Gobetti died in Paris in 1926 after a beating; the old Liberal leaders had backed Mussolini |
| Trade unions | Strikes in 1925 | Banned by the Vidoni agreement and the law of 1926 |
| Exiles (*fuorusciti*) | Turati, Sforza, Sturzo, Nitti and others abroad, later Giustizia e Libertà (1929) | Watched by Fascist agents; had little effect inside Italy |

Opposition did not come only from these groups. The Church kept its own organisations, and the monarchy, army and big business, though they supported the government, kept their own institutions. Fascism never replaced them, which marks the limits of its control in 1926.

:::key How far had Mussolini gained control by 1926?
1. **Formal power**: nearly complete. One party, no parliamentary check, no free press, decrees without debate, a political police and a special court.
2. **Real power**: considerable, but shared. The king (who could, in theory, dismiss him, and did in 1943), the army, the Church and big business kept real independence.
3. **Support**: wide among the middle classes and elites, passive among the rest, with active opposition crushed or exiled.
4. **Method**: by violence (the squads), by manoeuvre (the dual policy) and by law (the 1925–26 decrees), with the active or passive help of the traditional elites.
:::

## Why did Mussolini gain control? Weighing the causes

Questions on this topic ask you to rank factors. Here are the five main ones and a way to compare them.

| Factor | The case that it was decisive | The case against |
|---|---|---|
| The weakness of liberal Italy | Without unstable governments, a divided parliament and a mutilated victory there would have been no crisis for Fascism to exploit | Many countries had war damage and strikes without a Fascist dictatorship; the Liberals were not powerless and had defeated D'Annunzio |
| Fascist violence and the dual policy | The squads destroyed the Left in the countryside, and the threat of the March on Rome forced the king to negotiate | The squads could not have beaten the army; the violence worked only because the state tolerated it |
| Fear of the Left | It made the elites tolerate or finance the squads | The Left was already in retreat by 1921; the fear exceeded the danger |
| The king and the elites | The king's decision on 28 October and the elites' support in the Matteotti crisis made Mussolini prime minister and kept him there | They acted because Fascism seemed to offer order, so their choice depended on the Fascists' strength |
| Mussolini himself | His flexibility, speeches and sense of timing gave Fascism a leader and a strategy | He did not plan the March on Rome as an act of war, and he nearly fell in 1924 |

A strong answer separates the *conditions* that made a Fascist success possible (the war, the crisis, the Left's fear) from the *choices* that made it happen (the Liberals' tolerance, the king's refusal, the elites' support) and the *Fascist methods* that exploited both (squadrismo, the dual policy, law). It then argues which of these did most. A defensible judgement is that Fascism was a minority movement that could not have taken power by force, so its success depended on the choice of the king and the elites to let it, a choice made in a crisis that the liberal governments had failed to solve and that the squads' violence had made worse; and that Mussolini turned that appointment into a dictatorship because the same elites, and the divided opposition, would not remove him in 1924.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
Historians disagree about why Fascism triumphed in Italy. Their explanations include:
- **A Marxist view.** Palmiro Togliatti and others argued that Fascism was the tool of the landowners and big business, who used it to crush the working class. The evidence of funding and police help supports this, but it does not explain why many middle-class people joined, or why the elites could not control Mussolini once he was in power.
- **The "parenthesis" view.** The liberal philosopher **Benedetto Croce** called Fascism a moral sickness, a parenthesis in Italian history caused by the war. This defends liberal Italy, but critics say it ignores how many Liberals backed Mussolini.
- **Liberal Italy's failure.** **Gaetano Salvemini** and others stressed the weakness of the political system.
- **Adrian Lyttelton**, *The Seizure of Power: Fascism in Italy, 1919–1929* (1973), argued that Fascism was a mass movement of the middle classes and ex-servicemen, which won because the elites and the state allowed it, and which then built a dictatorship by stages.
- **Denis Mack Smith**, *Mussolini* (1981), gave a critical biography which stressed Mussolini's opportunism, vanity and lack of principle.
- **Renzo De Felice**, whose biography appeared in volumes from 1965 to 1997, took Mussolini's ideas more seriously, stressed the movement's modernising side and argued that Fascism had substantial popular consent. His critics said that he made the regime look better than it was.
- **R. J. B. Bosworth**, *Mussolini* (2002), pictured a dictator who was both a charismatic leader and a very ordinary man, and stressed the violence.
:::

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 1.1
1. **Define the criteria first.** For "Assess the reasons for Mussolini's appointment in 1922": criteria could be *necessity* (without this factor, would it have happened?), *immediacy* (which factor was the final trigger?) and *contribution* (how much did it add?).
2. **Spend a paragraph on each of three or four factors**, each ending with a sentence that says how much it mattered and compares it with another factor.
3. **Use precise evidence** (dates, numbers, names): the 156 and 100 seats of 1919, the 400,000 factory occupiers, "about 35 deputies" in 1921, 28 October and the state of siege, 60 per cent and 374 seats in 1924.
4. **Keep the question's dates in view.** "1922" means the appointment; do not write the whole story of the dictatorship. For a question on "1922–26", you need the Matteotti crisis.
5. **Judge.** End with a view that says which factor mattered most and why, and what would have changed if it had been absent.
:::
## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, and the total is out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'The weakness of liberal Italy was the main reason Mussolini came to power in 1922.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge that is mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement that is partly substantiated; a good range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criterion: which factor, if removed, would most probably have prevented the appointment of 31 October 1922?
- For (liberal weakness): five governments and four prime ministers in 40 months (June 1919–October 1922); proportional representation; PSI and PPI would not cooperate; Giolitti would not use force in 1920; no government stopped the squads; Facta's hesitation.
- Against 1 (Fascist action): squadrismo (20,000 members in 1920, over 200,000 in 1921); smashing the leagues; the August 1922 strike; the dual policy; the threat of the March.
- Against 2 (the king and the elites): the king's refusal on 28 October against a garrison of 28,000 and 25,000–30,000 poorly armed blackshirts; Salandra, Giolitti and Confindustria.
- Against 3 (fear of the Left and the economy): the Biennio Rosso, the occupation of 400,000 workers, inflation; but the Left was already in retreat by 1922.
- Judgement: weakness was the necessary condition and the king's decision the decisive act; define "liberal Italy" to include the monarchy and the political class, and the view is stronger.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Liberal Italy was very weak after the war. There were many governments and none of them lasted long because there was proportional representation and the parties could not agree. The governments could not deal with strikes and inflation, and the Fascists were able to use violence against the Socialists without being stopped. This shows that the weakness of liberal Italy was the main reason Mussolini came to power in 1922."

*Commentary.* Relevant and accurate, but general ("many governments", "none of them lasted long") and with no figures, dates or names. The last sentence asserts the conclusion without testing it against any other factor. Table A: Level 3 (about 8). Table B: Level 3 (about 7). To move up: give the evidence precisely, say *how* weakness produced the appointment, and set it against other factors.

**A Level 4 paragraph (an alternative factor, but no comparison).** "However, Fascist violence was also important. From autumn 1920 the squads, led by local bosses such as Balbo in Ferrara and Farinacci in Cremona, destroyed the socialist leagues and cooperatives of the Po valley. Membership rose from about 20,000 at the end of 1920 to over 200,000 a year later. In August 1922 the squads broke the general strike by occupying Milan's town hall, and this showed that Fascism could do what the government could not. By October 1922 Mussolini could threaten the government with the March on Rome."

*Commentary.* Precise and focused evidence, and a real alternative factor, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). But the paragraph never says how much this factor mattered compared with weakness, and does not notice that the squads succeeded *because* the state tolerated them. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs a **comparison and a judgement**.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The statement can be tested by asking which factor, if it had been absent, would most probably have prevented Mussolini's appointment on 31 October 1922. On this test the weakness of liberal Italy was a necessary condition of his success but not the main reason for it. The decisive reason was the decision of the king and the political elite on 28–29 October to appoint rather than to resist him, though that decision was itself the last and most serious failure of the liberal system.

The case for the statement is strong. The war left Italy with about 650,000 dead and a debt about five times larger than in 1914, and the system of proportional representation introduced in 1919 produced a Chamber in which the Socialists (156 of 508 seats) and the Catholic Popolari (100) could not work together and the Liberals could not govern alone. Between June 1919 and October 1922 there were five governments under four prime ministers. They could not solve inflation or unemployment, which reached about half a million in 1921. They could not agree on a policy for the Biennio Rosso: Giolitti's refusal in September 1920 to use troops against 400,000 workers who had occupied the factories was wise, but it left landowners and industrialists feeling unprotected. Above all they allowed violence to go unpunished. Prefects and police gave the squads transport and weapons, magistrates did not prosecute, and Giolitti even placed the Fascists on his National Bloc list in 1921, in the hope of taming them. A state that could not control a party of 200,000 members could not stop a march on its capital. Without this weakness, the squads could not have flourished and there would have been no crisis for Mussolini to exploit.

Yet weakness describes an opportunity, not a cause, and other democracies with strikes and debts did not produce fascist governments. What turned the opportunity into a Fascist government was Fascist action. Squadrismo destroyed the strength of the Left in the Po valley and Tuscany in 1921–22, which made Fascism the champion of the propertied classes, and the party grew from about 20,000 members at the end of 1920 to over 200,000 a year later. By August 1922, when the squads broke the Socialist general strike and seized Milan's town hall while the authorities looked on, Mussolini could present himself as the only man who could restore order. His dual policy, with talks with Giolitti and Salandra on one side and the threat of the March on Rome on the other, made it impossible to rule without him. This is a strong reason, but it cannot be the main one because it depended on tolerance. The blackshirts who gathered near Rome on 27–28 October numbered about 25,000 to 30,000, many with only sticks, and the garrison of Rome had about 28,000 regular soldiers. If the army had been ordered to fire, the march would almost certainly have collapsed.

The reason that it was not is the king's decision. Victor Emmanuel III refused on the morning of 28 October to sign the state of siege which his own prime minister had asked for. He feared civil war, doubted the army's loyalty (his cousin the Duke of Aosta was thought to be sympathetic to the Fascists), and was advised by Salandra and others that Mussolini could be brought into a coalition and made respectable. The industrialists of Confindustria, the Vatican and many generals did not oppose this outcome. It was a choice, not a necessity. Had the king signed, Mussolini's appointment on 31 October would have been impossible, however weak the government.

The fear of the Left, the last of the factors, is best seen as the reason the elites made that choice. The factory occupations of September 1920 and the strikes of 1919–20 convinced landowners and employers that the state could not protect them. Yet the revolution was never close: the Socialists were split, the Communists had broken away in January 1921, and by 1922 unemployment had weakened the unions. The fear was greater than the danger. It explains why property-owners paid for squads and why the king's advisers preferred Mussolini to a showdown, but it does not by itself explain why they chose him over a Liberal coalition.

Overall, the weakness of liberal Italy was the main *condition* for Mussolini's rise, because it made Fascist violence possible and the Fascist threat credible, but the main *reason* for his appointment on 31 October was the king's decision, taken under the influence of a political elite that thought Fascism could be tamed. If 'liberal Italy' is taken to include the monarchy and the old political class, then the statement is largely right, because the king's failure of nerve was a failure of the liberal system itself. If it means the instability of parliament, it is only part of the answer, because the instability would not have produced a Fascist prime minister without Fascist violence and the king's choice. The most convincing view is therefore that liberal weakness created the opening, Fascist action widened it, and the king walked through it."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** (what would have prevented the appointment?) and uses it throughout. Each paragraph weighs one factor against the question, with precise figures, dates and names (Table B, Level 5: about 14). It distinguishes **conditions from reasons**, tests the Fascist and Left factors against each other, and reaches a judgement that depends on how the key term is defined (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30. The evidence is not a list: each fact is there to prove a point.
:::

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'The Matteotti crisis strengthened Mussolini's position.' Assess this view.
**What the levels want.** This is an "Assess" question about a *change*. Level 5 asks for criteria and a sustained balance: not "did it help or harm?" but *when* and *in what sense*.

**Plan.**
- Criterion: "position" means (a) hold on office and (b) freedom to act without limits. Compare the short term (June–December 1924) with the long term (1925–26).
- Weakened: the murder shocked the public; De Bono and Rossi resigned; newspapers attacked the government; the Aventine Secession began; the king might have dismissed him; the party was shaken; Mussolini's own explanation (that he was not involved) was not believed.
- Strengthened: the opposition's failure (Aventine, no general strike, divided); the king's inaction and the Senate's vote of confidence; the radical Fascists forced him on 31 December; the 3 January 1925 speech; laws of 1925–26 that destroyed the opposition; Farinacci then Turati disciplined the party.
- Judgement: weakened for six months, strengthened permanently, because the crisis showed that no one would stop him, and he could then remove the checks which had constrained him.

**A Level 5 conclusion and one paragraph.** "The Matteotti murder was dangerous to Mussolini in the short term. In the six months after 10 June 1924 he lost his police chief and his press chief, was attacked by newspapers that had been friendly, and faced more than a hundred opposition deputies who had withdrawn to the Aventine in the hope that the king would dismiss him. His own hard-liners, who feared that he would give way, forced him to act on 31 December 1924 when a group of militia consuls demanded a crackdown. If the king had used his powers, the regime might have ended in 1924.

But the opposition did not use the crisis, and the king did not act, and the effect was to make Mussolini stronger than before. The Aventine deputies had left the only place where they had a voice. The Socialists and Communists refused to unite, and no general strike was called. The Senate gave Mussolini a vote of confidence in June 1924. Then Mussolini took the initiative. On 3 January 1925 he accepted 'political, moral and historical responsibility', challenged the Chamber to impeach him, and ordered the arrest of opponents. Between December 1925 and November 1926 the Head of Government law, the press laws and the November decrees removed parliament's power and banned all other parties. None of this could have been done in 1923, when he still depended on a coalition and on Liberal votes. The crisis removed the pretence that he governed with parliament and the elites' belief that he could be replaced.

Overall, the statement is true in the long term, if not in the short term. The crisis threatened Mussolini for a time, but it ended with his opponents defeated, his critics in the party silenced and the checks on him gone. It was less a test he survived than an opportunity he used."

*Commentary.* Criteria are clear (hold on office and freedom to act) and time is used to organise the argument. Weaknesses and strengths are both given with evidence. The conclusion distinguishes the short-term and long-term effect and reaches a clear judgement. Level 5 on both tables, provided the evidence is as precise throughout the essay as it is here.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the importance of squadrismo in Mussolini's rise to power." [30]

A student wrote:

"Squadrismo began in 1920. The squads wore black shirts and they went round towns beating up socialists and making them drink castor oil. They burned down trade union buildings and newspaper offices. Many people were killed. Balbo was the leader in Ferrara and Farinacci was the leader in Cremona. The squads marched on Rome in October 1922. Mussolini became prime minister. Squadrismo was therefore very important in his rise to power."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are mostly accurate, but the paragraph **narrates** and does not analyse. It does not say what squadrismo achieved (the destruction of the Left in the countryside, a mass membership, the credibility of the March), who allowed it, or what else mattered. "Therefore very important" is an assertion, and the claim that the squads *won* power in October 1922 is wrong: the king appointed Mussolini.

**The fix.** Make a claim, use the detail as proof, and set a limit. "Squadrismo was important because it made Fascism a mass force and showed the elites that Mussolini could do what the government would not. Between the end of 1920 and the end of 1921 membership rose from about 20,000 to over 200,000, mostly in the Po valley and Tuscany, where squads led by ras such as Balbo and Farinacci destroyed the Socialist leagues and cooperatives that landowners hated, and landowners paid for them. But squadrismo could not by itself bring power: the blackshirts at Rome in October 1922 were no match for the garrison, and the squads succeeded only because police and magistrates let them. It was the king's refusal to sign the state of siege which turned violence into office." Now each fact is evidence, and there is a limit and a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was the "mutilated victory", and why did it hurt the liberal governments?
A: The nationalist claim (D'Annunzio's phrase) that Italy had won the war but lost the peace, because it did not get Fiume, most of Dalmatia or colonies. It made governments look weak abroad and humiliated, and it gave nationalists and ex-servicemen a grievance against the politicians who made the peace.
Q: Give three results of the Fiume affair.
A: A national hero showed that a government could be defied by volunteers; the army and police showed they might not act against nationalists; the style of black shirts, salute and balcony speeches was copied by Fascism. It ended with the Treaty of Rapallo (12 November 1920) and the "Bloody Christmas" of 24–29 December 1920.
Q: Why was the Biennio Rosso important for the rise of Fascism even though revolution was never close?
A: Because it frightened landowners, industrialists and the middle classes, who believed the state could not protect them, so they supported or tolerated Fascist squads. The factory occupations (September 1920) involved about 400,000 workers.
Q: What was the dual policy?
A: Mussolini's combination of legal politics (the National Bloc of 1921, talks with Liberal leaders, the PNF with a conservative programme) with squad violence and the threat of the March on Rome, which made him look both respectable and indispensable.
Q: What happened on 27–29 October 1922?
A: Blackshirts gathered near Rome (27–28 October); Facta's cabinet asked the king to sign a state of siege; the king refused on the morning of 28 October; on the 29th he invited Mussolini to form a government; Mussolini came by train and took office on 31 October.
Q: What did the Acerbo Law provide, and when was the election held under it?
A: The list with the most votes, if it had at least 25 per cent, would get two-thirds of the seats. The law passed the Chamber on 21 July 1923; the election was held on 6 April 1924, and the government lists won about 65 per cent of the votes and 374 of 535 seats.
Q: Why did Mussolini survive the Matteotti crisis?
A: The king did nothing and the Senate gave a vote of confidence; the opposition left the Chamber and refused to call a strike or to unite; the elites preferred him to chaos; he held the police and the Militia, and the party's extremists pushed him on 31 December 1924 to act.
Q: Name three steps taken in 1925–26 that created a dictatorship.
A: The Head of Government law (24 December 1925); the press laws and the end of the free press; the Palazzo Vidoni agreement and the law banning strikes (1925–26); the November 1926 decrees that dissolved all other parties, restored the death penalty and set up the Special Tribunal; also the replacement of elected mayors by podestà.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★ Assess the impact of the First World War on Italy up to 1922.
A: Indicative content: about 650,000 dead and a huge debt, inflation and unemployment; the mutilated victory and the nationalist backlash, with D'Annunzio and Fiume; the squeeze on the middle classes, and ex-servicemen and arditi with no outlet; the radicalisation of workers (Biennio Rosso) and the fear it caused; the strain on a parliament with proportional representation and no stable majority. Challenge: some causes were older (the north–south gap, trasformismo, a narrow political life); the war did not make fascism inevitable. A Level 5 answer sets criteria (long-term damage, short-term shock) and judges which effects mattered most, e.g. that the war's economic and political effects mattered more to Fascism's success than the grievance of 1919, which only a minority felt.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'D'Annunzio's seizure of Fiume did more to help Mussolini than to harm the liberal state.' Evaluate this view.
A: Support: Fiume showed that volunteers could defy a government; its rituals (black shirts, salute, balcony oratory) became Fascist style; it embarrassed Nitti and Giolitti; it kept the "mutilated victory" alive. Challenge: the liberal state ended it by diplomacy (Rapallo) and force (Bloody Christmas, December 1920); D'Annunzio had no party; Mussolini had not joined him and gained nothing directly; the main damage to liberal Italy came from the economy and the squads. Level 5 judges, e.g., that Fiume gave Fascism a model and a mood, but a movement was needed, and Mussolini's organisation, not D'Annunzio's adventure, took power.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why Fascism gained mass support in Italy between 1919 and 1922.
A: Support: war veterans and nationalism; economic hardship of the middle classes; fear of the Left after the Biennio Rosso; landowners' funding of the squads; tolerance by the police and army; the PNF (November 1921) with a more conservative programme; membership from about 20,000 to over 200,000 in a year. Challenge: Fascism was strong only in particular regions (Po valley, Tuscany, Lombardy) and weak in Turin and the south; many supporters joined because the squads were winning; the 1921 election gave the Fascists only about 35 seats. Level 5 uses criteria such as which groups, which regions and which stage, and judges that mass support was mainly the middle classes and landowners reacting to the Left in 1920–22, grown by success and by elite tolerance.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The Biennio Rosso was the main reason why landowners and industrialists supported Fascism.' Evaluate this view.
A: Support: strikes, land occupations and the factory occupations of September 1920 (400,000 workers); the leagues in the Po valley; fear of Russian-style revolution; landowners paid the squads. Challenge: by 1921 the Left was in retreat and divided (Livorno split, January 1921); economic motives (strike-breaking, tax cuts, cheap labour); Giolitti's own tolerance; the Church and army supported anti-socialism too; industrialists, unlike landowners, paid for Fascism only late and backed Mussolini once he proved that he could govern. Level 5 judges that fear created the first support, but the continued support after 1921 came from calculation and from what Fascism delivered.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the part played by Victor Emmanuel III in Mussolini's gaining of power between 1922 and 1926.
H: Divide the period into two moments: October 1922 and 1924–26.
H: In each, ask what the king could have done and why he did not.
H: Then compare with other actors (Liberal leaders, the army, the Church, the opposition) and judge.
A: October 1922: he refused the state of siege on 28 October (fears of civil war and of the army's loyalty, the Aosta rumour, advice from Salandra), then invited Mussolini on the 29th. 1924–26: he did not dismiss Mussolini after Matteotti, relying on a Chamber that was no longer sitting; signed the Acerbo Law, the Head of Government law of December 1925 and the decrees of November 1926. Other actors: Liberal leaders (Giolitti, Salandra) supported Mussolini; Diaz and Badoglio were cautious; the Pope withdrew support from the Popolari; the opposition left the Chamber. Level 5 criteria: could the event have happened without the king? Judges, e.g., that the king's role was decisive in 1922 (a signature could have stopped Mussolini) and permissive in 1924–26 (he could have removed him, but he was one of several who preferred Mussolini to risk).
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the extent of Fascist control of Italy by 1926.
A: Support for extensive control: one party, no free press, the Head of Government law (24 December 1925), decrees without debate, the November 1926 decrees, the Special Tribunal and confino, podestà and prefects, the Militia, the unions (Vidoni 1925, law of April 1926). Limits: the king still appointed and could dismiss the prime minister; the army, Church and big business kept their independence; the Fascist party itself was divided between the ras and the Duce; many Italians were passive; the regime was less totalitarian than its slogans. Level 5 weighs formal against real control, and judges, e.g., that control of politics was almost complete but control of society was not.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Between 1922 and 1926 Mussolini created a dictatorship by legal means.' Evaluate this view.
H: List the steps, then sort them into legal (laws and decrees) and illegal (violence, fraud).
H: Ask what "legal" means when the king signed laws passed by a Chamber elected under intimidation.
A: Legal: Mussolini took office by royal appointment; the Chamber gave him confidence and decree powers in November 1922; the Acerbo Law (July 1923); the Head of Government law (24 December 1925); the law of 31 January 1926; the November 1926 decrees; the king signed all. Not legal in spirit: squad violence before 1922; intimidation and fraud in April 1924; the murder of Matteotti; the Militia as a party army; arrests after 3 January 1925 without legal basis; the Chamber voting under pressure. Level 5 defines "legal" (form against legitimacy) and judges, e.g., that the steps were legal in form, which is why the king and the elites accepted them, but that the conditions under which they were passed (violence and fraud) made the whole process illegitimate.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Opposition to Mussolini between 1922 and 1926 failed mainly because it was divided.' Evaluate this view.
A: Support: Socialists split into PSI, PSU and Communists; Popolari and Socialists would not unite; the Aventine deputies were not agreed on strategy; no general strike. Challenge: the opposition faced violence and a hostile state; the king and the Church, which could have helped it, did not; the Vatican withdrew support from the Popolari; Liberal leaders had backed Mussolini; Amendola was beaten and died in 1926, Gobetti was attacked and died in exile in 1926; the press was silenced. Level 5 judges that division weakened the opposition, but its defeat depended more on the choices of the king and the elites, and on the Fascists' control of force.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Fascism came to power in Italy because the propertied classes supported it.' Evaluate this view.
H: Name the propertied groups: landowners, industrialists, the middle classes, the Church. What did each do, and when?
H: Compare with a factor that does not depend on property: the king's decision, or Mussolini's methods.
A: Support: landowners funded squads; industrialists welcomed strike-breaking and, after 1922, backed Mussolini (Vidoni agreement, October 1925); the middle classes were the core membership; Giolitti, Salandra and the king represented the propertied elite; the Marxist interpretation (Togliatti). Challenge: the movement's early programme was radical and anti-capitalist, with mass support from ex-servicemen, students and the lower middle class; industrialists were slow and divided (many first backed Giolitti); Confindustria opposed some Fascist ideas; the king's decision and the liberal leaders' mistakes were political, not economic; Lyttelton stresses the autonomy of the movement. A* judgement: property-owners did not create Fascism, but their money, their tolerance and the state's weakness let it win, and they supported Mussolini more firmly after 1922 than before.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Mussolini would have failed in October 1922 if the king had signed the state of siege.
H: Compare the sides' strength (25,000–30,000 blackshirts, 28,000 soldiers) with the other forms of pressure on the king.
H: Ask what Mussolini could have done in Milan, and whether the dual policy had other routes.
A: Support: the blackshirts were poorly armed and outnumbered; the army had better arms and under orders would probably have dispersed them; Mussolini stayed in Milan, with Switzerland near; Facta's government was ready to fight; the March was a bluff. Challenge: it is not certain that all of the army would have obeyed (rumours about Aosta and some generals); the squads controlled much of the north and centre; Mussolini could have waited and continued negotiations with Salandra and Giolitti, who wanted him in a coalition; the same elites that supported him might have found another route to office within months; failure in Rome might have fed civil conflict. A* judgement: the March would very probably have failed as a military action, and Mussolini's chance of power in October 1922 would have gone; but the political conditions that gave Fascism its strength would have remained, so a Fascist role in government later was likely. Failure of the march, not of Fascism.

:::recap
- **Italy after 1918**: about 650,000 dead, a debt five times the pre-war level, inflation and unemployment; the "mutilated victory" (Treaty of London promises against Saint-Germain); D'Annunzio at Fiume (12 September 1919 to December 1920).
- **Instability**: proportional representation (1919); PSI 156 and PPI 100 of 508 seats; five governments in 40 months (Nitti, Giolitti, Bonomi, Facta twice); Biennio Rosso, factory occupations of September 1920 (about 400,000 workers).
- **Fascism**: founded 23 March 1919; no seats in 1919; squadrismo from 1920 (ras: Balbo, Farinacci, Grandi); about 35 seats in 1921; PNF in November 1921; the dual policy.
- **1922**: Fascist strike-breaking in August; Naples congress 24 October; March on Rome 27–28 October (about 25,000–30,000 blackshirts against 28,000 troops); the king refused the state of siege on 28 October; Mussolini PM on 31 October at the age of 39; a coalition cabinet.
- **Dictatorship by stages**: Grand Council (December 1922), Militia (January 1923), Acerbo Law (July 1923), election of 6 April 1924 (about 65 per cent, 374 of 535 seats).
- **Matteotti**: speech 30 May 1924, murder 10 June, body found 16 August; the Aventine Secession; the king's inaction; speech of 3 January 1925.
- **Laws, 1925–26**: Head of Government law (24 December 1925), press laws, Vidoni agreement, law of 31 January 1926, banning of parties and Special Tribunal (November 1926).
- **Control by 1926**: complete in form, shared in fact with king, army, Church and big business; support among middle classes and elites; opposition divided, exiled or silenced.
