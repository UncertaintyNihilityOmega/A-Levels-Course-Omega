:::why
There is no other question in modern history on which so many careful historians have been so divided for so long. The disagreement is not about whether Franz Ferdinand was shot or whether Germany invaded Belgium. It is about what those facts mean: whether a government chose war, whether Europe stumbled into it, whether it was avoidable. Each answer also tells you about the person who gave it. A German historian writing in 1961 asked a different question from a British statesman writing in 1933, who asked a different one from an Australian historian writing in 2012. Paper 3 asks you to read an unseen extract from this debate and say what it shows about its author's interpretation and approach. This chapter gives you the debate, historian by historian, with the time and place that shaped each one.
:::

## How far were the tensions likely to lead to war?

The first question in the syllabus is the oldest and the most difficult: if you take the tensions of c.1890–1914 (the rivalries, alliances, armaments and Balkan crises of the previous chapter) and add them up, does the war of 1914 follow? Historians answer in three ways.

:::key Three answers to "was war likely?"
1. **Yes: a system that was bound to break.** The rivalries were so deep, the arms race so costly and the alliances so rigid that sooner or later a crisis would end in war. Many accounts written after 1918 suggested this. It treats 1914 as the outcome of **long-term** pressures.
2. **No: tension was normal, and the war came from decisions.** Europe had survived crises in 1905, 1908–09, 1911 and 1912–13 without war. Each was settled by diplomacy. So the long-term tensions did not make war *inevitable*; what mattered was what ministers *chose* in July 1914. This is the view of those who stress **short-term** causes and contingency.
3. **In between: tensions made war possible, not certain.** The tensions created the conditions in which a particular set of decisions could lead to war. This is the position of many modern historians, including Clark.
:::

The evidence cuts both ways. For "likely": the naval race, the army laws of 1912–13 and the Russian Great Programme; the alliance blocs; the war plans that made speed essential; the sense, recorded in 1912 in Müller's diary, that Germany's leaders thought war would come. For "not likely": the string of crises that did not lead to war; a sense, among many politicians and bankers in 1913–14, of détente (Anglo-German agreements on the Baghdad railway and Africa were nearly settled); and the fact that very few of those who took the decisions of July 1914 expected a long world war.

Two historians gave this question lasting form.

- **A.J.P. Taylor**, in *War by Timetable: How the First World War Began* (1969), argued that the railway timetables and mobilisation plans of the Great Powers made it impossible to halt once mobilisation began: war came less from aggression than from a system of plans that gave leaders no time. Taylor was reacting against the Fischer thesis, published eight years earlier, and wrote with wit and provocation, as he often did.
- **James Joll**, in his inaugural lecture at the London School of Economics, *1914: The Unspoken Assumptions* (25 April 1968), and then in *The Origins of the First World War* (1984), argued that documents and plans cannot explain 1914 alone. Decision-makers carried **unspoken assumptions** (beliefs that they never needed to state): the idea that nations compete in a struggle for survival, a fear of decline, a conviction that war was a test of national vitality and a belief that the other side was bound to attack. Joll's lesson for Paper 3: an extract about "mentalities", "assumptions" or "fear" is an interpretation of 1914 that looks beyond decisions and plans to the ideas of those who made them.

:::def Long-term and short-term causes
**Long-term causes** are developments over decades that created the conditions for an event (here: rivalries, alliances, arms races, nationalism). **Short-term causes** are the decisions and events immediately before it (here: Sarajevo and the July Crisis). Historians argue about which should carry the weight: those who stress long-term causes tend to see war as likely; those who stress short-term causes tend to see it as the result of choices.
:::

## The decisions of key individuals

The second syllabus area is how far the war resulted from the choices of particular people. This is the heart of the July Crisis story in the previous chapter, and it is where different historians pick different people.

| Individual | Case that they bear heavy responsibility | Case that they were constrained or misjudged |
|---|---|---|
| **Wilhelm II** | Gave the blank cheque on 5 July; belligerent speeches and marginal notes | On 28 July wrote that Serbia's reply removed any reason for war; proposed a halt in Belgrade; had little control of detail |
| **Bethmann Hollweg** | Confirmed the pledge; took a "calculated risk" and hoped for a local war | Urged Vienna to negotiate on 29–30 July; lost control of events and of the generals |
| **Moltke** | Said in December 1912 that war was unavoidable; pressed for mobilisation in late July | Had inherited a plan that offered no alternative |
| **Berchtold and Conrad** | Wanted war with Serbia and drove the ultimatum | Feared the Empire's collapse; Tisza resisted until 14 July |
| **Nicholas II and Sazonov** | Ordered mobilisation; Sazonov knew it risked war | The Tsar cancelled and reinstated the order; Russia was reacting to Austria's declaration of war on Serbia |
| **Poincaré** | Strengthened Russia's confidence on 20–23 July | Visited Russia as a routine alliance gesture; had little influence over its decisions |
| **Grey** | Kept Britain's position unclear, so that Germany may have misjudged | Could not promise help the Cabinet and Commons would not give |
| **Pašić and Apis** | Serbia's officers armed the assassins | The government sent a vague warning and made a conciliatory reply; how far it controlled the officers is unknown |

:::key What the argument about individuals tells you
A historian who builds an argument on **individuals** is usually (but not always) arguing for contingency or for a particular state's responsibility: if decisions mattered, someone could have chosen differently. A historian who concentrates on **structures** (alliances, plans, domestic pressures) usually argues that individuals had little freedom. In Paper 3 extracts the choice of verbs gives it away: "decided", "chose" and "gambled" point to individuals; "forced", "compelled" and "made inevitable" point to structures.
:::

## Article 231 and the war-guilt debate, 1919–1920s

The debate began with the peace treaty. **Article 231** of the Treaty of Versailles (28 June 1919) reads: "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." In the Allies' eyes it was the legal basis for reparations: they asked Germany to pay for damage, not to confess a crime. The Germans read it as a verdict of moral guilt, and called it the **war-guilt clause** (*Kriegsschuldklausel*).

That reading mattered more than the drafting. All German parties, from the Nationalists to the Social Democrats, rejected it, and the German government set out to overturn it by scholarship.
- **The Foreign Office's innocence campaign.** From 1919 the Foreign Ministry's war-guilt section (the *Kriegsschuldreferat*) organised propaganda and research. In 1921 the *Zentralstelle für Erforschung der Kriegsursachen* (Centre for Research into the Causes of the War) was set up to publish and distribute work that showed Germany's innocence, and to support foreign writers who agreed.
- **Document collections.** The Foreign Office published *Die Grosse Politik der Europäischen Kabinette, 1871–1914*, about forty volumes of German diplomatic papers, in the 1920s. The documents were real, but the editors were officials of the state under accusation, and what they selected is part of the story: other governments published their own collections (for Britain, *British Documents on the Origins of the War, 1898–1914*, edited by G.P. Gooch and Harold Temperley, 1926–38). Each side presented its version.
- **A claim about time and place.** German historians in the 1920s mostly accepted the premise that the verdict of 1919 was an injustice to be corrected, and the Weimar Republic funded their work. That is a reason to treat the German scholarship of the 1920s as an *approach* shaped by politics, and not only a scholarly conclusion.

:::def Article 231 (the war-guilt clause)
Article 231 of the Treaty of Versailles, signed on 28 June 1919: the Allied and Associated Governments affirmed, and Germany accepted, Germany's responsibility (with her allies) for causing all the loss and damage suffered by the Allies as a result of the war "imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies". It was the basis of reparations, and Germans regarded it as a charge of guilt.
:::

:::exam The signal words
An extract from the 1920s or 1930s will often show its place and time in its vocabulary: an interest in "the so-called war guilt", in "the verdict of Versailles", or in "documents" published by governments. When you see this you can say that the historian is reacting to Article 231, and explain what Article 231 said and why Germans resented it, which is exactly the AO1 knowledge Table B rewards.
:::

## Shared responsibility: Lloyd George, Fay and Barnes

In the later 1920s a new view appeared, mostly outside Germany: that **no single country** was responsible, and that the treaty's verdict was therefore wrong. This is the **shared responsibility** (or **revisionist**) interpretation. Three works are the core.

- **Harry Elmer Barnes**, an American historian, *The Genesis of the World War* (1926), argued that Russia and France bore the principal responsibility for the war and that Germany had been unfairly blamed. His purpose was openly political: to dismantle the war-guilt clause. (On a visit to Europe Barnes met the exiled Wilhelm II, who, Barnes reported, was happy that Barnes did not blame him for starting the war.)
- **Sidney Bradshaw Fay**, an American historian at Harvard, *The Origins of the World War* (two volumes, 1928), the most important scholarly work in this school. Fay argued that Germany and Austria did not plot a European war and that the verdict of Versailles was historically unsound. Responsibility for the immediate crisis lay chiefly with Austria-Hungary, Serbia and Russia, with all the Powers sharing some blame; he blamed in particular the system of secret alliances and military plans. His conclusions were welcomed in Germany, where the official campaign against the war-guilt clause publicised revisionist work of this kind.
- **David Lloyd George**, who had been Britain's prime minister from 1916 to 1922 and a signatory of the treaty, gave the view its best-known phrase in his *War Memoirs* (six volumes, 1933–36). He wrote that "the nations slithered over the brink into the boiling cauldron of war" without any sign of fear. By this he meant that no leader had quite meant war: the nations had glided or stumbled into it, through folly and poor judgement.

Why did this view become popular in Britain and the USA in the 1920s and 1930s?
- **The documents.** The collections published after 1919 (in Germany, Austria, Britain, France, and by the Bolshevik government, which published tsarist diplomatic papers) showed that no government had a clear plan for a European war in 1914, and that every government's leaders had made mistakes.
- **Disillusion with the war.** After the slaughter, many in the West no longer believed the war had been a crusade against German aggression. The idea that all had blundered fitted a mood of regret.
- **Doubts about the peace.** Many people thought the treaty too harsh, because it demanded reparations that Germany could not pay. If Germany was not solely guilty, the treaty was unjust.
- **Politics.** Lloyd George wrote the *War Memoirs* in the 1930s as a former prime minister defending his own role (and attacking his rivals, such as Asquith's government in 1914, which he said had failed to state Britain's position early enough). His view is as much a statement about his time and his career as about 1914.

Some historians have argued that this view also had a longer effect: by the 1930s the idea that Germany had been unjustly blamed in 1919 made it easier for British politicians to accept Hitler's claim that the treaty should be revised. This is a claim about influence that is itself debated, and a Paper 3 answer should treat it as such.

:::key Shared responsibility in four lines
1. **Claim:** no one power was responsible; every government made mistakes; the "war guilt" verdict is wrong.
2. **Evidence:** the diplomatic documents; the confusion of the July Crisis; the Franco-Russian and Austro-German commitments.
3. **Approach:** documents-based, international, anxious to correct a wrong done to Germany; strong in America and Britain in the 1920s and 1930s.
4. **Criticism:** it set out to reverse a verdict, so it risked reading the documents with a conclusion in mind; and some of the German material it used had been selected by the German Foreign Office.
:::

## The Second World War and the debate, 1945–59

The Second World War reopened a question that had seemed settled. Two things changed.

First, **the shadow of Hitler.** Once Nazi Germany had caused a second war, a harder question arose: had Germany also been to blame for the first? Some historians reasoned that if the shared responsibility view had been wrong, it had helped Hitler. Others argued the opposite: that tracing Nazi aggression to Wilhelmine Germany blamed a whole nation for what a regime of 1933–45 did.

Second, **the Cold War and the new Germany.** West Germany, founded in 1949, was a vital ally against the Soviet Union, and the Allies had good reasons not to reopen old accusations. In 1951 French and German historians produced an agreed statement on 1914 at a conference at **Mainz**, in which the French historian **Pierre Renouvin** and the German historian **Gerhard Ritter** took part. The agreement concluded that the documents do not allow historians to attribute to any government or people in 1914 a premeditated will to bring about a European war. It was part of a wider effort to make French and German school textbooks less hostile, and it formed the background against which Fischer's work was later seen as a break. It is a useful example of how **time and place** shape history: the 1951 consensus was written in a divided Europe in which France and Germany needed each other.

So by the late 1950s the established view was a **moderate shared responsibility**: Europe had blundered into war; Germany had made mistakes; nobody had planned it. Gerhard Ritter's own account in *Staatskunst und Kriegshandwerk* (*The Sword and the Scepter*, 1954–68) defended German policy as largely defensive. This is the view Fischer was to overturn.

## The Fischer thesis, 1959–69

In 1961 a Hamburg professor, **Fritz Fischer** (1908–99), published a book that overturned the consensus. *Griff nach der Weltmacht* ("Grasp for world power"; in English *Germany's Aims in the First World War*, 1967) followed a 1959 article. Its subtitle was the war-aims policy of imperial Germany, and its argument was that Germany's war aims were not a product of the war but of long-held ambitions. In 1969 *Krieg der Illusionen* ("War of illusions", in English 1975) carried the argument back to the pre-war years, from 1911 to 1914.

:::key The Fischer thesis in five claims
1. **War aims.** Germany's war aims in 1914 were far-reaching: mastery of the Continent, a weakened France and Russia, a Belgium under German control, a central European economic union and a colonial empire in Africa. They are set out in Bethmann Hollweg's **September Programme** of 9 September 1914.
2. **Continuity.** These aims did not appear in September 1914. They continued the pre-war *Weltpolitik*, the ambitions of industry and of nationalist pressure groups.
3. **Responsibility in July 1914.** The German government encouraged Austria-Hungary's war on Serbia and accepted, perhaps wanted, the risk of a wider war. The **blank cheque** was an act of policy, not an accident.
4. **Preparation.** The "**War Council**" of 8 December 1912 and the army law of 1913 show that war was being prepared for 1914, before Russia and France became too strong.
5. **The primacy of domestic politics** (*Primat der Innenpolitik*): the causes of German aggression lay inside Germany. The ruling elites, frightened by the rise of the Social Democrats (who became the largest party in the Reichstag in January 1912, with 110 seats), looked abroad for a success that would keep the old order safe.
:::

:::def Primat der Innenpolitik
"The primacy of domestic politics": the view, used by Fischer and by historians who followed him, that a state's foreign policy is shaped more by its internal tensions (class conflict, party politics, the fear of reform) than by pressure from outside. It reverses an older view (the *Primat der Aussenpolitik*) that the external situation of a state decides its policy.
:::

**The evidence.** Fischer worked in German state archives, including the Reich Chancellery files held in what was then East Germany (at Potsdam), and read the memoranda of industrialists, the Navy and pressure groups, along with the September Programme and part of the diary of Kurt Riezler.

**What it shows about his approach.** Fischer asked a **question of intentions**: what did the German government want, and what did it do to get it? He sought the answer in German politics and in government papers, not in the system of alliances; and he **judged**, which most German historians of his generation avoided. His approach was also a historian's reaction to his own time and place: a West German professor, writing in the years of the Eichmann trial (1961) and just before the Frankfurt Auschwitz trials (from December 1963), in a society beginning to ask hard questions about the continuity of German history. (Historians connect the timing of the controversy to the questions of a younger generation, though you should treat this link as an interpretation and not as Fischer's own statement.)

**Criticisms.** Critics said: Fischer used a wartime document (the September Programme) to explain a pre-war decision; the evidence that Germany *planned* a war in July 1914 was largely inferred; the 8 December 1912 conversation was not a decision; he looked at Germany almost alone and did not set German aims beside those of France, Russia, Austria and Britain; and the idea of a straight line from Wilhelm to Hitler (which he developed later) was too simple.

## The German reaction and the Fischer controversy, 1961–64

The reaction was immediate and angry. West German historians saw Fischer's work as an attack on the nation's honour, and as a revival of the war-guilt accusation of 1919 that they thought had been disproved.

- **Gerhard Ritter** (1888–1967), the leading conservative historian of Germany and an author of the 1951 Mainz agreement, attacked Fischer in the *Historische Zeitschrift* in 1962, accusing him of reviving Versailles's war-guilt charge. Ritter's own work, *Staatskunst und Kriegshandwerk* (1954–68), defended Bethmann Hollweg's policy as essentially defensive.
- **Public attacks.** The President of the Bundestag, Eugen Gerstenmaier, criticised Fischer, and the Bonn government, at the urging of some historians, withdrew promised funds for Fischer's lecture tour of the United States in 1964.
- **The Berlin Historikertag, October 1964.** At the conference of German historians in West Berlin, Ritter, Egmont Zechlin and Erwin Hölzle spoke against Fischer, and the question of German responsibility dominated the meeting. It became a public event, reported in the newspapers.
- **Support.** Younger historians, among them **Imanuel Geiss** (*Julikrise und Kriegsausbruch 1914*, 1963–64, a collection of documents) and later **Hans-Ulrich Wehler** and **Volker Berghahn**, defended him, and outside Germany his work was widely welcomed. In Britain the historian **John Röhl** later strengthened the case with new evidence on the Kaiser's circle.

Why was the reaction so strong? There were scholarly objections (above), but historians also point to **patriotism and fear of national guilt** (a generation that had been through two lost wars did not welcome a verdict that returned them to 1919), to **generation** (the young historians were readier than their teachers to look at Germany's past critically), and to **politics** (West Germany in the Cold War needed to be taken seriously as a partner). A good Paper 3 answer says that the same events can be read in many ways because the readers care about different things.

:::exam Fischer in an extract
If an extract names "the German government", "world power", "continuity", "pressure groups" or "domestic politics", or says that the verdict of 1919 was "nearer the truth than its critics allowed", it is probably arguing in the Fischer tradition. If it names "the so-called war-guilt lie" or argues that Germany was "forced into a defensive war", it is arguing against it. **Label with a because**: "This is an anti-revisionist account **because** it revives the verdict of 1919 that revisionist historians of the 1920s had attacked."
:::

## Challenges to Fischer, 1960s–1990s

The Fischer debate led to refinements rather than a simple victory for either side. By the 1970s most historians accepted that Germany's leaders bore a heavy share of responsibility, but they disagreed about **what** they had intended.

:::def Calculated risk
A phrase associated with the diary of **Kurt Riezler**, a close aide to Bethmann Hollweg, for the German policy in July 1914: to back Austria-Hungary's war on Serbia, accepting that Russia might intervene and a continental war might follow, while hoping that Russia, France and Britain would draw back. It sits between a plan for war and an accident: Germany took a gamble knowingly, but did not necessarily want the larger war.
:::

- **Egmont Zechlin**, a Hamburg historian, joined the criticism in the 1960s, arguing that German war aims had developed in the course of the war and that Fischer's continuity was overdrawn.
- **Karl Dietrich Erdmann** edited the Riezler diaries (*Tagebücher, Aufsätze, Dokumente*, 1972). He used them to argue that Bethmann's policy was a calculated risk, not a plan for a war of conquest. The diaries became part of the controversy: Fischer's supporters read them as showing that Bethmann accepted a continental war; others argued about their meaning, and later some historians questioned whether the July 1914 entries had been reworked after the event; and the historian **Andreas Hillgruber** also drew on Riezler's idea of a policy of risk.
- **Wolfgang J. Mommsen**, a German historian, took a middle position. He accepted that domestic tensions pushed Germany's leaders towards taking risks, but he did not see a single plan; he saw an empire whose rulers could not cope with rapid social change and the arrival of mass politics, and whose policy was a response of fear as much as ambition. His account of the controversy was thought judicious by other historians.
- **Joll and Taylor** (above) pulled in other directions, away from German intention towards assumptions and timetables.

Where did this leave the Fischer thesis? Most historians accepted that Germany's leaders encouraged Austria's war, and that the pledge of 5–6 July was a decision with eyes open. Few now say that the war was planned for the day it started. The idea of a calculated risk is the most widely accepted middle ground.

## Modern interpretations: other nations' responsibility, 1991–2013

After the Cold War, new evidence (the opening of archives in Austria, Russia and Serbia) and new questions led historians to look at the other Great Powers. Four works matter.

**Samuel R. Williamson, Jr, *Austria-Hungary and the Origins of the First World War* (1991).** An American historian re-examined the policy of Vienna. His argument is that Austria-Hungary, not Germany, made the crucial decisions for war in the summer of 1914. Its leaders saw Serbia as an existential threat to a multinational empire, concluded that it must be destroyed, and did not wait to be restrained. Germany's blank cheque removed a restraint, but did not originate the policy. *Approach:* the Habsburg archives, the links between domestic and foreign policy, and a willingness to place a weak power's fear at the centre. *Criticism:* it can underplay Berlin's encouragement of Vienna.

**Niall Ferguson, *The Pity of War* (1998).** A British historian, an economic and financial historian by training and editor of *Virtual History* (1997), set out a provocative argument. Germany's war was not planned; the German navy was not the threat to Britain that British leaders claimed; and the greatest error was Britain's: its decision to enter turned a European conflict into a world war and, in Ferguson's view, damaged Britain more than a German-dominated Europe would have done. The book asks counterfactual questions ("what if Britain had stayed out?"). *Approach:* economics and finance, the logic of decisions, counterfactuals. *Criticism:* it plays down the German leadership's recklessness and the strategic reasons for Britain's fear.

**Sean McMeekin, *The Russian Origins of the First World War* (2011).** An American historian argued that Russia bears real responsibility. Its central ambition was control of Constantinople and the Straits; Foreign Minister Sazonov knew in July 1914 that mobilisation against Austria would mean war with Germany and pressed it anyway; the decision for general mobilisation turned a Balkan crisis into a European war. McMeekin said a watered-down Fischer thesis "can stand no more", meaning that the old story of German guilt no longer worked. *Approach:* Russian and French archives; the view from St Petersburg. *Criticism:* critics argued that it overstates Russian planning and asks why Russia would choose 1914.

**Christopher Clark, *The Sleepwalkers: How Europe Went to War in 1914* (2012).** An Australian historian at Cambridge, in a book that sold in huge numbers, argued that there was "no smoking gun" in the story, because "there is one in the hands of every major character". His argument has these parts:
- *A system.* Europe in 1914 was a multipolar system of several Powers, each affected by the others' decisions, in contrast to the two-bloc pictures of the Cold War.
- *Serbia.* Serbian nationalism and the officers of the Black Hand were a source of danger, and Clark called Serbia's reply of 25 July "a highly perfumed rejection" of Austria's demands.
- *Several decisions.* Russia, France, Austria, Germany and Britain all made choices that increased the risk.
- *Sleepwalkers.* Europe's leaders were "watchful but unseeing", unaware of what their decisions would bring. Clark proposed asking **how** the war came, not **why**, and said that the war was a tragedy, not a crime story with a culprit.
*Approach:* narrative history with a focus on decision-makers and their information and fears; a reaction against the search for a single culprit. *Criticism:* critics, including Annika Mombauer, said it spreads responsibility too thinly and plays down Germany's decisions in July; defenders reply that it restores balance.

Two other books of the centenary belong beside these. **Margaret MacMillan** (Canadian), *The War That Ended Peace* (2013), stressed decision-making by small groups of leaders, above all in Russia, Germany and Austria-Hungary, and the failure of imagination. **Max Hastings** (British), *Catastrophe* (2013), argued that German encouragement of Austria's move against Serbia was decisive, a view that is closer to Fischer than to Clark.

**Four modern cases, side by side**

| Historian and work | Who or what is at the centre | Key evidence | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Williamson (1991) | Vienna's fear of Serbia | Habsburg records, the 7 July council, the ultimatum | Underplays Berlin's role |
| Ferguson (1998) | Britain's decision to enter | Economics, naval and army calculations, the 1914 Cabinet | Underplays Germany's recklessness |
| McMeekin (2011) | Russia's ambitions and mobilisation | Russian and French documents, Sazonov | Overstates Russian planning |
| Clark (2012) | A system of Powers and their decisions | Memoirs, diplomatic papers, Serbian networks | Spreads blame thinly |

## How the time and place of writing shaped each interpretation

This is the syllabus's fourth reason for disagreement, and it is the best way to turn a list of historians into an *approach*.

| Historian and date | Time and place | What it did to the approach |
|---|---|---|
| German Foreign Office, 1920s | Germany after Versailles, under the war-guilt charge | Selection of documents, innocence campaign |
| Fay and Barnes, 1926–28 | USA after disillusion with the war and opposition to the treaty | A documents-based case for shared responsibility |
| Lloyd George, 1933–36 | Britain, a former prime minister defending his record | A memoir that glides over blame |
| Mainz agreement, 1951 | France and West Germany reconciling | A moderate view |
| Fischer, 1961–69 | West Germany, a new generation facing the Nazi past | A critical, intentions-based case against his own nation's elites |
| Ritter, 1962–64 | A conservative historian of the older generation | Defence of German policy as defensive |
| Williamson and McMeekin, 1991 and 2011 | After the Cold War, with archives opened in Vienna and Russia | A shift of attention away from Berlin |
| Clark, MacMillan and Hastings, 2012–13 | The centenary; a united Europe; worries about the rise of new powers | A "how" question, a system, a tragedy |

## The centenary, 2012–14

The hundredth anniversary changed the debate in three ways.
- **Volume and reach.** Several major books appeared in 2012–13, and Clark's became a best-seller in Germany as well as in Britain. Public interest was higher than at any time since the 1960s.
- **A new balance in Germany.** Some German historians and commentators said the Fischer controversy now belonged to the past, and that a more balanced picture, in which Germany shared responsibility, had become possible. Others, including Annika Mombauer, argued that Germany's decisions in July 1914 remained central and that the new books gave them too little weight.
- **Politics and memory.** In Britain the centenary led to a public quarrel. In January 2014 the Education Secretary, Michael Gove, wrote that the war had been a just war and criticised those who saw it as futile; historians, among them Richard Evans, replied. The debate shows how the First World War was still a subject of present-day political argument. Commentators in 2013–14 also drew comparisons between the Europe of 1914 and the twenty-first-century rise of new powers, which gave the "system" view of the war a fresh audience.

For Paper 3 the point is that **the time at which a historian writes shapes the question** as well as the answer: a 2013 extract that asks "how did Europe stumble?" belongs to a world after the Cold War and the European Union, as a 1961 extract that asks "what did Germany want?" belongs to a world coming to terms with Nazism.

## The importance of contingency over motive

The last syllabus area is a way of thinking, not a school.

:::def Contingency
The idea that events depend on particular circumstances, decisions and chances that could have gone otherwise, so that an outcome was not fixed in advance. A historian who stresses contingency over motive asks "what happened, and what choices made it possible?" instead of "who wanted it?".
:::

The central book is **Holger Afflerbach and David Stevenson (eds), *An Improbable War? The Outbreak of World War I and European Political Culture before 1914*** (2007), the result of a conference at Emory University in 2004. Its chapters question the idea that war was inevitable: they argue that many contemporaries thought a general European war improbable, that there were real signs of détente before 1914, and that the war came from a particular sequence of decisions. Clark and MacMillan write in the same spirit.

:::key Motive or contingency?
- **Motive** asks who wanted the war and why: Fischer on Germany, Williamson on Vienna, McMeekin on Russia. The answer is a person or a state.
- **Contingency** asks which choices, accidents and misunderstandings made the war happen: the Kaiser's halt in Belgrade proposal that Vienna did not take up; the Tsar's cancelled and reinstated order; a British Cabinet saved from a split by Belgium. The answer is a sequence.
- **The test**: each approach explains a different thing. Motive is good at explaining *why someone acted*. Contingency is good at explaining *why it turned out as it did*. The best answers show how an extract combines them, or why one is missing.
:::

## Reading an extract: signposts for each school

You cannot know which historian wrote a Paper 3 extract, but you can tell which tradition it belongs to from what it stresses and what it leaves out.

| If the extract stresses … | … it probably argues … | Knowledge to explain and test it |
|---|---|---|
| The German government, aims, domestic fear of reform, continuity | Germany bore the main responsibility (Fischer tradition) | Navy Laws, 8 December 1912, army law 1913, blank cheque, September Programme, SPD in 1912; test with Kaiser's halt proposal, other powers' aims |
| "No one wanted war", "all the Powers", "blunders", secret alliances | Shared responsibility (Fay, Lloyd George) | Alliances, the confusion of July, Grey's ambiguity; test with the blank cheque and Germany's ultimatums |

## Worked example: a full answer on a practice extract

:::eg Paper 3 [40]: What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer.
**Practice extract F** (about 190 words)

Germany's leaders did not stumble into war in 1914. Since the 1890s the Reich had pursued a policy of expansion, and by 1912 its rulers had concluded that a war for world power could not be avoided and ought to come before Russia grew too strong. When Austria-Hungary asked for support after Sarajevo, Berlin gave it without reservation. It knew that a war between Austria and Serbia might become a continental war, and it was ready to accept that risk. The aims set out in the first weeks of the war, which would have made Germany master of the continent, grew out of aims that had existed before it began. The roots of this policy lay inside Germany. Its rulers, afraid of democracy and of the rising Social Democrats, hoped that a successful war would keep the old order safe. To understand 1914 we must therefore look first at the German government and the German ruling class. The verdict of 1919, long dismissed as victors' justice, was nearer the truth than its critics allowed, and the responsibility of the German leadership was a heavy one.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Fritz Fischer, Griff nach der Weltmacht (1961) and Krieg der Illusionen (1969). The words are not Fischer's.*

**Plan (read, find, test).**
- Contrast and judgement words: "did not stumble"; "ready to accept that risk"; "grew out of"; "roots … lay inside Germany"; "look first at"; "nearer the truth than its critics allowed".
- **Main interpretation (two linked aspects):** (1) Germany's leaders deliberately pursued world power and in July 1914 knowingly accepted the risk of a continental war, so the war was no accident; (2) the cause lay in German domestic politics and aims, so responsibility rested heavily on the German leadership, and the verdict of 1919 was essentially right.
- Sub-messages: the pre-war aims anticipated those of September 1914; Berlin gave Austria unreserved support; in 1912 the rulers decided war could not be avoided; the war should come before Russia grew stronger.
- Approach: German government and elite as the centre; intentions and aims; domestic politics as cause; a sense of continuity; reply to an older view ("long dismissed"); readiness to judge.
- Knowledge to use: Weltpolitik and the Navy Laws; 8 December 1912 and the army law of 1913; the Russian Great Programme; the blank cheque of 5–6 July; the Kaiser's halt proposal of 28 July and Bethmann's telegrams; the SPD in January 1912; the September Programme of 9 September 1914; Austria's own decision (7 July council).

**A Level 3 answer (about 20 of 40).** 
"The extract says that Germany's leaders did not stumble into war. It says that Russia was growing strong, and that the Social Democrats were rising. It says that the aims of September 1914 were set out in the first weeks. So the historian thinks that Russia was growing too strong, and that Germany had to fight before it was too late, which means the war was a defensive war to protect Germany. In 1914 Austria-Hungary and Serbia were at war, and Germany declared war on Russia on 1 August and France on 3 August and then invaded Belgium, and Britain declared war."

*Commentary.* The answer picks up sub-messages (Russia's growth, the Social Democrats) and builds a **different interpretation** (a defensive war) that the whole extract rejects: the extract says the leaders *chose* a war for world power. The knowledge is a narrative of declarations, not tied to a claim. **Level 3** in Table A (10) and Table B (10), about 20 of 40.

**A Level 5 answer (about 32 of 40).** 
"The main message is that Germany's leaders wanted a war for world power, and that the July Crisis was an act of German policy. The historian says Berlin 'gave it without reservation' when Austria asked for support and 'was ready to accept that risk' of a continental war. This is supported by what is known of the blank cheque of 5–6 July 1914: the Kaiser told Austria's ambassador that Austria could count on Germany's 'full support' and should not delay, and the Chancellor, Bethmann Hollweg, confirmed it on 6 July. The historian also says that the aims of September 1914 'grew out of' earlier aims. This fits the Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900 and Bülow's call in 1897 for a 'place in the sun', which show an ambition for world power before the war. The 'war council' of 8 December 1912 is the best example of the historian's claim that by 1912 the rulers had decided war could not be avoided: Moltke said it was unavoidable and wanted it 'the sooner the better'.

The extract also argues that the causes lay inside Germany, among rulers afraid of democracy. That fits the election of January 1912, when the Social Democrats became the largest party with 110 seats. The historian's closing sentence says that the verdict of 1919 was 'nearer the truth' than its critics allowed. The historian is therefore making a case against the view in the 1920s that Germany had been unfairly blamed. The approach is to look at German government and intentions."

*Commentary.* A detailed, accurate explanation of the first aspect (July 1914 and the pre-war aims) with precise knowledge, a good start on the second (domestic politics) and a hint of the approach. But the answer does not explain how the two aspects form **one** argument, does not test the claim with evidence that cuts the other way, and gives the approach only a sentence. **Level 5** in Table A (16) and Table B (16): about 32 of 40.

**A Level 6 answer (about 37 of 40).** 
"The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. Its main interpretation is that Germany's leaders deliberately pursued world power and, in July 1914, knowingly accepted the risk of a continental war, and that the reason lay in German domestic politics, so that the verdict of 1919 was essentially right and the responsibility of the German leadership was heavy.

The first aspect is about decision and intention. The historian says Berlin 'gave it without reservation' when Austria asked for support, 'knew' that the war might become a continental war and 'was ready to accept that risk'. The evidence for this is the blank cheque. On 5 July 1914 the Austro-Hungarian ambassador reported that the Kaiser said Austria could count on Germany's full support and should not delay, and on 6 July the Chancellor confirmed it; the Kaiser then went on his annual cruise so that nothing would look unusual. The historian's claim that rulers 'had concluded by 1912 that war could not be avoided' fits the 'war council' of 8 December 1912, when Moltke said war was unavoidable and wanted it soon; it fits the army law of 1913; and the expectation, widespread among German officers, that Russia's Great Programme would make Russia too strong by about 1917 explains 'before Russia grew too strong'. But the claim needs testing. Austria-Hungary took its own decision to attack Serbia (the council of 7 July, the ultimatum of 23 July, the declaration of 28 July), and Berlin's leaders showed signs of a different policy in the last days: the Kaiser on 28 July proposed that Austria halt in Belgrade, and Bethmann sent telegrams on 29–30 July urging restraint on Vienna. These show that the leaders hoped for a limited war and not that they planned the continental one. The historian's phrase 'ready to accept that risk' is chosen carefully: it describes a calculated risk, which the evidence supports, and not a plan, which it would not.

The second aspect is about cause: 'The roots of this policy lay inside Germany.' Here the evidence is the rise of the Social Democrats, who in the Reichstag election of January 1912 became the largest party with 110 seats, and the fear of democracy among the Prussian elite, together with the nationalist leagues and the lobbying of industry for expansion. The aims in the September Programme of 9 September 1914 (a Belgium under German control, a weakened France and a central European economic union) are used by the historian as the proof that the war aims 'grew out of' those pre-war ambitions. A test: the September Programme was written during the Battle of the Marne, by a government that expected to win; it may show what Germany wanted when it was winning, not why it started the war. The extract leaves this out, and it also leaves out the aims and decisions of other Powers, which suggests that its approach is to explain the war from **inside Germany**.

This is the approach. The historian asks what the German government wanted and why it wanted it, finds the answer in German politics and in state papers, and judges. The phrase 'long dismissed as victors' justice' shows that the extract is written against the interpretation of the 1920s and 1930s that held that responsibility was shared; it is therefore fair to call it **anti-revisionist**, because it revives the verdict of Article 231 that the revisionists had attacked. The two aspects fit together: if the causes were domestic, then the decisions of July 1914 are the product of German policy and not of the alliance system, and so responsibility falls mainly on Germany. The conclusion about responsibility is the logical end of the approach."

*Commentary.* The opening sentence states the **whole** interpretation and its two aspects. Each aspect is explained with the extract's own words and tested with precise knowledge (the dates of the blank cheque; 8 December 1912; 7 July, 23 July and 28 July; the halt proposal; January 1912; 9 September 1914). The approach is explained from the extract, shown to fit the interpretation, and the label "anti-revisionist" is used with its *because*. **Level 6** in Table A (19) and Table B (18): about 37 of 40. About 700 words, which is a little long for 55 minutes: a 550-word version that keeps the opening, one test and the approach would earn the same level.
:::

"The historian is part of the revisionist school who thought Germany was guilty. Revisionists said Germany started the war because of the Navy Laws and Weltpolitik. Practice extract G is printed in the box under "Practice" below. Suppose your plan for it says: *approach: documents-based, international, aimed at the war-guilt verdict.* Here are three ways to write it.

**A Level 5 paragraph (Table A: sound, but does not explain the whole).** "The historian's approach is to argue against the verdict of Versailles. This fits the interpretation exactly. If every government's papers show mistakes and no plan, then responsibility is shared and the verdict is wrong. This belongs to its time: in the late 1920s many people in Britain and the USA thought the treaty too harsh, the governments of the former belligerents had published their papers, and German officials had published *Die Grosse Politik* and were encouraging writers abroad. Knowing that, I can say that the approach is revisionist (it revises the verdict of 1919) and that its strength is its use of documents, while its limit is that the German documents had been selected by a state with an interest in the outcome."

*Commentary.* The approach is tied to the interpretation by a sentence of explanation ("If … then …"), supported by the extract's own words, placed in its time with accurate knowledge (Article 231, the published documents, *Die Grosse Politik*) and given a label with its *because*. That is complete understanding: Level 6.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What did Article 231 say, and why did Germans object to it?
A: It stated that Germany and her allies were responsible for all the loss and damage caused by the war "imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies". It was the basis of reparations, but Germans read it as a declaration of guilt, and all parties rejected it.
Q: What was the war-guilt campaign of the 1920s?
A: The German Foreign Office's effort, through its war-guilt section, the Centre for Research into the Causes of the War (1921) and the publication of *Die Grosse Politik* (about forty volumes), to show that Germany was not solely responsible, supported by writers abroad.
Q: Who were Fay and Barnes, and what did each argue?
A: American historians of the 1920s who attacked the verdict of Versailles. Barnes (*The Genesis of the World War*, 1926) blamed Russia and France principally; Fay (*The Origins of the World War*, 1928) said responsibility was shared, with Austria-Hungary, Serbia and Russia chiefly responsible for the crisis and the alliance system a deeper cause.
Q: What did Lloyd George say about how the war began, and where?
A: In his *War Memoirs* (1933–36) he wrote that the nations "slithered over the brink into the boiling cauldron of war": nobody had quite meant it.
Q: What was agreed at Mainz in 1951?
A: French and German historians (Renouvin and Ritter among them) agreed that the documents did not allow the attribution to any government or people of a premeditated will to bring about a European war in 1914.
Q: Summarise the Fischer thesis in three claims.
A: Germany's war aims (the September Programme, 9 September 1914) continued pre-war ambitions for world power; the German government in July 1914 encouraged Austria's war and accepted the risk of a continental war (the blank cheque), and had prepared for war since the "war council" of 8 December 1912; the causes lay in German domestic politics (the primacy of domestic politics).
Q: How did the German historians react to Fischer in 1961–64?
A: Ritter attacked him in 1962 for reviving the war-guilt charge; the Bundestag president criticised him and funding for his American lecture tour was withdrawn; at the Berlin Historikertag in October 1964 Ritter, Zechlin and Hölzle spoke against him; younger historians (Geiss, later Wehler and Berghahn) supported him.
Q: What is a "calculated risk" and who supported the idea?
A: A phrase linked to Riezler's diary for Bethmann's policy of backing Austria while hoping for no wider war. Erdmann (editor of the diaries, 1972) and Hillgruber used it against Fischer's view that Germany wanted a continental war.
Q: Name the four main non-German cases and the historian associated with each.
A: Austria-Hungary (Williamson, 1991), Britain (Ferguson, *The Pity of War*, 1998), Russia (McMeekin, 2011), and a system of Powers with no single culprit (Clark, *The Sleepwalkers*, 2012).
Q: What does "contingency over motive" mean?
A: Asking what choices and chances made the war happen, rather than who wanted it; the argument of *An Improbable War?* (2007) and of Clark and MacMillan.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Explain how the time and place of writing shaped the approach of **Fischer** in 1961–69, and say how a historian writing in 2013 might ask a different question.
H: Think of West Germany in the 1960s: the generation, the Nazi past, the Cold War.
H: Think of 2013: the centenary, the European Union, the post-Cold War archives.
A: Fischer wrote in West Germany in the years of the Eichmann trial and the Frankfurt Auschwitz trials, when a younger generation was beginning to ask about the continuity of German history; the Cold War made Germany an ally, and the older historians' resistance shows how much national honour was involved; his question (what did the German government want?) and his judgement fit this world. In 2013 historians such as Clark, writing in a united Europe and after the opening of archives in Vienna, St Petersburg and Belgrade, asked how a continent of several powers came to war, and whether blame was the right question. :::recap
- **Shape of the debate**: 1919 Article 231 → 1920s German innocence campaign (*Die Grosse Politik*) → shared responsibility (Barnes 1926, Fay 1928, Lloyd George 1933–36) → Mainz 1951 → Fischer (1959, 1961, 1969) and the controversy (Ritter 1962; Berlin Historikertag 1964) → calculated risk (Zechlin, Erdmann 1972, Mommsen) → other nations (Williamson 1991, Ferguson 1998, McMeekin 2011) → Clark (2012), MacMillan and Hastings (2013) → centenary 2014; contingency (Afflerbach and Stevenson 2007).
- **Fischer**: war aims continuous with pre-war ambition; German government accepted a continental war in July 1914 (blank cheque; war council 1912); primacy of domestic politics.
- **Critics**: wartime document, inference, narrow focus on Germany; the middle ground is a "calculated risk".
- **Others**: Austria-Hungary (Vienna's fear of Serbia); Russia (the Straits, Sazonov, mobilisation); Britain (could have stayed out); Clark (a system, decisions in several capitals, "how not why").
- **Time and place** shape every approach: Weimar Germany, 1920s America, Lloyd George in the 1930s, West Germany in the 1960s, the post-Cold War archives, the centenary.
- **Labels**: revisionist (1920s historians rejecting the verdict of Versailles), anti-revisionist (reviving it); credited only when the extract supports them.
- **Method**: main interpretation (two aspects); sub-messages; knowledge to explain and test; approach; time and place.
