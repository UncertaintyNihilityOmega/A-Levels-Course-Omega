## The causes of the 1905 Revolution

### Nicholas II and the lack of political reform

**Nicholas II** became Tsar at 26 when Alexander III died in October 1894. He was gentle in manner, devoted to his family and conscientious, but he was weak in judgement and fiercely convinced that autocracy was a sacred trust handed down by his father. In January 1895 delegations from the zemstvos came to St Petersburg to congratulate him on his accession. Some zemstvo members hoped that he would allow them a voice in government. Nicholas answered them in a speech which became famous: he said that he had heard that some people had been carried away by "senseless dreams" of the participation of zemstvo representatives in the affairs of internal government, and that he would uphold the principle of autocracy as firmly and unflinchingly as his late father (adapted from English translations). Liberals concluded that the new reign would bring no reform.

:::key Nicholas II and the regime's refusal to reform, 1894–1904
1. **The principle**: Nicholas believed that his duty to God and to his dynasty was to hand on the autocracy intact.
2. **The practice**: he rejected zemstvo requests for a say (1895), and the government's answer to unrest was repression, not reform.
3. **Ministers divided**: Witte favoured modernisation of the economy without political change; Plehve, Interior Minister from 1902 to 1904, relied on the police.
4. **The result**: educated Russians, zemstvo liberals and professionals, who might have been allies in a reformed system, became opponents.
:::

The result was an unusual situation. Russia's economy and society were changing quickly (see below), but the political system stayed exactly as Alexander III had left it. This **resentment caused by the lack of political reform** united many different groups. Nobles in the zemstvos, professional people, workers, peasants and non-Russian nationalities all had their own grievances, but all of them could blame a government that refused to listen.

### Witte, industrialisation and economic problems

**Sergei Witte**, Finance Minister from 1892 to 1903, carried the state-led industrialisation of Chapter 1 much further. His programme:
- **Railways**: the state built and bought lines, and the Trans-Siberian Railway was pushed on across Asia. By 1900 Russia had about 53,200 km of line open, more than twice the figure for 1880.
- **The gold standard (1897)**: the rouble could be exchanged for gold, which reassured foreign lenders and investors.
- **Foreign capital**: French, Belgian, British and German money flowed into mines, metals and railways.
- **Tariffs and taxes**: high protective tariffs shielded new factories, and the state raised money through indirect taxes and, from 1894, a state monopoly of the sale of vodka.
The results were striking. On some estimates industrial output grew by about 8 per cent a year in the 1890s, and the Donbass, Baku, St Petersburg, Moscow and Poland became great industrial regions.

But the policy had costs, and these became **economic problems** for the regime.
- **Taxation.** The burden fell on the peasants, who paid indirect taxes on tea, sugar, kerosene and vodka, and still owed redemption payments for their land until 1907. Their arrears grew.
- **Bad harvests.** Poor harvests in 1901 and 1902 sent prices up, and in the spring of 1902 peasants in Poltava and Kharkov provinces attacked the estates of landowners. The government sent troops. The countryside was becoming restless.
- **Depression and unemployment.** From 1900 to 1903 Russia suffered an industrial depression: the railway boom slowed, credit tightened in Europe, banks and firms failed, and thousands of workers lost their jobs. Workers still in work faced wage cuts, long hours and fines.
- **Overcrowded cities.** St Petersburg and Moscow grew fast and urban workers lived in barracks and cellars. Many workers were former peasants with ties to the land, so economic hardship in the village and in the town fed the same anger.

:::def Gold standard
A monetary system in which a country's paper currency can be exchanged at a fixed rate for gold. Witte introduced it in Russia in 1897 to attract foreign investment and loans.
:::

### The opposition parties

The refusal of political reform meant that all opposition was illegal and underground, and most of it was driven abroad or into secret groups.

**The Socialist Revolutionaries (SRs).** Founded in 1901–02 from the Populist tradition (see Chapter 1), led by Viktor Chernov. They looked to the **peasants** and wanted land to be taken from the nobles and shared out. They also used **terror**: their Combat Organisation killed the Interior Minister Sipyagin (1902), his successor Plehve (28 June 1904) and, in February 1905, the Tsar's uncle Grand Duke Sergei. The Combat Organisation's leader, Evno Azef, was in fact a police informer, a fact discovered only in 1908. The SRs were the largest revolutionary party in the countryside.

**The Social Democrats (SDs).** The Russian Social Democratic Labour Party held its first congress at Minsk in 1898; most of its leaders were arrested within weeks. They followed Marx and looked to the **industrial workers**. In 1902 **Lenin** published *What Is to Be Done?*, arguing that workers would not reach revolutionary understanding on their own and that a small, disciplined party of professional revolutionaries was needed to lead them. At the Second Congress (held in Brussels and London in July–August 1903) the party split over how to define a party member. Lenin's supporters, who won a vote on other issues and so were called the **Bolsheviks** ("majority"), wanted a tight party of activists; **Martov** and his supporters, the **Mensheviks** ("minority"), wanted a broader membership. The split became permanent.

**The liberals.** Zemstvo gentry and professionals formed the **Union of Liberation** (1903–04), which demanded a constitution and civil rights. From November 1904, with political meetings forbidden, they held "banquets" at which toasts and speeches called for reform, an idea borrowed from France in 1847–48 (the **banquet campaign**).

**Nationalities and peasants.** Poles, Finns, Georgians, Armenians and the Jews of the Pale resented Russification (Chapter 1) and later joined the revolution. The peasants, hungry for land, had no party before 1905 but they had their grievances, as the risings of 1902 had shown.

:::def Socialist Revolutionaries
A Russian revolutionary party, founded in 1901–02, which aimed to give the land to the peasants and used terror against officials. It was the heir of the Populists and the biggest revolutionary party among the peasants.
:::

:::def Social Democrats
The Marxist Russian Social Democratic Labour Party, founded in 1898, which aimed at a workers' revolution. In 1903 it split into Bolsheviks (led by Lenin) and Mensheviks (led by Martov).
:::

### Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War

By 1900 Russia was expanding in the Far East. She leased Port Arthur from China (1898), built the Chinese Eastern Railway across Manchuria and moved into Korea. Japan, which saw Korea as vital to her security, offered a deal and was refused. Nicholas and his advisers underestimated Japan; some officials, such as Plehve, were said to hope that a short victorious war would distract Russia from its internal troubles (a claim historians question). On **27 January 1904** (9 February New Style) Japan attacked the Russian fleet at Port Arthur without a declaration of war.

The war went badly for Russia.
- **Distance and transport.** Almost everything had to travel along the single-track Trans-Siberian Railway, which was not complete round Lake Baikal until 1904, so supplies and reinforcements arrived slowly.
- **Leadership.** Russian commanders were often cautious and quarrelled with each other; the army relied on outdated tactics.
- **Defeats.** Port Arthur, besieged for months, surrendered on **20 December 1904**. The huge battle of **Mukden** (February–March 1905) was lost. The Baltic Fleet, sent half way round the world in a voyage of about seven months, was almost totally destroyed by the Japanese at **Tsushima** (14–15 May 1905).
- **Cost.** Estimates of Russian dead run from roughly 43,000 to 71,000.

The war ended in the **Treaty of Portsmouth** (23 August 1905, 5 September New Style), mediated by US President Theodore Roosevelt. Witte led the Russian delegation and secured relatively mild terms: Russia gave up Port Arthur and the southern half of Sakhalin and accepted Japan's interests in Korea, but paid no indemnity.

The war's importance for the revolution was threefold: it exposed the incompetence of the government, it caused shortages and unemployment in the cities, and the defeats made protest look patriotic. Many Russians blamed the autocracy for national humiliation. Defeat did not cause the revolution, which had deep roots, but it made the revolution possible by taking away the regime's prestige.

:::key Why was there a revolution in Russia in 1905? Four causes
1. **No political reform**: the Tsar's refusal of any voice in government united liberals, workers, peasants and nationalities against him.
2. **Economic problems**: heavy taxation and bad harvests in the countryside, depression and unemployment in the towns (1900–03), and the strains of Witte's rapid industrialisation.
3. **Organised opposition**: SRs, SDs and liberals gave grievances a voice and a plan.
4. **Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War**: it destroyed the regime's prestige and made the Tsar's government look incompetent.
The war and Bloody Sunday were the **triggers**; the first three were the **underlying conditions**.
:::

## The events of 1905

### Bloody Sunday, 9 January 1905

The industrial workers of St Petersburg had been organised, with police approval, in the **Assembly of Russian Factory and Mill Workers**, led by an energetic priest, **Father Georgy Gapon**. In early January 1905 a strike began at the huge Putilov ironworks and spread: within days 150,000 workers in St Petersburg were out. Gapon and a group of workers drew up a petition to the Tsar. It was respectful in tone, addressing him as "Sovereign", and said that the workers were treated "not like humans but like slaves". It asked for an eight-hour day and better pay and, above all, for political rights and for elections to a **Constituent Assembly** by universal, secret and equal suffrage (adapted from the translation by Daniel Field). The petition also asked for an end to the war.

On Sunday **9 January** columns of workers with their wives and children, carrying icons and portraits of the Tsar, marched towards the Winter Palace. Estimates of the number of marchers range from about 7,000 to 50,000. Nicholas was not in the city; he was at Tsarskoye Selo. The authorities had put thousands of troops in the streets. At several places, including the Narva Gate and Palace Square, soldiers fired on the crowds. The government's own figures were about 100 to 130 killed and about 300 wounded; other estimates run higher, to several hundred dead, and opposition claims put the number in the thousands. Gapon survived and left Russia.

:::def Bloody Sunday
The shooting of peaceful workers by soldiers in St Petersburg on 9 January 1905 (Old Style; 22 January New Style), as they marched to petition Nicholas II. It destroyed the workers' faith in the Tsar and began the revolution.
:::

The effects were out of all proportion to the numbers killed. Before 1905 many workers and peasants believed that the "little father" Tsar was on their side and that the evil was in his officials. Bloody Sunday shattered that belief. In January 1905 about 414,000 workers went on strike across Russia, in St Petersburg, Moscow, Warsaw, Riga and Tiflis.

### Strikes, mutinies and peasant risings

- **Strikes.** The number of strikers in 1905, according to the official statistics collected by the Ministry of Trade and Industry (the chart below), was about 2.86 million, about thirty-three times the number in 1903. Workers' demands were economic (wages, hours) and political (a constituent assembly, free speech).
- **Liberals and professionals.** In May 1905 professional associations (lawyers, doctors, teachers, engineers and writers) joined in a **Union of Unions**, demanding a constituent assembly. Zemstvo and town leaders held congresses. Universities were closed by student strikes.
- **The armed forces.** In June 1905 the crew of the battleship ***Potemkin*** of the Black Sea Fleet mutinied against their officers over rotten food and brutality; the ship sailed to Odessa, where there were strikes and riots, and then to Romania. The mutiny was suppressed (others followed at Kronstadt and Sevastopol later in 1905), but it showed that the armed forces were not wholly reliable.
- **Peasants.** In the summer and autumn peasants attacked landowners' estates, seized timber and grain and refused to pay rents and taxes, especially in the central black-earth provinces and the Baltic. A **Peasant Union** was formed that summer and demanded that the land be transferred to those who worked it. National movements in Poland, the Baltic provinces and the Caucasus rose against Russification.
- **The government's first response.** In August 1905 the Interior Minister, Bulygin, proposed an elected "Duma" that would only **advise** and would be chosen by a very narrow vote. The opposition rejected it.

### The October general strike and the Soviet

In September a printers' strike in Moscow spread, and in the first week of October the railways stopped. Within days there was a **general strike** that closed the railways, post and telegraph, shops, factories and many offices across Russia. The workers of St Petersburg set up a council, the **Soviet of Workers' Deputies**, to lead the strike; **Leon Trotsky**, a young socialist then close to the Mensheviks, became its most prominent leader. The government could neither run the railways nor rely on the troops, many of whom were still in the Far East.

### The October Manifesto, 17 October 1905

Faced with the strike, the Tsar's advisers split. Witte told Nicholas that he had two choices: a military dictatorship, or concessions. The Tsar's cousin, Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich, was proposed as dictator but refused. Nicholas, who disliked concessions, gave way. On 17 October 1905 he signed the **Manifesto on the Improvement of the State Order**, drafted by Witte, known as the **October Manifesto**. It promised:
1. **Civil liberties**: real inviolability of the person and freedom of conscience, speech, assembly and association.
2. **A wider franchise**: classes of the population who had no vote were to be invited to take part in the elections to the Duma, as far as possible.
3. **A Duma with a veto**: no law would come into force without the approval of the State Duma, and its elected members would be able to supervise the legality of officials' acts.
(Adapted from English translations.) Witte became **Chairman of the Council of Ministers** (in effect the first prime minister).

Nicholas explained to his mother that he had been forced to act. His real attitude shaped what happened next.

:::def October Manifesto
The declaration of 17 October 1905 in which Nicholas II, under the pressure of a general strike, promised civil liberties, a wider franchise and a State Duma whose approval would be needed for laws. It did not set up a constitution or make ministers answerable to the Duma.
:::

### The response to the Manifesto

The Manifesto split the opposition.
- **Liberals** divided. Those who wanted more formed the **Kadets** (Constitutional Democrats, led by Pavel Milyukov), who demanded a real parliament with control of the government. Those who accepted what had been granted formed the **Octobrists** (Union of 17 October, led by Alexander Guchkov), who supported the Tsar's reforms and wanted order.
- **The right** formed the **Union of the Russian People**, whose gangs, the Black Hundreds, attacked Jews, students and revolutionaries in a wave of pogroms in October.
- **The left** distrusted the Manifesto. The SDs and SRs wanted a republic and a constituent assembly. Workers continued to strike, and the Soviet declared a new general strike in November.
- **Crushing the Soviet.** In December the government arrested the leaders of the St Petersburg Soviet, among them Trotsky. The Moscow Soviet called an armed uprising. In the **Moscow uprising of December 1905** workers built barricades, and the Presnya district was shelled by artillery before troops, brought from St Petersburg, put it down. Revolts in the Baltic provinces, Poland, the Caucasus and Siberia were crushed by punitive expeditions.

### The Fundamental Laws, April 1906

Before the first Duma met, the Tsar issued the **Fundamental Laws** (23 April 1906; 6 May New Style). They defined the new order and were deliberately designed to preserve the Tsar's power.
- **Article 4:** "The Supreme Autocratic Power belongs to the Emperor of All the Russias. Obedience to his authority, not only for fear but also for conscience's sake, is commanded by God Himself" (adapted from English translations). The old description of the Tsar's power as "unlimited" was dropped, but the word "autocratic" remained.
- The Tsar controlled **foreign policy, the army and the Church**, appointed and dismissed ministers (who were not answerable to the Duma), and could **dissolve** the Duma and call new elections.
- **Law-making** needed the agreement of the Tsar, the **Duma** and the **State Council**, which was turned into an upper house, with half its members appointed by the Tsar and half elected.
- **Article 87** allowed the Tsar and his ministers to pass emergency decrees while the Duma was not sitting, which lapsed unless the Duma later confirmed them. The Duma could not use this to change the Fundamental Laws or the electoral law, but the government used it to avoid the Duma on important matters.
- The Duma could not control the court's finances, state loans or the army's recruitment, and ministers could be questioned but not removed by the Duma.
Witte resigned in April 1906, mistrusted by the Tsar, but not before he had arranged a huge loan of about 2.25 billion francs from French banks, which saved the government's finances.

## The Dumas, 1906–14

:::def The Duma
The elected lower house of the Russian parliament (the State Duma), created by the October Manifesto of 1905 and the Fundamental Laws of 1906. It could pass or reject laws and question ministers, but it did not choose or control the government, and the Tsar could dissolve it.
:::

| Duma | Dates (Old Style) | Character | End |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | 27 April–8 July 1906 | Elected by a wide, indirect franchise; the SRs and Bolsheviks boycotted it; the Kadets were the largest party (about 180 of nearly 500 deputies); demanded land reform and ministers answerable to the Duma | Dissolved after 72 days. Many Kadets met at Vyborg in Finland and called on the people to refuse taxes and recruits, without effect |
| Second | 20 February–3 June 1907 | The left did much better, since the SDs and SRs took part; hostile to the government | Dissolved after about 100 days; SD deputies arrested; the electoral law was rewritten (the **Third of June coup**) |
| Third | 1 November 1907–1912 | Elected under the new law, which gave landowners and the wealthy most of the weight; Octobrists and parties to their right had a majority | Sat for its full five-year term; co-operated with Stolypin on many laws |
| Fourth | 15 November 1912 onwards | Similar to the Third; more criticism of the government from 1913 | Met into the First World War |

The electoral law of **3 June 1907** was issued by decree, which broke the Fundamental Laws. It cut the representation of peasants, workers and non-Russian peoples and divided voters into groups (curiae) so that a landowner's vote counted for many times more than a worker's; a commonly given calculation is that one landowner's vote equalled the votes of some 260 workers. It was a change by force that gave the regime a Duma it could work with.

:::key How far did the Duma change Russia?
1. **Real change**: a legal parliament existed for the first time; there were legal parties, newspapers and trade unions (with limits); the Duma debated the budget, questioned ministers and passed laws.
2. **Limits**: the Tsar kept supreme power, appointed ministers, could dissolve the Duma, issue decrees under Article 87 and change the electoral law by decree.
3. **Verdict**: the Duma changed the form of Russian government more than its substance. Until 1914 it was an arena for criticism but not a rival centre of power.
:::

## Reasons for the survival of the Tsarist regime

In the autumn of 1905 the regime looked finished. By 1907 it was secure. Historians give several reasons, and an essay must weigh them.

### The loyalty of the armed forces

The Potemkin and other mutinies showed what could go wrong. But most of the army, especially the Guards and the Cossacks, stayed loyal, and by the winter the troops from Manchuria were returning home under their officers. The regime used them: the Moscow uprising was put down by the Semyonovsky Guards, and punitive expeditions restored order in the Baltic provinces and Siberia. Without the army the regime would have fallen. The peace with Japan in August 1905 was therefore a crucial precondition, and Witte's success at Portsmouth helped the Tsar more than it helped Witte.

### The nature and extent of opposition

The revolution of 1905 was in reality many revolutions with different aims, and they were never united.
- **Workers** wanted better pay, shorter hours and political rights. **Peasants** wanted land. **Liberals** wanted a parliament and a constitution. **Nationalities** wanted autonomy. These aims sometimes conflicted: liberals feared the peasants' attacks on property, and the peasants and workers had little contact.
- The revolutionary parties were **divided** (SDs split into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks; the SRs used terror while the SDs rejected it). Some leaders were abroad, and the Okhrana infiltrated the parties.
- The movement lacked a **single leader and a single centre**: the St Petersburg Soviet was the nearest thing and was arrested in December.
- Peasant risings were **local and unco-ordinated**, and many soldiers were peasants who obeyed their officers.

### Concessions, divisions and loans

The **October Manifesto** is the best-known reason for the regime's survival. It did three things: it split the liberals from the revolutionaries (Octobrists accepted it, Kadets did not); it ended the general strike, because the strikers believed that they had won; and it gave the Tsar time to bring back the troops. But it did not by itself save the regime. The December rising and the peasant revolts continued after it, and what ended them was force. The French loan of 1906 is also important: without it the government could not have paid its officials and troops.

### Repression

After the Moscow uprising the regime turned to force and kept it up for years. **Pyotr Stolypin**, who became Interior Minister in April 1906 and Prime Minister in July 1906, set up **field courts-martial** in August 1906, after a bomb attack on his house on Aptekarsky Island in which his daughter was injured. These courts could try and execute suspects within 24 hours. The noose became known as "**Stolypin's necktie**". Estimates of the number of people executed by these and other courts between 1906 and 1909 run to roughly 3,000. Thousands more were exiled or imprisoned. The Okhrana's agents broke up the parties, and the Black Hundreds attacked their enemies with official tolerance.

:::key Why did the Tsarist regime survive 1905–07?
1. **The army stayed loyal** and was available once the war with Japan ended.
2. **The opposition was divided** by class, party and nationality, with no common leader.
3. **The October Manifesto split the liberals from the revolutionaries** and ended the general strike.
4. **Repression** (courts-martial, exile, the Okhrana) broke the revolutionary parties from 1906.
5. **A French loan** (1906) kept the government solvent.
6. **The Fundamental Laws and the Third of June law** kept power with the Tsar and the propertied.
:::

## The extent of change between 1905 and 1914

### Stolypin and the countryside

**Pyotr Stolypin** (Prime Minister 1906–11) was an energetic governor who combined firmness with a plan. He said that the aim was to create a class of peasants who owned their land and had an interest in order, often summed up as "a wager on the strong and sober". His speech to the Second Duma on 10 May 1907, answering the revolutionary left, ended with the words usually translated: "They need great upheavals; we need a great Russia."

Stolypin's **agrarian reforms** were introduced by decree on 9 November 1906 (under Article 87) and later passed by the Duma.
- **Leaving the commune.** Any peasant could now ask to leave the commune and hold his strips as private property. Under the decree, a peasant in a commune that had not repartitioned its land for 24 years could claim title at once, and a law of 1910 treated communes that had never repartitioned as already converted to private ownership.
- **Consolidation.** Peasants could ask for their scattered strips to be combined into a single holding (a *khutor* or an *otrub*) in order to farm more efficiently.
- **The Peasant Land Bank** gave cheap credit to peasants to buy land (including from nobles), and the redemption payments had been abolished from 1 January 1907.
- **Migration.** The state helped peasants to move to Siberia and Central Asia with cheap railway tickets and land grants. About 2.8 million people moved east between 1908 and 1913, according to one standard estimate (by the historian John Thompson), and a minority came back.

What was the impact? Historians differ.

**The case for success.**
- Peasant unrest declined after 1907, though repression mattered too.
- Many peasants did leave the commune, and a class of more productive independent farmers began to appear. Grain output and exports rose, and 1913 was a bumper harvest.
- Siberia developed fast, with new villages, dairy farming and butter exports.
- Stolypin hoped for a generation of peace to complete the process, and some historians (such as Abraham Ascher, who has written about Stolypin) think the reform was promising.

**The case for limited success.**
- It was a reform of **land tenure**, not a solution to **land hunger**: the peasants' real problem was too little land for a growing population, and the land of the nobility was not touched.
- By 1914 most peasant households were still in the commune. Estimates of how many left, or received title, or consolidated their holdings, range from about 10 per cent to about a quarter of households, depending on what is counted (applications, title deeds or consolidated farms). One account puts the number who applied to leave at around two million by 1915 and those who consolidated at around 1.2 million.
- Many peasants resisted, because the commune gave security. Land captains and officials often forced the changes through, which made them unpopular.
- **Time ran out.** Stolypin was shot on **1 September 1911** at the Kiev opera by Dmitry Bogrov, a revolutionary who was also an informer of the Okhrana, and died on 5 September. By then the Tsar's favour had waned: Stolypin had clashed with conservative nobles and with the Tsar over using Article 87 to force a bill through the State Council in March 1911. War in 1914 cut short any chance to see the reforms through. The Soviet historians judged the reform a failure, and western historians are divided.

:::def Stolypin's agrarian reforms
A series of measures from 1906 to 1911 to break up the peasant commune and create a class of independent peasant landowners, loyal to the regime, by allowing peasants to leave the commune, consolidate their land, borrow from the Peasant Land Bank and migrate to Siberia.
:::

:::warn Do not say Stolypin's reforms "failed" or "succeeded" without evidence
The best answers weigh. Say: "the reforms increased the number of independent farmers and helped Siberian settlement, but by 1914 they had changed the lives of only a minority; and the land hunger remained." Remember also that the reforms were meant to help the regime politically. Judging them requires a criterion: their effect on production, on peasants' lives, or on political stability.
:::

### Industry between 1905 and 1914

The depression ended in 1909, and from then industry grew quickly: coal, iron, oil and textiles all expanded, railways were extended and foreign capital returned. Russia remained one of the world's fastest-growing economies up to 1914, although it started from a low base. Key developments:
- **Banks and syndicates.** Banks, many with foreign links, took a growing part in industry, and cartels in metals and coal controlled production.
- **A growing, concentrated workforce.** Big factories in St Petersburg and Moscow brought tens of thousands of workers together. Many workers were still tied to their villages, but a skilled, literate group in the cities began to form.
- **Working conditions** remained poor. Trade unions, legalised in 1906, were harassed by the police, and the Duma's laws on insurance (1912) were limited.

### The return of unrest

The impact of industrial change was political as well as economic. After the lull of 1908–10 (see the strike chart), strikes rose again. In **April 1912** troops shot at strikers in the Lena goldfields in Siberia, killing and wounding many (estimates of the dead range from about 80 to about 270). The massacre led to a wave of protest strikes across Russia, with about 683,000 strikers in 1912 on the official count, though other estimates are higher. Bolshevik influence grew in St Petersburg, and in July 1914, as the French President visited the capital, a general strike with barricades broke out. Russia in 1914 was not a society at peace. Historians argue about whether the regime was strengthening or whether it was heading for a second revolution.

:::key How far had Russia changed by 1914?
1. **Politically**: a Duma, legal parties and press, but the Tsar's powers intact; little change in substance.
2. **Economically**: fast growth in industry and a start to peasant reform; the vast majority of people were still poor peasants.
3. **Socially**: a larger, more concentrated working class and a stronger middle class in business and the professions, but no way for them to take part in power.
4. **Stability**: unrest was low in 1908–10 but rising again in 1912–14.
:::

## Weighing the outcomes: how serious was the threat, and why did the regime survive?

:::key A judgement you can defend
The 1905 Revolution was a real threat because it combined **workers, peasants, liberals, soldiers and nationalities** and shut down the economy. But it was **many revolutions with different aims**. The regime survived because the army stayed loyal, the opposition was divided, the October Manifesto split the liberals from the revolutionaries, a foreign loan paid for the recovery and repression finished the job. The essential point is that the Tsar made **concessions to survive, not to share power**. He kept the Fundamental Laws, the army and the Church, and used the Duma as a prop. So the outcome was an **uneasy settlement**: the regime had survived but had learned nothing about how to reform.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **Abraham Ascher**, *The Revolution of 1905*, two volumes (1988 and 1992, subtitled *Russia in Disarray* and *Authority Restored*), is the standard detailed history. He stresses the revolution's many strands and the regime's recovery of its nerve.
- **Orlando Figes**, *A People's Tragedy* (1996), places 1905 within a longer crisis of the old regime: the gap between rulers and ruled and the Tsar's failure to adapt.
- **Sheila Fitzpatrick**, *The Russian Revolution* (1982), a short history that treats 1905 as a rehearsal in which the main groups of 1917 took shape.
- On Stolypin, historians divide between those who see a wise reformer cut short and those who think the reforms came too late and touched too few peasants.
You can use this at AS to show that the same events can be read as a near-collapse or a survival, but Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why there was a revolution in Russia in 1905.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "There was a revolution in 1905 because of the Russo-Japanese War. Russia lost battles at Port Arthur and Tsushima. Then there was Bloody Sunday in January when soldiers fired on workers. The workers went on strike and there was an October Manifesto. The peasants were poor and there were bad harvests."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes. It does not say how any of these produced a revolution. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The first reason was the Tsar's refusal of political reform. Nicholas II had told the zemstvos in 1895 that hopes of sharing power were 'senseless dreams', so liberals, professionals and workers who wanted a voice in government had no legal way to ask for one. This made opposition illegal and pushed it underground, where the SRs and the SDs organised. A second reason was economic distress. Witte's industrialisation relied on heavy indirect taxes on the peasants, bad harvests in 1901 and 1902 led to peasant risings, and the depression of 1900–03 threw workers out of work, so there was anger in both town and country."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was the Russo-Japanese War. Defeats at Port Arthur (December 1904), Mukden and Tsushima (May 1905) exposed the incompetence of the government, brought shortages and unemployment, and removed the regime's prestige. When troops fired on a peaceful march led by Father Gapon on 9 January 1905, workers who had believed that the Tsar was on their side lost that belief, and 414,000 struck that month.

These factors were linked. The refusal of reform meant that grievances had no outlet; economic hardship made workers and peasants ready to act; the war removed the regime's prestige and its troops; and Bloody Sunday turned discontent into revolt. The decisive factor was the refusal of political reform, because without it the economic distress and the war would have produced complaints that a more flexible regime could have met with concessions, as Russia's later history shows. The war and Bloody Sunday were the triggers, but the Tsar's refusal to share power was the underlying cause that turned them into a revolution."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (underlying causes against triggers), and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

Sovereign! We, workers and inhabitants of the city of St Petersburg, of various estates, come to You. We are treated not like humans but like slaves who must suffer a bitter fate and keep silent. Order that elections to the Constituent Assembly be conducted under universal, secret and equal suffrage.

*Adapted from the petition prepared by Father Georgy Gapon and a group of St Petersburg workers for presentation to Nicholas II, 9 January 1905 (translation by Daniel Field).*

**Source B**

We lay upon the government the duty of carrying out Our unshakable will: to grant the population the foundations of civil liberty, based on real inviolability of the person and freedom of conscience, speech, assembly and association; to invite to the Duma, as far as possible, those classes of the population now deprived of the vote; and to establish as an unbreakable rule that no law shall go into force without the approval of the State Duma.

*Adapted from the Manifesto on the Improvement of the State Order (the October Manifesto), issued by Nicholas II, 17 October 1905.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the demands for political reform in Russia in 1905.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is a petition from workers asking the Tsar for elections. Source B is the Tsar's Manifesto promising a Duma. They are different because one is from the workers and one is from the Tsar. The Manifesto gave the workers what they wanted so they are similar."

*Commentary.* A difference in authorship, supported, but the similarity is a claim without evidence from the sources and there is no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share a belief that the people should have a part in making laws and enjoy basic freedoms. Source A asks for 'elections to the Constituent Assembly', and Source B promises that 'no law shall go into force without the approval of the State Duma'. Both therefore accept the idea of an elected body with real power, and both link it to the rights of ordinary people: A complains of being treated 'like slaves', and B promises 'civil liberty' and 'freedom of … speech, assembly and association'.

The differences are in the extent and in who holds power. Source A asks for a Constituent Assembly elected by 'universal, secret and equal suffrage', which would write a constitution and so put the nation above the Tsar. Source B offers only a Duma, and promises to extend the vote 'as far as possible' to those who lack it, not to everyone. It does not mention a constitution, and it speaks of 'Our unshakable will': the reform is a gift from the Tsar and not a right. A is a request from below, addressed to 'Sovereign'; B is an order from above.

Context and provenance explain both. Source A was written in January 1905 for a peaceful procession, by a priest whose workers' association had police approval. Its respectful language ('Sovereign') reflects the workers' belief that the Tsar would listen, and its demands, though political, were an appeal to him. Its purpose was to persuade, and it was never delivered, because troops fired on the marchers on 9 January. Source B was issued nine months later, after the general strike had paralysed the railways, with the war lost and Witte warning the Tsar that the only alternative was dictatorship. It was drafted by Witte and aimed to end the strike and divide the opposition; that explains why it grants the principle but is vague on the franchise and silent on a constituent assembly. A is better evidence of what the workers and many Russians wanted in January; B is better evidence of what the regime was prepared to concede under pressure in October. The gap between them shows how far the demands had moved: the Tsar gave in October less than was asked in January, and the liberals and socialists went on asking for more."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (Bloody Sunday, the general strike) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The October Manifesto was the main reason the Tsarist regime survived 1905.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the Manifesto (17 October) ended the general strike; split the liberals into Kadets and Octobrists; gave the Tsar time; Witte became Chairman of the Council of Ministers.
- Against 1, the army: loyal, home from Manchuria after Portsmouth (5 September New Style); crushed the Moscow rising (December); punitive expeditions.
- Against 2, divisions: workers, peasants, liberals and nationalities wanted different things; the SDs and SRs differed; no common leader.
- Against 3, repression and money: Soviet arrested (December); courts-martial from 1906; the French loan of 1906; the Okhrana.
- Against 4, the Fundamental Laws (April 1906) took back much of what the Manifesto had promised.
- Judgement: the Manifesto saved the regime in October 1905; the army and repression saved it from December 1905 to 1907. Criterion: what the regime would have lost without each.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The October Manifesto was the main reason the regime survived. The general strike of October 1905 had stopped the railways and the factories and the Tsar was in danger. On 17 October he issued the Manifesto, which promised civil liberties, a wider vote and a Duma that had to approve laws. The workers thought they had won and the strike ended. The liberals split into the Kadets and the Octobrists. So the Manifesto saved the regime."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts. The paragraph does not say what would have happened without the Manifesto or what happened after it. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the Manifesto did not end the revolution. The St Petersburg Soviet called a new strike in November, and in December workers rose in Moscow and built barricades in the Presnya district. What defeated them was force: the Semyonovsky Guards, brought from St Petersburg, shelled the district, and punitive expeditions suppressed risings in the Baltic provinces and Siberia. The army could be used because the war with Japan had ended with the Treaty of Portsmouth in September, and because the great majority of the troops remained loyal, despite mutinies such as the *Potemkin*. Without that loyalty, a Manifesto would have been a stage in the regime's fall, not a means of survival. In 1906 the government added a second line of defence: the courts-martial of Stolypin, which executed roughly 3,000 people between 1906 and 1909, and a loan of about 2.25 billion francs from French banks, which paid the troops and officials.

The nature of the opposition also helped. The revolution was many revolutions: workers wanted wages and rights, peasants wanted land, liberals wanted a parliament, and the nationalities wanted autonomy. The SRs used terror, which the SDs rejected, and the SDs were themselves divided into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks. These divisions meant that no single movement could take over the state, and the regime could defeat its enemies one at a time. The Manifesto was clever precisely because it exploited this: it detached the moderate liberals from the workers.

Overall, the October Manifesto was the decisive reason the regime survived the crisis of October 1905, because it ended the general strike, which was the most dangerous moment. But it was not the main reason for survival through 1905–07 as a whole. The Manifesto bought time; the army, the divisions in the opposition and repression then decided the outcome, and the Fundamental Laws of April 1906 took back the Manifesto's promises in substance. If the criterion is what the regime would have lost without it, the army was the more important, since a regime with a loyal army could survive without the Manifesto but not the reverse."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (what the regime would have lost without each factor) and separates the crisis of October from the whole period 1905–07. With an opening paragraph on the case for the Manifesto, this is Level 5 (18–20).

*Adapted from the closing words of a speech by Pyotr Stolypin, Chairman of the Council of Ministers, to the Second Duma, 10 May 1907, answering the revolutionary deputies. The wording is adapted from English translations.*

Number of strikers each year in factories under the Factory Inspectorate: 1904, 24,904; 1905, 2,863,173; 1906, 1,108,406; 1907, 740,074; 1908, 176,101; 1909, 64,166.

*Adapted from V. I. Lenin, "Strikes in Russia", December 1913, quoting official statistics of the Ministry of Trade and Industry. Lenin was a Bolshevik leader writing for workers.*

**Source D**

**Question.** How far do these sources support the view that the Tsarist regime survived the revolution of 1905 by restoring its authority, not by changing?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support (restoration of authority): A (the Tsar keeps supreme autocratic power), B (Stolypin's firm line against the revolutionaries), C (the strikes collapse after 1906), D (the first two Dumas dissolved and the electoral law changed to produce a compliant third). Challenge (some change): A (the old word "unlimited" is absent and a Duma exists), D (the Duma survived and met for a full term in 1907–12), B ("a great Russia" implies reform, not just repression), C (strikes were a real challenge in 1905–07; they were not removed by 1909 by authority alone).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: an official law, strong evidence of what the Tsar intended to keep. But it is a statement of principle, issued before the first Duma met, and it says nothing about how the Fundamental Laws worked. Its significance is that it came six months after the Manifesto of October 1905 and took much of it back.
- **B**: a minister's speech to a hostile Duma, made to rally support and to answer the left; it shows Stolypin's intentions and his political skill, but not their results. It was delivered in 1907, just before the Second Duma was dissolved, so it is evidence of the government's confidence.
- **C**: statistics gathered by the government and quoted by a revolutionary with a purpose: to show the rise of the labour movement. Lenin said the statistics covered only factories under the inspectorate, and a worker might be counted more than once. It is good evidence for the pattern of strikes, weaker for the reasons, since falling strikes could result from repression and unemployment as well as from a restored authority.
- **D**: a factual table of dates, reliable, but selective: the dissolutions show the government's power and not the Dumas' role. It does not mention that the Third Duma co-operated and passed laws.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "The sources support the view that the regime restored its authority. Source A shows that the Tsar kept 'supreme autocratic power' in the Fundamental Laws, issued only six months after the October Manifesto, and Source D shows that the first two Dumas were dissolved after 72 days and about 100 days, with the electoral law rewritten on 3 June 1907 so that the Third Duma could sit for a full term. Source C shows the revolution's collapse from 2.86 million strikers in 1905 to 64,000 in 1909, and Source B shows a minister confident enough to dismiss the revolutionaries in the Duma. Together they describe a regime that took back the ground it had given. However, the sources also qualify the view. The word 'unlimited' no longer appears in Source A, a Duma sat throughout (Source D), and Stolypin's 'great Russia' (Source B) was backed by land reform, so that the regime did try to change the countryside. Source C also reminds us that the strikes of 1905–06 were what forced the concessions in the first place, and that the fall to 1909 owed as much to repression and unemployment as to a restored authority. On balance the sources support the view to a considerable extent: the regime survived by restoring its authority and by choosing the form of change that cost it least power. But the survival also depended on partial change, a parliament and a land reform, so the view is too simple. It was restoration with a degree of adaptation."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and context; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify, and uses own knowledge (the October Manifesto, the Stolypin reforms, repression). The judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How successful were Stolypin's agrarian reforms?" [20]

A student wrote:

"Stolypin was Prime Minister from 1906 to 1911. He wanted to help the peasants so he let them leave the commune and have their own farms. He set up the Peasant Land Bank to give them loans. He also helped people move to Siberia and about 2.8 million went there. He was shot in 1911 at the opera in Kiev by a revolutionary. His reforms were very successful because many peasants became richer and the country was better off."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is **narrative and assertion**: it lists the reforms and then declares them "very successful" without evidence, ignores the limits and never uses a criterion. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then weigh. "Stolypin's reforms were successful in their aim of creating a class of independent farmers in some regions, but they reached only a minority of peasants and did not solve land hunger. About 2.8 million people moved to Siberia between 1908 and 1913 and the number of independent farms grew, but estimates suggest that only between one tenth and one quarter of households left the commune or gained title to land by 1915, and the land of the nobility was untouched. If success means helping the regime, unrest in the countryside fell after 1907, but repression played a part. A fair judgement is that the reforms were promising but limited in their effect by 1914." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give two reasons why liberals were disappointed with Nicholas II in his early reign.
A: He told the zemstvo delegations in January 1895 that hopes of sharing power were "senseless dreams"; he kept Pobedonostsev's and Plehve's policies of repression and refused any elected body, so the educated had no legal way to take part in politics.
Q: What were the differences between the Bolsheviks and the Mensheviks in 1903?
A: They split at the Second Congress over party membership: Lenin's Bolsheviks wanted a tight party of activists led by professional revolutionaries; Martov's Mensheviks wanted a broader party with a wider membership.
Q: Give three ways in which the Russo-Japanese War helped cause the 1905 Revolution.
A: Defeats (Port Arthur, Mukden, Tsushima) destroyed the regime's prestige; the war brought shortages, higher prices and unemployment; the army and navy were committed far from home, and national humiliation made protest look patriotic.
Q: What happened on Bloody Sunday, and why did it matter?
A: On 9 January 1905 troops fired on a peaceful march led by Gapon to petition the Tsar. Around 100 to 130 were killed on the government's count, and more by other estimates. It destroyed the workers' belief in the Tsar and set off strikes across Russia.
Q: What did the October Manifesto promise, and what did it not promise?
A: Civil liberties, a wider franchise and a Duma whose approval laws needed. It did not set up a constitution, make ministers answerable to the Duma or end the Tsar's control of the army and foreign policy.
Q: How did the Fundamental Laws of 1906 limit the Duma?
A: The Tsar kept "supreme autocratic power", appointed ministers, controlled the army and foreign policy and could dissolve the Duma; the State Council, half appointed, could veto laws; Article 87 allowed decrees when the Duma was not sitting.
Q: What was the Third of June coup of 1907?
A: The Tsar dissolved the Second Duma and rewrote the electoral law by decree, weighting the vote in favour of landowners and the wealthy to produce a more conservative Third Duma.
Q: Give two measures of Stolypin's agrarian reforms and one limit on their impact.
A: Peasants could leave the commune and consolidate their holdings; the Peasant Land Bank lent money and the state helped migration to Siberia. Limit: by 1914 only a minority of households had left the commune, and land hunger remained.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Russia was defeated in the Russo-Japanese War of 1904–05.
A: Indicative content: distance and the single-track Trans-Siberian Railway, incomplete round Lake Baikal until 1904, so supplies and troops arrived slowly; poor leadership and quarrels among commanders; outdated tactics and equipment; underestimating Japan, which had a modern navy and army; the fall of Port Arthur (December 1904), the defeat at Mukden (1905) and the destruction of the Baltic Fleet at Tsushima after a seven-month voyage; discontent at home reduced the government's will to continue. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: Russia's geographical and administrative weaknesses were made worse by poor leadership, and a judgement says which mattered most, e.g. the distance from the centre of power made the others fatal.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Nicholas II issued the October Manifesto in 1905.
A: Indicative content: the general strike of October 1905 had stopped the railways and many factories; the Soviet showed the workers' organisation; troops were unreliable or still in the Far East and the Potemkin mutiny had shown mutiny was possible; Witte warned that the alternative was military dictatorship, and the Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich refused to be dictator; the Bulygin Duma of August had been rejected; the regime needed to divide the opposition; the war with Japan had ended in September. Level 4 connects the background (defeat and unrest) with the trigger (the general strike) and judges which mattered most; e.g. the strike was the immediate cause but the war and Bloody Sunday had made the regime weak enough for it to succeed.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War was the main cause of the 1905 Revolution.' How far do you agree?
A: For: defeats destroyed the regime's prestige; shortages and unemployment in the cities; the army and navy were absent or beaten; the revolution began in January 1905 when the war was going badly; defeats at Tsushima and Mukden were followed by the Potemkin mutiny and the October strike. Against: discontent with the lack of political reform; the economic problems of the depression of 1900–03, bad harvests and taxation; the opposition parties (SRs and SDs) and the liberal banquet campaign had been active since 1902–04; Bloody Sunday was the spark; the peasants' risings of 1902 predate the war. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "what turned discontent into revolution", and judges, e.g. that the war was the trigger and the lack of reform the underlying cause.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How successful were the Dumas as a way of changing the way Russia was governed between 1906 and 1914?
A: Success: a legal parliament; debates and criticism of ministers; legal parties and press; the Third Duma sat a full term and passed laws; Octobrists co-operated with Stolypin. Limits: the Fundamental Laws left the Tsar supreme; ministers not answerable; the first two Dumas dissolved after months; electoral law changed by decree on 3 June 1907; Article 87 allowed rule without the Duma; the State Council could veto. Level 5 uses a criterion, e.g. whether the Duma limited the Tsar's power, and judges: not very successful as a restraint on power, though it changed the political life of the country.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about relations between the Tsar and his people.
A: Similarities: both address the ruler as the source of justice and order and claim to speak for the good of Russia; both appeal to loyalty and to faith in the Tsar. Differences: A is a petition from below, a request for justice from a ruler, with a hint that the workers are ready for sacrifice; B is a command from above, calling for service and an end to "sedition", with the Tsar as the authority. A shows trust in the Tsar, B shows the Tsar's distrust of the people. Provenance: A, in January 1905, was written for a peaceful march that ended in bloodshed; B, in April 1881, was issued to reassure supporters and warn opponents after the assassination of Alexander II. Level 4 explains the contrast with the 24 years between them, the change in the workers' relation to the Tsar, and judges which is the better evidence of relations at its date.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources A–D in the worked example above ("Paper 1 (b) [25]"). How far do these sources support the view that Stolypin's policies were the main reason the regime survived after 1905?
H: Decide which sources are about Stolypin's own policies (B) and which are about other things (A, C, D).
H: Ask what each source shows about repression, reform or the Duma.
H: Use your own knowledge of the courts-martial and the agrarian reforms to judge Source B.
A: Support: B shows Stolypin's combination of firmness and a positive aim; D shows the third Duma, after the electoral law of 1907, co-operating with him; C shows strikes falling after 1906, partly under the courts-martial. Challenge: A shows the structure of power that existed before and apart from Stolypin; C's fall began in 1906 (before he had much effect) and was also due to the earlier loss of the revolution; the regime had survived the worst of 1905 before Stolypin became Prime Minister in July 1906 (army, October Manifesto, loans). Evaluation with knowledge: B is a speech, so it shows intention, not effect; C is Lenin's selection; D is selective; A is law, not practice. Level 5 judges how far: Stolypin consolidated the regime's survival in 1906–11 by repression, the Third of June law and land reform, but the regime had already survived the immediate crisis, so the sources support the view only to a limited extent.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Russia changed little between 1905 and 1914.' How far do you agree?
H: Choose areas of change: politics, the economy, the countryside, society and unrest.
H: For each, compare 1905 with 1914 and weigh whether the change was of form or of substance.
A: Little change: the Tsar kept supreme power and the Duma was weak; the Fundamental Laws and the Third of June law restored control; most people were still peasants in communes; poor working conditions and the rise of strikes in 1912–14. Change: a legal parliament and parties; faster industrial growth and a larger, more concentrated working class; Stolypin's reforms and Siberian settlement; the end of redemption payments; trade unions and a freer press, with limits. Level 5 uses a criterion (changes in the distribution of power, in the economy, or in people's lives) and judges, e.g. that the form of government changed more than its substance, but economic and social change was substantial, so the answer is "to a limited extent politically, to a considerable extent economically".
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ The two volumes of Abraham Ascher's history of 1905 are called *Russia in Disarray* and *Authority Restored*. How far does the evidence of 1905–14 support the claim that authority was restored after the disarray of 1905?
H: List the evidence of disarray in 1905 and the evidence of restored authority in 1906–11.
H: Then look at 1911–14: was authority still restored, or was disorder returning?
A: Disarray: strikes of 2.86 million, the Potemkin and other mutinies, the October general strike, the Soviet, peasant and national risings, the Moscow uprising. Restoration: the October Manifesto split the opposition; the army crushed the risings; Stolypin's courts-martial; the Third of June law; the fall of strikes to 64,000 by 1909; the French loan; economic recovery after 1909. Challenge: authority was restored by force, not by consent; the Tsar's favour for Stolypin waned and Stolypin was killed in 1911; the Lena massacre (1912) and the strikes of 1912–14, including July 1914, show returning disorder; peasants' land hunger, workers' conditions and the liberals' grievances were unresolved. A* judgement: authority was restored in the sense of control by 1907–10, but the base was narrow and was wearing thin by 1914, so "restored" is accurate for the state's power but not for its legitimacy.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Witte, in his memoirs, described the October Manifesto as the only way out of the crisis of 1905. How far should a historian trust memoirs of this kind?
H: What was Witte's position and his own interest in 1905? When and why might he have written?
H: How could you test his account against other evidence?
A: Strengths: Witte was a leading participant, close to the Tsar and the decision; he knew the discussions; his memoirs contain details and show the alternatives (dictatorship) that he presented. Weaknesses: written later, with hindsight, by a man dismissed in 1906 and keen to justify his policy and criticise the Tsar and his rivals; memoirs tend to enlarge the author's role; they may be selective about the pressure of the strike and the generals. Test: compare with the Tsar's own letters, the accounts of other ministers, the sequence of events (the general strike, Portsmouth, the loyalty of troops) and the results (the Manifesto's effects). Judgement: useful evidence of Witte's view and of the choices put to the Tsar, but needs corroboration, especially for his claim that there was no other way.
:::

:::recap
- **Causes**: Nicholas II's refusal of reform (1895 "senseless dreams"); Witte's industrialisation with heavy taxes (gold standard 1897); bad harvests 1901–02; depression 1900–03; SRs (1901–02, terror), SDs (1898; Bolsheviks and Mensheviks 1903), liberals (Union of Liberation, banquets 1904); defeat by Japan (Port Arthur 20 December 1904, Mukden, Tsushima 14–15 May 1905).
- **1905**: Bloody Sunday 9 January (Gapon; official count about 100–130 dead, other estimates higher); about 2.86 million strikers; Potemkin 14 June; Treaty of Portsmouth 23 August; general strike and Soviet in October; October Manifesto 17 October; Moscow uprising in December.
- **Settlement**: Fundamental Laws 23 April 1906 (Article 4, Article 87); Dumas: First 27 April–8 July 1906, Second 20 February–3 June 1907, electoral law of 3 June 1907; Third 1907–12; Fourth from 1912.
- **Survival**: army loyal; opposition divided; the October Manifesto split the liberals; repression (field courts-martial from 1906; roughly 3,000 executed 1906–09); French loan of about 2.25 billion francs.
- **Stolypin** (Prime Minister 1906–11): decree of 9 November 1906; commune leaving, consolidation, Land Bank, Siberia (about 2.8 million migrants 1908–13); estimates of households affected range from about 10 per cent to about a quarter; shot 1 September 1911, died 5 September.
- **To 1914**: fast industrial growth after 1909; strikes down to 1910, then up (Lena April 1912; 683,400 strikers in 1912; July 1914).
- **Judgement**: the regime survived by restoring authority and by limited change; it conceded to survive, not to share power.
:::
