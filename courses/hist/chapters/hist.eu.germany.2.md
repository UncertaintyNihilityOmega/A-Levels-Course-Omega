:::why
In March 1848 it seemed that the old order in Germany had collapsed in a matter of days: Metternich fled Vienna, the King of Prussia rode through Berlin wearing the black, red and gold colours of German unity, and an elected parliament met in a Frankfurt church to write a constitution for a united Germany. Eighteen months later the parliament had been dispersed by soldiers, its leaders were in prison or exile, and the princes were back in charge. Understanding why teaches you how to weigh the strength of a movement against the strength of the forces opposing it. It also explains why the next stage of German history, unification under Bismarck, was achieved by Prussian power and not by parliaments.
:::

## The spread of revolution in the German states

### The spark: Paris, February 1848

On 22–24 February 1848 the people of Paris rose, King Louis-Philippe abdicated and a republic was proclaimed. The news travelled across the Rhine within days, helped by the railways and the telegraph. Germans had expected something like this for a long time; what surprised them was the speed with which governments gave way.

### March in the south-west and the other states

The movement began in the south-west, where the liberals were strongest. On 27 February a mass meeting at **Mannheim** in Baden demanded freedom of the press, juries, a citizens' militia and a German parliament. On 5 March, 51 liberals met at **Heidelberg** and called for a "Pre-Parliament" of representatives from all German states. Crowds gathered in town after town with petitions, and the rulers, many of them frightened, conceded. The "March demands" were much the same everywhere:
- freedom of the press, speech and assembly;
- trial by jury;
- citizens' militias (armed guards) in place of reliance on the army;
- liberal ministers (the "March ministries") and constitutions;
- a German national parliament;
- in the countryside, an end to feudal dues, as peasants in the south-west attacked castles and burned records, as in the French Great Fear of 1789.

The southern and middle states agreed one after another. In Baden, Württemberg, Saxony and Hanover new liberal ministers took office. In Hesse-Darmstadt the liberal **Heinrich von Gagern**, who would become the leader of the Frankfurt Parliament, became head of the ministry in March. In Bavaria, King Ludwig I, already unpopular for his affair with the dancer Lola Montez, abdicated on 20 March in favour of his son Maximilian II. Even the Federal Diet gave way: it allowed the states to end censorship and adopted the black, red and gold colours on 9 March.

### The fall of Metternich

The decisive blow came in Vienna. On 13 March 1848 a crowd of students and workers gathered at the meeting of the Lower Austrian estates to demand a constitution and the end of censorship. The troops opened fire and killed several demonstrators; workers from the suburbs joined, and the court feared a rising. The Emperor Ferdinand I, who was weak-minded and left government to others, dismissed **Metternich**, who resigned and fled to England. A few days later Ferdinand promised a constitution. The man who had been the symbol of the whole conservative system for thirty-nine years had fallen in a day.

The fall had effects far beyond Vienna. The empire's other peoples took their chance: in Hungary, Louis Kossuth and the Magyars won their own government in the "April Laws", Italians rose in Milan and Venice, and the Czechs of Bohemia demanded autonomy. The Habsburg Empire, held together by its army and its court, seemed to be breaking up. This helps explain the later course of events: Austria was too busy saving itself to take part in the German movement, but it was also the strongest conservative force, and once it recovered, it turned back to Germany.

### Revolution in Prussia

Berlin was the second great test. The king, **Frederick William IV**, was a man of strong beliefs and nervous temperament; he believed that kings ruled by God's grace, but he also had a romantic love of German history and wanted to lead Germany in his own way. During March he faced petitions, crowds and demonstrations. On 18 March he announced a package of concessions: censorship was to end, the United Diet was to be called, and Prussia was to join a reformed German federation. A large crowd gathered in the Palace Square to cheer. Two shots were fired as soldiers moved to clear the square (it is still unclear by whom, and probably by accident), the crowd believed it had been betrayed, and barricades went up across the city. Soldiers and citizens fought through the night. About 250 to 300 people were killed, most of them civilians: the cemetery of the "March fallen" in Friedrichshain received 254 graves. The king's soldiers held the palace but not the streets.

On 19 March the king published a proclamation, "To My Dear Berliners". He said that a "terrible misunderstanding" had taken place, blamed a band of troublemakers, many of them foreigners, and promised to withdraw the troops if the barricades were cleared. That evening the bodies of those killed were carried into the palace courtyard, and the king, who had been forced to come out on to the balcony, bared his head to them. The troops left Berlin. On 20 March his brother, Prince William, the officer most associated with the army and with hard-line views, left for England.

On 21 March the king rode through the streets wearing a black, red and gold armband, and issued a proclamation "To my people and to the German nation" in which he said that he had taken the old German colours and that "Prussia is henceforth merged in Germany". In late March he appointed a liberal ministry led by two Rhineland businessmen, Ludolf Camphausen and then David Hansemann. An elected Prussian National Assembly met in Berlin in May to write a constitution.

:::key The role of Frederick William IV in 1848
1. **He gave way under pressure**: he withdrew the troops (19 March), saluted the dead, and wore the German colours (21 March) because he feared losing his throne.
2. **He never accepted the principle behind the revolution**: that power came from the people. He believed in monarchy by divine right and thought of the concessions as favours he could take back.
3. **He kept the army**, the court and the Junker nobility on his side. When the army returned to Berlin in November, the concessions were reversed.
4. **His personality mattered**: emotional, indecisive, deeply religious, he swung between conciliation and rage, which made him unreliable for both liberals and conservatives.
:::

### The Pre-Parliament and the elections

The liberals of the south-west wanted to move fast, before the princes recovered. About 570 of them, from most German states but mostly from the south-west, met in the church of St Paul's in Frankfurt as the **Pre-Parliament** from 31 March to 3 April. It rejected the proposal of the radicals, led by Friedrich Hecker and Gustav Struve, to declare itself a permanent revolutionary body and to proclaim a republic. Hecker and Struve left, and Hecker started a small, unsuccessful rising in Baden in April. The Pre-Parliament arranged for elections in every state to a **National Assembly** at Frankfurt. The vote went to all "independent" adult men (in some states this excluded servants and those on poor relief), which was a very wide franchise for the time, though women had no vote. Elections were held in April and May.

## The Frankfurt Parliament

### Formation

The **Frankfurt Parliament** opened on 18 May 1848 in the Paulskirche (St Paul's Church), Frankfurt. About 800 deputies were elected for the whole Confederation, including Austrian Germany, and in practice about 400 to 500 usually attended. Heinrich von Gagern was elected president on 19 May.

:::def Frankfurt Parliament
The National Assembly elected in April and May 1848 by the men of the German states, which met in St Paul's Church, Frankfurt, from 18 May 1848 to write a constitution for a united Germany. It ended in 1849 when the King of Prussia refused its offer of the imperial crown.
:::

Who were the deputies? Most were educated men of the middle class: lawyers, civil servants, judges, professors, teachers and clergy. Very few were craftsmen or peasants, and none were women. Contemporaries called it the "professors' parliament" (sometimes mockingly). The deputies were mostly liberals, of the moderate kind who wanted a constitutional monarchy, with a smaller group of radicals on the left (led by Robert Blum, a Leipzig bookseller) who wanted a republic and wider social reform, and a smaller group of conservatives on the right. Groups met in clubs named after inns, such as Casino (moderate liberals) and Deutscher Hof (the left). These were the first political parties in German history, though they were loose.

### The first actions: a government without power

The first task was to create a government. On 29 June 1848 the Parliament elected the Archduke John of Austria as **Imperial Regent** (*Reichsverweser*), the head of a Provisional Central Power. He was a Habsburg prince known for his liberal opinions, and he was chosen to win Austria's goodwill. The Federal Diet dissolved itself and handed over its powers on 12 July. The Regent chose ministers who were responsible to the Parliament.

But the Provisional Central Power rested on nothing. It had **no army** (the troops were those of the states, who swore loyalty to their own kings), **no civil service**, **no tax revenue** of its own and no power to enforce its orders. The Regent's authority existed only as long as the states obeyed him, and the strongest states did not intend to.

This was shown by the first test, the war over **Schleswig-Holstein**. In March 1848 the King of Denmark tried to bind Schleswig, which had a mixed German and Danish population, more tightly to Denmark. The two duchies rose in revolt, and German nationalists everywhere wanted to help. The Confederation asked Prussia to send troops, and the Prussian army drove the Danes out. But Britain, Russia and Sweden objected to a German power on the Baltic, Denmark's navy blockaded German ports, and Frederick William IV, who did not like helping a rebellion against another king, gave way. On **26 August 1848** Prussia signed the **Malmö armistice** with Denmark without consulting Frankfurt. The deputies, faced with the humiliating fact that the Parliament could not even control a war over its own territory, first rejected the armistice, then on 16 September accepted it by a narrow vote because they had no means to fight on. Angry mobs attacked the deputies who had voted for it. On 18 September the crowd murdered two of them, Prince Felix Lichnowsky and General Hans von Auerswald, and the Parliament had to call in Prussian and Austrian troops to restore order in its own city. The episode showed that the Parliament depended on the very armies that would later destroy it.

### The Basic Rights

The Parliament spent the autumn and winter debating its constitution, and began with a list of rights. The **Basic Rights of the German People** were approved on 27 December 1848. They declared equality before the law (the nobility "as an estate" abolished), freedom of the press without censorship, freedom of religion, assembly and association, trial by jury, and protection of property. They were published as law, and several states promptly announced they would not accept them. The restored Federal Diet formally annulled them on 23 August 1851. Yet they had lasting influence: later German constitutions, including the Weimar constitution of 1919 and the Basic Law of 1949, drew on this tradition.

### The constitution and the German question

The harder question was what Germany was. Two answers competed.
- **Grossdeutsch** ("Greater German"): a Germany that included the German-speaking lands of Austria, perhaps with all of Austria's empire.
- **Kleindeutsch** ("Smaller German"): a Germany without Austria, led by Prussia.

The Parliament faced the problem in October 1848, when it decided that no part of the new Reich could be linked to non-German lands under the same ruler (the effect was to require Austria to divide). This was impossible for Austria. About 36 million people lived under the Habsburgs, of whom only about 8 million were German speakers; Hungarians, Czechs, Italians, Poles and Slavs made up the rest. Austria's government, under **Prince Felix zu Schwarzenberg** from November 1848, said it would keep its empire whole and rejected the demand. In March 1849 Austria issued its own constitution for a unified empire. Meanwhile Czech nationalists had refused to take part in the German movement at all: the historian František Palacký, invited to Frankfurt in April 1848, replied that he was a Czech, not a German, and that the Austrian Empire had to be preserved as a protection for the smaller peoples of Central Europe.

In December 1848 Gagern became head of the Reich ministry and proposed the kleindeutsch plan: a federal state without Austria, with a close union between the Reich and Austria. The Parliament accepted it in early 1849, by narrow votes. On 27 March 1849 the Parliament voted for an hereditary emperor by 267 votes to 263, and on 28 March it passed the constitution and elected **Frederick William IV** Emperor of the Germans. He received 290 votes with 248 abstentions.

The **constitution of 1849** was the most liberal in Germany. It made a federal Reich of the territory of the old Confederation, with a hereditary emperor who had only a suspensive veto, a two-house parliament (a house of the states and a house of the people elected by all adult men), and the Basic Rights.

### The crown refused

A deputation of 32 members of the Parliament went to Berlin. On **3 April 1849** Frederick William IV told them that he was honoured but could not accept the crown without the agreement of the other German princes and free cities. A month later he issued a formal rejection. He had other reasons that he gave in private: he thought the crown of a German emperor was one that only the princes could give, not an assembly, and he is reported to have said that a crown picked up from the gutter would chain him to the revolution like a dog collar. He also feared Austria and Russia. Historians such as Christopher Clark (*Iron Kingdom*, 2006) stress that his refusal was not just stubbornness: it followed from his belief in legitimacy and God-given monarchy.

### Why the Parliament collapsed

After the king's refusal, the Parliament had nothing to stand on. Twenty-eight small states accepted the constitution, but Prussia, Austria, Bavaria, Saxony and Hanover rejected it. Prussia and Austria recalled their deputies in April–May 1849. The remaining deputies moved to Stuttgart in May, and on 18 June 1849 Württemberg troops dispersed the "rump".

:::key Reasons for the collapse of the Frankfurt Parliament
1. **No power**: no army, no money, no civil service. It depended on Prussia and Austria for troops (the Schleswig-Holstein affair and the Frankfurt riots showed it).
2. **Divisions among the deputies**: moderates against radicals; grossdeutsch against kleindeutsch; Catholics and Protestants; and no agreement on the social questions that concerned artisans and peasants.
3. **Slowness and idealism**: months of debate on rights and principles while the old rulers regained their strength in Vienna and Berlin.
4. **The recovery of the conservatives**: the army defeated the revolutions in Prague, Vienna and Berlin in 1848, so by the time the constitution was finished the Parliament faced rulers who no longer needed it.
5. **The refusal of the crown** by Frederick William IV, the last blow: without a ruler to accept it, the constitution was a piece of paper.
:::

### The May risings and their defeat

The refusal of the crown provoked a last burst of revolutionary action, the "campaign for the Imperial Constitution". In May 1849 there were risings in Dresden (3–9 May; the composer Richard Wagner and the Russian anarchist Mikhail Bakunin were involved), in the Rhineland and the Palatinate, and in Baden, where much of the army joined the rebels and the Grand Duke fled. Prussian troops, led by Prince William, crushed these risings. The fortress of Rastatt in Baden, the last stronghold, surrendered on 23 July 1849. Many rebels were shot or imprisoned, and thousands of others fled abroad, many to the USA. They are known as the **Forty-Eighters**; Carl Schurz, who escaped from Rastatt, later became a US senator and Secretary of the Interior.

### The impact of the 1848 revolutions

:::key What the revolutions changed, and what they did not
**Failure.** The aims of the revolutionaries were not achieved: no united Germany, no liberal constitution that lasted, and the Confederation restored. Many leaders were in exile.

**Lasting results.**
1. **Constitutions and parliaments** now existed in many states. Prussia kept a constitution (the imposed one, revised in 1850) with a two-house Landtag, however unequal its franchise.
2. **Feudal dues were ended**: peasants in the German states, as in Austria, gained freedom from them in 1848–49 and mostly lost interest in further revolution.
3. **Political experience**: parties, newspapers and associations had developed in a way that could not be reversed.
4. **The Basic Rights** and the constitution of 1849 became models for later German constitutions.
5. **A change of attitude**: many liberals concluded that unity would come through power rather than parliaments. In 1853 the writer August Ludwig von Rochau used the word **Realpolitik** for politics based on power and not on ideals, and many liberals later backed Bismarck.
6. **A larger gap between Austria and Prussia**, because the Parliament had shown that unity without Austria was possible.
:::

## Reasons for the reassertion of Austrian control

The revolutions failed because the old powers recovered. Two sets of reasons explain this: the strength of the conservatives and the weakness of the revolutionaries.

### The strength of the conservatives

Austria shows the pattern best.
- **The army**. The Austrian army stayed loyal to the emperor, partly because it was made of soldiers of many nationalities who had no common national feeling to share with the rebels, and partly because the officers were loyal to the dynasty. Field Marshal **Radetzky** held northern Italy and defeated the Piedmontese at Custoza (July 1848) and Novara (March 1849). General **Windischgrätz** bombarded Prague in June 1848 to crush the Czech rising. After the October rising in Vienna he and the Croatian governor Josip Jelačić took the city by force on 31 October 1848. Robert Blum, a deputy of the Frankfurt Parliament who had joined the Vienna rebels, was shot on 9 November despite being a deputy with parliamentary immunity.
- **The court and the nobility**: the court moved to Olmütz, and Ferdinand abdicated on 2 December 1848 in favour of his 18-year-old nephew Franz Joseph. Prince Felix zu Schwarzenberg became chief minister in November 1848. He was determined to rebuild the empire's power and had no interest in German liberal dreams.
- **Divisions among the empire's peoples**: Czechs, Croats and Slovaks distrusted the Magyars and the Germans. The court played them off against each other. Croat troops under Jelačić fought the Hungarians; Austria used Russian help in 1849 and Hungary surrendered at Világos on 13 August 1849.
- **The peasants**: Austria abolished serfdom and feudal dues in 1848. Peasants, the majority, were satisfied and stayed quiet.
- **Russia**: Tsar Nicholas I, who feared revolution on his borders, sent an army against Hungary in 1849.

In **Prussia** the same pattern played out more quietly. The king's army remained loyal, and the court party of conservatives, the "camarilla", pressed him to act. In November 1848 the king appointed a conservative ministry under Count Brandenburg, moved the Assembly out of Berlin, and sent General Wrangel and 13,000 troops into the city (10 November). On 5 December he dissolved the Assembly and issued his own constitution. It gave a two-chamber parliament, but the king kept command of the army, an absolute veto and the right to rule by emergency decree. On 30 May 1849 he changed the franchise by decree to a **three-class system**.

:::def Three-class franchise
The Prussian voting system introduced by decree on 30 May 1849. Voters were divided into three classes according to the direct taxes they paid, so that each class paid one third of the tax of the district; each class elected one third of the electors, who then chose the deputies, and voting was open. A few rich voters in the first class had as much weight as the many poor voters in the third. It lasted until 1918.
:::

### Divisions between the revolutionaries

:::key Why the revolutionaries were divided
1. **Liberals against radicals**: the moderates wanted constitutional monarchy and order; the radicals (Hecker, Struve, Blum) wanted a republic and social reform. When the Parliament called in Prussian and Austrian troops against the Frankfurt rioters in September 1848, the moderates showed that they feared the people.
2. **Middle class against workers and artisans**: artisans asked the Parliament to restore guild privileges, which the liberal middle class, who believed in free trade, would not do. After the June Days of 1848 in Paris, where workers rebelled and were crushed, the middle classes feared "red" revolution more than they feared the princes.
3. **Nationalities**: Germans, Czechs, Poles, Danes and Hungarians had conflicting claims, and German nationalists were not ready to give up lands where Slavs or Danes lived. This made enemies of potential allies.
4. **North against south, Protestant against Catholic**: the kleindeutsch solution put Catholic south Germans under a Protestant Prussian emperor.
5. **Grossdeutsch against kleindeutsch**: the central argument of the Parliament.
:::

Add to this the fact that the revolutions were not simultaneous. Each revolt was crushed separately, and the revolutionaries in Frankfurt, Berlin and Vienna never had a joint command. The conservatives could use the army against one at a time.

## Changing relations between Austria and Prussia after 1848

By 1849 Austria had saved its empire, but Prussia had not given up its ambition to lead Germany. The next decade is a story of rivalry, which ends in the 1860s with war. This section follows the main steps.

### The Erfurt Union and the humiliation of Olmütz

After the failure of Frankfurt, Frederick William IV's adviser **Joseph von Radowitz** proposed a different scheme: a **Prussian Union**, a federal union of the German states under Prussian leadership, with a constitution based on the Frankfurt one but giving the princes more power and keeping the three-class vote. Prussia signed a Three Kings' Alliance with Saxony and Hanover on 26 May 1849 (Saxony and Hanover soon dropped out), and many of the smaller states joined the Union. An Erfurt Parliament of the Union met from 20 March to 29 April 1850.

Schwarzenberg would not accept a Prussian-led Germany. He revived the old Federal Diet in Frankfurt in May 1850 (many states returned to it) and the two powers were soon opposed. The crisis came in **Hesse-Kassel**. In September 1850 the Elector Frederick William I, who had been in conflict with his parliament over taxes, fled his capital and asked the Federal Diet for help. The Diet agreed, and Bavarian troops, backed by Austria, marched towards Hesse on 1 November. Prussia, which controlled the military roads through Hesse that linked its eastern and western territories, sent troops there and mobilised its army. Tsar Nicholas I, who disliked the Prussian Union as a child of revolution, told Prussia at Warsaw that he supported Austria. On 8 November Prussian and Bavarian troops met at **Bronzell**, where a single shot killed a horse.

The Prussian government was divided. The war party wanted to fight, but the army was not ready, Radowitz resigned, and the new Prussian prime minister, **Otto von Manteuffel**, preferred a settlement. On **29 November 1850** he met Schwarzenberg at Olmütz in Moravia. Prussia agreed to:
- demobilise its army;
- abandon the Union (the Erfurt scheme);
- withdraw its troops from Hesse-Kassel and allow Austria and Bavaria to restore the Elector;
- attend conferences at Dresden on reforming the Confederation.

Prussians called this the **humiliation of Olmütz**.

:::def Humiliation of Olmütz
The name given in Prussia to the Punctation of Olmütz of 29 November 1850, by which Prussia, faced with Austrian and Russian opposition and unready for war, abandoned its Union plan and accepted the restored Federal Diet under Austrian leadership.
:::

But was it a total victory for Austria? The Dresden Conferences (December 1850 to May 1851) were meant to reform the Confederation. Schwarzenberg's plan was to bring the whole Habsburg Empire into the Confederation, which would have created a central European bloc of about 70 million people dominated by Austria, with a customs union to follow. Prussia, many middle states, Britain and France all opposed it. In the end the conferences broke up in May 1851 without reform, and the old Federal Diet was restored as it had been before 1848.

:::key How far was Olmütz a victory for Austria?
**For.** Prussia dropped the Union, demobilised, left Hesse to Austrian and Bavarian troops and accepted the restored Diet. Austria had Russia's backing. Prussians felt humiliated and said so.

**Against.** Schwarzenberg failed to bring the whole empire into the Confederation (Dresden), and the Confederation was restored with no reform. Austria needed Russia's help in 1849–50, so it had become dependent on a foreign power that might not help again. Prussia kept its army, its leadership of the Zollverein and its equal vote in the Diet. Manteuffel and Bismarck defended the retreat as prudent.

**Judgement.** A real but limited and short-term victory: Austria won the crisis of 1850, but did not make itself the master of Germany and could not afford another contest.
:::

### Economic issues: the Zollverein

The two powers also quarrelled over trade. After 1849 Austria's minister of commerce, **Karl Ludwig von Bruck**, wanted a customs union of all Central Europe, including Austria. The Zollverein treaties were due to expire at the end of 1853, and several of the middle states (Bavaria, Württemberg, Saxony, Hesse) wished to include Austria to balance Prussia. Prussia was determined to keep Austria out: if she were admitted, Prussia would lose control. In 1851 Prussia got Hanover's customs union (the Steuerverein) to join in a treaty, and then threatened to dissolve the Zollverein and rebuild it without the states which opposed her. The middle states, who depended on the revenue and trade, backed down: in 1853 the Zollverein was renewed for twelve years (to 1865), and Austria received only a trade treaty (19 February 1853) with lower tariffs and a promise of further talks. Prussia had defeated Austria on the issue that mattered most for the future.

:::warn Do not confuse the Zollverein with the Confederation
Austria was a member of the Confederation but not of the Zollverein. The Federal Diet at Frankfurt was Austria's field; the Zollverein was Prussia's. After 1850 Austria won the first contest (Olmütz) and Prussia won the second (the Zollverein renewal of 1853). A good answer says which of these mattered more for the long run.
:::

### Economic development and growth in Prussia

In the 1850s Prussia boomed. Railways spread, ironworks and coal mines in the Ruhr, the Saar and Silesia grew, and joint-stock banks made credit available for industry. German coal output rose from about 5 million tonnes in 1850 to about 12 million in 1860 (rounded figures from B. R. Mitchell's tables), most of it from Prussian mines. The Zollverein made Prussia the centre of this growth, and the money gave the state more revenue for its army and its civil service.

### Economic and financial problems facing Austria

Austria's economy grew too, but its state finances were weak. The revolutions and the wars of 1848–49 had been expensive, the army was large, and the government spent more than it earned in most years. The state had borrowed heavily and paid a large share of its revenue in interest. The **Crimean War** (1853–56) made things worse. Austria sided with Britain and France against Russia in 1854 and mobilised its army along the Russian border, which was very costly and gave it nothing in return; it also turned Russia, the power that had saved it in 1849, into an enemy. Austria was left without allies. In 1859 the war with France and Piedmont in Italy led to defeats at Magenta (4 June) and Solferino (24 June) and the loss of Lombardy at Villafranca (11 July 1859), and the cost forced Austria to ask for loans and to introduce reforms in 1860.

### Political issues

**The growth of liberalism.** In Prussia, political life revived in the late 1850s. Frederick William IV suffered a stroke in 1857, and in October 1858 his brother Prince William became Regent. He named a more moderate ministry, and liberals called the change the **New Era**. In 1859 the liberal nationalists founded the **National Association** (*Nationalverein*) at Frankfurt on 16 September, with Rudolf von Bennigsen as a leader, to work for a united Germany under Prussian leadership (a kleindeutsch solution). The centenary of the poet Schiller's birth in November 1859 was marked by national festivals. In Austria, in contrast, the government under Alexander Bach ruled without a parliament.

**Prussia's international position.** After Olmütz Prussia's weakness was plain. In the Crimean War it stayed neutral and was at first left out of the peace congress at Paris; it was admitted only in March 1856. But neutrality kept its army intact and its finances sound. In 1859, when Austria went to war with France, Prussia mobilised troops on the Rhine but did not fight; the war ended before it could intervene, and Prussia had gained prestige without any cost.

| 1850s | Austria | Prussia |
|---|---|---|
| Population (about 1850) | About 36 million, many nationalities | About 16 million, mostly German |
| Government | Absolutism under Bach, no parliament | Imposed constitution of 1848–50 with a Landtag; the New Era from 1858 |
| Economy | Growing, but behind Prussia in coal, iron and railways; high tariffs; outside the Zollverein | Growing fast; the centre of the Zollverein; coal, iron and railways |
| Finances | Heavy debts and deficits; Crimean and Italian wars | Sound; neutrality in the Crimean War |
| Diplomacy | Victorious at Olmütz (1850) but isolated after the Crimean War | Humiliated at Olmütz but neutral and free to choose its friends |

### Bismarck's role up to 1859

**Otto von Bismarck** was born in 1815 into a Junker family of Brandenburg. In 1848 he was a conservative royalist who opposed the revolution and the Frankfurt crown. In December 1850 he defended Manteuffel's retreat at Olmütz in the Prussian parliament, saying that a great state should act from its own interests and not fight for causes that were not its own. That won him the king's attention. In 1851 Frederick William IV sent him to Frankfurt as Prussia's envoy to the Federal Diet, where he stayed until 1859.

At first Bismarck expected cooperation with Austria on conservative lines. He soon changed his mind. He found that Austria treated Prussia as a junior partner at the Diet: according to his memoirs, in 1851 the Austrian envoy was the only delegate who smoked, and Bismarck lit a cigar as a sign of equality (a story that you should treat as his own later account). More seriously, he saw that Austria would use the Diet and the Zollverein question to block Prussia. During the Crimean War he argued against supporting Austria. In a letter to Manteuffel in April 1856 he wrote that Germany was becoming too small for the two powers, who were ploughing the same disputed field. In 1859 he urged Prussia to take advantage of Austria's war in Italy, though the government did not act. He was moved to St Petersburg as ambassador in 1859, which took him away from Frankfurt but gave him knowledge of Russia.

:::key Bismarck's attitude towards Austria, 1851–59
1. **From cooperation to hostility**: he began as a conservative who respected Austria, and learned in Frankfurt that Austria would not treat Prussia as an equal.
2. **Prussian interest first**: he believed that Prussia must act in its own interest, not for Austria, the German cause or an ideology.
3. **Austria as the obstacle**: by the mid-1850s he saw that Germany could not hold both powers, and that sooner or later Prussia would have to confront Austria.
4. **Realistic and flexible**: he would later use liberal nationalism for his own ends. He did not yet have the power to act.
:::

:::key Why relations between Austria and Prussia changed
1. **Politics**: Olmütz made Prussians resent Austria, and the revival of liberal and national opinion (Nationalverein, the New Era) turned towards Prussia.
2. **Economy**: Prussia's growth and the Zollverein gave it strength that Austria, handicapped by debt, could not match.
3. **Diplomacy**: Austria's isolation after the Crimean War and the loss of Lombardy in 1859 left it weaker, while Prussia kept its freedom of action.
4. **People**: Bismarck's view of Austria hardened in Frankfurt, preparing him for the policy of the 1860s.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the Frankfurt Parliament failed.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The Frankfurt Parliament met in 1848 in St Paul's Church. It elected Archduke John as Regent and wrote the Basic Rights. It passed a constitution and offered the crown to Frederick William IV in 1849. He refused it and the Parliament was dispersed at Stuttgart in June 1849."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it is a narrative of what happened. No sentence says why. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was that the Parliament had no power. It had no army, so it depended on the troops of the states, which swore loyalty to their own rulers and not to the Regent. This was shown in the Schleswig-Holstein crisis, when Prussia signed the Malmö armistice (26 August 1848) without consulting Frankfurt and the deputies had no choice but to accept, and again in September 1848, when the Parliament had to call in Prussian and Austrian troops to stop a riot in its own city. A second reason was that the deputies were divided. Grossdeutsch deputies wanted Austria to be included, kleindeutsch deputies wanted a Prussian emperor, and moderates and radicals disagreed about how far to go. Austria would not divide its empire, so the grossdeutsch solution was impossible, while the kleindeutsch solution was passed only narrowly, by 267 votes to 263 on the hereditary emperor."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how the three work together:

"A third reason was the king's refusal. When the Parliament offered Frederick William IV the imperial crown on 28 March 1849, he told the deputation on 3 April that he could not accept without the agreement of the princes, and in private he scorned a crown offered by an assembly. Without a ruler to accept it, the constitution had no force.

These factors were linked. The Parliament's lack of power meant that it could achieve nothing against the wishes of Austria and Prussia. Its divisions made it slow, and the time it spent debating rights and the German question during the autumn and winter of 1848 gave the old rulers time to recover: Windischgrätz took Vienna in October and Prussian troops entered Berlin in November. By March 1849 the king of Prussia no longer needed the Parliament's crown, and he could afford to refuse it. The decisive factor was therefore the Parliament's lack of an independent power base, because both the divisions and the refusal were possible only because the princes still controlled the armies. The king's refusal was the immediate cause, but the Parliament was doomed before it was made."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (power, time and the king's choice), and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

Before the law there is no distinction of estates. The nobility as an estate is abolished. All privileges of estate are abolished. All Germans are equal before the law. All public offices are open to all who are qualified.

*Adapted from section 7 of the law on the Basic Rights of the German People, published by the Frankfurt Parliament, 27 December 1848 (section 137 of the constitution of 28 March 1849).*

**Source B**

The citizens entitled to vote shall be divided, according to the amount of direct tax they pay, into three classes, so that each class pays one third of the tax of the district. Each class shall elect one third of the electors, who shall then choose the deputies. The vote shall be given openly.

*Adapted from the decree of King Frederick William IV of Prussia on the election of the second chamber, 30 May 1849.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about attitudes to equality during the revolutions of 1848–49.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that all Germans are equal before the law and the nobility is abolished. Source B divides voters into three classes by tax. So Source A is about equality and Source B is not. They are from different dates, so they are different."

*Commentary.* One clear difference, supported, but no similarity and no explanation of why they differ. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share some ground: both are texts of authority that set out rules for the whole of a state or nation after the revolutions of March 1848, and both accept a legal and representative framework. Source B provides for elections and a chamber of deputies, which the king had not allowed before 1848, and Source A assumes a state of law in which offices are open to talent. Neither is an absolutist document.

On equality, however, they are far apart. Source A abolishes the nobility 'as an estate', ends 'all privileges of estate' and declares that 'all Germans are equal before the law'. Source B builds inequality into the vote: by dividing voters according to the tax they pay into three classes that each choose a third of the electors, it makes a small number of rich men as powerful as a large number of poor ones. The requirement that the vote be given 'openly' also lets employers and landlords see how people voted. Source A treats equality as a principle, Source B treats political weight as a reward for wealth.

The context explains the contrast. Source A was passed by a Parliament elected by a wide male franchise in the revolution's high point, and dominated by liberal lawyers, professors and officials who wanted the abolition of noble privilege and legal equality. It is a statement of what they hoped Germany would be. Source B was issued by the king by decree in May 1849, after he had dissolved the Prussian Assembly (5 December 1848), refused the Frankfurt crown (3 April 1849) and when his army was crushing the May risings. Its purpose was to ensure that property and the conservatives would control the Prussian Landtag. The sources are also different in what they can tell us: Source A was never applied, since several states refused to publish the Basic Rights, and it was annulled in 1851. Source B was applied and lasted until 1918. Source A is better evidence of what the revolutionaries wanted, while Source B is better evidence of what the revolution actually left behind."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the date and author of each) and through the purpose of each source; a final comparison of the sources' value. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: To what extent was the humiliation of Olmütz a victory for Austria?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: Prussia dropped the Union, demobilised, left Hesse-Kassel to Austrian and Bavarian troops, accepted the restored Diet; Russia backed Austria; Prussian opinion called it a humiliation.
- Against 1, what Austria failed to achieve: the Dresden plan to bring the whole empire into the Confederation (about 70 million people) collapsed in May 1851.
- Against 2, long-term position: Austria depended on Russia; the Zollverein was renewed in 1853 under Prussia, with Austria receiving only a trade treaty.
- Against 3, Prussia's gain: it kept its army, its place in the Diet and its economic leadership; Manteuffel and Bismarck presented the retreat as prudent.
- Judgement: a real victory in the crisis, but a limited and temporary one. Criterion: what did each power gain or lose in the long term?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The humiliation of Olmütz was a victory for Austria. Prussia had put forward the Erfurt Union, which would have created a German state led by Prussia, and Schwarzenberg was determined to stop it. In November 1850 Prussia mobilised but, when the Tsar supported Austria, it gave way. At Olmütz on 29 November 1850 Manteuffel agreed to demobilise, to give up the Union and to withdraw from Hesse-Kassel so that Austrian and Bavarian troops could restore the Elector. The old Federal Diet was restored in 1851. So Austria had defeated Prussia and had again become the leading power in Germany."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but it explains only the case for. The final sentence asserts a lasting Austrian leadership without testing it. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with the other side merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet Austria's victory was limited. Schwarzenberg wanted more than the defeat of the Union: his aim at the Dresden Conferences of 1850–51 was to bring the whole Habsburg Empire into the Confederation, creating a block of some 70 million people under Austrian leadership, with a customs union to follow. Prussia, many of the middle states, Britain and France opposed this, and the conferences ended in May 1851 with the old Federal Diet restored as it was. Austria had won back the position of 1847, but not gained the dominance it sought. The settlement also depended on Russia. Austria had needed Russian troops to defeat Hungary in 1849 and Russian diplomacy in 1850, and a victory that relies on a foreign protector is weaker than it looks.

Within a few years the economic balance moved against Austria. In 1851–53 Prussia isolated the middle states that wanted to bring Austria into the Zollverein, and the Zollverein was renewed in 1853 for twelve years, with Austria offered a trade treaty and nothing more. Prussia's coal and iron industries and railways grew fast in the 1850s, while Austria faced debts and deficits, which the Crimean War, where it mobilised without gaining anything, made worse. Prussia, though humiliated, kept its army, its equal vote in the Diet and its leadership of the Zollverein. Bismarck, who defended the retreat in December 1850 and went to Frankfurt in 1851, learned there that Austria would use the Diet against Prussia, and drew the lesson that Prussia should avoid fighting before she was ready.

Overall, Olmütz was a victory for Austria in the narrow sense that it settled the crisis of 1850 on Austrian terms. But it was not a victory in the sense that mattered, which was lasting leadership of Germany. Austria could not follow it up at Dresden or in the Zollverein, and its financial and diplomatic position grew worse. The statement is therefore true of the event, but misleading as a description of its consequences: Olmütz delayed the contest between Austria and Prussia rather than deciding it."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (immediate settlement against long-term leadership). With an opening paragraph on the case for, this is Level 5 (19–20).

**Source A**

I have today taken the old German colours and placed myself and my people under the venerable banner of the German Reich. Prussia is henceforth merged in Germany.

*Adapted from the proclamation "To my people and to the German nation", issued by King Frederick William IV of Prussia, Berlin, 21 March 1848.*

**Source B**

A crown that an assembly makes is a circlet baked of dirt and clay. The only crown a Hohenzollern may wear is one that God gives, and the princes, not the people, must bring it to him.

*A close paraphrase of a letter of King Frederick William IV of Prussia to Christian von Bunsen, 13 December 1848. The words "dirt and clay" are the king's own.*

**Source C**

I am honoured by the trust of the Assembly, but I cannot accept the crown without the free agreement of the crowned heads and free cities of Germany. I shall take no decision until the governments have decided whether the constitution is acceptable to them.

*Adapted from the reply of King Frederick William IV to the deputation of the Frankfurt Parliament, Berlin, 3 April 1849.*

**Source D**

Prussia will demobilise its army, and Austria will do the same. Prussia will withdraw its troops from Hesse-Kassel, except from the military roads, and leave the restoration of order there to Austria and Bavaria. The two governments will send delegates to conferences at Dresden to discuss the reform of the German Confederation.

*Adapted summary of the Punctation of Olmütz, agreed between Prussia and Austria, 29 November 1850.*

**Question.** How far do these sources show that Frederick William IV supported the cause of German unity?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (Prussia "merged in Germany", German colours). Qualify: B (unity, but only by the princes' gift), C (a conditional answer that does not close the door). Challenge: D (he gave up the Union and accepted Austrian leadership of the Confederation), and B and C (he refused the crown).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a public proclamation, issued two days after barricade fighting in Berlin (18–19 March) had killed 200 to 300 people, after the king had pulled his army out of the city and while his brother had left the country. Its purpose was to calm Berlin and win the liberals. That makes it strong evidence of how weak the king felt in March, but weak evidence of what he believed: he soon said he had been forced, and Prussia was not "merged" into anything.
- **B**: a private letter to a diplomat who was sympathetic to German unity, so more candid than a public speech; it shows the king's real objection, which was to a crown given by the people, not to unity as such. But it was written before the offer, and a letter written to impress or tease Bunsen can overstate. Strong evidence of his principles, but we cannot tell how sincere his phrase was.
- **C**: a formal reply to a deputation, in the language of diplomacy. It does not say no; it hides the refusal behind the princes' consent, which suited a king who wanted to avoid blame. Good evidence of what he chose to say in public, less good for his private intentions, which B shows.
- **D**: an agreement between two governments under pressure. Its purpose was to avoid war, and it was signed by his minister Manteuffel, not by the king himself. It shows what Prussia had to accept in November 1850, not what the king wished, and it needs context: he had supported the Erfurt Union in 1849–50 and had backed Radowitz, which shows a different attitude.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Source A shows the king publicly embracing unity in March 1848, but the context weakens it: it was issued under the pressure of the Berlin barricades, and the king later said he had been forced. Sources B and C show a more consistent position: in private (B) and in public (C) he rejected a crown given by an assembly, which he saw as the child of revolution, and he insisted that only the princes could offer it. Source D shows him, through Manteuffel, abandoning the Prussian Union and accepting Austrian leadership in 1850. These sources suggest that he supported German unity only on his own terms: unity by the princes and not by the people, and with Prussia in the lead. My own knowledge supports this, for he sponsored Radowitz's Union in 1849–50, which was a plan for unity under Prussia with a three-class vote and strong princes, and then dropped it when Austria and Russia opposed it. The sources therefore show conditional rather than sincere support: he favoured a Germany of the princes and was hostile to a Germany of the people. They agree only to a limited extent, because the clearest statement of support (A) is the one least reliable as evidence of belief."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1848–50; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Why did the Frankfurt Parliament fail?" [10]

A student wrote:

"The Frankfurt Parliament opened on 18 May 1848 in St Paul's Church and Heinrich von Gagern was its president. It elected the Archduke John as Regent in June 1848. It argued for a long time about Schleswig-Holstein and about whether to have a grossdeutsch or kleindeutsch Germany. In December 1848 it published the Basic Rights and in March 1849 it passed a constitution. The king of Prussia was offered the crown but he refused it in April 1849. Then the Parliament was closed. This was why it failed."

**The level.** Accurate detail in the right order, but it is **narrative**: it tells what happened and ends with a sentence that asserts "this was why it failed" without explaining. It reaches Level 2 (3–5): "addresses causation but may be entirely descriptive".

**The fix.** Turn each event into a reason and connect them. "The Frankfurt Parliament failed above all because it had no power of its own. It had no army, so when Prussia made peace with Denmark at Malmö in August 1848 without consulting it, it could do nothing, and when rioters attacked it in September it had to ask Prussian and Austrian troops for help. Because it was dependent on the states, its fate depended on the two great powers, and when the conservatives recovered in Vienna and Berlin in the autumn of 1848 it had nothing to stand on. The king's refusal in April 1849 only revealed this weakness." Now each fact is evidence for a reason, and the reasons are linked.

**Source A**

I am a Bohemian of Slav descent. I cannot take part in the work of preparing a German parliament, for a united Germany would weaken Austria. Truly, if the Austrian state had not existed for ages, it would be necessary, in the interest of Europe and of humanity itself, to hurry to create it. It protects the smaller peoples of Central Europe from Russia.

*Adapted from the open letter of the Czech historian František Palacký declining an invitation from the Committee of Fifty, set up by the Frankfurt Pre-Parliament, Prague, 11 April 1848. The sentences on Austria are close to his words; the rest summarises his argument.*

**Source B**

I take a bold step, and I tell you: we must create the provisional central power ourselves. Without a central authority the Parliament cannot act, and the states cannot be brought together.

*Adapted from a speech by Heinrich von Gagern, President of the Frankfurt Parliament, 24 June 1848. The first sentence is his famous "bold step" (kühner Griff); the second summarises his argument.*

**Source C**

The continued existence of Austria as a united state is a necessity for Germany and for Europe. The empire will not be broken up. How its relation to the German Reich will be arranged cannot yet be settled.

*A close paraphrase of the declaration of the Austrian prime minister, Prince Felix zu Schwarzenberg, to the Austrian parliament at Kremsier, 27 November 1848.*

**Source D**

The only sound basis of a great state, and this is what distinguishes it from a small state, is state egoism and not romanticism. It is unworthy of a great state to fight for a cause that is not in its own interest.

*Adapted from the speech of Otto von Bismarck, a conservative deputy, defending the agreement at Olmütz in the Prussian parliament, 3 December 1850.*

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Metternich fell from power in March 1848.
A: Indicative content: the news of the French revolution (24 February) showed that a government could fall; liberals and students in Vienna demanded a constitution and an end to censorship; the economic crisis of 1846–47 had left workers in the suburbs hungry and angry; on 13 March the troops fired on demonstrators and the workers joined; the court and the emperor lacked the will or the unity to fight, and Metternich was blamed as the symbol of the system. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: long-term hatred of the system, the economic crisis and the example of Paris produced the crowd, and the court's panic made him a scapegoat. It ends with a judgement on the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Frederick William IV refused the imperial crown in April 1849.
A: Indicative content: he believed in monarchy by divine right and thought that an assembly elected by the people could not give a crown; he feared that accepting it would tie him to the revolution (his "dirt and clay" remark); the princes had not agreed, and Austria and Russia would have opposed it; he had already regained control of Prussia by the army and the November 1848 coup, so he no longer needed to appease the liberals. Level 4: connect his principles with the changed balance of power: by April 1849 he could afford to refuse.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Frankfurt Parliament failed because it lacked an army.' How far do you agree?
A: For: no army or money of its own; dependent on the states' troops (Schleswig-Holstein and Malmö, the Frankfurt riots of September 1848); the princes' armies later dispersed the rump and crushed the May risings. Against: the Parliament's divisions (grossdeutsch/kleindeutsch, moderates/radicals); its slowness; the recovery of the conservatives in Austria and Prussia; the king's refusal of the crown; lack of attention to the social questions of artisans and peasants. Level 5 uses a criterion, such as "what made the Parliament unable to turn a vote into reality", and judges, for example, that the lack of force was the underlying cause, because an army would have enabled it to override its divisions, while the king's refusal was the event that exposed it.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far was the strength of the conservatives the main reason for the failure of the 1848 revolutions in Germany?
A: For: the army stayed loyal in Austria and Prussia (Windischgrätz at Prague and Vienna, Wrangel in Berlin); the court and Schwarzenberg; the nobility; Russian help against Hungary; the princes' ability to reverse concessions. Against: the revolutionaries' divisions (liberals against radicals, middle class against workers, nationalities against one another, grossdeutsch against kleindeutsch); the Parliament's lack of power; the satisfaction of the peasants after feudal dues ended; the middle class's fear of social revolution. Level 5 judges, for example, that the conservatives' strength was decisive in the end because the army ended every revolution, but that it was the revolutionaries' divisions that gave the conservatives time and the chance to use it.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and C (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about the future of the Austrian Empire in 1848.
A: Similarities: both argue that Austria must remain a united state and not be divided; both present this as a European necessity and not only an Austrian one; both reject the idea of dissolving it into German and non-German parts. Differences: A is a Czech liberal declining to join Germany, who defends Austria as a protector of the small Slav peoples against Russia; C is the government of the Habsburgs asserting that the empire will not be broken up, in a way that leaves open its relation to Germany; A speaks for a people, C for a state; A is from April 1848 when the empire seemed ready to fall, C from November, after Vienna had been retaken. Explanation and provenance: A is an open letter by a historian and nationalist leader who wanted a federal Austria; C is the declaration of a conservative minister who wanted a centralised one. Level 4 explains the differences through these contexts and judges which is better evidence of the real position.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources support the view that the failure of the 1848 revolutions in Germany was caused by the strength of the conservatives?
H: Decide which sources show conservative strength (C, D) and which show revolutionary weakness or division (A, B).
H: Use your own knowledge to judge Source B: was the "bold step" of a central power enough without an army?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose before deciding how much weight it carries.
A: Support: C shows a confident Austrian government that will not divide its empire, weeks after retaking Vienna; D shows a Prussian conservative arguing from state interest, after the retreat at Olmütz. Challenge or qualify: A shows that the revolutionaries' own nationalities were divided, since a Czech leader refused to join the German movement; B shows the Parliament creating a government without any force to support it, which supports the argument of the Parliament's weakness. Evaluation: A is the open letter of one man, but it expresses a widely shared Czech view; B is a speech meant to persuade the deputies, so it overstates; C is a political declaration that served to reassure the army and the court; D was given to defend a policy in a parliament, so it justifies a retreat. Level 5 judges how far: the conservatives' strength was real and decisive (C, D), but the sources show the revolutionaries' divisions and lack of power (A, B) as equally important.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Economic growth was the main reason why Prussia became stronger than Austria in the 1850s.' How far do you agree?
H: Set a criterion: stronger in what sense (economy, army, diplomacy, finances)?
H: Compare the effects of the Zollverein renewal, the Crimean War and the war of 1859 with the growth of coal, iron and railways.
A: For: Prussian coal output more than doubled in the 1850s; railways, iron and banks grew; the Zollverein gave revenue and influence; the 1853 renewal showed the middle states needed Prussia's market. Against: Austria's weakness came as much from debt, the cost of the Crimean War and isolation, and the defeat of 1859; Prussian neutrality kept its finances sound; the growth of liberal and national opinion turned to Prussia (Nationalverein, New Era); Bismarck's view of Austria. Level 5 uses the criterion, for example "what changed the balance between the two", and judges that economic growth was the foundation, but diplomatic decisions (neutrality) and Austria's own errors decided how far it mattered.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ A. J. P. Taylor argued that 1848 was a turning point at which German history failed to turn. How far does the evidence of 1848–59 support this view?
H: What would "turning" have meant? A liberal, constitutional Germany? Which changes did take place?
H: Consider the Basic Rights, the Prussian constitution, the end of feudal dues, Realpolitik and Bismarck.
A: Support: the revolutions failed, the Parliament was dispersed, liberals were exiled or turned to power politics, Prussia imposed a three-class vote and Austria returned to absolutism. Challenge: lasting changes did occur (constitutions in Prussia and other states, the end of feudal dues, political parties and associations, the Basic Rights as a model); unification came in 1871 in a form built on a constitution and parliament; the revolutions' failure was not certain (the Parliament did produce a majority for a kleindeutsch constitution). A* judgement: 1848 changed Germany less than the revolutionaries hoped, but the idea of a failed turning ignores the changes below the surface and the fact that a liberal Germany was not a realistic option without the armies of Austria and Prussia.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ The king's private letters are sometimes used as better evidence than his public statements for his attitude to German unity. How far is this true of Frederick William IV in 1848–50?
H: Compare the proclamation of 21 March 1848 with the letter to Bunsen in December 1848.
H: What does each source's purpose do to what it says?
A: Strengths of private letters: not meant to persuade; show his principles (divine right, the princes' gift of a crown). Weaknesses: letters may be written to impress, tease or reassure the recipient (Bunsen was a liberal sympathiser); they are selective survivals; he changed his mind by mood. Public statements: show what he thought necessary to say and how weak he felt (21 March under the barricades, 3 April 1849 with the door left open); they were followed by action (the Erfurt Union) that shows his aims. Judgement: private letters are better for his principles, public statements and actions for his policy; the best evidence compares the two.
:::

:::recap
- **Spread of revolution**: Paris (24 February 1848), Mannheim (27 February), March ministries in the south-west and middle states; Metternich resigned on 13 March; Berlin barricades 18–19 March; Frederick William IV withdrew his troops and wore the German colours on 21 March.
- **Frederick William IV**: yielded under pressure, never accepted popular sovereignty, regained control with the army (November–December 1848), imposed a constitution (5 December 1848) and the three-class franchise (30 May 1849).
- **Frankfurt Parliament**: opened 18 May 1848; Archduke John Regent (29 June); no army or money; Malmö (26 August) and the riots of 18 September; Basic Rights (27 December); constitution passed 27–28 March 1849 (267 to 263 for a hereditary emperor; 290 votes for Frederick William IV).
- **Collapse**: the king refused on 3 April 1849; Prussia, Austria and others rejected the constitution; rump dispersed at Stuttgart on 18 June 1849; May risings crushed (Rastatt, 23 July).
- **Why the conservatives won**: loyal armies (Radetzky, Windischgrätz, Wrangel), the court and nobility, Russian help in Hungary (Világos, 13 August 1849), contented peasants. **Why the revolutionaries lost**: liberals against radicals, middle class against artisans, nationalities, grossdeutsch against kleindeutsch.
- **Impact**: constitutions, the end of feudal dues, political experience and Realpolitik, but no unity or lasting liberal government.
- **Austria and Prussia**: the Erfurt Union, Hesse-Kassel and Olmütz (29 November 1850); the Dresden Conferences failed (May 1851); the Zollverein renewed in 1853 without Austria; Prussian growth; Austrian finances and the Crimean War; the Nationalverein (1859); Bismarck at Frankfurt (1851–59).
- **Judgement**: Olmütz was an Austrian victory in the crisis, but not in the long run.
:::
