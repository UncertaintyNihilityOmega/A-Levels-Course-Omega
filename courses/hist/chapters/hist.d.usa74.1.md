:::why
In the summer of 1974 a President resigned in disgrace, the economy was shrinking while prices rose, and drivers queued at petrol stations. Over the next six years Americans lived through the worst peacetime inflation in their history, a second oil shock, a nuclear accident at Three Mile Island and the seizure of their embassy in Tehran. In 1980 they chose a 69-year-old former actor who told them that government was the problem. Paper 4 asks you to judge how serious the problems of the late 1970s really were and why they changed American politics. To do that you need the story in order, and a way of weighing problems that were partly bad luck, partly the fault of leaders and partly the result of changes that no President could control.
:::

## What is the question really asking?

The key question asks how **significant** the problems of the late 1970s were. "Significant" can mean serious at the time, or important because of what followed. A strong answer uses both meanings, and tests every problem by four questions:
1. **How deep was it?** Did it hurt many Americans, or only some?
2. **How long did it last?** A one-year shock matters less than a decade of decline.
3. **Whose fault was it?** The President's decisions, Congress, the world economy, or long-term change.
4. **What did it change?** Above all, how far did it help to bring Reagan and the New Right to power in 1980?

Keep these four tests in mind. The chapter tells the story in order, then comes back to them in the section "How significant were the problems?"

## The end of the long boom: stagflation and the oil shocks

### Why the economy broke down

From 1945 to about 1973 the US economy grew almost without a break. Wages rose with productivity, unemployment was usually between 4 and 6 per cent, and the government used what economists call **Keynesian** policy, adjusting taxes and spending to keep the economy steady. Most economists believed that there was a trade-off: higher unemployment meant lower inflation and the reverse. In the 1970s both rose together.

:::def Stagflation
A combination of stagnation (slow or falling growth and high unemployment) and high inflation at the same time, which Keynesian economics could not easily explain or cure, because the usual remedy for one made the other worse.
:::

Several causes came together:
- **The cost of the 1960s.** Johnson paid for the Vietnam War and the Great Society without raising taxes enough, and the extra demand pushed up prices.
- **The end of the old money system.** On 15 August 1971 Nixon ended the dollar's convertibility into gold and imposed temporary wage and price controls. The dollar fell, import prices rose, and when the controls were lifted in 1973–74 prices jumped.
- **Foreign competition and slow productivity.** West Germany and Japan now sold cars, steel and electronics in American markets, and output per worker grew more slowly than before. Companies that could not raise productivity passed higher wage costs on in prices, and union contracts often tied pay to prices, so inflation fed itself.
- **The oil shocks.** The US had become a large importer of oil: roughly a third of the oil it used in 1973 came from abroad. After the Yom Kippur War began on 6 October 1973, the Arab members of the oil producers' organisation OAPEC (the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries, oil-exporting states acting together) cut supplies to countries that had backed Israel, including the US, and the embargo lasted until March 1974. The world price of oil roughly quadrupled. Drivers queued at petrol stations, Nixon signed a national 55 mph speed limit in January 1974, and the cost of everything that used energy rose.
- **A second shock.** The Iranian Revolution in 1978–79 cut Iranian oil exports. OPEC (the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, the wider group of oil exporters) raised prices again, and by 1980 the price of crude had more than doubled. Petrol lines returned in the summer of 1979.

The recession that followed the first shock lasted from November 1973 to March 1975, the longest since the 1930s, and real output fell in both 1974 and 1975. The figures below, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, show what ordinary Americans lived with.

:::def Misery index
The unemployment rate added to the inflation rate, devised by the economist Arthur Okun as a rough measure of economic hardship. Carter used it against Ford in 1976 and Reagan used it against Carter in 1980. It treats a point of unemployment and a point of inflation as equally painful, which is a simplification.
:::

Read the table with care. Inflation was above 11 per cent in 1974 and again in 1979–80, and the misery index peaked at 17.6 in 1975 and 20.6 in 1980 (calculated from annual averages). In between, the economy recovered: output grew by about 5 per cent a year from 1976 to 1978 and unemployment fell from 8.5 to 5.8 per cent by 1979. That matters for the question, because the late 1970s were not one long collapse. Carter's economy was strong in some ways, and it was inflation and energy, not mass unemployment, that destroyed his reputation.

:::warn Do not write "the 1970s were a decade of recession"
The recession of 1973–75 was deep, and there was another short one in January–July 1980, but 1976–79 saw growth and falling unemployment. Say instead that the 1970s were a decade of **inflation and insecurity**, in which growth was slower and prices were far less predictable than before. Precise claims, with years and figures, earn Table B marks.
:::

### Why the economy matters to the whole chapter

Stagflation did three things that run through the rest of the chapter. It destroyed the confidence of Keynesian economists, because their policies did not seem to work. It squeezed families: real incomes stopped rising while prices and, through "bracket creep" (inflation pushing people into higher tax brackets), taxes rose. And it made voters ready to try a different idea. Each President of these years, Ford, Carter and then Reagan, had to answer one question: what do you do about inflation and unemployment together?

## Ford's presidency, 1974–77

### An accidental President

**Gerald Ford**, a Michigan Republican and the House Minority Leader, had become Vice President in December 1973 when Spiro Agnew resigned over corruption charges; Nixon chose him and Congress confirmed him under the Twenty-fifth Amendment (the rule that the President nominates a new Vice-President when the post falls vacant, and Congress must approve). On 9 August 1974 he took the oath as the first President who had been elected neither President nor Vice President. His first words to the country set the tone: "Our long national nightmare is over."

He was decent, open and well liked in Congress, and for a few weeks the country welcomed a change from the secrecy of the Nixon years. Then he made the decision that defined his presidency.

### The pardon of Nixon

On **8 September 1974** Ford issued a "full, free, and absolute pardon" to Nixon for any offences he had committed or might have committed against the United States between 20 January 1969 and 9 August 1974. Nixon had not been charged. Ford explained that the country needed to put Watergate behind it, and that a trial would divide the nation for years.

The reaction was fierce. Critics said that the pardon put Nixon above the law and suggested a secret bargain, that Nixon had given up the presidency in return for it. Investigators and historians have found no evidence of such a deal, but the suspicion stuck. His press secretary, Jerald terHorst, resigned in protest. Gallup found Ford's approval rating falling from 71 per cent in mid-August 1974 to 50 per cent by late September, and it was 42 per cent at the end of the year. Later opinion softened: by the 1980s most Americans told pollsters that the pardon had been right.

Ford tried to balance it on 16 September 1974 with a limited clemency programme for Vietnam draft evaders and deserters, who could earn it by two years of alternative service. The programme won few takers and pleased neither side.

:::key The significance of the pardon
1. **It cost Ford public trust at the moment he most needed it.** He had promised openness, and for many it now looked like a deal.
2. **It helped the Democrats.** In the midterm elections of 5 November 1974 they gained 49 seats in the House (to about two-thirds of its members) and 75 new Democrats, the "Watergate babies", entered Congress, many of them elected as outsiders who owed nothing to party leaders.
3. **It helped Carter later.** In 1976 Carter's promise never to lie to the people drew directly on the distrust of Washington that Watergate and the pardon had created.
4. **Its effect should not be overstated.** Ford's economic record and his narrow defeat in 1976 had other causes too, and most Americans came to accept the pardon.
:::

### Stagflation: Ford's economic record

Ford inherited an economy sliding into recession with inflation at about 11 per cent in 1974. His response changed course with the facts.

- **"Whip Inflation Now."** On 8 October 1974 he asked Congress and the nation for voluntary restraint: save, cut waste, plant a vegetable garden. Supporters wore WIN buttons. It was a campaign of slogans, backed by a proposed 5 per cent surtax (an extra tax added on top of the normal tax) on higher incomes, and it proved a joke: it could not touch the oil shock or the wage-price spiral, and by the end of 1974 the main danger was recession and not inflation.
- **A tax cut.** In January 1975, with unemployment rising, Ford reversed himself and proposed a tax cut. Congress went further, and the **Tax Reduction Act** he signed on 29 March 1975 cut taxes and sent out rebates worth more than 20 billion dollars. Unemployment peaked at about 9 per cent in May 1975 (its annual average was 8.5 per cent), and the recession ended in March 1975.
- **Fiscal policy.** Ford was a fiscal conservative. He used the veto against spending bills he thought inflationary: he cast 66 vetoes in his 29 months in office, and Congress overrode 12 of them. Deficits rose all the same, to about 74 billion dollars in 1976, because the recession cut tax revenue and the 1975 tax cut cost money. The Democratic Congress, in turn, passed the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, creating its own Congressional Budget Office, so that it no longer had to rely on the President's figures.
- **Result.** By the election year inflation had fallen to under 6 per cent and growth had returned, but unemployment was still 7.7 per cent in 1976. Ford got no credit for the recovery.

### Energy and the cities

**Energy.** Ford wanted to end price controls on oil so that higher prices would cut demand and increase supply. The Democratic Congress wanted to protect consumers. The compromise was the **Energy Policy and Conservation Act**, which he signed on 22 December 1975. It required car makers to raise average fuel economy to 27.5 miles per gallon by 1985, created a **Strategic Petroleum Reserve** of oil stored against another embargo, and phased price controls out slowly. It was the first national fuel-economy law, but it did not cut America's dependence on imported oil, which rose in the later 1970s.

**New York City.** By 1975 New York City could not pay its debts. On 29 October 1975 Ford announced that he would veto any bill to bail it out, and the next day's Daily News headline read "Ford to City: Drop Dead". He never said those words, but the headline became the symbol of his coldness to the cities, and it probably cost him votes in New York in 1976. When the threat of default came closer, Ford agreed to seasonal federal loans, which Congress approved and he signed on 9 December 1975. The city repaid them in full. The affair showed a President defending balanced budgets while a major city faced collapse, and it showed how far public finance had become a political issue.

### Social divisions: the busing crisis

Ford also faced the legacy of the civil rights era. In 1974 a federal judge, W. Arthur Garrity, ruled in *Morgan v. Hennigan* (21 June) that the Boston School Committee had deliberately kept Black and white pupils in separate schools, and ordered that pupils be bused between neighbourhoods. The first phase began in September 1974. In South Boston, a working-class Irish-American district, whose pupils were sent to the Black neighbourhood of Roxbury and the reverse, white parents boycotted schools and crowds stoned buses; the violence returned through 1975–76. In April 1976 a photograph, "The Soiling of Old Glory", showed a white teenager attacking a Black lawyer with a flagpole outside Boston City Hall and became an image of the crisis. Louisville, Kentucky, had its own busing protests in 1975.

The Supreme Court's decision in *Milliken v. Bradley* (25 July 1974), by five votes to four, limited the remedy: a federal court could not order pupils bused across the boundary between a city and its suburbs unless the suburbs had themselves been shown to discriminate. That protected the suburbs, and it encouraged white families to move out of the cities.

Ford said that he disagreed with the remedy of busing but that the courts' orders had to be obeyed, a position that satisfied few. The politics were hard: opposition to busing was strong among white voters in the North and South alike, including many Democrats, but it was also clear that the law, after *Brown v. Board of Education*, required desegregation. Busing split the old Democratic coalition of Southern whites, Northern white workers and Black Americans, and it became one of the issues that moved white working-class voters towards the Republicans and the New Right.

### Distrust of politics after Watergate

The loss of trust ran deeper than the pardon. The American National Election Studies found that the share of Americans who trusted the federal government to do the right thing just about always or most of the time fell from 77 per cent in 1964 to 36 per cent in 1974 and 25 per cent in 1980, before recovering to 44 per cent in 1984. Vietnam, the Pentagon Papers and Watergate had all damaged faith in government, and in 1975 the investigations made it worse.

- The **Church Committee**, a Senate select committee chaired by Frank Church of Idaho, investigated the intelligence agencies from January 1975. It exposed CIA plots to assassinate foreign leaders, the FBI's campaign against Martin Luther King and other critics, and the illegal opening of mail. Ford set up the Rockefeller Commission on the CIA, and in 1976–77 the Senate and House created permanent intelligence committees.
- Congress passed laws to tighten control of the executive: the **War Powers Resolution** (1973, over Nixon's veto), the amendments to the **Freedom of Information Act** (1974, over Ford's veto), the **Privacy Act** (1974) and the **Federal Election Campaign Act** amendments (1974), which limited donations and created the Federal Election Commission.
- The balance of power had shifted. The "imperial presidency" that Johnson and Nixon had built was over, and a President now faced a Congress that was assertive, independent and suspicious. Carter inherited that Congress, and it helps to explain why he found it so hard to pass his plans.

:::warn Do not confuse Ford's two kinds of problem
Ford's problems were of three kinds that need separating: **inherited** ones (the oil shock, the recession, the distrust left by Watergate), **forced** ones (New York City, Boston) and **self-made** ones (the pardon, WIN). A strong answer says which kind each was, because "significant" for a President means "how much was his fault".
:::

### The election of 1976

Ford had to fight his own party first. **Ronald Reagan**, the former Governor of California, challenged him from the right, attacking détente (the easing of tension with the USSR) and the Panama Canal talks (negotiations to hand the canal to Panama, which ended in Carter's treaties; see [Carter's foreign policies, 1977–81](#hist.d.usa74.3)), and won several primaries, but Ford took the nomination at Kansas City in August 1976 by 1,187 delegates to 1,070. He chose Senator Bob Dole as his running mate. He narrowed a large Democratic lead in the polls, but in the second television debate on 6 October he said that there was "no Soviet domination of Eastern Europe", and when pressed named Poland as free of it (the Poland gaffe), a slip that he could not clear up for days.

:::key What the Ford years show about the problems of the 1970s
1. The economy had broken the old rules, and Ford, like his successor, could find no policy that cured inflation and unemployment together.
2. The pardon and the revelations of 1975 deepened the distrust of Washington that Watergate began.
3. Busing, the cities and energy showed that social and economic problems now produced political divisions inside both parties.
4. Ford was beaten narrowly, 48.0 per cent to 50.1 per cent, so the voters' dissatisfaction was real but not yet a revolt. Most of the pressure for change was still to come.
:::

## Carter's victory in 1976

### Why Carter won

**James Earl "Jimmy" Carter**, a Georgia peanut farmer, former naval officer and one-term Governor of Georgia (1971–75), was almost unknown nationally in 1975. He won the Democratic nomination, and then the presidency, because of who he was and because of what the voters were tired of.

:::key Reasons for Carter's victory in 1976
1. **He was the outsider.** After Vietnam, Watergate and the pardon, "Washington" was a term of abuse. Carter ran against it: he had never worked in the capital, and promised a government as good as its people.
2. **His appeal on honesty.** His most repeated pledge was that he would never lie to the voters. In 1976 that was a political programme, and it fitted the mood created by Watergate far better than Ford's record as a Washington insider who had pardoned Nixon.
3. **His faith and his region.** He was a born-again Southern Baptist (born again means having had a conversion experience) who taught Sunday school. Newsweek called 1976 the "year of the evangelical" (evangelical Protestants stress personal faith and conversion), and Gallup found that about a third of American adults described themselves as born again. Carter was also a Southerner who could win back the white South that had drifted to Nixon, Wallace and Goldwater. He carried every Southern state except Virginia, and also held the great majority of Black voters.
4. **The disillusion with the Republicans.** Inflation, unemployment of 7.7 per cent, Watergate and the bitter Republican primary fight against Reagan all hurt Ford.
5. **A united Democratic Party, for once.** The 1976 field of Democratic candidates was divided and Carter took the nomination early; the traditional Democratic coalition of the South, Black voters and trade unionists came back together for one election.
:::

The victory was narrower than it looked in the summer, when he led Ford by 20 points or more. The final result was 297 electoral votes to Ford's 240 (a faithless Ford elector in Washington state cast his vote for Reagan), and 50.1 per cent of the popular vote to 48.0 per cent. Carter carried Ohio and Wisconsin, with 36 electoral votes between them, by narrow margins, and had Ford won both he would have been elected. Carter had won with an unusual coalition, and it was a fragile one: Northern liberals, who had backed his rivals in the primaries, distrusted him; Southern whites had come back to the Democrats, but only for him; and the Black voters whose turnout had saved him expected a great deal.

### Carter's style and his relations with Congress

Carter signalled a new style at once. After his inauguration on 20 January 1977 he walked part of the way from the Capitol to the White House, sold the presidential yacht, and in February gave a televised "fireside chat" in a cardigan, asking Americans to turn down their thermostats. On his first full day he pardoned most Vietnam draft evaders. He appointed a Cabinet of experienced figures such as Cyrus Vance and Harold Brown, but relied heavily on Georgians around him, Hamilton Jordan and Jody Powell, who had little experience of Washington.

This outsider's style created problems with Congress, where the Democrats held about two-thirds of the seats in the House and 61 of the 100 in the Senate.
- **He disliked bargaining.** A former engineer and naval officer, he drew up long, detailed programmes and expected members of Congress to judge them on their merits. He saw the horse-trading of Capitol Hill as the corruption he had promised to end. Speaker Thomas "Tip" O'Neill and Senate Majority Leader Robert Byrd found his staff slow to consult them.
- **He started fights with his own side.** In February 1977 he put 19 water projects, which many Democratic members of Congress wanted for their states, on a "hit list" for cancellation, and Congress restored most of the money.
- **He attempted too much at once.** Energy, tax reform, welfare reform, hospital costs, Social Security, the Panama Canal treaties and a Cabinet reorganisation all arrived in his first two years.
- **Scandal and carelessness.** His friend and Budget Director Bert Lance resigned on 21 September 1977 after questions about his banking; the affair damaged Carter's promise of a higher standard of honesty.
- **A Congress that had changed.** As the section on Ford showed, the post-Watergate Congress was assertive. The new members elected in 1974 and 1976 owed their seats to nobody, and committee chairmen had less power to deliver votes. Even a President who knew Congress well would have struggled.

Carter's real successes in Congress came where he found allies: the **Department of Energy** (4 August 1977), the **Civil Service Reform Act** (1978), the **Airline Deregulation Act** (24 October 1978, which removed government controls on airline fares and routes), the new **Department of Education** (1979) and the Alaska lands bill (1980), which protected more than 100 million acres. The Panama Canal treaties (1977, giving Panama control of the canal by 1999) and the Camp David Accords (1978, the framework for peace between Egypt and Israel) were foreign-policy successes (see [Carter's foreign policies, 1977–81](#hist.d.usa74.3)), but each cost him political capital at home.

### Fiscal policy and the fight against inflation

Carter first tried to stimulate the economy, in the Keynesian way. In January 1977 he proposed a rebate of 50 dollars for each taxpayer, then withdrew it in April when the economy grew faster than expected. From 1978, as inflation rose again, he changed to restraint. His programme of October 1978 asked for voluntary limits on pay and price rises, and he named the economist Alfred Kahn, the architect of airline deregulation, his "inflation czar". Voluntary guidelines failed, as Ford's WIN campaign had, and in November 1978 the dollar fell so far that the administration organised a package of about 30 billion dollars to support it.

Carter's economic record was therefore mixed: the deficit was small by later standards and almost halved between 1976 and 1979, and the economy created about 10 million jobs between January 1977 and January 1981, but inflation roughly doubled from its 1976 level. His party was also divided over what to do. Trade unions and liberals led by Senator Edward Kennedy wanted jobs programmes and national health insurance; the Humphrey–Hawkins Act of October 1978 set a goal of 4 per cent unemployment but with no means of enforcing it. Carter, caught between inflation and the left of his own party, satisfied neither.

The decisive action was not his own. In July 1979 he named **Paul Volcker**, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, to chair the Federal Reserve. On 6 October 1979 the Fed announced that it would stop targeting interest rates and control the money supply (the amount of money in circulation) instead, the **"Saturday night special"**. Interest rates rose sharply; the prime rate (the rate banks charge their best borrowers) hit a record 21.5 per cent in December 1980. The measure was intended to break inflation, and in the end it did, but it deepened the short recession of 1980 and pushed unemployment up before Carter faced the voters.

:::def Monetarism
The theory, associated with the economist Milton Friedman, that inflation is caused by too fast a growth in the money supply, so that the way to end it is for the central bank to restrain the money supply, even at the cost of high interest rates and unemployment in the short term.
:::

### Energy: the crisis that would not go away

Energy was Carter's first big project and the problem that most defined his term. On 18 April 1977 he told the nation on television that the energy crisis was "the moral equivalent of war". His **National Energy Plan** of more than 100 proposals aimed to cut oil use by taxes on gas-guzzling cars, to force energy-saving and to raise the price of domestic oil and gas to world levels, so that demand fell.

It was a sensible plan and a political failure. Congress, where oil and gas states had strong voices, rejected the central tax on crude oil, and it took until November 1978, a year and a half later, to pass a **National Energy Act**, in five parts, that dealt mainly with natural gas prices, conservation and tax credits for insulation and solar power. The Act was much weaker than Carter had wanted.

Then events overtook it. After the Iranian Revolution, oil prices rose and there were new petrol lines in the spring and summer of 1979. On 28 March 1979 the Three Mile Island nuclear plant near Harrisburg, Pennsylvania, suffered a partial meltdown, the worst accident in US nuclear power history, and public support for the nuclear option collapsed. In April 1979 Carter announced the gradual removal of price controls on domestic oil, to be completed by October 1981, together with a "windfall profit" tax on the oil companies; the tax became law on 2 April 1980. On 30 June 1980 he signed the **Energy Security Act**, which set up a Synthetic Fuels Corporation (with 20 billion dollars at first) to produce fuel from coal and shale, and promoted gasohol and solar power.

:::key Why Carter's energy policy fell short
1. **The scale of the problem.** The US depended on imported oil, and prices were set by a foreign cartel and by events in Iran. No President could cure that quickly.
2. **Congress.** Producer states, consumers and the oil companies each blocked the parts that hurt them; by the time a law passed the plan was a collection of compromises.
3. **Carter's method.** He sent a very large plan, without enough preparation with Congress, and explained it in terms of sacrifice at a time when voters blamed the government and the oil companies for the shortage.
4. **Bad luck.** The Iranian Revolution and Three Mile Island came just as the plan was being passed.
Yet the laws did lay foundations: the Department of Energy, oil decontrol (completed by Reagan in January 1981) and fuel-economy standards cut American oil imports, which fell sharply in the early 1980s.
:::

### The decline of Carter's reputation

By 1979 Carter's standing had collapsed. Gallup's approval figure for him was about 75 per cent in early 1977 and had fallen to about 28 per cent by the summer of 1979. Inflation, the lines at the petrol stations and the sense that the White House was not in control all counted. On 15 July 1979, after ten days at Camp David talking with advisers, business and religious leaders and ordinary citizens, he gave a televised address. It is remembered as the "malaise speech", although he never used the word.

:::note Evidence: Carter's address of 15 July 1979
*Adapted from President Jimmy Carter, address to the nation on energy and national goals, 15 July 1979. The extract is shortened, with every cut marked, and the words are the speech's own.*

"… all the legislation in the world can't fix what's wrong with America. … It is a crisis of confidence. It is a crisis that strikes at the very heart and soul and spirit of our national will. … The erosion of our confidence in the future is threatening to destroy the social and the political fabric of America. … Human identity is no longer defined by what one does, but by what one owns."

*Provenance and use.* A televised speech by the President to the whole nation, written to change the mood of the country. It shows the problem as Carter saw it by mid-1979, a failure of national spirit and not only of policy, and it was well received at first. But it also shows why his critics thought it a weakness: it seemed to blame the people, and Reagan's 1980 message of optimism answered it directly. Two days later (17 July) Carter asked for the resignations of his entire Cabinet and accepted several, including those of Joseph Califano and Michael Blumenthal. The shake-up looked like panic and wiped out most of the speech's good effect.
:::

In the months that followed, his troubles multiplied. On 4 November 1979 Iranian students seized the US embassy in Tehran and held 52 Americans for 444 days; the failed rescue attempt of 24 April 1980 (Operation Eagle Claw) cost eight servicemen's lives and led to the resignation of Secretary of State Vance. The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 ended détente (see [Carter's foreign policies, 1977–81](#hist.d.usa74.3)). At home, Edward Kennedy announced on 7 November 1979 that he would challenge the President for the Democratic nomination, which Carter won only after a bitter series of primaries (see [The USA, 1974–93 (2)](#hist.d.usa74.2)). And in the first half of 1980 inflation passed 13 per cent and interest rates soared.

:::key Carter's domestic policies: how far was he to blame?
1. **Inheritance and bad luck:** the oil shocks, stagflation, the post-Watergate Congress and the lasting distrust of government were not of his making.
2. **His own style:** the outsider's contempt for bargaining, the overloaded agenda and the disorder in his White House made things worse. The 1977 "hit list" and the Lance affair damaged him early.
3. **The divided Democratic Party:** Kennedy liberals wanted spending and national health insurance; Carter, worried about inflation, wanted restraint.
4. **A mixed record:** deregulation, the Departments of Energy and Education, Alaska lands and the appointment of Volcker were lasting; but inflation, energy and Iran decided the voters' verdict.
:::

## The rise of the New Right

### What the New Right was

Alongside the economic crisis, a political movement was growing on the right. The **New Right** was not one organisation but a coalition of groups, united by dislike of the liberal changes of the 1960s and by a belief that government had grown too large and too intrusive.

:::def New Right
A loose conservative movement that grew in the 1970s and joined four strands: free-market conservatives who wanted lower taxes and less regulation, social conservatives and evangelical Christians who opposed abortion, feminism and gay rights, anti-communist hawks who opposed détente, and white working-class and Southern voters angry about busing and crime. It differed from the "Old Right" of Robert Taft and the Goldwater campaign in using mass methods, such as direct mail, television and church networks, to reach ordinary voters.
:::

### Influence of religious groups, including evangelical Christians

For most of the twentieth century many evangelical Protestants had kept out of party politics. Three things changed that in the 1970s. First, the Supreme Court's decisions on school prayer (1962–63), abortion (1973) and then the controversy over the tax status of private Christian schools made them feel under attack by secular government. Second, the success of television preachers such as Falwell and **Pat Robertson** (founder of the Christian Broadcasting Network) gave them a national network and mailing lists. Third, Carter's presidency, which began with an evangelical in the White House, disappointed many of them. Carter's support for the ERA, his lukewarm stand on abortion, and the White House conference on the family in 1980 (which was divided over how to define "the family") all alienated them.

The **Moral Majority**, founded in 1979 by Falwell with the help of Weyrich, Viguerie and other organisers, set out to register evangelical voters and to oppose abortion, the ERA, the legalisation of homosexuality and what it saw as the decline of the family and of American strength. Falwell claimed millions of members; historians think the real numbers were smaller, but its influence on the vote was real. Reagan spoke to thousands of pastors at a National Affairs Briefing in Dallas in August 1980 and told them that he supported what they were doing.

Historians disagree about what first brought evangelical leaders into politics. Paul Weyrich later pointed to the campaign over the tax-exempt status of segregated Christian schools, and in 1978 the Internal Revenue Service's proposal to remove it from many of them did draw a huge volume of protest mail; Randall Balmer has argued that this, more than abortion, started the Religious Right. Others, such as Daniel K. Williams, stress that opposition to abortion, feminism and changes in the family was central from the mid-1970s. Both can be true, and the point for the exam is that the movement grew from several grievances at once.

### The reaction against social change

- **Abortion.** After *Roe v. Wade* (1973) the National Right to Life Committee organised across the country. Congress passed the **Hyde Amendment** in 1976, which stopped most federal Medicaid payments for abortions, and the movement spread through Catholic and evangelical churches. It was one of the few issues that united Catholic voters and evangelical Protestants.
- **The Equal Rights Amendment.** Schlafly argued that the ERA would take away women's legal protections and bring women into combat. When the original ratification deadline of 1979 approached with 35 states in favour, Congress extended it to June 1982, but no further state ratified, and five rescinded. The defeat of the ERA was the New Right's first great victory over the feminist movement.
- **Gay rights.** In 1977 the singer Anita Bryant led a "Save Our Children" campaign in Dade County, Florida, and voters repealed the county's ban on discrimination against gay people by about 69 per cent to 31 per cent on 7 June 1977. In California in November 1978 voters rejected, by about 58 per cent to 42 per cent, the Briggs Initiative to bar gay teachers from schools (it was opposed by Reagan as well as by Carter). The defeat shows that the New Right did not win every fight, but the campaigns brought thousands of church members into politics.
- **The counter-culture.** The same voters disliked what they saw as the drugs, sexual freedom, protest and disrespect for authority of the 1960s and early 1970s (see [A time of challenges (4): challenges to authority](#hist.d.usa61.4)). They gave Nixon his "silent majority" in 1969 and George Wallace his support in 1968; in the late 1970s many of them moved to the New Right.

### The end of the consensus on the role of the state

Underlying all this was a change in economic ideas. After the Second World War both parties had broadly accepted that government should manage the economy and provide a safety net: the **Keynesian consensus**. Stagflation broke the consensus.

- **Ideas.** Economists such as Milton Friedman (Nobel Prize 1976) argued that government intervention made inflation worse. The Laffer curve, which Arthur Laffer is said to have sketched in 1974, suggested that if tax rates were high enough, cutting them might raise revenue. Journalists such as Jude Wanniski popularised **supply-side economics** (defined in the box below) in *The Way the World Works* (1978).
- **Think tanks and business.** The Heritage Foundation (1973), the Cato Institute (named in 1977) and the American Enterprise Institute produced policy papers; the Business Roundtable (1972) and the Chamber of Commerce lobbied, and after the 1974 campaign-finance law the number of business political action committees (PACs) grew several-fold, while those of labour grew much more slowly.
- **The tax revolt.** On 6 June 1978 Californians passed **Proposition 13** by about 65 per cent to 35 per cent. It cut property taxes to 1 per cent of 1975 values, limited later rises and required a two-thirds vote in the legislature for new taxes. Within months other states were cutting taxes, and Congress was considering the Kemp–Roth plan for a 30 per cent cut in income tax rates over three years.
- **Doubt about the Great Society.** Many voters, including some who had been Democrats, came to believe that Johnson's programmes had cost much and achieved little.

:::def Supply-side economics
The belief that the best way to raise output is to cut taxes and regulation so that people and firms have more reason to work, save and invest (the "supply" of goods), instead of managing total demand as Keynesians did. Its supporters said that tax cuts could partly pay for themselves; its critics called it "trickle-down" economics.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: the 1970s as a turning point)
Three well-known books give three emphases. This is a short adapted summary, not a quotation.
- **Bruce Schulman**, *The Seventies* (2001), sees the decade as a "great shift" in which Americans moved away from the liberal faith of the 1960s towards a politics of the market and the individual, and the South and West gained influence.
- **Dominic Sandbrook**, *Mad as Hell* (2011), stresses anger and populism: the crisis of the 1970s was a crisis of confidence in the elites, and Reagan's victory was the outcome of years of mistrust.
- **James Patterson**, *Restless Giant* (2005), places the period in a longer story: Americans were anxious and divided, but the country remained prosperous and powerful, and many of the changes of the decade (the growth of conservatism, of religion in politics and of women's role) lasted.
Use them to support your own argument about what the problems were and what they changed; do not list them.
:::

## Reagan's victory in 1980

### The campaign

**Ronald Reagan**, born on 6 February 1911, was a former film actor, a spokesman for General Electric and Governor of California (1967–75). His speech for Goldwater, "A Time for Choosing" (27 October 1964), had made him a national conservative figure; he came close to beating Ford for the nomination in 1976. In 1980 he beat a field of Republicans, among them George H. W. Bush, who called Reagan's tax-cutting plan "voodoo economics" and then became his running mate at the Detroit convention in July.

His appeal rested on four things: a simple optimistic message ("Let's make America great again"), a talent for television, a record as a successful governor and an ability to speak both to the New Right and to ordinary voters worried about prices. He promised to cut taxes by 30 per cent over three years, balance the budget, reduce regulation and rebuild defence. In his only debate with Carter, on 28 October 1980 in Cleveland, he ended by asking voters whether they were better off than they had been four years earlier.

Carter ran a campaign that tried to make the election a choice about risk: he warned that Reagan, a former actor aged 69, would be dangerous in a nuclear crisis and divisive at home. But the hostages were still in Iran on election day, 4 November, the anniversary of their seizure. A third candidate, the Republican Congressman **John Anderson**, running as an independent, attracted liberal and moderate voters who disliked both major candidates.

| 1980 | Electoral votes | Popular votes | Share |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ronald Reagan (Republican) | 489 | 43.9 million | 50.7% |
| Jimmy Carter (Democrat) | 49 | 35.5 million | 41.0% |
| John Anderson (independent) | 0 | 5.7 million | 6.6% |

Reagan won 44 states. Carter held Georgia, Maryland, Minnesota, Rhode Island, West Virginia, Hawaii and the District of Columbia, 49 votes in all, and lost every Southern state except his home state. The Republicans also gained 12 seats in the Senate and won control of it for the first time since the 1950s; Senators Frank Church, George McGovern and Birch Bayh were beaten, Bayh by a young Indiana Republican, Dan Quayle. In the House the Republicans gained 33 seats but the Democrats kept their majority.

### Why Reagan won

The syllabus lists four reasons. Weigh them, do not just list them.

:::key Reasons for Reagan's victory in 1980
1. **Reagan's personal appeal.** Optimism, skill on television and a record in California gave him a trust that Carter had lost. Polls showed many voters who did not agree with all his policies still found him likeable and reassuring. But he was also seen by some as old and dangerous, and in the early autumn Carter led some polls.
2. **Economic issues and the spread of supply-side doctrine.** Inflation of 13.5 per cent, prime rates over 20 per cent and a misery index of 20.6 gave Reagan the most powerful question of the campaign. His tax-cut plan was a clear alternative to Carter's call for restraint, and Kemp–Roth and Proposition 13 had made tax cuts popular.
3. **The perceived failures of Carter at home and abroad.** At home, inflation, energy and weak leadership; abroad, the hostage crisis, Afghanistan and the failure of Operation Eagle Claw gave an impression of weakness, even though some of the problems were not his fault.
4. **Social change and the rise of the New Right.** Evangelical voters, anti-feminist and anti-busing voters, and working-class Democrats who disliked what they saw as liberal social policy moved to Reagan. Many of them became known as **Reagan Democrats**.
:::

How should these be ranked? **The economy was probably the most important**, because it is the reason that cut across all groups. The surveys of voters in 1980 found the economy and inflation to be the issue most often named. Reagan's personality was important because it let him turn dissatisfaction into a vote for himself, and Carter's failure to inspire confidence meant that voters who were dissatisfied were willing to take a risk. The New Right was the most important organised force and gave Reagan his activists, money and doorstep campaign, but alone it was too small to win: Reagan's majority came from voters who had voted for Carter in 1976. Anderson's 6.6 per cent probably did harm Carter in a few states, but Reagan's margin was too large for this to alter the result.

:::warn Do not write that Reagan won because of the hostage crisis
The hostages were an important part of the impression of weakness, but the polls had swung to Reagan late in the campaign mainly because of the economy and the debate. And Carter's problems with inflation, with Congress and with his own party had begun long before 4 November 1979. Make the hostage crisis one factor, and show that you know it was not the only one.
:::

## How significant were the problems?

Now apply the four tests from the start of the chapter.

| Problem | How deep? | How long? | Whose fault? | Effect on 1980 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stagflation (inflation, unemployment) | Very deep: it hurt almost every family | Most of the decade; inflation peaked in 1974 and 1979–80 | Mainly inherited and world forces; Carter's response weak | The most important single cause of Carter's defeat |
| Energy and the oil shocks | Deep: queues, prices, dependence | 1973–74 and 1979–80 | Mostly foreign events; Congress blocked Carter's plan | Fed the sense of national decline |
| Distrust after Vietnam and Watergate | Very deep: trust halved between 1964 and 1980 | From the late 1960s onwards | Nixon and the Vietnam leaders; deepened by the pardon | Created the opening for outsiders, Carter in 1976 and Reagan in 1980 |
| Social divisions (busing, abortion, ERA, gay rights) | Deep for some, remote for others | Continuing | Mixed: courts, movements and the parties' choices | Gave the New Right its voters |
| Presidential weakness | Moderate and growing | 1977–80 | Carter's own style; a post-Watergate Congress | Made voters ready to change President |
| Hostage crisis and Afghanistan | Deep in symbol; limited in daily life | 1979–81 | Not Carter's fault, but he was judged on it | A late blow to a weakened President |

:::key A judgement you can defend
The most significant problem was **stagflation and the energy crisis**, because it was deep, lasted most of the decade, hurt almost all Americans and could not be solved by the usual Keynesian remedies. But it became politically decisive because of three others working with it: the **distrust of government** that made voters doubt that anyone in Washington could solve it, the **weakness of Carter's leadership** that made it seem that nobody was trying, and the **rise of the New Right**, which supplied a message, an organisation and a candidate. Alone, the economic problems might have produced a Democratic recovery under a more skilful President; together with the others they produced a change of direction.
:::

**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained and balanced argument, a clear and supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Failure" needs a test. Criteria: (1) did he achieve his own aims? (2) how much was beyond his control? (3) what lasted? Support for the view: energy plan cut down (April 1977 to November 1978); welfare, health and tax reform not passed (the Revenue Act of 1978 cut capital gains taxes, the taxes on profit from selling shares or property, the opposite of what he wanted); the "hit list" and the Lance affair; inflation 5.8 per cent (1976) to 13.5 per cent (1980); approval 75 per cent to 28 per cent; defeat 489–49. Challenge: inheritance (1974 inflation 11.0 per cent), the post-Watergate Congress and oil-state senators; lasting acts (Departments of Energy and Education, deregulation of airlines, trucking and rail, Alaska lands, Volcker). Judgement: a political failure, a more mixed policy record, so "failure" is too simple.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Carter's presidency was mostly a failure in domestic affairs. He tried to deal with energy in 1977 and gave his speech about the moral equivalent of war but Congress did not pass his plan. There was also high inflation and unemployment and long queues for petrol in 1979. In July 1979 he made the malaise speech and then sacked his Cabinet. He did some good things, like creating the Department of Energy and the Department of Education. But he lost the 1980 election to Reagan by a lot, so his presidency was a failure."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, and it has both sides, so Table B is Level 3. But it is a **list of failures followed by a list of successes**, with a conclusion that rests on the election result alone. There are no criteria, no test of what "failure" means, and no sense of how much was Carter's fault. Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If failure means not achieving what he set out to do, the claim is largely justified. His National Energy Plan of April 1977 proposed a tax on crude oil to bring its price to the world level, but the Senate rejected that tax, and the five-part National Energy Act that passed in November 1978 was a weak compromise. But if failure means the results of his own mistakes, the claim is weaker. *Commentary.* Two criteria are applied and tested against evidence. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs a third criterion (what lasted), the contrast between a political and a policy verdict, and a sustained judgement that answers "domestic affairs" as a whole.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is partly valid. Judged by what Carter promised, a government that was honest, efficient and capable of mastering energy and inflation, his domestic record was a political failure, and the voters said so in 1980. Judged by what he changed, and by how far the problems were his to solve, 'failure' is too simple. I shall use three criteria: whether he achieved his own aims, how far events and Congress lay beyond his control, and what lasted.

On his own aims the record is poor. His central project, the National Energy Plan of April 1977, asked for a tax on crude oil to bring domestic prices to world levels. The Senate, where oil-state members were strong, rejected it, and the National Energy Act of November 1978 dealt mainly with natural gas prices and tax credits. His plans for welfare reform and national health insurance did not pass, and his tax proposals of 1978 ended in an Act that cut taxes on capital gains, the reverse of his intention. He damaged his own standing with the 'hit list' of 19 water projects in February 1977 and the resignation of his Budget Director, Bert Lance, in September 1977, which dented the promise of a higher standard of honesty. His relations with a Congress in which the Democrats held about two-thirds of the House were so poor that he could not use his majority. And inflation, the problem by which he was judged, rose from 5.8 per cent in 1976 to 11.3 per cent in 1979 and 13.5 per cent in 1980, taking the misery index to 20.6. On this test the view is justified.

But much of the problem was not of his making. Inflation was 11.0 per cent in 1974, before he took office, and the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979 followed war and revolution in the Middle East. The Congress he faced was the post-Watergate Congress of 1975–80, with the committee chairmen weakened, young members owing nothing to the leadership and a deep suspicion of the executive, which would have frustrated a more skilful President. He was also unlucky: Three Mile Island on 28 March 1979 came as he was asking Congress for support for nuclear power, and the Iranian Revolution came as his energy law was passing. Part of the failure was a failure of luck.

Third, some of what he did lasted. The Departments of Energy (1977) and Education (1979), the deregulation (the removal of government controls on prices and routes) of the airlines in 1978 and of trucking and the railroads in 1980, and the banking act of March 1980 moved policy towards the markets that Reagan is often thought to have begun. The Alaska lands Act of 1980 protected more than 100 million acres. Above all, in July 1979 he appointed Paul Volcker to the Federal Reserve, and Volcker's decision of 6 October 1979 to control the money supply began the policy that broke inflation. The tragedy was that it raised interest rates to 21.5 per cent by December 1980 and so hurt Carter before it helped Reagan.

Overall, then, the view is justified as a political judgement and unjustified as a judgement of policy. Carter failed to master Congress, to explain his plans or to deliver lower inflation within four years, and that is why he lost by 489 electoral votes to 49. But the economy's disorder was inherited, and the policies that later ended inflation, deregulation and a tighter money supply, were begun in his term. The fairest verdict is that Carter failed politically more than he failed in domestic policy, and that he was a President who paid the political price for the policies that others later profited from."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): three explicit criteria, a balanced argument in which each side is tested against the other, and a sustained judgement that separates the political from the policy verdict. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise and selected detail (April 1977; November 1978; 19 projects; 5.8, 11.3 and 13.5 per cent; 28 March 1979; 6 October 1979; 21.5 per cent). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 700 words, 35 to 40 minutes.

Falwell's Moral Majority, founded in 1979, registered evangelical voters, and Reagan's speech to thousands of pastors in Dallas in August 1980 showed how seriously he took them. Direct-mail fundraisers such as Richard Viguerie raised money for conservative candidates, and in the Senate elections of 1980 Republicans defeated Church, McGovern and Bayh and gained control of the chamber. Yet Carter, a born-again Christian, had won a large share of the evangelical vote in 1976, and evangelicals alone were too few to explain a victory of 489 electoral votes to 49."

*Commentary.* A criterion (the 1980 election) is applied with accurate detail and tested. Level 4. The answer still needs the second criterion (ideas and policy), the limits, and a judgement on the **extent** of significance.

**A Level 5 conclusion** (after paragraphs on organisation, on ideas, and on limits).

"Overall, the rise of the New Right was highly significant, but its significance lay in *what it changed in American politics* more than in *how many votes it delivered in 1980*. Its direct electoral effect was real but limited: it gave Reagan activists, money and the evangelical vote, and helped to defeat liberal senators, but Reagan's landslide depended on voters who had backed Carter in 1976 and who were moved mainly by inflation of 13.5 per cent and a sense of national weakness. Its effect on ideas was greater. Proposition 13, Kemp–Roth and the think tanks ended the Keynesian consensus on which both parties had worked since 1945, and made tax cuts the starting point of debate. The defeat of the ERA, and the entry of evangelical churches into party politics, changed the Republican Party for a generation. Its limits are equally clear: the Briggs Initiative was defeated in California in 1978, and the strands of the movement, free-market conservatives and social conservatives, wanted different things from government. I conclude that the New Right was a necessary condition of Reagan's victory, since without it there would have been no message, organisation or candidate, but not a sufficient one, since without stagflation and Carter's failures it would have remained a minority."

*Commentary.* A clear judgement on the **extent** of significance, with a distinction (direct and indirect effect, necessary and sufficient) that the paragraphs have earned. Table A Level 5; Table B depends on the detail in the paragraphs before it.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the reasons for Reagan's victory in the 1980 election." [30]

A student wrote:

"Reagan won the election in 1980 because he was a very good speaker and everyone liked him. He had been an actor in Hollywood and was good on television. Carter was weak and he could not get the hostages home from Iran, which made him look foolish. Reagan also promised to cut taxes, which people liked. So in November Reagan won with 489 votes and Carter lost. All these reasons together are why Reagan won."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): four reasons are named but each is one sentence of assertion, they are not compared, and the final sentence ("all these reasons together") is a substitute for a judgement. Table B Level 2 (4–6): the knowledge is accurate but thin. "489 votes" is not explained (electoral votes), nothing is said of inflation, the New Right or Anderson, and "the hostages" is the only fact about Carter.

**The fix.** Choose a criterion and rank. "Reagan won above all because of the economy. Inflation was 13.5 per cent in 1980, the prime rate had reached 21.5 per cent in December, and the misery index stood at 20.6, so his question in the debate of 28 October, whether voters were better off than four years earlier, spoke to nearly everyone. His personality mattered because it made his remedy believable: he was a sunny and experienced communicator in a year when Carter seemed to have lost control. The hostage crisis and Afghanistan added to the impression of weakness, but the voters' complaints about Carter had begun long before 4 November 1979." Now each fact is evidence for a ranked argument, and the paragraph is on its way to Level 4 or 5.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What was stagflation, and give three causes of it in the 1970s.
A: A combination of slow growth or recession and high inflation at the same time, which Keynesian policy could not cure because the remedy for one made the other worse. Causes: the cost of Vietnam and the Great Society; the end of the gold-dollar link in 1971 and the lifting of price controls in 1973–74; the oil shocks of 1973–74 (OAPEC embargo; price roughly quadrupled) and 1979 (Iranian Revolution); slow productivity growth and foreign competition.
Q: What did Ford do on 8 September 1974, and what were the effects?
A: He granted Nixon a full, free and absolute pardon for any offences in office. His approval fell from 71 per cent in August to 50 per cent by late September (Gallup); his press secretary resigned; critics suspected a deal; the Democrats gained 49 House seats in November 1974. Most Americans later accepted it.
Q: How did Ford respond to inflation and recession?
A: "Whip Inflation Now" (8 October 1974), a campaign of voluntary restraint that failed; then, as unemployment rose to about 9 per cent, the Tax Reduction Act (29 March 1975); 66 vetoes of spending bills (12 overridden); the Energy Policy and Conservation Act (22 December 1975). Inflation fell to 5.8 per cent in 1976, but unemployment was 7.7 per cent.
Q: Why did busing in Boston cause so much conflict?
A: Judge Garrity's order of 21 June 1974, applied from September, bused pupils between white working-class South Boston and Black Roxbury; white parents felt that their children and neighbourhoods were being used to solve a problem the suburbs had escaped (*Milliken v. Bradley*, 25 July 1974, protected the suburbs). There were boycotts and violence in 1974–76. Opposition to busing moved white voters of both parties towards the New Right.
Q: What did the Church Committee find, and what changes followed Watergate?
A: From 1975 it exposed CIA assassination plots, FBI harassment of King and others, and illegal mail-opening. Congress passed the War Powers Resolution (1973), the Freedom of Information Act amendments (1974), the Privacy Act (1974) and campaign-finance reforms (1974), and created permanent intelligence committees (1976–77). Trust in government fell from 77 per cent (1964) to 36 per cent (1974) and 25 per cent (1980) in the ANES surveys.
Q: Give four reasons for Carter's victory in 1976.
A: He was an outsider after Watergate; he promised honesty; he was a born-again Southerner who carried every Southern state but Virginia and held the Black vote; Ford was damaged by the pardon, the economy (unemployment 7.7 per cent) and the challenge from Reagan. The result was 297–240 (50.1 per cent to 48.0 per cent).
Q: What was Carter's National Energy Plan, and how far did it succeed?
A: A set of more than 100 proposals (April 1977) to cut oil use through taxes and conservation and to raise domestic prices to world levels. The Senate rejected the crude-oil tax; the National Energy Act (November 1978) was a weak compromise. Later: decontrol of oil (from April 1979), the windfall profit tax (2 April 1980), the Energy Security Act (30 June 1980). The Department of Energy was created on 4 August 1977.
Q: What did Carter say on 15 July 1979, and what was the effect?
A: That the country faced "a crisis of confidence" in its national will and that "all the legislation in the world can't fix what's wrong with America". The speech was at first well received, but two days later the dismissal of Cabinet members made it look like panic, and critics said that it blamed the people. His approval was about 28 per cent that summer.
Q: What was Volcker's "Saturday night special", and what did it do?
A: On 6 October 1979 the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker switched from targeting interest rates to controlling the money supply. Interest rates soared (prime rate 21.5 per cent in December 1980) and unemployment rose, but inflation fell from 13.5 per cent in 1980 to 3.2 per cent in 1983.
Q: What was the New Right? Name its strands and two events that show its strength.
A: A coalition of free-market conservatives, social conservatives and evangelicals, anti-communist hawks, and angry white working-class and Southern voters. Events: Proposition 13 (6 June 1978, about 65 per cent to 35 per cent); the Moral Majority (1979); the defeat of the ERA (stalled at 35 states); the Senate victories of 1980.
Q: Give the result of the 1980 election and four reasons for it.
A: Reagan 489 electoral votes and 50.7 per cent of the popular vote; Carter 49 and 41.0 per cent; Anderson 6.6 per cent. Reasons: Reagan's appeal; the economy (inflation, the misery index) and supply-side promises; Carter's perceived failures at home and abroad (hostages, Afghanistan, energy); the New Right and the shift of evangelical and working-class voters.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the success of Ford's policies towards the economic problems of 1974–77.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: did the policies treat the actual problem; did they work; what was in his control. Success: the recession ended in March 1975; the Tax Reduction Act (29 March 1975) helped recovery; inflation fell from 11.0 per cent (1974) to 5.8 per cent (1976); vetoes restrained spending; the Energy Policy and Conservation Act (22 December 1975) started fuel-economy standards. Limits: WIN (8 October 1974) was ineffective and badly timed; unemployment peaked at about 9 per cent (May 1975) and was 7.7 per cent in 1976; the deficit reached about 74 billion dollars; no real answer to energy dependence; the New York affair. A Level 5 answer separates the inherited causes (the oil shock) from Ford's own choices and judges the result against what was possible.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The pardon of Nixon was the main reason why Ford lost the election of 1976.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: the pardon (8 September 1974) cut his approval from 71 to 50 per cent in six weeks; the Democrats gained 49 House seats in 1974; it fed the distrust that Carter exploited; Carter's honesty message. Challenge: Ford narrowed the gap and lost by only 2 points; the economy (unemployment 7.7 per cent); the challenge from Reagan, which split the party; the Poland gaffe (6 October 1976); "Ford to City: Drop Dead"; the Democrats' advantage in a post-Watergate year; Carter's appeal. Level 5 uses a criterion, such as how widely and how lastingly each factor acted, and judges, for example, that the pardon was a necessary part of a wider distrust but that the economy and the Reagan challenge decided the closeness of the result.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why Carter won the presidential election of 1976.
A: Indicative content. Outsider status and the Watergate mood; honesty; a born-again Southern Baptist in the "year of the evangelical"; the South (every Southern state but Virginia) and Black voters; Ford's weaknesses (pardon, economy, the Reagan challenge, the Poland gaffe); a united Democratic coalition for one election. Limits: a narrow win (50.1 per cent to 48.0 per cent; Ohio and Wisconsin decided it) from a summer lead of 20 points or more. Level 5 ranks: the Watergate-era distrust of Washington was the necessary condition; Carter's own appeal and campaign explained why it was he, not another Democrat, who benefited.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that Carter's energy policy was a failure.
A: Indicative content. Failure: the central tax rejected; a plan that took until November 1978 to pass in diluted form; imports and prices rose in 1979; the lines of summer 1979; Three Mile Island; the 15 July speech. Success: the Department of Energy (1977); decontrol of oil (from 1979); the windfall profit tax (2 April 1980); the Energy Security Act (30 June 1980) and its synthetic fuels programme; conservation and fuel-economy standards cut imports in the early 1980s; the problem was global. Level 5 sets the criterion (aims; control; lasting results) and judges that the plan failed politically but laid foundations.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Carter's difficulties with Congress were caused mainly by his own style.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate what Carter did (the hit list, the Lance affair, an overloaded agenda) from what he faced (a post-Watergate Congress).
H: Compare his record with Ford's: did a more experienced President get on better with the same Congress?
A: Indicative content. Support: his contempt for bargaining; the "hit list" of 19 water projects (February 1977); an overloaded programme (energy, tax, welfare, health, Panama); Bert Lance (21 September 1977); a Georgian staff with little Washington experience; weak relations with O'Neill and Byrd; the quarrel with Kennedy over health insurance. Challenge: the post-Watergate Congress of 1975–80 was more assertive and divided (Ford's 66 vetoes, 12 overridden); weaker party leaders; oil-state senators; the Panama Canal treaties and the Airline Deregulation Act did pass; economic conditions limited what any President could do. Level 5 judges that style was a main cause of the early failures but that the structure of Congress made difficulty likely.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons for the rise of the New Right in the 1970s.
H: Distinguish the causes of each strand: taxes, religion, anti-communism, race and busing.
H: Consider long-term change (the 1960s backlash) and short-term triggers (Roe, Proposition 13, stagflation).
A: Indicative content. Reaction against the 1960s (the "silent majority"); Roe v. Wade (1973) and the ERA; busing; stagflation and the end of faith in Keynesian management; Proposition 13; the disillusion with Vietnam and détente; organisation (think tanks, direct mail, television preachers, PACs); Carter's disappointment of the evangelicals. Level 5 weighs the necessary conditions (the 1960s backlash and stagflation) against the organisers' skill, and judges which strands (economic, religious, racial) mattered most.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Economic problems were the main reason for Reagan's victory in 1980.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which voters moved to Reagan, and what did they say mattered?
H: How far were the economic problems the cause of the other reasons (Carter's weak reputation, the New Right's appeal)?
A: Indicative content. Support: inflation 13.5 per cent, prime rate 21.5 per cent, the misery index of 20.6; the debate question of 28 October; Kemp–Roth and Proposition 13 made tax cuts popular; the economy was the issue most often named in 1980 voter surveys. Challenge: the hostages and Afghanistan (impressions of weakness); Reagan's personality; the New Right and Reagan Democrats; Carter's own errors and the Kennedy challenge; Anderson. Level 5 judges that the economy was the main reason, but that it worked through the other reasons: it made voters ready for Reagan's optimism and for the New Right's attack on government.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess how far the problems of the late 1970s were beyond the control of the President.
A: Indicative content. Beyond control: the oil shocks of 1973 and 1979; the recession of 1973–75; the post-Watergate Congress; distrust inherited from Vietnam and Watergate; Iran. Within control: Ford's pardon and WIN; Carter's handling of Congress, the 1977 hit list, the energy plan's design, the Cabinet shake-up of July 1979; the failure of Eagle Claw. Level 5 judges criteria such as whether a President could have chosen differently, and sees that the most serious problems (stagflation, energy) were largely beyond control, while the political damage was greater when Presidents handled them badly.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The problems of the late 1970s were exaggerated by those who gained from the idea of national decline.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare the data of 1976–78 (growth, falling unemployment) with the mood of 1979–80.
H: Who gained from the idea of decline: Reagan, the New Right, the Democratic left, Carter himself?
A: Support: growth of about 5 per cent a year in 1976–78; unemployment down to 5.8 per cent in 1979; about 10 million new jobs in Carter's term; Reagan and the New Right built a case on decline ("Let's make America great again"); Carter's own speech of 15 July 1979 spoke of a crisis of the spirit; Patterson's view that the US remained strong. Challenge: the misery index of 17–21 in 1974–75 and 1979–81 was real; trust in government fell from 77 per cent to 25 per cent; real hardship from inflation and energy shortages; Sandbrook and Schulman see a real crisis of confidence. A* judgement: the economic data support a mixed picture (inflation and energy were severe, growth and jobs were not), but the *political* crisis of confidence was real, and it was a fact about the voters' mood whatever the statistics said.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'In 1980 Americans voted against Carter more than for Reagan.' Evaluate this view.
H: What did Reagan's campaign promise that was new?
H: What happened to the Republican vote and the Democratic vote compared with 1976?
A: Support: Carter's approval (about 28 per cent in summer 1979; low in 1980); the Kennedy challenge showed that his own party had no faith in him; the economy and the hostages; Anderson's 6.6 per cent shows that many voters disliked both; Carter's share fell from 50.1 per cent to 41.0 per cent. Challenge: Reagan offered a clear programme (30 per cent tax cut, defence, smaller government) that had a base in the New Right and in Kemp–Roth; he took 44 states; the Senate Republicans gained 12 seats; the "Reagan Democrats". A* judgement: the election was first a verdict on Carter, but the size of the result (489–49) shows that Reagan's message gave it direction, and the later Reagan victories (1984) showed that it was more than a protest vote.
