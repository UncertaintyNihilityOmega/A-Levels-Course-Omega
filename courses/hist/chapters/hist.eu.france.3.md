## Governments of France, 1792–99

France had five different governments in seven years. Keep this table beside you: the questions often ask which body held power at a given moment.

| Government | Dates | Who held power |
|---|---|---|
| The National Convention | 21 September 1792 to 26 October 1795 | About 750 elected deputies who both made the laws and governed through committees |
| The Committee of Public Safety | April 1793 to October 1795 (real power July 1793 to July 1794) | Twelve deputies chosen by the Convention; the executive of the "revolutionary government" |
| The Thermidorian Convention | 28 July 1794 to October 1795 | The Convention taking back control after Robespierre's fall |
| The Directory | 2 November 1795 to 9 November 1799 | Five Directors and two councils under the Constitution of the Year III |
| The Consulate | from 10 November 1799 | Three Consuls, dominated by Bonaparte (see [France (4)](#hist.eu.france.4)) |

## The republic in danger, 1793

### The execution of Louis XVI

After the monarchy fell in August 1792 the Convention put the king on trial. On 16 and 17 January 1793 it voted him guilty and sentenced him to death by a narrow majority, rejecting appeals to the people and to delay. Louis XVI was guillotined in Paris on **21 January 1793**.

The execution made compromise impossible. The deputies who had voted for death were now bound to the Republic for their own safety, because a restored monarchy would punish them. Across Europe, rulers saw a regicide state that had to be fought. Inside France, many Catholics and moderates were shocked, and royalist feeling in the west and south hardened. Almost every problem of 1793 followed from this: **war, rebellion and division in the Convention itself**.

### The effects of war in 1793

France was already at war with Austria and Prussia (since April 1792). In the weeks after the execution the Convention declared war on Britain and the Dutch Republic (1 February 1793) and on Spain (7 March), and the **First Coalition** formed against it: Britain, the Dutch Republic, Spain, Austria, Prussia and Sardinia. The French armies, short of men after the volunteers of 1792 went home, were pushed out of Belgium in March, and on 5 April the commander General Dumouriez, after a defeat at Neerwinden, went over to the Austrians. By the end of July the border fortresses of Mainz and Valenciennes had fallen, and in August the great naval base of Toulon was handed to the British.

The war shaped government in three ways inside France:
- **Men.** On 24 February the Convention called for 300,000 more soldiers. The levy was resented, and it was the immediate spark for the rising in the Vendée.
- **Money and food.** Armies had to be fed and paid. The state printed more paper money (*assignats*) and requisitioned grain, which pushed up prices and made the government unpopular in the towns.
- **Suspicion.** Defeats and Dumouriez's treason convinced many that traitors were at work. The politicians who had led France into war in 1792, the Girondins, were blamed for the failures.

:::def The First Coalition
The alliance of European powers (Britain, Austria, Prussia, Spain, the Dutch Republic and Sardinia) that fought revolutionary France from 1793. It meant that in 1793 France faced invasion on the northern, eastern, southern and Spanish frontiers and a British blockade at sea.
:::

### Civil war: the Vendée and the federalist revolts

The Republic also faced rebellion from within.

**The Vendée.** In March 1793 villagers in the west, south of the Loire, rose in protest at the levy of 300,000 men. Behind that spark lay older grievances: many Catholics had rejected the Civil Constitution of the Clergy and the loss of their priests, peasants resented the way townspeople and the middle class had bought the Church's lands, and the region had little loyalty to the Paris government. Local nobles and former soldiers (Cathelineau, a carter, La Rochejaquelein, Charette) led the "Catholic and Royal Army". For months it defeated republican forces, but its attempt to take Nantes failed in June, and after a long march north the main army was destroyed at **Savenay** on 23 December 1793. Fighting and guerrilla warfare (*chouannerie*) went on in the west for years.

**The federalist revolts.** After the Girondins were arrested on 2 June (see below), some of their supporters left Paris and several large provincial cities, among them Lyon, Marseille, Bordeaux and Caen, rose against the Convention. They objected to the power of Paris and to the arrest of elected deputies; they were not royalists, and most called themselves republicans. Marseille was retaken in August, **Toulon** gave itself to the British on 28 August, and Lyon surrendered on 9 October after a siege.

Civil war is the key to the Terror. The Republic was fighting abroad and at home at once, and what it did to its enemies in the Vendée, at Lyon and at Toulon was the model for what it did elsewhere.

### Economic problems

War and revolution had wrecked the economy. The assignat, first issued in 1789 as a bond backed by confiscated Church land and then used as paper money, had lost more than half its face value by the summer of 1793, because the state kept printing more to pay its bills. As the currency lost value, prices rose, and the poor suffered first. In Paris, grocery riots broke out in February 1793, when crowds seized sugar, soap and candles. Peasants and merchants held back grain, hoping for higher prices or refusing assignats, and the winter of 1793–94 brought shortage again. The Convention had already tried one price control on grain (4 May 1793), but it was local and soon evaded.

These problems linked war to politics. A hungry Paris blamed hoarders and speculators, and demanded that the government punish them and fix prices. That was exactly the demand of the sans-culottes.

### Disagreement within the Convention

The Convention had no parties in the modern sense, but three groups mattered:

| Group | Who | What they wanted |
|---|---|---|
| The **Girondins** | Brissot, Vergniaud, Roland and their friends, many from provincial cities | A republic with limits on Paris and the crowd; had led the country to war in 1792; blamed for defeat in 1793 |
| The **Montagnards** ("the Mountain") | Robespierre, Danton, Marat, Saint-Just, supported by the Paris Commune and the sections | Strong central government, war to victory, concessions to the poor and the sans-culottes |
| The **Plain** (the *Marais*) | The majority of uncommitted deputies | Whichever side seemed safe; they backed the Mountain when the Girondins looked like losing |

The quarrel turned violent in the spring. The Girondins attacked Marat (he was put on trial in April and acquitted), and in May set up a Commission of Twelve to investigate the Paris Commune and arrested the journalist Hébert. The Paris sections replied with an insurrection. On **2 June 1793** the National Guard under Hanriot surrounded the Convention with cannon, and the deputies voted to place twenty-nine Girondin deputies under arrest. A Parisian crowd had purged the elected national assembly.

The consequences were severe. The Mountain now controlled the Convention, but it owed its power to the Paris crowd, and it had made enemies in the provinces. On 13 July Charlotte Corday, a young woman from Caen who sympathised with the Girondins, stabbed Marat in his bath. The assassination turned Marat into a martyr and made the language of plots and conspiracy a part of government.

### The influence of the sans-culottes

The **sans-culottes** ("without breeches": they wore long trousers rather than the knee-breeches of the gentry) were the Parisian shopkeepers, craftsmen, journeymen and wage-earners who were the active citizens of the sections. Their leaders were often small employers and professionals. They were not the poorest people in Paris, but they were the people who marched, packed the section meetings and sat in the Convention's galleries.

:::def Sans-culottes
The politically active ordinary citizens of Paris in 1792–95: artisans, shopkeepers, journeymen and wage-earners, organised in the 48 sections of the city. They demanded price controls, harsh punishment of hoarders and enemies of the Revolution, and direct democracy, and they dressed and spoke as equals, calling each other "citizen".
:::

**What they wanted.** Bread at a price they could pay; punishment of hoarders, speculators and "aristocrats"; a purge of suspect officials and generals; arms and an army of their own; and a say in government through their section assemblies. Their most radical spokesmen, the **Enragés** ("the angry ones": Jacques Roux, Jean Varlet, Théophile Leclerc), wanted direct price controls and the death penalty for speculators. Hébert, whose cheap newspaper *Le Père Duchesne* spoke in crude workers' language, and Chaumette at the Paris Commune, were their allies in the city government.

**What they did.** The sans-culottes used the *journées* (days of action): marches on the Convention to present petitions and demands, backed by the threat of force. The insurrection of 31 May–2 June 1793 expelled the Girondins. Another great demonstration on **4–5 September 1793**, in which they demanded action on food, a revolutionary army and measures against suspects, forced the Convention to make "terror the order of the day" in words, and in the following weeks to pass the Law of Suspects and the General Maximum.

**Their limits.** The sans-culottes could force the Convention's hand when they marched, but they could not govern. They disagreed among themselves, and were suspicious of the Mountain's leaders even while backing them. Once the Committee of Public Safety had the army and the revolutionary tribunal, it used them and then controlled them: Jacques Roux was arrested in September 1793 and killed himself in prison in February 1794; the Hébertist leaders were executed in March 1794; and the section assemblies were brought under the Committee's control. By 1795 the sans-culottes had lost the argument.

:::key What 1793 meant for the key question
1. **Survival**: France faced invasion, civil war and economic collapse at the same time, so the Convention had little chance of governing normally.
2. **Division**: the Girondin–Mountain quarrel ended in the first purge of the elected Convention by a Paris crowd.
3. **Pressure from below**: the sans-culottes did not control the government, but they made it act, and they shaped its main policies (price controls, the Law of Suspects, an army of the people).
4. **Method**: the answer to every crisis became more centralised power and more fear.
:::

## The Constitution of 1793 and the revolutionary government

### A constitution that was never used

On **24 June 1793**, a few weeks after the purge of the Girondins, the Mountain approved a new constitution, to show that the Republic had a lasting democratic basis. The **Constitution of 1793** was the most democratic of the whole Revolution:
- all men aged 21 or over could vote, with no property test;
- the Declaration of Rights prefaced to it said that society owes work or support to citizens in need, and declared (Article 35) that when the government violates the people's rights, insurrection is "the most sacred of rights and the most indispensable of duties";
- it was approved by a referendum in July 1793 of about 1.8 million votes for and only about 12,000 against, although a large part of the electorate did not vote.

It was never applied. On **10 October 1793** the Convention, on the report of Saint-Just, declared the government of France "revolutionary until the peace", which suspended the constitution. The Constitution of 1793 remained a promise. The sans-culottes of 1795 would march under the slogan "bread and the Constitution of 1793".

### The Committee of Public Safety

The real government was a committee. The **Committee of Public Safety**, set up by the Convention on 6 April 1793 with nine members, was at first dominated by Danton and had little success. After a reorganisation in July 1793, when Danton was dropped, and with the arrival of **Robespierre** on 27 July, it grew to twelve members and became the executive of the Republic. Its members mostly kept to separate jobs:

| Member | Job on the Committee |
|---|---|
| Lazare Carnot | The army: recruitment, supply and strategy; later called the "organiser of victory" |
| Robert Lindet | Food supply and the economy |
| Bertrand Barère | Chief spokesman to the Convention |
| Maximilien Robespierre, Georges Couthon, Louis-Antoine Saint-Just | General policy and the defence of the Revolution; Saint-Just also spent long periods with the armies |
| Billaud-Varenne and Collot d'Herbois | Policing the provinces and the Terror |
| Jean Bon Saint-André, Prieur de la Côte-d'Or and others | The navy, arms production and other technical work |

The historian R.R. Palmer called them *Twelve Who Ruled* (1941) and argued that they were serious, hard-working men who ran a war government of great energy. They were elected by the Convention, which renewed their powers every month.

The Committee worked through other bodies:
- The **Committee of General Security** controlled the police and arrests.
- The **Revolutionary Tribunal**, created on 10 March 1793 and run by the public prosecutor Fouquier-Tinville, tried political crimes in Paris.
- **Representatives on mission** were deputies sent to the departments and armies with power to arrest, requisition and dismiss officials.
- **Revolutionary committees** in every commune and section drew up lists of suspects and checked the citizens' "certificates of civic virtue".
- The **Law of Revolutionary Government** (14 Frimaire, 4 December 1793) centralised everything: local officials were replaced by "national agents" who answered to the Committee, and the representatives on mission were brought under its control.
- The sans-culottes' **armées révolutionnaires**, paid bands sent to requisition grain in the countryside, were set up in September 1793 and abolished in the spring of 1794, when the Committee no longer wanted armed men it did not control.

### Winning the war

The revolutionary government was effective at what it was set up to do. The decree of **23 August 1793** (the *levée en masse*) declared that all the French were in permanent requisition for the armies until the enemy was driven out: young men would fight, married men forge arms and carry supplies, women make tents and clothes and nurse, and the old go to the public squares to encourage the soldiers. In practice the decree meant a call-up of unmarried men aged 18–25 and a vast effort to equip them. Carnot merged the old royal regiments with the volunteers (the *amalgame*), promoted officers by merit and enforced discipline. By 1794 the French had put about three-quarters of a million men in the field, the biggest army Europe had seen. Victories followed: Hondschoote (8 September 1793), Wattignies (October 1793), the recapture of Toulon in December 1793 (where a young artillery officer, Napoleon Bonaparte, made his name) and **Fleurus** on 26 June 1794, which reopened Belgium.

## Robespierre and the Terror

### Robespierre

**Maximilien Robespierre** was born in Arras in 1758. A lawyer, he was a deputy in the Estates-General and then a leader of the Jacobin Club in Paris. He was nicknamed "the Incorruptible" because he lived simply, took no bribes and spoke of virtue. He admired Rousseau's idea of the general will, and believed that a republic of equal citizens needed virtuous people to survive. He joined the Committee of Public Safety at 35 and became its best-known voice. The historian Peter McPhee, in *Robespierre: A Revolutionary Life* (2012), presents him as a committed believer in democracy rather than a cold dictator. Yet he never held a post above his colleagues. His power rested on his reputation with the Jacobins and the sans-culottes, and on his speeches in the Convention.

### The Terror: what it was and how it grew

:::def The Terror (the Reign of Terror)
The period, usually dated from September 1793 to July 1794, when the revolutionary government used mass arrests, special courts and the guillotine to crush opposition, in the belief that the Republic could be saved only by fear and justice carried out without delay.
:::

The Terror was not planned in one decree. It grew in steps, each one a response to a crisis:
- **March 1793**: the Revolutionary Tribunal, to try traitors and rebels.
- **5 September 1793**: under sans-culotte pressure the Convention declared, in the phrase of the day, that "terror" was the order of the day.
- **17 September 1793**: the **Law of Suspects** allowed the arrest of anyone who by conduct, remarks, writings or associations showed themselves "partisans of tyranny or federalism". It also covered nobles and relatives of émigrés who had not shown constant loyalty to the Revolution, and people who could not show a certificate of civic virtue. Because "suspect" meant almost anything, it put every French person at risk.
- **October 1793**: the trials of Marie Antoinette (executed 16 October) and of twenty-one leading Girondins (executed 31 October).
- **Autumn 1793 to spring 1794**: repression of the Vendée and the federalist cities. At Lyon, renamed *Ville-Affranchie* ("Freed Town"), hundreds were shot or guillotined; at Nantes, the representative Carrier ordered the drowning of prisoners in the Loire; in the Vendée, in early 1794, General Turreau's "infernal columns" marched through the countryside burning villages and killing the inhabitants, rebels and civilians alike. Historians' estimates of deaths in the war in the Vendée, on both sides and including civilians, run from about 150,000 to well over 200,000, and historians still debate the exact figures and how best to describe the repression, but not that it killed large numbers of civilians.
- **10 June 1794**: the **Law of 22 Prairial** began the **Great Terror** in Paris (see below).

### The revolutionary dechristianisation and the Cult of the Supreme Being

Part of the revolutionary government's drive for control was against the Church. In autumn 1793 representatives on mission and local militants closed churches, melted down bells, forced priests to resign, and encouraged clergy to marry. A new **revolutionary calendar** (adopted in October 1793, counting from 22 September 1792) replaced Sundays with a ten-day week and renamed the months (Thermidor, Prairial and so on). On 10 November a **Festival of Reason** was held in Notre-Dame cathedral. Many peasants and many sans-culottes were offended, and Robespierre disliked the campaign, which he thought gave the Republic's enemies a gift. In May 1794 he led the Convention in recognising the existence of a Supreme Being and the immortality of the soul, and on **8 June 1794** presided over the **Festival of the Supreme Being**. His enemies mocked him as a would-be high priest.

### Factions: left and right

By early 1794 the Committee faced critics on both sides. On the left stood the **Hébertists**, who wanted more terror, tighter price controls and more dechristianisation. On the right stood the **Indulgents**: Danton and Camille Desmoulins, whose newspaper *Le Vieux Cordelier* argued that the war was being won and the Terror should be relaxed. The Committee struck both. Hébert and other leaders of his group went to the guillotine on **24 March 1794**; Danton, Desmoulins and their allies on **5 April**. Robespierre had removed the two groups that threatened the government, but also two groups that had supported the Revolution from its start, and he was left with fewer friends. Deputies began to wonder who would be next.

### The Great Terror, June–July 1794

The **Law of 22 Prairial** (10 June 1794), proposed by Couthon and backed by Robespierre, simplified the Revolutionary Tribunal. It widened the definition of enemies of the people, removed defence counsel and witnesses where evidence was "moral", and allowed only two verdicts: acquittal or death. Deputies of the Convention could now be sent to the Tribunal without the Convention's vote (a clause the deputies quickly forced the Committees to withdraw). In the 47 days between 10 June and 27 July, about 1,400 people were guillotined in Paris, more than in the whole previous period of the Revolutionary Tribunal. The victims came from every class. Victory at Fleurus on 26 June made the law harder to defend: the enemy was no longer at the gates.

### The scale of the Terror

Historians use Donald Greer's study *The Incidence of the Terror* (1935) as the starting point. He counted about **16,600 death sentences** passed by tribunals and military commissions across France, which is the "about 17,000" in most books. Other points to remember:
- Paris accounted for only about 2,600 of the sentences, roughly one in six. Most were passed in the west and south-east, where the Republic was fighting civil war.
- Most of the victims were commoners: about 8 per cent were nobles and about 6.5 per cent clergy. Peasants and workers made up a large part of those executed, many for taking part in rebellion.
- Many more died without sentence: thousands in prisons from disease and hardship, and others in summary executions during the repression of the Vendée and Lyon. Total deaths are therefore much higher than 17,000. Estimates of deaths under the Terror, not counting the Vendée war, run to about 30,000–40,000 or more, and historians' totals differ with the evidence and definitions they use.
- Several hundred thousand people (a figure around 300,000 is often given) were arrested as suspects.

:::key Why the Terror happened: four explanations
1. **Emergency**: war, civil war and economic collapse in 1793 convinced the Convention that normal justice was too slow. This is the "circumstances" view, associated with historians such as Albert Mathiez, and with Palmer.
2. **Pressure from the sans-culottes**: they demanded terror against hoarders and suspects, and the Convention gave way. This is the emphasis in the work of Albert Soboul (*The Parisian Sans-culottes and the French Revolution*, 1958).
3. **Ideology**: Robespierre's belief that virtue needed terror, and the revolutionary idea of the people's will against its enemies, made repression seem right. François Furet, in *Interpreting the French Revolution* (1978), saw the Terror as growing from the logic of revolutionary ideas rather than only from circumstances.
4. **Power and fear**: the Committee used terror to keep control over rivals, and the fear of conspiracy was itself a cause of repression.
The best answers do not choose one: they argue which cause came first and which made the Terror last after the emergency of 1793 had passed.
:::

:::warn Keep the dates of the Terror exact
Say "September 1793 to July 1794" for the Terror and "10 June to 27 July 1794" for the Great Terror. Do not write that the Terror began with the execution of Louis XVI or that it ended with Robespierre's death on 28 July only: Thermidor ended the Committee's power, but the White Terror that followed killed people too. Never say that the guillotine was used only in Paris.
:::

## The economy under the Terror

The revolutionary government tried to deal with prices by law. On **29 September 1793** the Convention passed the **General Maximum**, which fixed the price of essential goods (bread, meat, wine, firewood, salt, soap, oil and so on) at one-third above their prices in 1790, and wages at one-half above their 1790 levels. The penalty for breaking it could be arrest as a suspect.

How did it work?
- **For the government**: the Maximum, together with requisitions of grain and goods for the armies, ensured that the army was fed and that the poor of Paris got bread, at a price they could pay, for several months. The state also set up arms workshops and gunpowder works: in 1794 a vast arms factory worked in Paris.
- **Against it**: Sellers hid goods or sold on the black market; peasants held back grain; shops emptied; and queues grew. The prices of goods were set against 1790, but the money in which they were paid kept losing value. Workers also disliked the wage limit, which lowered the earnings of many. In July 1794 the Paris Commune published a wage scale that cut pay in many trades, and it cost Robespierre's government working-class support just as Thermidor came.

When Robespierre fell, the Convention moved quickly to end controls. The Maximum was abolished on **24 December 1794**. The result was disaster in the winter of 1794–95, one of the coldest of the century: prices rose several times over, the assignat collapsed, and the poor of Paris went hungry. This was the setting for the risings of 1795.

:::key How well did the revolutionary government handle the economy?
- **Success**: the army was fed and equipped; the poor were protected for a time; fraud and speculation were punished.
- **Failure**: the Maximum could not be enforced without constant force, and the underlying problem (the falling currency and falling production) was untouched. Abolishing it in 1794 made things worse in the short term.
:::

## Thermidor and its aftermath, 1794–95

### The fall of Robespierre

By July 1794 the Great Terror had frightened the deputies, the military crisis had passed with Fleurus, and the Committee itself had split: Carnot, Billaud-Varenne and Collot d'Herbois quarrelled with Robespierre, and Robespierre stopped attending the Committee for several weeks. His enemies in the Convention, among them men whom he had threatened for corruption or cruelty in their missions (Tallien, Fouché, Fréron, Barras), feared they were on a list. In a speech on 26 July (8 Thermidor) he hinted at traitors in the Committees and the Convention without naming them. That night each deputy feared he was the target.

On **27 July (9 Thermidor)**, when Robespierre and Saint-Just rose to speak, the deputies shouted them down. The Convention ordered the arrest of Robespierre, his brother Augustin, Couthon, Saint-Just and Le Bas. The Paris Commune tried to rescue them and the leaders were freed and gathered at the Hôtel de Ville, but few sections answered the call: the sans-culottes, angry about wages and the executions, did not rise to defend their old ally. Troops loyal to the Convention under Barras entered the Hôtel de Ville that night. Robespierre was wounded in the jaw (whether he shot himself or was shot is uncertain). On **28 July** Robespierre, Saint-Just, Couthon and about twenty others were guillotined, with about seventy members of the Commune the next day.

### The Thermidorian reaction

The deputies who overthrew Robespierre were nearly all men who had taken part in the Terror. They blamed it on him alone, but they did dismantle the machinery of the revolutionary government:
- The Law of 22 Prairial was repealed (August 1794) and the Revolutionary Tribunal reformed.
- The Committee of Public Safety lost its powers and was made one of several committees, with a third of its members changing every month.
- The Paris Commune was abolished, and the power of the sections limited.
- The **Jacobin Club** was closed in November 1794; surviving Girondins were readmitted in December; prisoners were released; Carrier, who had ordered the drownings at Nantes, was tried and executed in December.
- Economic controls were scrapped (the Maximum, December 1794).

This was the **Thermidorian reaction**: a swing away from the Terror and the sans-culottes towards the propertied classes, who were now afraid of both the royalists and the Jacobins.

### The White Terror, 1794–95

Once the repression stopped, revenge began. The **White Terror** (white was the colour of the Bourbons, though not all those who carried it out were royalists) was a wave of violence against former Jacobins and "terrorists", mostly in 1795:
- In **Paris**, gangs of well-dressed young men, the *jeunesse dorée* ("gilded youth"), led by Fréron, beat up Jacobins and sans-culottes in the streets and broke into the Jacobin Club.
- In **Lyon, Marseille and the south-east**, groups such as the "Companions of Jesus" (or "of the Sun") killed local officials and Jacobins and massacred prisoners in the jails in the spring and summer of 1795. Authorities often looked the other way.
- In the west, royalist rebels (the *chouans*) revived their attacks, and in June–July 1795 a landing of émigré troops at Quiberon, backed by Britain, was defeated by General Hoche.

Historians cannot give a firm total of the victims of the White Terror, which was local, unofficial and poorly recorded: they ran to hundreds in the south-east, with higher estimates elsewhere. It differed from the Red Terror in being unofficial; but the government's failure to punish it showed how weak the Convention was.

### The Parisian risings of 1795

The winter of 1794–95 brought hunger. In Paris the price of bread rose out of reach, the daily ration fell, and many people died of cold and want. Two risings followed.

- **Germinal (1 April 1795).** Crowds invaded the Convention shouting for bread and the Constitution of 1793. The Convention declared a state of siege and the army cleared the streets without much resistance. Several Montagnard deputies were arrested.
- **Prairial (20–23 May 1795).** A larger rising. On 20 May the crowd broke into the hall and killed a deputy, Féraud, whose head was carried on a pike. The crowd's demands (bread, the Constitution of 1793, the release of arrested deputies) found some sympathy among the Montagnard deputies who remained, but the Convention called in regular troops and loyal national guards. The sections of the working-class faubourg Saint-Antoine were disarmed, and six Montagnard deputies were sentenced to death (some killed themselves).

The risings ended the sans-culottes as a political force. The government had shown that it would use troops against the Paris crowd, which could no longer claim to speak for the Revolution. The army became the final judge of political disputes in Paris, a lesson that the next regime would not forget.

:::key Instability, 1793–95: the pattern
1. **1793**: war, civil war and hunger lead to the Terror under the Committee of Public Safety.
2. **1794**: Thermidor ends the Committee's rule but not the instability. Revenge replaces repression (the White Terror).
3. **1795**: hunger in Paris produces the Germinal and Prairial risings, which the army defeats.
4. **Result**: by mid-1795 neither the Jacobins nor the crowd could rule, and the deputies of the Convention wanted a constitution that would keep both the crowd and the monarchy out.
:::

## The Directory, 1795–99

### The Constitution of the Year III

In the summer of 1795 the Convention wrote a new constitution, approved on **22 August 1795** and ratified by a plebiscite. It was the work of men who had lived through the Terror and the crowd's invasion of the Convention, and who feared dictatorship, the mob and a return of the monarchy.

The **Constitution of the Year III** set up:
- **Two councils** with the power to make laws. The **Council of Five Hundred** (members at least 30 years old) proposed laws; the **Council of Ancients** (250 members, at least 40 years old, married or widowed) approved or rejected them. One-third of each council was re-elected every year.
- **An executive Directory** of **five Directors**, chosen by the Ancients from a list drawn up by the Five Hundred. One Director retired each year. The Directors appointed the ministers and the commissioners who watched over local government. They could not propose laws, and could not dissolve the councils.
- **A property franchise.** Men over 21 who paid a direct tax (active citizens) voted in primary assemblies for **electors**, and only men who owned or rented property of a set value could be electors. The system favoured the propertied, and it ended the democratic suffrage of 1793.
- A **Declaration of Rights and Duties**, which put "duties" alongside rights, and kept the Republic.

:::def Directory
The executive government of France from 2 November 1795 to 9 November 1799 under the Constitution of the Year III: five Directors, chosen by the Council of Ancients from a list drawn up by the Council of Five Hundred, with a property-based electorate for the two councils.
:::

### The aims of the Directory

The authors of the constitution wanted:
- **To avoid dictatorship**: powers were split between two councils and five Directors, so that no person or committee could repeat the Committee of Public Safety.
- **To avoid mob rule**: the property franchise and the indirect election of the councils kept the sans-culottes out of politics.
- **To keep the Republic and the Revolution's gains** against royalists: the buyers of national lands, the former Jacobins and the Convention's own members all needed a regime that would keep the Bourbons out.
- **To bring back order and law**: an end to the Terror, to the revolutionary committees and to government by emergency.

### The two-thirds decree and Vendémiaire

The Convention feared that free elections would send royalists to the new councils. So it passed the **two-thirds decree** (August 1795), which said that two-thirds of the new councils' members must be chosen from the outgoing Convention. Royalists and many moderates in Paris called it a trick to keep the Convention's men in power. On **5 October 1795 (13 Vendémiaire)** royalist National Guards from the Paris sections marched on the Convention. The deputy Barras was put in charge of its defence, and he gave a command to a young general, **Napoleon Bonaparte**, aged 26, who had artillery brought up and swept the attackers from the streets near the church of Saint-Roch with cannon fire (later remembered as the "whiff of grapeshot"). The regime began with troops firing on Parisians, and with a general it would not be able to do without.

The first five Directors were Barras, Reubell, La Révellière-Lépeaux, Letourneur and Carnot, all former members of the Convention; every one had voted for the death of Louis XVI.

### Problems facing the Directory

:::key The Directory's problems
1. **Money**: the state was bankrupt in practice. The assignat was almost worthless, tax collection had collapsed in the confusion, and the war and the army cost far more than taxes brought in.
2. **War**: France was still fighting Britain and Austria. Armies needed pay and supplies, and generals were becoming powerful figures.
3. **Enemies on both sides**: royalists on the right, who won votes in 1797, and neo-Jacobins and radical egalitarians on the left, such as Babeuf. A regime built on the political middle had no solid support.
4. **A weak constitution**: no veto, no right of dissolution, no way to settle a conflict between executive and legislature; and annual elections for a third of the councils, so that every year brought a new contest.
5. **Legitimacy and order**: the regime was the child of the Convention and of force. Brigandage and royalist guerrilla war (the *chouannerie*) continued in the west and south, and the Church question (priests who refused the oath) remained unsolved.
:::

### Finance: success and failure

The Directory inherited a ruined currency. In 1795–96 it kept printing assignats until they were worth a tiny fraction of their face value, and in March 1796 it replaced them with *mandats territoriaux*, a new paper money tied to national lands, which also collapsed within months. In early 1797 it gave up paper money and returned to coins (silver and gold) as the basis of currency. This ended the inflation, but there was now too little money in circulation, and the state's debts were huge.

On **30 September 1797** the minister of finance, Ramel, with the Councils' approval, carried out the **bankruptcy of the two-thirds**: only one-third of the national debt was kept (and paid), and the other two-thirds were paid in bonds (*bons*) that could be used to buy national land and quickly lost most of their value. Rentiers, who lived on state interest, were ruined; but the state's yearly burden of interest fell sharply. The Directory also improved tax collection: in November 1797 it created a direct-tax agency in each department, and in 1798 it introduced new taxes such as the tax on doors and windows. The Consulate later built on this administration.

Yet its finances remained fragile. Tax arrears were large, officials and soldiers were paid late or not at all, and the state depended on **war booty**: in Italy, Bonaparte's army raised vast sums from the territories it conquered, and sent treasure and works of art to Paris. Peace would have cut off that money, which is one reason the Directory could not end the war.

### The army and the war

On the frontiers the Directory had many victories. In 1796–97 Bonaparte's campaign in northern Italy beat the Austrians (Lodi, Arcole, Rivoli) and led to the Treaty of **Campo Formio** (17 October 1797), which ended the war with Austria; only Britain remained at war. France set up sister republics in the Netherlands, Italy and Switzerland. But in 1798 Bonaparte sailed with an army to Egypt, where the British navy under Nelson destroyed the French fleet at the Nile (August 1798), and in 1799 the Second Coalition drove the French out of Italy and invaded Switzerland, until Masséna won at Zurich in September.

The impact inside France mattered more than the details of the battles:
- **Conscription.** The Jourdan law (5 September 1798) made military service compulsory for men aged 20–25 in peacetime as well as war.
- **The generals.** Successful commanders (Hoche, Bonaparte, Moreau, Augereau) became national heroes with armies loyal to them. The Directory used generals to protect itself and the generals learned that they could decide politics.
- **Money.** The Directory relied on conquests for revenue.

### Political instability: the three coups

The weakness of the constitution showed in the way the Directory dealt with its opponents: it kept breaking its own rules.

- **Babeuf's Conspiracy of the Equals (1796).** François-Noël (Gracchus) Babeuf, a journalist who had been a sharp critic of the Thermidorians, led a small group that wanted equality of goods, common ownership of land and a return to the Constitution of 1793. A text written by a writer close to them, Sylvain Maréchal, said that the French Revolution was only the forerunner of another and greater revolution. The group was betrayed to the government, and Babeuf was arrested on 10 May 1796 and executed in May 1797. The plot was tiny, but the Directory treated it as proof of a Jacobin danger.
- **Fructidor (4 September 1797).** In the elections of April 1797 royalists and conservatives won many seats. Three of the five Directors (Barras, Reubell and La Révellière) feared a royalist takeover, and with the help of troops sent by General Augereau they carried out the **coup of 18 Fructidor**. The elections in 49 departments were annulled, two Directors (Carnot and Barthélemy) and dozens of deputies and journalists were removed and deported or driven into hiding, and laws against émigrés and priests were reintroduced. The Directory had saved itself by force from the right.
- **Floréal (11 May 1798).** In the next elections neo-Jacobins did well. The Directors and the Councils annulled the results for more than a hundred seats, this time attacking the left.
- **Prairial (18 June 1799).** In 1799, after defeats in Italy and Switzerland, the Councils turned on the Directors, forcing three of them out (Treilhard, La Révellière and Merlin de Douai). Sieyès, who had entered the Directory in May 1799, was now its leading figure; he wanted a stronger executive and a new constitution, and he looked for a general to provide the force.

In all three cases the constitution was broken by those who ought to have defended it, and in the first the army was the decisive force. After Floréal the elections ceased to matter, because the Directory had shown that it would cancel any result it disliked.

### Corruption and public opinion

The Directory had an unfortunate reputation. Barras and some of the Directors lived in comfort; speculators, army contractors and financiers such as Ouvrard made fortunes; in Paris a society of display grew up in which wealthy people spent heavily. Poor people suffered from inflation and the loss of the protections of the Maximum. People reacted with weariness. Many respectable people were tired of coups and wanted order, and this feeling is the main reason Bonaparte's seizure of power in 1799 was accepted so easily.

### The record: success and failure

Historians such as Martyn Lyons (*France under the Directory*, 1975) have argued that the Directory did more than its reputation suggests.

| Area | Success | Failure |
|---|---|---|
| **Financial** | Paper money ended and a metal currency restored in 1797; the national debt cut by two-thirds in 1797; a direct-tax administration begun in 1797 | Rentiers ruined; tax arrears and unpaid officials and soldiers; dependence on war loot; no lasting solution |
| **Military** | Victory over Austria (Campo Formio, 1797); Zurich (September 1799) saved the frontier; the Jourdan law (1798) created a lasting method of conscription | Egypt disaster (1798); the loss of Italy in 1799; the war continued; the army's loyalty went to its generals, not to the government |
| **Political** | Held the middle ground between royalists and Jacobins for four years; no return to the Terror; most laws and local government kept going | Three coups (1797, 1798, 1799) broke the constitution; elections were cancelled when they went wrong; corruption; no popular support; the army decided who ruled |

:::key How well was France governed, 1793–99?
1. **The Committee of Public Safety** governed with great energy: it won the war and kept the Republic alive, but at the cost of the Terror and the end of normal law.
2. **The Thermidorian Convention** ended the Terror but could not feed Paris or stop the White Terror, and it relied on the army to survive the risings of 1795.
3. **The Directory** gave France four years without a return to the Terror and began a financial and administrative recovery, but it was never secure: it broke its own constitution three times, depended on generals, and was unpopular.
4. **Judgement**: neither system produced stable government. The Committee delivered survival by emergency; the Directory delivered survival by compromise and coups. Each ended with a general taking power.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the Committee of Public Safety introduced the Terror in 1793.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "In 1793 France was at war with many countries. There was a rebellion in the Vendée and a lot of people did not like the Convention. There were food shortages and the sans-culottes wanted prices to go down. The Committee of Public Safety had Robespierre on it, and it brought in the Law of Suspects and the guillotine."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but a list of things that were happening. The answer never explains how any of them led to the Terror: "and" replaces "because". Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The first reason was war. By the summer of 1793 France faced the First Coalition on every frontier, Dumouriez had gone over to the enemy and the fortresses of Mainz and Valenciennes had fallen. The Committee believed that traitors were helping the enemy, so it needed a way to punish treason quickly: the Law of Suspects of 17 September made almost anyone liable to arrest. A second reason was civil war. The rising in the Vendée from March and the federalist revolts at Lyon, Marseille and Toulon after the arrest of the Girondins meant that the Republic was fighting its own people, and the Committee used terror to crush these revolts."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained, with precise support (names, dates, the Law of Suspects), and each linked to the Terror. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the pressure from Paris and show how the factors work together:

"A third reason was pressure from the sans-culottes. The Paris crowd was hungry, because the assignat was losing value and grain was being held back, and it blamed hoarders and traitors. On 4–5 September 1793 a crowd marched on the Convention to demand a revolutionary army and measures against suspects, and the Convention responded by making terror 'the order of the day'. The Committee, which had come to power on the back of the purge of the Girondins on 2 June, could not afford to lose the support of Paris, so it adopted the sans-culottes' demands: the Law of Suspects, the General Maximum on 29 September and the revolutionary army.

These reasons were connected. War and civil war created the sense of emergency and the fear of conspiracy; hunger turned that fear into a popular demand for action; and the Committee, which depended on Paris for its power, turned the demand into law. The most important reason was the military and civil emergency, because without it neither the sans-culottes nor the Committee would have had an argument for suspending ordinary justice: by September 1793 the Republic was fighting on every frontier and in the west and south at once. But pressure from below decided the timing and the shape of the Terror."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (emergency produced fear; hunger and the crowd turned it into policy; the Committee's reliance on Paris made it give way), and a supported judgement about the main reason. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

If the spring of popular government in peacetime is virtue, the springs of popular government in revolution are virtue and terror together: virtue, without which terror is fatal; terror, without which virtue is powerless. Terror is nothing other than justice, prompt, severe and inflexible. It is therefore an emanation of virtue. The government of the Revolution is the despotism of liberty against tyranny.

*Adapted from a speech to the National Convention by Maximilien Robespierre, a member of the Committee of Public Safety, 5 February 1794.*

**Source B**

Liberty is only an empty phantom when one class of men can starve another with impunity. Equality is only an empty phantom when the rich, through monopoly, hold the power of life and death over their fellow citizens. The republic is only an empty phantom when the counter-revolution works every day through the price of food, which three-quarters of citizens cannot afford without tears. The Convention must strike at the speculators and hoarders.

*Adapted from a petition presented to the National Convention on 25 June 1793 by Jacques Roux, a radical priest, on behalf of the Cordeliers club and the Gravilliers section of Paris.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the purpose of revolutionary violence and government action in 1793–94.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is by Robespierre and says that terror is justice and is needed in a revolution. Source B is by Jacques Roux and says that the republic is an empty phantom when the rich starve the poor. They are different because A is about terror and B is about food. They are both speeches so they are biased."

*Commentary.* One difference, supported, but nothing on similarity, and "biased" is a general label with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources share the belief that the Revolution is in danger from enemies and that the government must act forcefully. Both speak in the language of principle: Source A says that 'virtue' and 'terror' belong together and that the Revolution is 'the despotism of liberty against tyranny', and Source B says that liberty and equality are 'empty phantoms' unless enemies are struck down. Both therefore see the use of the state's power against enemies as a way to defend liberty, not to destroy it.

They differ on who the enemy is and on what the purpose of force should be. In Source A the enemies are tyrants and conspirators in general, and terror is a permanent feature of revolutionary government, 'justice, prompt, severe and inflexible', applied by the government. In Source B the enemy is a specific one, the 'speculators and hoarders' who use 'the price of food' as a weapon against the people, and the demand is practical: the Convention must act on hunger. Source A's confidence is the confidence of a ruler justifying a system; Source B's anger is that of a critic who thinks the government is failing.

The context explains the difference. Source B was presented on 25 June 1793, the day after the Convention approved the Constitution of 1793 and three weeks after the Girondins were purged. Prices were rising, the Maximum had not yet been passed and the sans-culottes were pressing the Mountain to do more; Roux, one of the Enragés, was speaking for the Parisian poor from outside the government and attacking the Convention for neglecting them (he was arrested in September and died in prison). Source A comes eight months later, when the Committee of Public Safety controlled the government, had passed the Law of Suspects and the Maximum, and was fighting off both the Hébertists on the left, who wanted more terror, and the Indulgents on the right, who wanted less. Robespierre's purpose was to give the Terror a moral justification and to present the Committee's course as the correct middle line. So B is good evidence of the popular pressure that helped to bring the Terror about, and A is good evidence of how the leaders defined it once they had adopted it. Neither is neutral: B is a petition pressing a demand, A is a justification by a man in power."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences supported with quotation; the differences explained through the situation of each author (outside critic, ruler) and their purpose; a final comment on what each is good evidence for. A **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The Directory was a failure.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: three coups (Fructidor 1797, Floréal 1798, Prairial 1799) broke the constitution; the army decided politics; corruption and unpopularity; war went badly in 1798–99; unpaid officials; it collapsed in November 1799 with almost no resistance.
- Against: it avoided a return to the Terror and to monarchy for four years; ended paper money in 1797 and cut the debt; began direct-tax administration (1797); victories (Italy 1796–97, Campo Formio, Zurich 1799); the Jourdan law; the Consulate built on its foundations.
- Judgement: criterion = did it achieve what it was set up to do (a stable republic of the middle) and did it last? Judge: a partial success in finance and administration, a failure in politics.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The Directory was a failure because it could not keep power by legal means. In 1797 the Directors were afraid that the royalists who had won the elections would take over, so on 18 Fructidor they used troops to annul the elections in 49 departments and to remove two Directors. In 1798 they did the same to the neo-Jacobins at Floréal, and in 1799 the Councils forced out three Directors at Prairial. This shows that the constitution did not work, and so the Directory was a failure."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts. Other points are not mentioned. An answer made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet it would be wrong to call the Directory a complete failure, because in financial and administrative matters it did more than its reputation suggests. The Directory inherited worthless assignats and, after the failure of the *mandats territoriaux* in 1796, ended paper money in 1797 and returned to metal coin, which stopped the inflation that had ruined the poor since 1793. On 30 September 1797 it cancelled two-thirds of the national debt, sharply reducing the burden of interest, and in November 1797 it began a direct-tax service in the departments which the Consulate would use. On the frontiers it won the war against Austria, ending it at Campo Formio in October 1797, and in September 1799 Masséna's victory at Zurich saved the frontier after a bad summer. For four years it also prevented what many feared most, a return to the Terror or to the Bourbons. These were real successes, achieved in the most difficult conditions of any government of the Revolution.

But these successes were incomplete and dependent on the army. The bankruptcy of 1797 ruined rentiers, the tax system never produced enough to pay the soldiers and officials on time, and much of the revenue came from war booty, which meant that the Directory could not afford peace. More seriously, the constitution itself failed. Because the Directors could not dissolve the councils, and the councils could not remove the Directors, every quarrel between them was settled by force, as in 1797, or by intrigue, as in 1799. When the elections of 1797 and 1798 went against the government, it annulled them, which destroyed its claim to rule by consent. By 1799 it had no friends: the royalists and the Jacobins hated it, the propertied classes thought it corrupt, and Sieyès was looking for a general to overthrow it.

Overall, the Directory was a failure as a political system but not as a government of administration and finance. It is the political failure that matters more for the question, because a regime is judged first on whether it can keep power by legitimate means, and the Directory could not: it survived only by the army and fell to the army. But the label 'failure' is too simple: the Directory's financial and military successes explain why France was strong enough for Bonaparte to build on in 1799–1802."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph analyses a perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (can a regime keep power by legitimate means?) and separates political from financial and administrative failure. With an opening paragraph on the case for failure, this is Level 5 (18–20).

Question: "How far did the sans-culottes control government policy in 1793–94?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The sans-culottes were the ordinary people of Paris who wore trousers and not knee-breeches. They wanted lower prices. On 4–5 September 1793 they marched to the Convention and demanded action. **The level.** Accurate and relevant detail, but it is a list of demands and results, and the conclusion is asserted. It does not ask whether the sans-culottes forced these laws or whether the Committee wanted them anyway, and it ignores what happened later (the arrest of Roux, the execution of Hébert, the abolition of the revolutionary army in 1794). It reaches Level 2 (5–8): relevant description, few explicit links to the question, and one side only.

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use the detail as proof, and test it. "The sans-culottes had great influence in September 1793 but did not control policy. On 5 September the crowd forced the Convention to put terror on the agenda, and the Law of Suspects and the Maximum followed. But the Committee had its own reasons for each: the Law of Suspects answered the fear of treason after Toulon, and the Maximum guaranteed food for the army. Once the Committee felt secure it turned on its allies: Roux was arrested in September 1793, the revolutionary armies were abolished in the spring of 1794, and Hébert and his friends were executed on 24 March. So the sans-culottes could push the government when it was weak, but it controlled them when it was strong." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

Q: What was the Law of Suspects, and why did it matter?
A: A law of 17 September 1793 that allowed the arrest of anyone who by conduct, remarks, writings or associations seemed an enemy of the Revolution, as well as nobles and relatives of émigrés without proof of loyalty. It mattered because "suspect" had no clear limit, so the law made every citizen liable to arrest and launched mass arrests.
Q: What did the General Maximum do, and why did it fail?
A: On 29 September 1793 it fixed prices of essential goods at one-third above their 1790 level and wages at one-half above. It failed because goods disappeared or were sold on a black market, and because the money kept losing value; workers also resented the wage limit. It was abolished on 24 December 1794.
Q: Why did Robespierre fall on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794)?
A: Deputies feared they were next after the Law of 22 Prairial and his speech on 26 July hinting at traitors; the military emergency had eased after Fleurus; the Committee was split; and the Paris workers, angered by wage cuts, did not rise to defend him.
Q: What were the Germinal and Prairial risings of 1795, and what was their result?
A: Hunger-driven risings in Paris on 1 April and 20–23 May 1795, when crowds invaded the Convention demanding bread and the Constitution of 1793. Troops put them down, the faubourgs were disarmed and the sans-culottes ceased to be a political force.
Q: Describe the structure of government under the Constitution of the Year III.
A: Two councils (Five Hundred proposed laws; Ancients, 250 members, approved or rejected them) and an executive Directory of five Directors chosen by the Ancients from a list from the Five Hundred, one retiring each year, elected through a property franchise with indirect elections.
Q: What was the two-thirds decree, and what happened on 13 Vendémiaire?
A: Two-thirds of the members of the new councils had to come from the outgoing Convention. Royalists in the Paris sections rose against it on 5 October 1795 and were beaten by troops under Barras and Bonaparte.
Q: What happened at Fructidor in 1797, and why?
A: On 4 September 1797 the Directors Barras, Reubell and La Révellière, supported by troops, annulled the elections in 49 departments and removed two Directors and many deputies, because royalists had won the spring elections.
Q: What was the "bankruptcy of the two-thirds", and what did it achieve?
A: On 30 September 1797 the Directory paid only one-third of the national debt in cash and the other two-thirds in bonds that soon lost most of their value. It cut the state's annual burden of interest but ruined bondholders.
