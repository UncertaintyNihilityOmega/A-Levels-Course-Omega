This mock prints all three questions so that every student can sit the one on the topic they have studied. Sit **one** question only, in 75 minutes, with a pen and paper and nothing else: no notes, no textbook, no timeline. If you want more practice, sit the others on another day.

Q: [40 marks] (#hist.interp.ww1.2) **Section A: The origins of the First World War.** Read this extract and then answer the question that follows it.

*Practice extract A. Original writing for this mock, in the manner of a historian. It is not the work of any real historian.*

We still speak of 1914 as if it were a trial, with a defendant waiting to be found guilty. The search has produced more heat than light, because it begins from a mistake about how this war started. By the last days of July the statesmen of Europe were no longer deciding; they were being carried. Each Great Power had spent a generation preparing a plan for a war that none of them wanted, and every plan depended on speed. An army that waited for its enemy to move first would lose the race to the frontier. Mobilisation, which had once been a warning, had become the first move of the war itself, and a government that ordered it could not afterwards call it back.

Consider what the plans demanded. The German staff had to beat France in a few weeks, by marching through Belgium, before Russia's slow-moving millions could arrive. The Russian staff had prepared to mobilise against Germany and Austria-Hungary together. The French plan was an immediate offensive. Each timetable was drawn up in peacetime by technicians, and each fitted into the others like the parts of a clock. When Russia mobilised on 30 July, it was not announcing a plan of conquest; it was protecting itself, as its generals told the Tsar it must, since a partial mobilisation could not be arranged. When Germany sent its ultimatums on 31 July, it was doing what its own timetable required. On 1 August the Kaiser, hoping that Britain might hold France back, asked his Chief of Staff to send the armies east only. He was told that it could not be done. The statesman had asked for a choice, and the soldier showed him a railway map.

None of this makes the leaders wicked, and none of it makes them blameless. It makes them subjects of a system that they had accepted without examining. They had handed the planning of war to the soldiers, and when the crisis came the soldiers' plans were the only plans there were. A diplomatic settlement was possible only so long as it could be reached before the trains began to run. The days between Sarajevo and the first order to mobilise were not wasted, but they were few, and the ministers used them as if there were more.

The lesson is for those who prefer a villain. No government in 1914 meant to begin the war that began. It arrived because the instruments of peace could not be set against the instruments of war once the second had been wound up. If we wish to know who was to blame, we shall do better to ask who built the machine.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer. [40]

A: **Mark scheme: Section A, practice extract A.** One answer, marked twice: Table A (AO4, interpretation and approach) out of 20 and Table B (AO1, knowledge) out of 20. Accept any other valid response that is supported from the extract.

**The main interpretation, in two linked aspects.**
- **Aspect 1: the war came from the military plans and their timetables.** Once the crisis reached mobilisation, the plans (the German need to beat France quickly through Belgium, the Russian plan to mobilise against Germany and Austria-Hungary together, the French offensive) made war close to automatic. Mobilisation had become the first move of the war, could not be recalled, and left the statesmen only a few days to find a diplomatic way out.
- **Aspect 2: so the search for a guilty government is a mistake.** No government meant to begin the war that began. The leaders were responsible for accepting a system they had not examined, and for handing the planning of war to the soldiers, not for planning aggression. The question "who was to blame?" should become "who built the machine?".
- **Together:** the cause of 1914 was a system of plans that outran the control of the men who had approved them, and so the verdict of guilt, on Germany or on anyone, misreads how the war began. Explaining both aspects, and that they are one argument, reaches the top of Table A. Explaining one reaches Level 5.

**Sub-messages (they support the main interpretation; a student who treats one as the main message is at Level 4 or Level 3).** Mobilisation had become the first move of the war. Russia mobilised to protect itself, because partial mobilisation could not be arranged. The German plan needed speed and a march through Belgium. The Kaiser's request on 1 August to send the armies east only was refused, because the deployment could not be changed. The leaders had handed the planning of war to the soldiers. The days between Sarajevo and the first mobilisation were few. Those who want a villain are mistaken.

**The approach.** The historian explains events through impersonal structures (plans, railways, timetables) more than through the aims of individuals; concentrates on the last days of July and on military planning, not on the long causes or on diplomacy; is sceptical of moral judgement and of the idea of a trial; writes with irony and with images (a clock, a machine, a railway map) that suggest a determinist reading in which the room for choice shrinks as the crisis goes on. Approach and interpretation are related: a historian who looks at the machinery will find a system, and one who looks at the leaders will find choices. The extract's concern with systems that escape human control may suggest a writer of the nuclear age, but the mark scheme does not require that inference.

**Labels.** The extract gives a structural, "war by timetable" explanation (the phrase and the argument are A. J. P. Taylor's, 1969; naming him is not required, but the label **structural** or **determinist** is earned if explained and supported, for example from "being carried", "could not afterwards call it back" and "the soldiers' plans were the only plans"). **Anti-Fischer** or **revisionist** is credited only in part: the extract rejects the trial model and the question of guilt, but it does not defend German aims or say that Germany was innocent. **Shared responsibility** fits in part, because no one government is guilty, but its cause is plans, not the conduct of several powers. **Fischer thesis, "Germany guilty"** cannot be credited as the extract's own view: it is the view that the extract rejects. A label that is only named, with no support from the extract, earns nothing.

**Knowledge that supports the claims (Table B).**
- *Aspect 1.* The Schlieffen–Moltke plan (Schlieffen's memorandum of 1905, modified by Moltke): defeat France in about six weeks through Belgium, then turn east; Russia's Plan 19 (revised 1912), mobilisation against both Germany and Austria-Hungary; France's Plan XVII (1913), an offensive on its German frontier; Russian partial mobilisation announced on 29 July, and general mobilisation ordered on 30 July (the Tsar cancelled and then confirmed the order under pressure from Sazonov and the generals) and effective on 31 July, the generals having said that partial mobilisation was technically difficult; the German "state of imminent danger of war" and the ultimatums of 31 July (twelve hours to Russia; France asked whether it would stay neutral); declarations of war on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August); the Kaiser and Moltke on 1 August, after a telegram from London (Lichnowsky) that suggested Britain might keep France neutral: Moltke said that the deployment to the west could not be turned to the east without chaos; the "Willy–Nicky" telegrams of 29 July to 1 August.
- *Aspect 2.* No government planned this war for 1914: Bethmann Hollweg's "calculated risk" in the hope of a local war; the Kaiser's "halt in Belgrade" proposal of 28 July and Bethmann's pressure on Vienna to negotiate on 29 and 30 July; Britain's Cabinet, divided until the ultimatum to Belgium of 2 August, and the declaration of war on 4 August; the alliances were defensive and each power decided for itself.

**Knowledge that tests the claims (a Level 6 answer uses some, and says where the extract is stretched).**
- *On aspect 1.* The timetables did not start the crisis: Austria-Hungary decided to act against Serbia after Sarajevo (28 June), had the blank cheque of 5–6 July, sent its ultimatum on 23 July with 48 hours to answer, rejected Serbia's reply of 25 July and declared war on 28 July, before Russia mobilised. The plans themselves were choices: Moltke the Younger dropped Germany's eastern-deployment alternative in 1913, and the plan's march through Belgium meant a war with Britain (the Treaty of London of 1839). Moltke's own remarks at the War Council of 8 December 1912 ("the sooner the better") and his urging of Austria on 30 July to mobilise against Russia show a soldier who pressed for war, not only a technician; the account of 1 August comes largely from Moltke himself. Bethmann Hollweg and the Kaiser could have given different orders on 29–30 July: the pressure on Vienna shows that political choices were still open. Russia's partial mobilisation of 29 July and Sazonov's pressure on the Tsar were political decisions.
- *On aspect 2.* The extract's refusal of blame is stretched by the blank cheque, the German ultimatums and the Fischer argument (the army law of 1913, the September Programme of 9 September 1914), which say that Germany's leaders knowingly accepted a continental war, and by Williamson's case that Vienna chose war to save the Empire. Britain was not subject to the timetables at all: its decision turned on Belgium, the military conversations with France since 1906 and the naval agreement of 1912.

**What each level looks like for this extract.**
- *Table A, Level 6 (18–20):* states the whole interpretation with both aspects and that they are one argument (a system of plans produced the war, so the search for a guilty government is mistaken); uses the extract's words; explains the approach (impersonal, structural, concentrated on the last days, sceptical of blame) and links it to the interpretation; earns or refuses the labels with support.
- *Level 5 (15–17):* explains one aspect fully and the other less well, or both without showing they are one argument; approach thin.
- *Level 4 (12–14):* partial understanding. A typical answer treats the Kaiser and Moltke exchange or the Russian mobilisation as the main point, or says only that the historian "does not blame anyone", without the explanation from the plans; or treats it as a Fischer-style or "Germany guilty" extract.
- *Level 3 (9–11):* explains sub-messages only, or builds a different interpretation: "the historian blames Moltke or the generals" (the extract blames the system and those who accepted it), or "the historian thinks the war was an accident" (the extract says it arrived by plans, which were built).
- *Levels 2 and 1 (1–8):* summary of the paragraphs, or references to some points.
- *Table B:* Level 6 (18–20) needs detailed accurate knowledge, entirely relevant, used to explain and test; Level 4 (12–14) is accurate but narrative or partly irrelevant (the July Crisis told as a list of dates); Level 2 (5–8) is some accurate knowledge, not linked.

**Traps.** Telling the story of July 1914 instead of testing a claim. Saying the extract is "wrong" or "right": the question is what you can learn about the historian. Reading the one anecdote (the Kaiser and the railway map) as the main message. Treating "Fischer" as a label for the extract. Saying that the historian claims that nobody is responsible: the extract says that the leaders accepted the system.

**A Level 6 opening (about 150 words).** "The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. It claims that the war was produced by the military plans of the Great Powers, whose timetables made mobilisation the first move of the war and left statesmen only a few days, and it claims that, because no government meant to begin this war, the search for a guilty party is mistaken: the question is who built the machine. The first aspect rests on the Russian mobilisation of 30 July, the German ultimatums of the next day and the Chief of Staff's refusal on 1 August to send the armies east only. But the claim is stretched at its start. Austria-Hungary's ultimatum of 23 July and declaration of war on 28 July, after the blank cheque of 5 to 6 July, were decisions, not timetables, and the plan that required the invasion of Belgium was a choice, made when Germany dropped its eastern alternative in 1913. The plans narrowed the choices; they did not make them."

Q: [40 marks] (#hist.interp.holocaust.2) **Section B: The Holocaust.** Read this extract and then answer the question that follows it.

*Practice extract B. Original writing for this mock, in the manner of a historian. It is not the work of any real historian.*

For a generation the history of the destruction of the Jews of Europe was written as a German story. It was told through Hitler's speeches, the memoranda of the SS and the decisions taken in Berlin, and it left the picture of a machine that ran in one direction with only German hands on the levers. The records of the occupied and allied countries tell a different story. The Germans did not have the men, or the knowledge of local languages, streets and neighbours, to do what was done. In France it was French police who carried out the round-up in Paris on 16 and 17 July 1942, and the Vichy government had passed its own statute against the Jews in October 1940 without being asked. In Slovakia the government agreed in March 1942 to the deportation of its Jews, and more than 57,000 were sent away that year. In Hungary, after the German occupation of 19 March 1944, it was the Hungarian gendarmerie that carried out the round-ups, and about 440,000 people were put on trains in under eight weeks.

These were not all men forced at gunpoint, even if some were pressed. Each government and each police force had its reasons: old hatreds, ambition, the wish to please a powerful neighbour, greed for property, the fear of being thought unreliable. A history that explains the killing by German policy alone cannot account for how much of the work was done by others, or for the speed with which it was done.

Yet it is equally clear that the outcome was not the same everywhere, and the difference was not made in Berlin. German pressure was constant and much alike in every country. In Denmark, where until the late summer of 1943 the government had kept control of its own administration and where the public made plain its hostility to persecution, more than 7,000 Jews were ferried to Sweden in October 1943, after a German official had let the date of the round-up be known. Bulgaria's government deported more than 11,000 Jews from the territories it had occupied, in Thrace and Macedonia, in March 1943, but refused to hand over the Jews of Bulgaria itself. What differed was the local political situation: whether a government still had room to say no, whether it saw advantage in saying yes, and whether its public cared.

The Holocaust was a European event, and Europeans made choices. A history that begins and ends in Berlin lets them off, and it cannot explain why some Jews lived and others died.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer. [40]

A: **Mark scheme: Section B, practice extract B.** One answer, marked twice: Table A (AO4) out of 20 and Table B (AO1) out of 20. Accept any other valid response that is supported from the extract.

**The main interpretation, in two linked aspects.**
- **Aspect 1: the Holocaust depended on non-Germans.** It was not done by Germans alone. Governments, police forces and neighbours in occupied and allied countries carried out much of the work, for their own reasons (old hatreds, ambition, pleasing a powerful neighbour, greed, fear), so a German-centred story cannot explain its scale or speed.
- **Aspect 2: outcomes differed because of local conditions, not German pressure.** Pressure was much alike everywhere; what decided whether Jews lived or died was whether a government had room to refuse, saw advantage in agreeing, and had a public that cared (Denmark and Bulgaria against France, Slovakia and Hungary).
- **Together:** the Holocaust was a European event in which Europeans made choices; responsibility, and the explanation of survival, lie in many capitals and towns, not only in Berlin. Both aspects, as one argument about local agency, reach the top of Table A.

**Sub-messages.** The writing of the history as a German story for a generation; the numbers of Germans were too few; each country's reasons differed; the figures for Denmark, Bulgaria, Slovakia and Hungary; German pressure was "constant and much alike". A student who takes the first sentence ("a German story") as the whole argument has found only a sub-message.

**The approach.** The historian works from the records of the occupied and allied countries, and criticises earlier historians for reading only German documents. The method is comparison between countries, using contrasting cases to isolate what made the difference. The interest is in agency and responsibility outside Germany and in the question "why did some live and others die?", more than in the origin of the policy. The extract implies a historian writing after the archives of those countries were opened (it says the records "tell a different story"), though the mark scheme does not require a date. Approach and interpretation are linked: a historian who looks at the local archives and compares countries will find local choices.

**Labels.** The extract is about how the killing was carried out and why outcomes varied, not about how the policy began. **Intentionalism, functionalism and structuralism are not about this question and cannot be credited as the extract's label**: nothing in it mentions Hitler's role, a plan, or the nature of the Nazi state. A student who forces one loses credit. It is also not **Goldhagen-style "eliminationist antisemitism"**: the extract lists several reasons, and old hatred is only one of them. "Perpetrator-centred", "comparative" or "European" are descriptions of the approach, credited if supported, not school labels.

**Knowledge that supports the claims (Table B).**
- *Aspect 1.* Vichy's statute of October 1940; the round-up of 16 and 17 July 1942, when French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in Paris, about 4,000 of them children; Slovakia's agreement of March 1942 and more than 57,000 deported that year; Hungary, occupied on 19 March 1944, with about 440,000 deported between 15 May and 9 July 1944 by the gendarmerie under German supervision (Eichmann's small team); Romania, where soldiers and civilians murdered thousands of Jews at Iași at the end of June 1941 and where, on the figure of the international commission of 2004, between 280,000 and 380,000 Romanian and Ukrainian Jews were murdered under Antonescu; the Ustaša in Croatia; Lithuanian and Latvian auxiliaries and the Jäger report of 1 December 1941 (137,346 killed in Lithuania); Jedwabne on 10 July 1941 and the debate over Jan Gross's account.
- *Aspect 2.* Denmark in October 1943 (more than 7,000 ferried to Sweden); Bulgaria's refusal to deport its own Jews in March 1943 but deportation of more than 11,000 from Thrace and Macedonia; Horthy's halt to deportations from Hungary in July 1944; the Italian army's refusal to hand over Jews in its zones of occupation before September 1943.

**Knowledge that tests the claims (a Level 6 answer uses some).**
- German decisions set the policy and German power stood behind every deportation: the Wannsee Conference of 20 January 1942, the Einsatzgruppen, the Reinhard camps of Belzec, Sobibor and Treblinka and Auschwitz-Birkenau (run by the SS, with Trawniki guards in some), most of the murder in occupied Poland; the Hungarian deportations began only after the German occupation, and Slovakia and Vichy France acted under German pressure and presence.
- The extract says German pressure was "much alike"; but Germany's leverage differed (Denmark was occupied but treated as a model protectorate until August 1943; Bulgaria and Hungary were allies; Poland was ruled directly), so the contrast may be partly a contrast in German power and priorities.
- Even where local help was freely given, the Germans created the conditions (invasion, the war in the East) in which it was offered.

**What each level looks like for this extract.**
- *Table A, Level 6 (18–20):* states both aspects as one argument about European agency and local conditions; explains the comparative, local-archive approach and what it leaves out (the origin of the policy, the German decisions); refuses the labels that do not fit; supports every claim with the extract's words.
- *Level 5 (15–17):* explains one aspect (usually "non-Germans helped") fully, the second less well or without linking.
- *Level 4 (12–14):* treats "it was a German story for a generation" or the Danish example as the main message; partial understanding.
- *Level 3 (9–11):* builds a different interpretation: "the historian says Europeans were antisemitic and so caused the Holocaust" or "the historian says the Germans were not responsible", or uses the Danish rescue to argue about resistance; or gives a different reading in each paragraph.
- *Levels 2 and 1 (1–8):* summary of the three paragraphs.
- *Table B:* Level 6 (18–20) uses the country cases accurately to explain and test the two aspects; Level 4 (12–14) is accurate but mostly the story of the Holocaust in general; Level 2 (5–8) is some correct detail, not linked.

**Traps.** Forcing intentionalism or functionalism onto an extract that is about local help. Reading "non-Germans helped" as "non-Germans were to blame instead of Germans". Treating Denmark as the only evidence. Giving figures with no source or the wrong country (Slovakia is not Hungary).

**A Level 6 opening (about 120 words).** "The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. It claims that the Holocaust could not have been carried out by Germans alone, because governments, police and neighbours in occupied and allied countries did much of the work for reasons of their own, and it claims that whether Jews lived or died depended less on German pressure, which was much alike everywhere, than on local conditions. The historian therefore moves responsibility, and the explanation of survival, from Berlin to many places. The first aspect fits what is known: French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in Paris on 16 and 17 July 1942, and Hungary's gendarmes helped to deport about 440,000 people in under eight weeks in 1944. But the extract says little about the German decisions, such as Wannsee on 20 January 1942, which gave those police their orders."

Q: [40 marks] (#hist.interp.coldwar.2) **Section C: The origins and development of the Cold War.** Read this extract and then answer the question that follows it.

*Practice extract C. Original writing for this mock, in the manner of a historian. It is not the work of any real historian.*

The usual question about the beginning of the Cold War is who pushed whom: whether Soviet expansion forced the Americans to act, or American ambition forced the Russians to defend themselves. Both answers treat Europe as a stage on which two great powers performed. That is a mistake. Look at who asked for what. In February 1947 it was the British government, near to bankruptcy after a hard winter, which told Washington that it could no longer support Greece and Turkey. In June 1947 General Marshall made an offer, but it was the foreign ministers of Britain and France who turned it into a conference, who invited Molotov, and who built from the response a plan for sixteen countries. In the winter of 1947 and 1948 it was Ernest Bevin who pressed for a western union and then for an American commitment to it. The Brussels Treaty of March 1948 was signed by five European states before the Americans had committed themselves to anything, and the North Atlantic Treaty was the answer to their request. The Americans were not dragged into Europe, and they were not simply pushing. They were invited.

Why did the Europeans ask? They feared Soviet power, and they feared no less that the United States would leave Europe, as it had left in 1919, and that Germany would rise again. A permanent American presence was a protection against both. This changes the question of blame. In the east the Soviet Union also built a sphere of influence, but there it had to impose what in the west was sought. The two halves of the continent were divided not by a single decision in Moscow or in Washington but by the choices of smaller states and by the fears that each side's defensive acts aroused in the other. Neither superpower wanted the division of Germany, and each took steps against the other's advantage that made it certain.

Historians who work in the papers of one capital find the plan of one power. Those who read the papers of the European foreign ministries find a continent that helped to make the order under which it lived. The Cold War in Europe was not made for Europeans. It was made, in part, by them.

What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins and development of the Cold War to explain your answer. [40]

A: **Mark scheme: Section C, practice extract C.** One answer, marked twice: Table A (AO4) out of 20 and Table B (AO1) out of 20. Accept any other valid response that is supported from the extract.

**The main interpretation, in two linked aspects.**
- **Aspect 1: the American presence in western Europe came by European invitation.** Britain, France and the other western states asked for American help (over Greece and Turkey, the Marshall offer, a western union and then NATO), because they feared Soviet power and a withdrawal of the United States, and a revived Germany. Europe was an agent, not a stage.
- **Aspect 2: blame cannot be placed on one superpower.** The division of Europe, and of Germany, came from the interaction of the choices of smaller states and of defensive steps that each side took and the other feared; neither superpower wanted a divided Germany, yet their actions made it certain.
- **Together:** the Cold War in Europe was a shared and interactive product, in which the Europeans themselves played a part; the question "who is to blame?" (Soviet expansion or American ambition) is the wrong one. Both aspects, as one argument, reach the top of Table A.

**Sub-messages.** Britain's notice on Greece and Turkey in February 1947; the foreign ministers of Britain and France made the Marshall offer into a plan; the Brussels Treaty preceded any American commitment; the Soviet Union imposed what the west sought; the fear of an American departure. A student who treats the first paragraph's list of dates as the main message has found only support for aspect 1.

**The approach.** The historian rejects the great-power framing of earlier debate (the "who pushed whom" question) and starts from the actions of smaller states; uses the papers of the European foreign ministries, not only American or Soviet records; is interested in agency, in security fears on both sides and in the interaction of defensive acts (the idea of a security dilemma); and is more concerned with how an order was built than with who was guilty. The extract's last sentences suggest a historian who thinks the earlier schools shared a blind spot. Approach and interpretation are linked: one who reads European papers will find European initiative.

**Labels.** The extract spreads responsibility to both superpowers and to the Europeans, and sees fear and defence on both sides, so **post-revisionist** is earned if explained and supported ("neither superpower wanted the division of Germany"; "defensive acts"). The "empire by invitation" idea can be named only as a description if the candidate knows it, with support from the extract. **Traditionalist** (blaming Soviet expansion) and **revisionist** (blaming American economic expansion) are what the extract's first sentences set aside; they cannot be credited as its view. **"New Cold War history"** (post-post-revisionism, built on the opened Soviet archives) is not supported by anything in the extract, which refers to European, not Soviet, papers, and should not be credited.

**Knowledge that supports the claims (Table B).**
- *Aspect 1.* Britain's notes to Washington of 21 February 1947 and the Truman Doctrine of 12 March 1947 (400 million dollars to Greece and Turkey); Marshall's speech of 5 June 1947; the Paris conference of 27 June to 2 July 1947, called by Bevin and Bidault, which Molotov left on 2 July; sixteen countries meeting from 12 July; about 13 billion dollars of aid in 1948–52; Bevin's proposal of a "Western Union" in January 1948; the Brussels Treaty of 17 March 1948 (Britain, France, Belgium, the Netherlands, Luxembourg); the Vandenberg Resolution of 11 June 1948 (which let the USA join regional defence agreements); the North Atlantic Treaty of 4 April 1949 (twelve states).
- *Aspect 2.* Soviet fears of a revived Germany and of the Marshall Plan; American currency reform in the western zones in June 1948 and the Soviet blockade of Berlin (24 June 1948 to 12 May 1949); the creation of the Federal Republic (23 May 1949) and the German Democratic Republic (7 October 1949); Soviet and American security arguments (the Cominform and the "two camps" speech of September 1947 against the Truman Doctrine).

**Knowledge that tests the claims (a Level 6 answer uses some).**
- *On invitation.* The invitation was not between equals: Britain, near to bankruptcy, and the rest of western Europe depended on American money and short of dollars; the Marshall Plan carried American conditions and aims (open markets, a revived western Germany), as revisionists stress; the Truman Doctrine speech of 12 March 1947 set out a world-wide ideological commitment that no European had requested.
- *On the east.* The Soviet Union's actions pushed the Europeans towards America: the coup in Czechoslovakia of 20 to 25 February 1948 prompted the Brussels Treaty and the vote for Marshall aid; Czechoslovakia and Poland accepted the invitation to Paris and then withdrew under Soviet pressure in July 1947; elections in Poland in January 1947 were rigged; the Soviet Union's control of eastern Europe was imposed, as the extract says, and so the "shared" blame may be less equal than the extract suggests.
- *On Germany.* The Western powers' decisions on Germany at the London conferences of 1948 were taken largely without Soviet agreement and so can be said to have made division certain; Soviet obstruction in the Allied Control Council and reparations from the eastern zone worked in the same direction.

**What each level looks like for this extract.**
- *Table A, Level 6 (18–20):* states both aspects as one argument (a European-made order, not a superpower's plan) and the rejection of the "who pushed whom" frame; explains the approach and its link to the interpretation; supports the label "post-revisionist" from the extract and refuses the others.
- *Level 5 (15–17):* explains invitation fully and shared responsibility less well, or the reverse.
- *Level 4 (12–14):* partial understanding. A typical answer treats the Marshall Plan or NATO story as the whole message, or reads it as blaming the Soviet Union because Europeans "feared" it.
- *Level 3 (9–11):* builds a different interpretation: "the historian is a traditionalist who blames Stalin" or "a revisionist who blames America", using a sub-message; or reads one interpretation in each paragraph.
- *Levels 2 and 1 (1–8):* summary of the three paragraphs.
- *Table B:* Level 6 (18–20) uses the events of 1947–49 accurately to explain and test; Level 4 (12–14) is accurate but a general narrative of the origins of the Cold War; Level 2 (5–8) some correct detail, not linked.

**Traps.** Naming "revisionism" or "traditionalism" because the topic is the Cold War, with no support from the extract. Narrating Yalta and Potsdam, which the extract does not touch. Reading "they were invited" as "so the Americans were innocent". Confusing the Truman Doctrine (March 1947) with the Marshall Plan (June 1947).

**A Level 6 opening (about 120 words).** "The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. It claims that the American presence in western Europe came because Europeans asked for it, out of fear of Soviet power, of an American departure and of Germany, and it claims that the division of Europe cannot be blamed on one superpower, since it came from the defensive acts of both and from the choices of smaller states. The historian therefore rejects the question 'who pushed whom?' and treats Europe as an agent. The first aspect is supported by the notes that Britain sent to Washington on 21 February 1947, by Bevin and Bidault's calling of the Paris conference in June 1947 and by the Brussels Treaty of March 1948, signed before any American commitment. But the extract is silent on the American aims that shaped the answer, such as the open markets that revisionists stress."
:::

## After the paper: mark it, then use the result

**1. Mark Table A first, then Table B.** Read your answer against the scheme for your section. For Table A ask only: did I state the whole interpretation, with both aspects, and say they are one argument? Then: did I explain the approach? Then pick the level. For Table B ask: was my knowledge accurate, detailed and relevant, and did I use it to explain and test the extract? Then pick that level. Mark the lower of two levels if you are between them. The page lets you enter your mark.

| Level | Table A (AO4): interpretation and approach | Marks | Table B (AO1): knowledge | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6 | complete understanding of the interpretation and the approach; explains all elements | 18–20 | detailed, accurate and entirely relevant | 18–20 |
| 5 | sound understanding; engages with elements without explaining the interpretation as a whole | 15–17 | detailed and mostly accurate, mainly relevant | 15–17 |
| 4 | partial understanding; identifies elements without explaining them; less important messages treated as equal or more important | 12–14 | mostly relevant and accurate | 12–14 |
| 3 | understands that there are interpretations, but explains only sub-messages; or argues an interpretation the whole does not support | 9–11 | generally accurate and relevant | 9–11 |
| 2 | summarises the main points; explanations lack validity | 5–8 | some accurate and relevant | 5–8 |
| 1 | references to some aspects of the extract; fragments | 1–4 | limited | 1–4 |

The two marks are added: 20 and 20 make 40. The same level in both tables gives a total between 36 and 40 at Level 6, 30 and 34 at Level 5, 24 and 28 at Level 4, 18 and 22 at Level 3, 10 and 16 at Level 2, and 2 and 8 at Level 1. The course does not convert a total into a grade, because Cambridge sets grade boundaries after each series.

**2. Name the fault.** Most lost marks in Paper 3 come from one of four faults. (a) You summarised the extract paragraph by paragraph (Level 2). (b) You found a sub-message and built a different interpretation on it (Level 3). (c) You found one real element but ranked a minor one above the main one (Level 4). (d) You explained the main interpretation in parts and never said it was one argument (Level 5). Check which of the four you did, and the scheme shows what the extract's main message was.

**3. Check the knowledge.** Count how many of your facts did a job: explained a claim or tested it. Facts that only told the story earn little. Go back to the debate chapter for your topic: [the origins of the First World War](#hist.interp.ww1.2), [the Holocaust](#hist.interp.holocaust.2) or [the Cold War](#hist.interp.coldwar.2), and to [How historians disagree](#hist.interp.skills) for the method.

**4. Sit a second extract.** Use the practice extracts at the end of your debate chapter, which are summaries of real historians' arguments, and then another of the questions in this mock on a different day. Look at the real past papers and mark schemes on the Cambridge site, and read how its examiners explain the main interpretation of a real extract.

:::warn What a mock cannot tell you
These extracts and this scheme were written for this course. A real extract may be a historian's compressed argument with an aside or a qualification in the middle, and a real mark scheme may name the main interpretation in different words. Treat your result as a guide to your skills, not as a prediction of a grade. Several real past papers, marked honestly, give a better picture than any one mock.
:::
