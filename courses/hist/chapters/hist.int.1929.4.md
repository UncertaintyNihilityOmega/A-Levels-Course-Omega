With model Paper 1 and Paper 2 answers built level by level.

:::why
On 1 September 1939 German forces crossed the Polish frontier and, two days later, Britain and France declared war. A conflict that was to kill tens of millions of people began over a port city and a strip of land that most people in Britain could not have found on a map. Why then, and why over Poland, when Germany had taken Austria and Czechoslovakia without a war? The question is the capstone of this whole option. It asks you to bring together Hitler's aims, the policy of the democracies, the weakness of the League and the sudden agreement between two enemies, Germany and the USSR, and to decide which of these mattered most.
:::

## Hitler's aims in foreign policy

Hitler's actions between 1933 and 1939 look like a series of crises. Historians ask whether they were also the stages of a plan. To answer, you need first to know what he said he wanted.

### The underlying aims

Hitler set out his ideas in *Mein Kampf* ("My Struggle"), written in 1924 while he was in prison after the failed Munich Putsch and published in two volumes in 1925 and 1926. He returned to the same themes in speeches and private conversations for the rest of his life. Five aims run through them.

:::def Lebensraum
"Living space": Hitler's idea that the German people needed more land to live on and to feed themselves, and that this land should be taken in the east, from Poland, Ukraine and Russia, by conquest and the removal or enslavement of the people who lived there.
:::

1. **To overturn the Treaty of Versailles.** Almost all Germans wanted this: the limits on the army, the loss of territory, the demilitarised Rhineland and the "war guilt" clause. It was the aim that Britain and France understood, and the one on which they were willing to give way.
2. **To unite all Germans in one state.** This meant Austria, the Germans of Czechoslovakia and Poland, and the free city of Danzig. This is the "Greater Germany" that Hitler could justify with the principle of self-determination, which the Allies themselves had used in 1919.
3. ***Lebensraum* in the east.** This was Hitler's distinctive aim, and it went well beyond revising Versailles. Germany, he said, was overcrowded and needed the farmland of eastern Europe. It was the aim that Britain and France did not understand until too late.
4. **To destroy communism.** Hitler's ideology joined hatred of Marxism with hatred of Jews, whom he blamed for both communism and capitalism. The USSR was therefore both his enemy and his intended victim. This is why the Nazi–Soviet Pact of 1939 was so surprising.
5. **To make Germany the leading power in Europe.** In *Mein Kampf* Hitler thought that Germany might win Britain's friendship or at least her neutrality, and he saw France as an enemy to be dealt with later. By 1939 he was less sure about Britain.

### The Hossbach Memorandum, 1937

The most quoted evidence that Hitler had a plan is a document of 1937. On 5 November 1937 Hitler held a meeting in the Reich Chancellery in Berlin with the War Minister (Blomberg), the army and navy commanders (Fritsch and Raeder), the air force commander (Göring) and the Foreign Minister (Neurath). Colonel Friedrich Hossbach, Hitler's military adjutant, took notes and wrote them up five days later.

According to Hossbach's record, Hitler said that the aim of German policy was to make secure and preserve the racial community and to enlarge it, so that "it was therefore a question of space", and that Germany's problem could be solved only by force. He said that he intended to deal with it at the latest by 1943–45, but that it might come sooner if France was paralysed by civil war or tied up in a war with another state. His "first objective", if the chance came, was to overthrow Czechoslovakia and Austria.

**How far does it prove a plan?** Historians point out several things.
- It records a **timetable and targets**: space in the east, Austria and Czechoslovakia first. The Anschluss and the Sudetenland crisis followed within a year.
- But the notes were **not an agreed plan**. They were written five days later from Hossbach's memory and notes, and Hitler did not see them. The meeting was a talk to his generals and Neurath, and part of its purpose may have been to push the army and the Foreign Ministry into supporting faster rearmament, because Blomberg, Fritsch and Neurath objected to the risks. All three lost their posts on 4 February 1938.
- The timetable was **not kept**. Hitler spoke of 1943–45 for the great war, but the war began in 1939 and against Poland, which the memorandum does not mention. Events, not a calendar, set the pace.

:::key How to use the Hossbach Memorandum
It is good evidence of Hitler's **intentions and priorities** in November 1937, and it is a **record made by an officer who was present**. It is weaker evidence of a **detailed plan**, because it is a summary written afterwards, it was a talk to persuade, and the war that actually came did not follow its timetable.
:::

### Programme or opportunism? The debate (stretch)

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **A.J.P. Taylor**, *The Origins of the Second World War* (1961), argued that Hitler was an **opportunist**, a statesman who wanted what most German leaders wanted and who took chances as they arose. For Taylor the war of 1939 came from mistakes on both sides, not from a plan. It caused an uproar, because it seemed to make Hitler an ordinary politician.
- **Hugh Trevor-Roper** replied in 1961, in an article in the magazine *Encounter*, that Hitler's ideas, set out in *Mein Kampf*, were the key: he had a **programme** of racial conquest and he followed it with great flexibility in method.
- **Richard Overy** and **Ian Kershaw** are among the later historians who place Hitler's aims at the centre but pay close attention to the regime and the circumstances. Overy has stressed the way the Nazi state prepared Germany's economy and forces for war; Kershaw has stressed Hitler's ideology and the way his subordinates "worked towards" his aims.
You can use this at AS to show that "Hitler's aims" is a judgement, not a fact, and to say that the best view combines both sides: a fixed aim (living space in the east) pursued with flexible methods and timing.
:::

**Evidence for a programme:** *Mein Kampf*; the Four-Year Plan of 1936 (Hitler's memorandum asked that the army be ready for war and the economy fit for it within four years); the Hossbach meeting; the secret directives of 1938 and 1939 for the attack on Czechoslovakia ("Case Green") and Poland ("Case White"); the rearmament, which took a very large share of national income.
**Evidence for opportunism:** Hitler's moves were often improvised (the plebiscite crisis of March 1938 forced the pace in Austria); in September 1938 he seems to have wanted a war and was denied one by Munich; in 1939 he expected Poland to be isolated and did not expect Britain to fight; and the great war with the USSR that his ideology demanded came in 1941, not on the Hossbach timetable.

:::key Hitler's aims: a defensible view
Hitler had **fixed aims** (to overturn Versailles, to unite the Germans and above all to win living space in the east) and **flexible methods** (diplomacy, bluff, threats and, when it suited, force). Between 1933 and 1938 the aims that most Germans shared (revising Versailles, union with Austria and the Sudeten Germans) hid the more extreme aim; from March 1939 the aim of living space could no longer be hidden.
:::

## The impact of Hitler's expansion, 1935–39

The detail of each step is in [chapter 3 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.3). Here the question is different: what did each step do to the **balance of power** and to Hitler's own confidence? For a "Why did war break out?" answer, this is the link between the aims and the outbreak.

| Step | What it did for Germany | What it showed |
|---|---|---|
| Rearmament and conscription, 16 March 1935 | A peacetime army of 36 divisions (about 550,000 men) and an air force; the start of the arms economy | That Britain and France would protest and not act |
| Anglo-German Naval Agreement, 18 June 1935 | Britain accepted German rearmament at sea (35 per cent) | That Britain valued a deal over joint action; the Stresa Front was split |
| Rhineland, 7 March 1936 | Germany could fortify her western frontier (the Westwall); France lost her easiest way of attacking Germany | That France would not move without Britain; Belgium chose neutrality in October 1936 |
| Anschluss, 12–13 March 1938 | Nearly 7 million people, resources and a long common frontier with Czechoslovakia and Hungary | That the Allies would not defend Austria, and Mussolini no longer protected it |
| Sudetenland, October 1938 | The Czech border fortifications; Czechoslovakia could no longer be defended | That a threat of war could win more than a conference: the democracies would force the victim to give way |
| Czechoslovakia, March 1939 | The Škoda works, the Czech army's weapons and a position on three sides of Poland; Slovakia became a client state | That Hitler's promises (to take no more Czech land) were worthless |
| Memel, 22–23 March 1939 | A Baltic port taken from Lithuania without a fight | That Germany could take small states' territory at will |

The cumulative effect is the key point. By the end of March 1939 Germany had a larger population (well over 70 million, against about 66 million in 1933), a better strategic position (the Westwall in the west, no Czech threat in the south), more weapons and a leader who believed that Britain and France would not fight. Each step made the next cheaper, and each one also **used up the room for compromise**: after Prague there was nothing left to give that Hitler would not take anyway.

## British rearmament in response to German expansion

British rearmament was a response to German rearmament, not a separate story. In [chapter 3 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.3) it appears as one of appeasement's successes (it "bought time"). Here the question is what Britain actually built and how far it had got by September 1939. The syllabus names three things: aircraft, preparation for air raids, and targets and progress by 1939.

### The development of aircraft

British planners believed that the air was where the next war would be decided. The RAF's expansion came in a series of "schemes", each raising the target:
- **Scheme A (July 1934)** aimed for 84 home-defence squadrons (about 1,250 front-line aircraft) by March 1939. It was the first answer to the news that Germany was building an air force.
- **Scheme F (February 1936)** aimed for 124 squadrons, with about 1,736 first-line aircraft, by March 1939. Most of the new squadrons were bombers, on the belief that a strong bomber force would deter Germany from attacking. **Sir Thomas Inskip** was made Minister for the Co-ordination of Defence in March 1936 to manage the programmes.
- **From late 1937 the emphasis shifted to fighters.** Inskip's review in December 1937, backed by the Air Ministry, put defence of Britain first: the argument was that Britain could survive a German "knock-out blow" from the air only with fighters, radar and guns, and that bombers could come later. Under the Commander-in-Chief of Fighter Command, Air Chief Marshal Hugh Dowding, the RAF built a system that joined radar, the Observer Corps, fighter bases and anti-aircraft guns.

The new aircraft were the key. The **Hawker Hurricane** (first flown in November 1935) entered service with 111 Squadron in December 1937, and the **Supermarine Spitfire** (first flown on 5 March 1936) with 19 Squadron in 1938: both were fast, eight-gun monoplanes, far better than the biplanes they replaced. A scheme of "shadow factories" from 1936 asked car makers to build aircraft and engines, so that output could grow quickly. British military aircraft production, from under 3,000 in 1938, rose to nearly 8,000 in 1939, close to Germany's (estimates vary). The bombers of the late 1930s (the Wellington, Whitley and Hampden) were medium bombers with limited range and bomb loads.

**Radar** was equally important. Robert Watson-Watt's team showed in February 1935 that radio waves could detect aircraft, and the government decided to build a chain of stations. The "Chain Home" stations were built first around the Thames estuary and then along the east and south coasts; by September 1939 about 20 were working. Radar gave Fighter Command warning of attackers, so that it did not have to keep aircraft in the air all the time.

### Preparation for air raids

The fear of the bomber, described in [chapter 3 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.3), led to the second part of rearmament: protecting civilians. The government set up an Air Raid Precautions Department in the Home Office in 1935, and the **Air Raid Precautions Act** (received royal assent in December 1937, in force from January 1938) forced local authorities to prepare plans. Over the next two years Britain:
- recruited hundreds of thousands of volunteer air raid wardens, firemen and first-aid workers (the crisis of September 1938 produced a surge of volunteers);
- issued **gas masks** to the whole population (the mass fitting in September 1938 was part of the Munich crisis);
- began to distribute the **Anderson shelter** from February 1939, a steel shelter for the garden, free to poorer households, with well over a million distributed by the outbreak of war;
- prepared **evacuation**: in the first days of September 1939 well over a million people, mainly schoolchildren, mothers with young children and teachers, left the big cities for the countryside;
- ordered a **blackout** at night.

### Targets and progress by 1939

By September 1939 Britain had come a long way, but the targets had not all been met. Scheme F's goal of about 1,736 first-line aircraft by March 1939 was not reached on time, because new types came into service late and factories took time to expand. Yet Fighter Command had about 27 squadrons of Hurricanes and Spitfires and a working radar chain, which in 1938 it had not. The army was still the weakest service, with limited conscription starting only in May 1939 and an expeditionary force for France of only a few divisions. Defence spending, as the chart in [chapter 3](#hist.int.1929.3) shows, was in 1938–39 more than three times its level in 1934–35.

:::key British rearmament by 1939: progress and limits
1. **Aircraft**: the priority moved from bombers to fighters in 1937–38; Hurricanes and Spitfires entered service; production rose to nearly 8,000 military aircraft in 1939.
2. **Radar and air defence**: about 20 Chain Home stations; a system that joined radar, the Observer Corps, fighters and guns.
3. **Civil defence**: wardens, gas masks, shelters, evacuation and blackout, planned under the ARP Act of 1937 and tested in the Munich crisis.
4. **Limits**: targets for bombers and the army were not met; Germany still had more bombers; and the changes mostly came in 1938–39, when Hitler had already gained the Sudetenland and Prague.
:::

## The development of German alliances

Hitler needed partners, or at least neutrals, to carry out his plans. Three agreements matter: the Anti-Comintern Pact, the Pact of Steel and the Nazi–Soviet Pact. For each you should be able to give the **motives of both sides**, long term and short term.

:::def Anti-Comintern Pact
An agreement of 25 November 1936 between Germany and Japan, joined by Italy on 6 November 1937 (and later by Hungary, Manchukuo and Spain), to share information about and act against the Communist International (Comintern). Its published text was not a military alliance, though a secret protocol bound each partner not to help the USSR if the other was attacked by it.
:::

### The Anti-Comintern Pact, 1936–37

*Motives.* **Germany** wanted a friend in the Far East that would tie down the USSR on its eastern side, and the pact fitted Hitler's anti-communism; it was largely the work of Joachim von Ribbentrop, then Hitler's adviser on foreign affairs, who became Foreign Minister in 1938. **Japan** wanted allies against the USSR and a counterweight to Britain and the USA, and its army favoured the link. **Italy**, which joined in 1937, had been isolated after the Abyssinian crisis, had worked with Germany in Spain, and left the League of Nations in December 1937. The pact was cheap: it committed no army to fight. Its importance was as a **signal** that the three states had common interests, and it shifted Germany away from its earlier cooperation with China. See [chapter 1 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.1) for the background.

### The Pact of Steel, 22 May 1939

On 22 May 1939 Germany and Italy signed in Berlin a full military alliance, the "Pact of Friendship and Alliance", which Mussolini called the **Pact of Steel**. Its central article provided that if one partner became involved in a war with another power, the other would come to its side at once with all its military forces.

*Motives.* **Mussolini** had two reasons. In the short term, after the occupation of Prague (March 1939) and his own seizure of Albania (7 April 1939), he did not want to be left behind or isolated by a Germany that was moving without him, and he wanted a share of the gains; he also hoped that an alliance would let him restrain Hitler. In the longer term the alliance matched his ambition for an Italian empire in the Mediterranean and Africa. **Hitler** wanted Italy bound to him, so that Britain and France would have to consider a Mediterranean front as well as a German one and Italy could not be drawn into a deal with them. The two did not agree on timing. A memorandum drawn up by General Cavallero and signed by Mussolini, sent to Hitler at the end of May 1939, said Italy would not be ready for a major war before about 1942, and Hitler ignored it. When the crisis over Poland came, Mussolini told Hitler on 25 August that Italy could not fight without massive supplies of raw materials and weapons; Italy declared "non-belligerence" at the start of the war and did not enter until June 1940. The Pact of Steel was therefore strong on paper and weak in practice, but it had helped to give Hitler confidence.

### The Nazi–Soviet Pact, 23 August 1939

The most surprising agreement of the decade was between two states that had called each other mortal enemies. On 23 August 1939 the German Foreign Minister, Ribbentrop, and the Soviet Foreign Minister, Vyacheslav Molotov, signed in Moscow a **non-aggression treaty**, announced the next day. It had a public part and a secret one.

:::def Nazi–Soviet Pact
The German–Soviet treaty of non-aggression signed in Moscow on 23 August 1939 by Ribbentrop and Molotov, with a secret additional protocol that divided eastern Europe into German and Soviet "spheres of influence". It freed Germany to attack Poland without fear of a war with the USSR.
:::

- **The public treaty** promised that neither side would attack the other (Article I), that if one were at war with a third power the other would give that power no support (Article II), and that neither would join any grouping aimed at the other (Article IV).
- **The secret protocol** divided eastern Europe. In the Baltic region, the northern frontier of Lithuania would mark the boundary of the German and Soviet spheres (so Finland, Estonia and Latvia fell in the Soviet sphere). If Poland were rearranged, the spheres would be bounded approximately by the line of the rivers Narew, Vistula and San, and whether an independent Polish state should survive would be decided later. The USSR's interest in Bessarabia, in Romania, was acknowledged.

*How it came about.* After the occupation of Prague, Britain and France began talks with the USSR on an alliance, but they went slowly: the British were suspicious of Stalin, the Poles and Romanians would not allow Soviet troops to cross their territory, and the Anglo-French military mission that reached Moscow in August 1939 went by sea, took several days, and was not given authority to sign. Stalin had, from his side, begun to look for a deal with Germany: in May 1939 he dismissed the Foreign Commissar Maxim Litvinov, who was Jewish and associated with collective security, and put Molotov in his place. A German–Soviet trade and credit agreement was signed on 19 August, Hitler wrote to Stalin on 20 August and Stalin agreed on 21 August to receive Ribbentrop, who flew to Moscow on 22–23 August.

**Motives: Hitler.** *Short term*: to isolate Poland. With the USSR neutral, Germany could attack Poland without a war on two fronts, and Britain and France, with no way to help Poland, might not fight. A related short-term reason was supplies: the USSR could send grain, oil and raw materials that a British blockade would otherwise stop. The campaign also had to start by the autumn, before the rains. *Long term*: none of this changed Hitler's aim of destroying the USSR and taking its land; the Pact was a tactical step.

**Motives: Stalin.** *Short term*: to avoid war in 1939. He had concluded after Munich, where the USSR was not consulted, that Britain and France wanted to push Germany east, and he did not trust them to fight (the slow talks of August confirmed it). He also faced a Japanese threat in the Far East (fighting at Nomonhan on the Manchurian border from May 1939). *Long term*: to gain time to rebuild a Red Army that had lost many officers in the purges of 1937–38, to win a buffer of territory in eastern Poland, the Baltic states and Bessarabia, and to watch the capitalist powers wear each other out.

**Effects.** The Pact removed the one thing that might have deterred Hitler, a large power ready to fight him in the east, and it left Poland with only a British and French promise that could not be carried out directly. On 17 September the Red Army entered eastern Poland as the protocol had planned. The Pact also shocked Japan, Germany's partner against communism. But it did not do what Hitler hoped: Britain and France honoured their guarantee. He had won a free hand in Poland, but not a free hand against the West.

:::key Why the alliances mattered for 1939
1. **Anti-Comintern Pact (1936–37)**: a signal of an anti-communist front; no military commitment.
2. **Pact of Steel (22 May 1939)**: a full alliance with Italy, strong on paper, weak in practice (Italy not ready until 1942, non-belligerent in September 1939).
3. **Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939)**: removed the threat of a war on two fronts and the prospect of an Anglo-French-Soviet alliance, and gave the signal for the attack on Poland on 1 September.
Each partner had short-term motives (security, territory, time, supplies) and long-term ones that clashed (Hitler's aim against the USSR; Stalin's wish to see the West weakened).
:::

## The attack on Poland

### German–Polish issues: Danzig and the Polish Corridor

Poland had been recreated in 1918 and given, by the Treaty of Versailles, two things that Germans resented: the **Polish Corridor**, a strip of former German land on the lower Vistula that gave Poland access to the Baltic Sea and, as a side effect, cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany; and, nearby, the **Free City of Danzig**, a port at the mouth of the Vistula whose people were overwhelmingly German (about 95 per cent) but which was placed under League of Nations protection and joined to Poland in a customs union, so that Poland could use it as a harbour.

:::def Danzig and the Polish Corridor
The Free City of Danzig was a mainly German port city placed under the League of Nations by the Treaty of Versailles and tied to Poland by a customs union. The Polish Corridor was the strip of land, mostly with a Polish population, that gave Poland a coastline and separated East Prussia from the rest of Germany.
:::

For fifteen years no German government accepted this settlement, but relations with Poland were not hopeless. In January 1934 Hitler signed a ten-year **non-aggression pact** with Poland, which helped him to split Poland from France, and in 1938 Poland shared in the carving-up of Czechoslovakia by taking the Teschen district on 2 October. Danzig was run by a Nazi Senate from 1933.

The crisis came after Munich. On 24 October 1938 Ribbentrop proposed to the Polish ambassador that Danzig return to Germany, that Germany be allowed to build an extra-territorial road and railway across the Corridor, and that Poland join the Anti-Comintern Pact, in return for a German guarantee of Poland's frontiers and an extension of the 1934 pact. The Polish Foreign Minister, Colonel Józef Beck, saw that Poland would become a German satellite, as Slovakia did, and refused. Poland would talk about Danzig's rights but would not give up the Corridor or her independence. Hitler concluded that Poland could no longer be a junior partner against the USSR and, after the occupation of Prague, had to be dealt with.

### The end of appeasement and the Anglo-French guarantee, March 1939

The occupation of Prague on 15 March 1939 (see [chapter 3 of this topic](#hist.int.1929.3)) and the seizure of Memel on 22–23 March convinced British ministers that Poland was next. On 26 March Poland rejected the German demands again. British ministers had also heard (some of the news was false, such as a rumoured German ultimatum to Romania) that Germany was preparing a move. On **31 March 1939** Chamberlain announced in the Commons that Britain would give Poland "all support in their power" if Polish independence was clearly threatened and Poland resisted, and France joined the guarantee. On 13 April the guarantee was extended to Romania and Greece. On 25 August 1939 the promise became a formal Anglo-Polish treaty of mutual assistance.

Why did Britain give a promise that she could not carry out directly? The reasons were these:
- **To deter Hitler.** Ministers thought that if Hitler knew Britain would fight he might hold back, as he had in May 1938.
- **To show that Britain had changed.** After Prague, public opinion and the Conservative Party wanted a firm stand. Giving way again would have cost the government its credibility.
- **Poland was seen as the last barrier.** If Poland fell, Romania and the Balkans (and their oil and grain) would be next, and Germany would be too strong.
- **To keep France in step and to leave the next move to Hitler.** The guarantee made the question of peace or war one for Germany to decide.
The weakness of the guarantee was that Britain and France could do little to help Poland, and that it made Poland (whose government was proud and stubborn, and which was already refusing German demands) less likely to negotiate.

Hitler was furious. On **28 April 1939**, in a speech to the Reichstag, he cancelled the Anglo-German Naval Agreement and the 1934 non-aggression pact with Poland, saying that Britain's policy was one of encirclement. On 3 April he had already issued a directive ("Case White") ordering the armed forces to be ready to attack Poland at any time from 1 September 1939.

### Disagreement between Hitler and the High Command

Hitler was not simply obeyed. The army leadership, the "High Command", was cautious and conservative, and several times it disagreed with him.
- **1938: the generals removed.** In February 1938 Hitler took the command of the armed forces himself, after the scandals that forced out the War Minister, Blomberg (who had married a woman with a police record) and the army commander, Fritsch (accused, falsely, of homosexuality). The new army commander, Walther von Brauchitsch, was more compliant, and a new High Command of the Armed Forces (OKW) under General Keitel answered to Hitler.
- **1938: Beck's resignation.** The Chief of the General Staff, Ludwig Beck, wrote memoranda warning that a war over Czechoslovakia would bring in Britain and France and that Germany would lose; when Hitler ignored him he resigned in August 1938. His successor, Franz Halder, and others, planned to arrest Hitler if he ordered an attack on Czechoslovakia, and the plan was abandoned when Chamberlain flew to Germany and Munich removed the need.
- **1939: the generals' doubts about a war with the West.** Most generals accepted a war with Poland, which they thought they would win quickly; their worry was that it would bring a long war against Britain and France, for which Germany was not ready. At a conference on 23 May 1939 Hitler told his commanders that Poland must be isolated and attacked, and that they must be ready to fight Britain as well. On 22 August, after the Pact, he told them that he expected Britain and France not to intervene effectively, especially now that the USSR had agreed to stay neutral.

The disagreement was real but limited. It was about **risk and timing**, not about expansion itself, and Hitler could remove or overrule his critics. By 1939 the generals who feared a general war were isolated, and the Pact with the USSR took away much of their case.

### Hitler's intentions and the outbreak of war

What did Hitler want in Poland in 1939? The evidence is that he wanted to **destroy Poland as an independent state** and to take Danzig and the Corridor, and that he hoped to do it in a **short, local war** which Britain and France would not enter. At the conference of 23 May he was recorded (by his adjutant Schmundt) as saying that Danzig was not the real issue, but the need for living space in the east, and that Poland would be attacked at the first suitable opportunity. He did not want a major war with the West yet, but he was prepared to risk one: he was confident that the Pact with Stalin would deter it, and he is reported to have called his enemies "little worms" (see [chapter 3](#hist.int.1929.3) for the caution needed with that remark).

The attack was fixed for 26 August, put off after the Anglo-Polish treaty of 25 August and because Mussolini said Italy could not fight, and then ordered for 1 September. In the last days Hitler tried to separate Britain from Poland by offering talks, and he put a set of demands to Poland on 29–30 August that the Poles had no time to answer. On 31 August the SS staged a fake Polish attack on a German radio station at Gleiwitz to provide an excuse.

At about 4:45 on the morning of **1 September 1939**, German forces crossed the Polish frontier, and the battleship *Schleswig-Holstein* bombarded the Polish garrison at Westerplatte in Danzig. The British government told Berlin on 1 September that it must withdraw or Britain would stand by Poland, and at 9 am on **3 September** the British ambassador handed over a final note that expired at 11 am. No reply came, and Chamberlain broadcast at 11:15 (see Source C below) that "this country is at war with Germany". France, after some delay, handed over a note at midday that expired at 5 pm, so France was at war from **5 pm on 3 September**. Australia and New Zealand joined that day, and South Africa and Canada followed within days. On 17 September the Red Army entered eastern Poland, as the secret protocol had allowed.

:::key Why Britain and France went to war in 1939 when they had not in 1938
1. **Prague** had shown that Hitler could not be trusted and that his aim was domination, not revision of Versailles.
2. **The guarantee** committed British and French honour, and giving way again would have destroyed their credibility.
3. **Public opinion** now supported resistance, and so did most of the Cabinet.
4. **Rearmament** had made Britain stronger in the air than in 1938, though the army was still small.
5. **Poland chose to fight**, and Hitler did not accept that the West would fight until it did.
:::

## Weighing the causes: why did war break out in 1939?

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How they worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | The Treaty of Versailles and German resentment; Hitler's ideology (living space, anti-communism); the weakness of the League | Created the aims and the opening |
| Medium-term | Hitler's expansion 1935–39; appeasement; the Depression; the failure of collective security | Made each step cheaper and gave Germany resources and confidence |
| Short-term | The occupation of Prague; the guarantee to Poland; Danzig and the Corridor; the Nazi–Soviet Pact | Made a clash over Poland likely and removed the Soviet deterrent |
| Trigger | Hitler's decision to attack Poland at the end of August; the refusal to withdraw after the ultimatum | Set off the war on 1 September and 3 September |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Hitler's **aims and decisions** were the main cause: another German leader might have revised Versailles, but only Hitler wanted a war of conquest in the east and chose the time to start it. But Hitler alone does not explain the **timing and the outbreak**. Appeasement and the weakness of the League let him grow strong; the Nazi–Soviet Pact removed the deterrent and made an attack on Poland possible; and Britain and France decided in 1939 to fight, which turned an attack on Poland into a general war. The strongest answers separate the cause of **German aggression** from the cause of **a world war**.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Britain guaranteed Poland in March 1939.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Hitler took over Czechoslovakia on 15 March 1939 and Britain gave a promise to Poland on 31 March. Chamberlain said Britain would help Poland if it was attacked. France made the same promise. Britain did this because Hitler was threatening Poland and Britain did not want him to take any more land."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it describes what happened and gives a general reason ("did not want him to take any more land") without explaining how Prague led to the guarantee. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was the occupation of Prague. Hitler had promised at Munich that he wanted no more Czech land, so when German troops entered Prague on 15 March, taking a non-German people, it showed that his aims were not limited to Germans or to revising Versailles. Chamberlain, who had believed that Hitler could be trusted, had to accept that he could not. A second reason was public opinion and the party. After Prague, many Conservatives and the press, and most of the public, turned against giving way again, and Chamberlain, who had been criticised for Munich, needed to show a firm line. A third reason was the threat to Poland. By the end of March there were reports, some false, of a German move against Poland, and Poland had refused the demands about Danzig and the Corridor."

*Commentary.* Three factors, each explained with accurate support. Level 3 because the answer lists them side by side and does not say how they link or which mattered most.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the aim of the guarantee and then show how the factors link:

"The aim of the guarantee was deterrence. Ministers thought that if Hitler knew that Britain and France would fight for Poland, he might hold back, as he had during the May crisis of 1938. A guarantee to Poland also made sense for strategic reasons: Poland was the largest state left in eastern Europe, and if it fell, Romania, and its oil and grain, would come within Germany's reach.

These factors were linked. Prague destroyed the belief that Hitler's demands were limited, and that destroyed the case for appeasement; public and party opinion then removed the political room to go on giving way; and the threat to Poland gave Britain a place to make a stand. The decisive factor was Prague, because without it Chamberlain would have continued to negotiate and public opinion would not have turned. The result was a policy change in two weeks: on 15 March Chamberlain said that Britain was not bound to act in Czechoslovakia, and on 31 March he promised to support Poland."

*Commentary.* Three factors plus the aim of deterrence, **connected** (Prague destroyed the argument for appeasement; opinion removed the room for it) and a supported conclusion that ranks the factors. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Hitler's foreign policy was the main cause of war in 1939.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- Criterion: "main cause" means the cause without which the war would not have broken out in 1939, and the one that explains the most.
- For: Hitler's aims (*Mein Kampf*, Hossbach); the steps of expansion 1935–39; the decision to attack Poland (Case White, 3 April; 22 August); the Pact used to isolate Poland.
- Against 1, appeasement: Britain and France let Germany grow, and Munich destroyed the Czech army.
- Against 2, the Nazi–Soviet Pact: Stalin's decision made the attack possible; Soviet distrust after Munich.
- Against 3, Britain and France: the guarantee, the choice to fight in 1939; and the weakness of the League.
- Judgement: Hitler's policy was the necessary cause; the others were permissive. The war needed Hitler but his chances were made by others.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Hitler's foreign policy was the main cause of war. In *Mein Kampf* he wrote that Germany needed living space in the east. In 1935 he introduced conscription, in 1936 he sent troops into the Rhineland, in 1938 he took Austria and the Sudetenland, and in March 1939 he occupied Prague. In April 1939 he ordered the army to prepare to attack Poland and on 1 September he invaded. This shows that Hitler's foreign policy was the main cause of war."

*Commentary.* Accurate, well supported and in order, but one-sided, and the last sentence only repeats the question. The answer never says why other factors were less important. A response made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet Hitler's policy does not explain why the war began in September 1939 and not earlier, nor why it became a general war. Two other factors were decisive. The first was the Nazi–Soviet Pact. Until 23 August the main obstacle to an attack on Poland was the prospect of a war on two fronts; Hitler, who had wanted to destroy the USSR, signed a deal with Stalin because he needed Poland isolated, and the secret protocol that divided Poland along the Narew, Vistula and San gave the attack its cover. The timing followed the Pact: the attack was fixed for 26 August, three days after it was signed. But the Pact was Stalin's choice as well as Hitler's: he had been shut out of Munich, the Anglo-French mission of August came without authority to sign, and he wanted time, territory and a buffer. The second factor was the decision of Britain and France to fight. Hitler's policy would have produced a local war in Poland; it was the guarantee of 31 March and the refusal to withdraw the ultimatum on 3 September that made it a world war. Appeasement helped Hitler to reach that point: the Rhineland, Austria and Czechoslovakia gave him a better frontier, more resources and the belief that Britain and France would not act, so that when the West did act, he was surprised.

Overall, Hitler's foreign policy was the main cause of war, but not the only cause. The test is what each cause did. Without Hitler's aims there would have been no demand for Poland and no war of conquest; but without the Pact he could not have attacked in 1939, and without the guarantee the war would not have been a general one. The decisions of Stalin, Chamberlain and Daladier shaped when and how the war started, but they responded to Hitler's initiatives, and the others' choices were reactions to his. The statement is therefore largely true of the cause of aggression, and only partly true of the outbreak of the world war."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph analyses an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion and separates the aggression from the general war. With a Level 3 opening on Hitler's policy, this is Level 5 (18–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
Use **Source A** and **Source B** in the box above.

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the purposes of the Nazi–Soviet Pact.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is the public treaty and says that Germany and the USSR will not attack each other. Source B is a secret protocol and says that they will divide Poland. So A is about peace and B is about dividing Poland. A is a public document, so it is not honest, and B is secret, so it is more honest."

*Commentary.* One clear difference, supported. No similarity. The comment on honesty is a general assertion with no context or explanation. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources are very different, but they have two things in common. Both are parts of the same agreement, signed in Moscow on 23 August 1939, and both are written in the language of diplomacy and in the name of both governments. Both also serve the same practical purpose: to make sure the two states would not fight each other. Source A promises that they will 'desist from any act of violence' against each other, and Source B sets a line, the Narew, Vistula and San, that divides the two states' interests so that they cannot collide in Poland.

The differences are about what each source reveals. Source A is a promise of peace between two states, and it goes further than that: Article II says that if either is attacked by a third power the other 'shall in no manner lend its support' to it. That is a promise of neutrality, and in August 1939 it meant that the USSR would not help Poland, Britain or France if Germany attacked Poland. Source B is about territory: it contemplates a 'territorial and political rearrangement' of the Polish state and even leaves open whether an 'independent Polish state' should continue to exist. A presents the Pact as defensive; B shows that it prepared a joint attack.

The context explains why one document was published and the other hidden. The treaty was announced to the world on 24 August, and its purpose was to stop a war on two fronts for Germany and to protect the USSR from a German attack, while showing that the USSR had a choice other than the Anglo-French talks that were then being held in Moscow. The protocol was secret because it dealt with the territory of third states, Poland and the Baltic countries, and revealing it would have exposed the Pact as an alliance for conquest, and might have turned Soviet communists and the West against it. As evidence, the treaty is reliable on what the governments wanted the world to see and on the binding promise of neutrality; the protocol is the better evidence of what they actually meant to do, and events confirmed it: Germany attacked Poland on 1 September and the Red Army entered eastern Poland on 17 September along roughly the line it described. Neither source shows the long-term aims of the two sides, and the treaty says nothing of the Soviet fear of Germany or the German plan for the USSR."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (public against secret, the Moscow talks, the later invasions) and through each source's purpose; a final judgement of what each is good evidence for. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
Use **Sources A to D** in the box above.

**Question.** 'The Nazi–Soviet Pact was the main reason why war broke out in 1939.' How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: D (the Pact's promise not to join any grouping aimed at the other removed the prospect of an Anglo-French-Soviet alliance); B (the cartoon shows the two dictators meeting over Poland's body: the Pact leads to Poland's fall). Challenge or qualify: A (Hitler's aims of "space", stated in 1937, long before the Pact, are an independent cause); C (Britain's decision to go to war on 3 September, after Hitler would not withdraw, is another cause: the Pact did not itself start the war).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: a record made by an officer present, five days after a meeting at which Hitler spoke to his service chiefs, so good evidence of his priorities in November 1937. But it is a summary written afterwards, not a plan, and it names no date for Poland. It shows an aim that existed before the Pact, so it supports the view that the Pact was not the first cause.
- **B**: a cartoon by one of Britain's best-known cartoonists, in a London evening paper, about four weeks after the Pact and three days after the Red Army entered Poland, which is why the meeting is over a dead Polish soldier. Its purpose was to show British readers the cynicism of the two dictators, who greet each other with insults in the language of politeness. It is a good source on British opinion in September 1939 and a weak one on the causes of the war, because it was drawn after the war began.
- **C**: a statement by the Prime Minister, a formal and public announcement of British policy, and so reliable on what Britain did and why: the ultimatum expired because Germany did not withdraw. It was written to justify the decision to the British public, so it puts the blame on Germany. It shows that the war became a general war because Britain chose to fight.
- **D**: an official text, very reliable on what the Pact said. But it is only the public treaty, and it says nothing about the secret protocol; it also does not say that Germany would attack Poland, only that the USSR would not join any grouping against Germany.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources D and B support the view that the Pact was decisive, though in different ways. Source D shows that the Pact took away the one thing that might have deterred Hitler: by promising that neither side would join a grouping aimed at the other, Stalin ruled out the Anglo-French-Soviet alliance that Britain and France had been trying to arrange since April, and on 23 August Hitler could attack Poland without fear of a war on two fronts. Source B, drawn four weeks later, shows how contemporaries saw the result, two enemies who had agreed over the body of Poland. But Source A challenges the view: it was written in 1937, when no Pact was imaginable, and shows that Hitler's aim of winning space by force was an independent cause, as Source C, the declaration of war by Britain, shows another independent cause: the war began on 3 September because Britain decided to fight over Poland. The Pact made the German attack on Poland possible and fixed its timing, but not its aims, and not the British and French decision. On balance the sources support the view only partly: the Pact was the immediate enabling cause of the attack, but the main reason war broke out was Hitler's aim, as A shows, combined with the decision of Britain and France to honour their promise, as C shows."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1937–39; the answer separates the cause of the attack on Poland from the cause of a general war; it judges "how far". Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was Hitler's plan for war the main cause of the outbreak of war in 1939?" [20]

A student wrote:

"Hitler planned the war from the start. In the Hossbach Memorandum of 1937 he said he would go to war by 1943–45 and take Austria and Czechoslovakia first. Then he did take Austria in 1938 and Czechoslovakia in 1939. This proves he had a plan and that he caused the war. In 1939 he invaded Poland and that started the war."

**The level.** The facts are accurate and relevant, but the paragraph **asserts** that the Memorandum "proves" a plan, and it does not test it. It does not say what kind of document the Hossbach Memorandum is (notes written afterwards), it ignores that the timetable (1943–45) was not kept and that Poland is not mentioned, and it does not consider any other cause. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "valid assertions with limited support".

**The fix.** Use the evidence to argue, and then test it. "The Hossbach Memorandum is good evidence that in November 1937 Hitler intended to win space by force and named Austria and Czechoslovakia as first targets, and the Anschluss and Prague fit that pattern. But it is not proof of a detailed plan: it is a summary written five days later by an adjutant, and Hitler spoke of 1943–45 for the great war, whereas the war began in 1939, over a country the memorandum does not mention, and began because of events that he did not control, such as the guarantee and the Pact. It shows an aim; the outbreak of war needed more." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

A: To overturn the Treaty of Versailles; to unite all Germans in one state (Austria, the Sudeten Germans, Danzig); *Lebensraum* (living space) in the east; to destroy communism; to make Germany the leading power in Europe.
Q: What is the Hossbach Memorandum, and why must it be used with care?
A: Notes of a meeting of 5 November 1937 at which Hitler told his service chiefs and the Foreign Minister that Germany needed space, by force if necessary, and named Austria and Czechoslovakia as first targets. Hossbach wrote it up five days later, Hitler did not approve it, and its timetable (1943–45) was not followed.
Q: Give two things the RAF and the government did between 1935 and 1939 to prepare for war in the air.
A: Built Hurricane and Spitfire fighters and moved the priority from bombers to fighters from late 1937; built the Chain Home radar chain (about 20 stations by 1939); passed the Air Raid Precautions Act (1937), issued gas masks, built shelters and planned evacuation.
Q: What did the Pact of Steel (22 May 1939) promise, and why was it weaker than it looked?
A: A full military alliance between Germany and Italy: if one was involved in war the other would join at once. Italy was not ready for a major war (Mussolini's memorandum said about 1942), and she stayed non-belligerent in September 1939.
Q: Give two short-term reasons for Stalin's decision to sign the Nazi–Soviet Pact and one for Hitler's.
A: Stalin: distrust of Britain and France after Munich and the slow talks of August; fear of fighting Germany alone, and Japanese pressure in the Far East. Hitler: to isolate Poland and avoid a war on two fronts, and to gain Soviet supplies.
Q: Why were Danzig and the Polish Corridor a cause of conflict?
A: The Corridor cut East Prussia off from the rest of Germany, and Danzig, a mainly German city under League protection, was tied to Poland by a customs union. Germany wanted Danzig back and a road and railway across the Corridor; Poland refused in 1938–39 because it feared becoming a German satellite.
Q: Why did Hitler and some generals disagree, and how far did it matter?
A: Generals such as Beck feared that attacks on Czechoslovakia and Poland would bring a war with Britain and France that Germany could not win. Hitler removed Blomberg and Fritsch (February 1938), Beck resigned (August 1938), and in 1939 the Pact with the USSR weakened the generals' case. The disagreement was about timing and risk, not about expansion.
Q: Give the times of the declarations of war on 3 September 1939.
A: The British ultimatum expired at 11 am and Chamberlain broadcast at 11:15; France declared war at 5 pm.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Hitler wanted to win living space in the east.
A: Indicative content: his racial ideology (the Germans as a people who needed land; Slavs to be dominated); the belief, set out in *Mein Kampf*, that Germany could not feed itself or be great without farmland and raw materials; hatred of communism, which he linked to the USSR and to Jews, so that the east was both a target and an enemy; the memory of 1918 and the idea that Germany had been "cheated"; the economic case in the Four-Year Plan of 1936 (raw materials, oil, grain). Level 4 (9–10) connects these: ideology gave the aim, economics gave the argument to the generals and industrialists, and the idea that the USSR was weak after the purges gave the opportunity; it judges which mattered most (ideology, because the economic reasons alone would not justify conquest).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Germany and Italy signed the Pact of Steel in May 1939.
A: Indicative content: Mussolini's wish not to be left behind after Prague and to share in Hitler's gains; his own seizure of Albania (7 April) and ambition in the Mediterranean; fear of being isolated after the League and Abyssinia; the Anti-Comintern Pact and the Axis as stages (1936–37); Hitler's wish to tie Italy to Germany and to threaten Britain and France with a second front; the belief that the alliance would deter Britain and France. Level 4 connects short-term events (Prague, Albania) with the long-term drift of the two dictatorships together, and judges that each wanted something different: Mussolini prestige and gains, Hitler a reliable ally.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Poland refused to give way to German demands over Danzig and the Corridor in 1939.
A: Indicative content: the Corridor was Poland's only access to the sea and Danzig her main port; the Polish government saw that a road and railway across the Corridor would make Poland a German satellite, as Slovakia had become; the demand that Poland join the Anti-Comintern Pact would end her independence from Germany; the experience of Czechoslovakia (what happened to a state that gave way); the British and French guarantee of 31 March, which strengthened her resolve; pride and the memory of Polish independence; mistrust of both Germany and the USSR. Level 4 connects the Polish fear of losing her independence with the guarantee that made refusal look safe, and judges which mattered more.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent did the Nazi–Soviet Pact make a German attack on Poland possible?
A: For: it removed the risk of a war with the USSR as well as Poland; the secret protocol divided Poland and the Baltic and gave Germany a free hand in the west of the country; supplies of oil, grain and raw materials broke the effect of a British blockade; the date of 26 August (then 1 September) followed it. Against: Hitler had already ordered Case White on 3 April and planned for an attack by 1 September; the German army was strong enough to beat Poland alone; the Pact did not stop Britain and France from entering the war; Germany might have attacked anyway, as the generals' campaign plans show. Level 5 (17–20) judges, for example, that the Pact made the attack **safe** rather than possible: Germany could have defeated Poland alone, but without the Pact the risk of a long two-front war would have been much higher and the attack might not have been ordered.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far did British rearmament change the military balance between Britain and Germany by September 1939?
A: Change: fighter squadrons with Hurricanes and Spitfires (about 27) instead of biplanes; the Chain Home radar chain (about 20 stations); civil defence and evacuation; production of military aircraft close to Germany's by 1939 (estimates vary); conscription from May 1939. Limits: Germany still had a larger bomber force and a larger, better-trained army; Britain's army was small; Germany had also grown stronger with Austria, the Sudetenland and Czech arms; the targets of Scheme F were not met on time. Level 5 judges, for example, that rearmament greatly improved Britain's defence against air attack, but did not close the gap on land, and that the balance turned more in Germany's favour in 1938–39 because of her territorial gains.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and C (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about responsibility for the outbreak of war in 1939.
A: Similarities: both are by leading figures (an adjutant recording Hitler, the British Prime Minister), both refer to force and to the failure of peace, and both are about the same conflict. Differences: A records Hitler's own statement of his priorities in 1937, so it points to Germany's intentions and to force; C records a British decision to declare war and presents the war as a failure of Chamberlain's "long struggle to win peace", putting responsibility on Germany by implication. A is a private record of a secret meeting; C is a public broadcast. Explanation: A is a summary written five days after a talk, for the record and unpublished until the Nuremberg trials; C was written to explain a war declaration to a nervous public and to show that Britain had done everything for peace. Level 4 explains the differences through this context and judges which is better evidence of responsibility (A for Hitler's aims; C for the British decision, and neither alone for the whole question).
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). 'Hitler had planned the war of 1939 from 1937.' How far do these sources support this view?
H: Which sources show Hitler's plans (A, B) and which show how Britain responded (C, D)?
H: Does A support "planned the war of 1939" or only "planned expansion"? Look at the dates in it.
H: Use your own knowledge of the Hossbach meeting, Case White and the Pact to evaluate A and B.
A: Support: A shows Hitler stating in 1937 that force was needed to solve Germany's problem of space and naming Czechoslovakia and Austria as first targets; B shows, in 1939, a secret division of the Baltic with the USSR, which prepared for an attack in the east. Challenge: A's timetable is 1943–45, not 1939, and does not mention Poland; B is a deal made in August 1939 with an enemy of Nazism, which does not look like a plan made in 1937; C and D show the war as a reaction to events (a final note, a guarantee given in March 1939, after Prague), not as the working of a plan. Evaluation with knowledge: A is a summary written five days later by an adjutant, a record and not a plan; B is a reliable text of a secret agreement and shows that by August Hitler was ready for war in the east; C is a public broadcast and D an official statement, both reliable on British actions. Level 5 judges how far: Hitler had a fixed aim (living space by force) from the 1920s and stated it in 1937, but the war of 1939 came by a different route and date, and depended on the Pact and on British and French decisions, so the view is true of the aim and not of a plan for 1939.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The guarantee to Poland made war more likely, not less.' How far do you agree?
H: Separate what the guarantee did to Hitler from what it did to Poland.
H: Compare 1938 (no guarantee, no war) with 1939.
A: Agree: the guarantee made Poland less willing to negotiate; it committed Britain and France to a war they could not fight directly; Hitler cancelled the Naval Agreement and the Polish pact on 28 April and ordered Case White on 3 April; he saw it as encirclement; it was not backed by a Soviet alliance, so it did not deter. Disagree: without it Germany would probably have taken Poland as she took Czechoslovakia; Prague showed that Hitler's aims were not limited, and the guarantee drew a line; the war was caused by Hitler's decision to attack, not by a promise that he was meant to respect; deterrence might have worked if Soviet talks had succeeded. Level 5 judges, for example, that the guarantee made a *general* war more likely by making Britain and France respond, but a war of some kind was likely anyway, because Hitler's aim was to destroy Poland.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ A.J.P. Taylor argued in *The Origins of the Second World War* (1961) that Hitler was an opportunist rather than a planner. How far does the evidence of 1935–39 support this view?
H: Sort the events into those that look planned (the Hossbach meeting, Case Green, Case White) and those that look improvised (the Austrian crisis of March 1938, the timing of the attack on Poland).
H: Then ask whether "opportunist" and "planner" are opposites.
A: Support: Hitler seized chances as they came (the Rhineland when Italy was distracted, Austria when the Schuschnigg plebiscite forced the pace); he spoke in 1937 of 1943–45 but acted in 1939; in 1938 he wanted war and settled for Munich; he expected Britain not to fight in 1939; the Pact was a sudden change of course. Challenge: *Mein Kampf* and the Four-Year Plan show fixed aims; the Hossbach meeting named targets that were attacked within 18 months; directives for Czechoslovakia and Poland show planning; the aim of living space was pursued in 1941. Hugh Trevor-Roper's reply (1961) stressed these aims. A* judgement: the best view is that Hitler had **fixed aims and flexible methods**, so Taylor is right about the means (opportunism) and wrong if he implies that there was no aim beyond revising Versailles.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ How far did the German generals restrain Hitler's foreign policy between 1937 and 1939?
H: Compare the effect of the generals in September 1938 and in August 1939.
H: Consider what Hitler did to remove opposition and whether the generals objected to the aims or the timing.
A: Restraint: Beck's warnings and resignation (August 1938) and the plot of Halder and others in September 1938 show a serious opposition; fear of a war with Britain and France made Hitler pause in the May crisis of 1938 and take the Munich settlement; the generals' doubts about readiness delayed some plans. Limits: Hitler removed Blomberg and Fritsch in February 1938 and took command himself; Beck's resignation did not stop him; the plotters did nothing once Chamberlain went to Munich, and Munich brought Hitler a victory that silenced them; in 1939 the generals accepted the attack on Poland, which they thought would be quick, and were reassured by the Pact; they disagreed about risk and timing, not about expansion. A* judgement: the generals could delay and shape but not stop Hitler, and by 1939 Hitler's success had silenced much of the opposition.

:::recap
- **Hitler's aims**: overturn Versailles, unite the Germans, living space in the east, destroy communism, make Germany the leading power; *Mein Kampf* (1925–26); the Hossbach meeting of 5 November 1937 (a summary written by Hossbach on 10 November: space, by force, by 1943–45 at the latest; Czechoslovakia and Austria first). Debate: A.J.P. Taylor (1961) opportunist; Trevor-Roper (1961) programme.
- **Expansion**: conscription (16 March 1935); Naval Agreement (18 June 1935); Rhineland (7 March 1936); Anschluss (12–13 March 1938); Sudetenland (October 1938); Bohemia and Moravia (15 March 1939); Memel (22–23 March 1939). Each step added resources and confidence.
- **British rearmament**: Scheme F (1936); fighters first from late 1937; Hurricane (1937) and Spitfire (1938); about 20 Chain Home stations; ARP Act 1937, gas masks, Anderson shelters, evacuation; about 27 fighter squadrons of Hurricanes and Spitfires by September 1939; limited conscription in May 1939.
- **Alliances**: Anti-Comintern Pact (Germany–Japan 25 November 1936; Italy 1937); Pact of Steel (22 May 1939; Italy not ready); Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939; Hitler to isolate Poland, Stalin for security, time and territory; secret protocol along the Narew, Vistula and San).
- **Poland**: Danzig and the Corridor; German demands of 24 October 1938 and refusal; guarantee of 31 March 1939; Case White (3 April); Hitler cancels the Naval Agreement and Polish pact (28 April); Anglo-Polish treaty (25 August); invasion (1 September); declarations of war (3 September: Britain 11 am, France 5 pm).
- **Hitler and the generals**: Blomberg and Fritsch removed (February 1938); Beck resigned (August 1938); a disagreement about risk and timing, not about expansion.
- **Judgement**: Hitler's aims and decisions were the main cause; appeasement, the Pact and the decision of Britain and France to fight explain the timing and why it became a world war.
