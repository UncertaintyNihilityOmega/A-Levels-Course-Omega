:::why
Between 1941 and 1945 Germany and its allies murdered about six million Jewish men, women and children, and killed millions of other people besides. No other crime has been studied so closely, and the evidence is overwhelming: the perpetrators' own orders, reports and speeches, the testimony of survivors, the camps themselves and the trials of the 1940s. Historians do not argue about whether it happened. They argue about how a modern state came to do it, who made it happen, and what could have stopped it. Those arguments are the subject of Paper 3, and you cannot follow them until you know the events. This chapter gives you the events in order, with their dates, their numbers and their sources, and marks the points where the historians of the next chapter take different roads.
:::

## European and German antisemitism before the Nazis

**Antisemitism** is hatred of, or discrimination against, Jews as Jews. The Nazis did not invent it. They found it in the cultures of Europe, they changed it and then they acted on it. The first job for a Paper 3 student is to be exact about what came before and what was new, because an extract may claim that the Holocaust grew out of old ideas, or that it was a break from them.

:::def Antisemitism
Hostility to Jews as a group, expressed in prejudice, discrimination or violence. Before the 1870s it was mainly **religious** (anti-Judaism); from the 1870s it was increasingly **racial**, treating Jews as a biological race whose characteristics could not be changed by conversion.
:::

### Religious hostility: the long background

For centuries Christian Europe treated Jews as outsiders. Church teaching blamed Jews for the death of Jesus. Medieval crowds believed false stories, such as the "blood libel" (that Jews murdered Christian children) and that Jews poisoned wells. Jews were expelled from England in 1290 and from Spain in 1492, forced to live in separate quarters (ghettos), banned from many trades and often made to wear a badge. In 1543 Martin Luther published *On the Jews and Their Lies*, a hate-filled attack that later antisemites quoted. Many Jews became moneylenders because they could not own land or join guilds, and that fed another false stereotype, of the greedy Jew.

This religious hatred had a limit that matters later: a Jew who converted to Christianity could, in theory, escape it. The racial antisemitism of the late nineteenth century removed that escape.

### Emancipation and a new hostility, 1789–1914

The French Revolution and later reforms gave Jews legal equality in one country after another. In Germany full equality was complete by 1871, when the Empire was founded. Jews entered business, medicine, law, journalism and the universities, and German Jews thought of themselves, in the main, as Germans of the Jewish faith. By 1933 they were fewer than 1 per cent of the population, but they were most visible in cities.

Emancipation did not end hostility; for some people it made it worse. Three developments mattered.

1. **Economic crisis.** The stock-market crash of 1873 and the long depression that followed led many to blame Jewish bankers and "liberals". Antisemitic leagues and parties appeared. In 1879 a journalist, **Wilhelm Marr**, published *The Victory of Judaism over Germandom* and helped to found a League of Antisemites; he is credited with making "antisemitism" a political word. The same year a respected Berlin historian, Heinrich von Treitschke, wrote that "the Jews are our misfortune", a phrase that antisemites repeated for sixty years. The court preacher Adolf Stoecker built a Christian Social movement that attacked Jewish influence.
2. **Nationalism.** The *völkisch* movement (from *Volk*, "people") said that Germans were bound together by blood and soil, and that Jews could never belong. This was a different idea from the old religious one, because no baptism could change a bloodline.
3. **Racial theory.** See the next section.

Antisemitism in Germany was one example of a European problem and not a German speciality. In the Russian Empire pogroms (violent attacks on Jewish communities) followed the assassination of Tsar Alexander II in 1881, and at Kishinev in April 1903 a mob killed 49 Jews. In France the **Dreyfus affair** (from 1894) divided the country when a Jewish army officer, Alfred Dreyfus, was falsely convicted of treason; he was not cleared until 1906. In Vienna **Karl Lueger** was elected mayor in 1897 on an antisemitic platform and served until 1910; the young Adolf Hitler lived in Vienna in the years before 1913. Romania, Poland and Hungary all had strong antisemitic movements. The forged *Protocols of the Elders of Zion*, produced in Russia and first published there in 1903, claimed to be the secret plan of a Jewish plot for world domination. In 1921 *The Times* of London showed that it had been copied from earlier fiction, but it was widely believed, translated into German in 1919–20 and reprinted by the Nazis.

### Racial ideas, Social Darwinism and eugenics

:::def Racial antisemitism
The belief that Jews are a biological race, distinct from and inferior or dangerous to other races, so that their characteristics are inherited and cannot be changed by conversion, education or assimilation.
:::

:::def Social Darwinism
The use of Charles Darwin's idea of natural selection to describe human societies, claiming that nations and races compete for survival and that the strong deserve to prevail. Darwin did not argue this, but others used his name to justify empire, war and racial hierarchy.
:::

:::def Eugenics
The attempt to improve a population by breeding: encouraging the reproduction of people thought "fit" and preventing that of people thought "unfit". The word was coined by Francis Galton in 1883. In Germany the same idea was called "racial hygiene" (*Rassenhygiene*) from 1895.
:::

In the nineteenth century writers who claimed to be scientific divided humanity into races ranked by quality. The French aristocrat Arthur de Gobineau argued in his *Essay on the Inequality of the Human Races* (1853–55) that race mixing caused decline. **Houston Stewart Chamberlain**, a British-born admirer of Wagner who settled in Germany, published *The Foundations of the Nineteenth Century* (1899), which described history as a struggle between Germanic ("Aryan") peoples and Jews. Social Darwinists applied competition between species to nations, and eugenicists wanted state action to protect "racial health". These ideas were international; eugenic laws were passed in the USA in the 1900s and 1920s, in Sweden and elsewhere. They were not specifically Nazi, but they gave Nazi doctors and officials a vocabulary of science and made killing seem like hygiene.

After the First World War a lawyer, Karl Binding, and a psychiatrist, Alfred Hoche, published *Permitting the Destruction of Life Unworthy of Life* (1920), arguing that the incurably ill and the severely disabled might be killed. Their pamphlet is evidence that the idea of killing "unfit" people was discussed by respectable professionals years before 1933, and it matters for the T4 programme below.

### The First World War, defeat and the "stab in the back"

About 100,000 German Jews served in the army in the First World War, and about 12,000 died. Yet in October 1916 the War Ministry ordered a "Jew count" (*Judenzählung*) to check whether Jews were avoiding the front, an insult to patriotic soldiers; the results were never fully published. After Germany's defeat in 1918 nationalists spread the **stab-in-the-back legend** (*Dolchstoss*): that the army had not been beaten in the field but betrayed at home by socialists, Jews and "November criminals". The new Weimar Republic, which Jewish politicians helped to found, was branded a Jewish republic. In June 1922 Walther Rathenau, the Jewish Foreign Minister, was murdered by right-wing extremists. In the crisis of 1923 and again after 1929, antisemitic propaganda found larger audiences.

:::key What the Nazis inherited and what was new
**Inherited.** A Christian tradition of hostility to Jews; stereotypes of the Jew as banker, outsider and conspirator; a nationalism that defined the nation by blood; racial science, eugenics and Social Darwinism; the *Protocols*; and a nationalist myth of betrayal in 1918.
**New in Nazi hands.** Antisemitism as the centre of a political programme and a state policy, backed by a one-party dictatorship; Jews as a **racial** enemy to be removed, with no escape by conversion; the identification of Jews with both capitalism and Bolshevism; and, from 1939–41, the willingness to turn the ideas into mass murder using the machinery of a modern state.
**For Paper 3.** Historians disagree about how much weight to give to the first list (the chapter after this one calls it the "continuity" argument) and how much to the second. Whatever the extract says, you can test it with both lists.
:::

## Nazi antisemitism

Hitler entered politics in Munich in 1919 as an army informer sent to watch a small nationalist group, the German Workers' Party. On 16 September 1919 he wrote a letter on "the Jewish question" to a man called Adolf Gemlich, in which he distinguished the "emotional" antisemitism of pogroms from what he called **rational antisemitism**, which should remove the Jews' rights by law and aim, in the end, at the removal of the Jews altogether. The letter shows how early his ideas were set, though not that he already planned mass murder. On **24 February 1920** the party, renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (the Nazi Party), announced its **25-point programme**. Point 4 said that only members of the nation could be citizens, that only people of German blood could be members of the nation, and that "no Jew" could therefore be a member of the nation. Other points demanded that Jews be treated as foreigners and expelled if the state could not feed everyone.

After the failed Munich putsch of November 1923 Hitler wrote ***Mein Kampf*** ("My Struggle") in prison; volume 1 appeared in 1925 and volume 2 in 1926. It sets out a world view that Hitler repeated until 1945: history is a struggle between races; the Aryan race is the creator of culture; the Jew is its parasite and destroyer, a "poison" in the body of the nation; and the Jews were behind both Marxism and the finance capital that Hitler thought ruined Germany. The book also links Jews with Germany's defeat in 1918, and it says that Germany needs *Lebensraum* ("living space") in the East. For Hitler, therefore, antisemitism was a theory of everything: it explained defeat, economic distress, democracy, communism and war.

:::def Nazi antisemitism
The Nazi belief that Jews are a dangerous race (not a religion) engaged in a world conspiracy, and that Germany's survival required their removal from public life, then from Germany and, in the end, from Europe. It combined older stereotypes, racial science and the myth of the "Jewish-Bolshevik" enemy.
:::

The party's propaganda poured out the message: Julius Streicher's weekly newspaper *Der Stürmer*, from 1923, printed obscene caricatures; Goebbels organised rallies and films; the SA beat up Jews in the streets. But historians such as Ian Kershaw point out that when the Nazis won 37.3 per cent of the vote in July 1932, voters were moved much more by unemployment, fear of communism, national humiliation and Hitler's promise of strong government than by antisemitism, and that Nazi leaders downplayed antisemitism in some election campaigns. This is a good fact to have in reserve for a question about whether ordinary Germans shared Nazi antisemitism.

## The persecution of the Jews in Germany, 1933–39

On **30 January 1933** President Hindenburg appointed Hitler Chancellor. In 1933 about 500,000 Jews lived in Germany, under 1 per cent of the population. Between 1933 and 1939 the regime drove them out of the economy, the professions and public life, made them second-class non-citizens and tried to force them to leave. The persecution came in waves, with pauses, and it was not a single plan carried out step by step. Pressure from the party, the SA and local officials sometimes pushed it faster than Hitler or the ministries wanted, and the regime stopped when it feared foreign boycotts or damage to the economy. The next chapter shows that this pattern is itself a subject of historical argument.

### 1933–34: exclusion begins

Within weeks of taking power the Nazis used violence and law together. SA men attacked Jews in the streets; the first concentration camp, at Dachau, opened on 22 March 1933 for political prisoners. On **1 April 1933** the party organised a **boycott** of Jewish shops, doctors and lawyers; SA men stood outside with placards. Many Germans ignored it, and it was called off after one day, but it showed the direction. On **7 April 1933** the **Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service** removed Jews, as defined by its "Aryan paragraph", and political opponents from state posts; Hindenburg won an exemption for front-line veterans and those who had held office since 1914, which was ended later. Similar laws followed against Jewish lawyers and doctors, and a law of April 1933 limited the number of Jewish pupils and students in schools and universities. On 10 May 1933 students and Nazis burned thousands of books by Jewish and other authors in German cities. The regime moved more slowly in 1934, when it needed the economy to recover and the army's goodwill.

### 1935: the Nuremberg Laws

In the summer of 1935 party radicals returned to street violence and local boycotts. On **15 September 1935**, at the annual party rally in Nuremberg, the Reichstag was called to meet and passed two laws that Hitler had asked officials to draft in a few days.

:::def The Nuremberg Laws
Two laws of 15 September 1935. The **Reich Citizenship Law** made only people "of German or related blood" Reich citizens, so that Jews became mere "subjects". The **Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour** banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and "Germans", and the employment of German women under 45 in Jewish households. A decree of **14 November 1935** defined who was a Jew by grandparents: a person with three or four Jewish grandparents was a Jew; those with one or two were *Mischlinge* ("mixed" people), who had a lesser status.
:::

The laws mattered in three ways. They took away citizenship in law, so that later measures could treat Jews as people with no rights. They defined Jewishness by descent and not by religion or belief, so that a baptised Christian with Jewish grandparents was a Jew in law, which is the heart of racial antisemitism. And they gave local officials a legal basis for more discrimination. In 1936, with the Berlin Olympics held from 1 to 16 August, the regime took down anti-Jewish signs for the visitors, a sign that it responded to foreign opinion.

### 1936–38: Aryanisation

:::def Aryanisation
The transfer of Jewish-owned businesses, property and jobs to non-Jewish Germans, by forced sale at low prices, by pressure and by law, from 1933 and at speed from 1937–38. Banks, firms and officials profited, and many ordinary Germans bought Jewish homes, furniture and shops.
:::

When Göring took charge of the Four-Year Plan in 1936 the economy was geared to war, and Jews' property was a resource. In April 1938 Jews had to register all property worth over 5,000 Reichsmarks. By 1938 the great majority of Jewish businesses had been closed or sold under pressure. Jews were barred from more professions, from parks and swimming pools, and in August 1938 men and women whose names were not on an official list had to add the names Israel and Sara. In October 1938 Jews' passports were stamped with a red "J". In **March 1938** Germany took over Austria in the *Anschluss*, and about 185,000 Austrian Jews were exposed at once to violence, robbery and humiliation, such as being forced to scrub the streets. In August 1938 Adolf Eichmann set up a Central Office for Jewish Emigration in Vienna that forced emigration by taking Jews' property in exchange for exit papers, and it became the model for the Reich. Over 100,000 Austrian Jews left in about eighteen months.

### Emigration, Évian and the refugees

The regime's aim, until 1941, was to make Germany **free of Jews** (*judenrein*) by forcing them to emigrate. By 1939 more than half of Germany's Jews had left, but they left with almost nothing, since they had to pay a flight tax and surrender most of their money. The difficulty was finding somewhere to go. Britain, the USA and others had immigration quotas and a requirement for sponsors or large deposits, and in the depression they feared competition for jobs. Palestine, under British rule, limited Jewish immigration in 1939. At the **Évian Conference** (6–15 July 1938), called by President Roosevelt, delegates from 32 countries met by Lake Geneva. Almost all expressed sympathy, and almost none offered to take more; only the Dominican Republic agreed to receive significant numbers. The Nazi press noted that the world did not want the Jews.

### Kristallnacht, 9–10 November 1938

On **7 November 1938** a 17-year-old Jewish refugee, **Herschel Grynszpan**, shot a German diplomat in Paris, Ernst vom Rath, after his own family had been among about 17,000 Polish-born Jews expelled from Germany at the end of October. Vom Rath died on 9 November, the anniversary of the 1923 putsch, when Nazi leaders were gathered in Munich. That evening Goebbels, the Propaganda Minister, gave a speech that led party leaders to organise a pogrom. During the night of **9–10 November** SA men and party members, often in plain clothes, burned synagogues, smashed and looted Jewish shops (the broken glass gave the event its Nazi nickname, *Kristallnacht*, "night of broken glass"; historians now often say the **November pogrom**), and attacked Jews in their homes. Police and fire brigades were told not to interfere, except to protect non-Jewish property. Over 1,400 synagogues were burned or destroyed; thousands of shops were smashed; hundreds of Jews died in the violence and its aftermath, including those who took their own lives; and about 26,000 Jewish men were arrested and sent to the camps at Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen, where many were beaten and humiliated and were released only if they promised to emigrate.

The regime then blamed the victims. At a meeting on **12 November 1938** chaired by Göring, it fined the Jewish community **1 billion Reichsmarks** as an "atonement payment", took the insurance money for damage, and issued decrees that excluded Jews from retail trade and from most other business and removed Jewish children from state schools. Göring said the aim was to push Jews out of the economy; in January 1939 the Reich Central Office for Jewish Emigration was set up under Heydrich. After the pogrom, panic emigration followed, and in Britain a scheme called the **Kindertransport** admitted about 10,000 Jewish children, without their parents, between December 1938 and the outbreak of war.

On **30 January 1939** Hitler made a speech to the Reichstag in which he said that if "international finance Jewry" plunged the nations into a new world war, the result would be "the annihilation of the Jewish race in Europe". He later referred back to this "prophecy" many times, including in December 1941. Historians read it in different ways: as a threat meant to frighten foreign governments into taking the Jews, as a sign of Hitler's intentions, or as both.

:::key What the persecution of 1933–39 shows
1. **Direction.** From 1933 the Jews were removed step by step from German life: from jobs and the professions, from citizenship, from their property, from the streets. By 1938 violence and the threat of violence were forcing them out.
2. **Method.** The regime combined law (April 1933, September 1935, November 1938) with violence from the party and the SA (April 1933, summer 1935, November 1938). The state turned the violence into law each time.
3. **Aim.** Until 1941 the declared aim was **emigration**, not murder. The regime was willing to use terror to achieve it, and it threatened worse.
4. **For the debate.** An intentionalist stresses the consistent direction and Hitler's 1939 prophecy; a structuralist or functionalist stresses the competition between party and state agencies and the improvised, radicalising pattern. Each can use the same facts (the next chapter).
:::

### Jewish responses in Germany, 1933–39

German Jews did not wait passively. In 1933 they formed the Reich Representation of German Jews (*Reichsvertretung*) under Rabbi **Leo Baeck**. They built Jewish schools for children expelled from state schools, adult education and the *Kulturbund*, a Jewish theatre and concert organisation that gave work to artists dismissed from German institutions. Charities helped the poor. Many Jews held on to hope that the regime would moderate, since German Jews had been loyal citizens for generations, and many emigrated as soon as they could find a country to take them. Victor Klemperer, a professor in Dresden who survived because his wife was not Jewish, kept a diary through the whole period that records the slow squeeze on daily life, and his published diaries are one of the best historical sources for the German Jewish experience.

## War and Poland, 1939–41

The Second World War began on **1 September 1939** when Germany invaded Poland. The invasion brought about 2 million Polish Jews under German rule at once, and the conquest of western Europe and the Balkans in 1940–41 brought more. Poland had the largest Jewish community in Europe, about 3.3 million people before the war. German policy in occupied Poland was brutal for everyone: the SS and army shot thousands of civilians in the first weeks, Poles were driven from the areas annexed to the Reich, and the rest of Poland was ruled as the **General Government** by Hans Frank.

War changed the question. Before 1939 the Nazis wanted to push Jews out; now millions more Jews were under their rule, borders were shut by the war, and the rhetoric of a racial war for living space made the "Jewish question" a security question for the army and SS. Heydrich ordered on 21 September 1939 that Jews in Poland be concentrated in cities near railways and that Jewish councils be set up.

### Ghettos and Judenräte

:::def Ghetto
A part of a town where Jews were forced to live, sealed off from the rest. In 1940–41 the Germans created ghettos across occupied Poland and later in the East. They were overcrowded, starved places that held people until they could be deported or killed, and they were also used for forced labour.
:::

:::def Judenrat
A Jewish council (plural *Judenräte*) that the Germans ordered each ghetto community to form. It had to carry out German orders, such as supplying labour, collecting taxes and, later, compiling lists for deportation, and it also ran food supplies, health, schools and welfare.
:::

The ghetto of Łódź was sealed on **30 April 1940** with about 160,000 people, and the **Warsaw ghetto** was sealed in November 1940 with over 400,000, nearly 30 per cent of Warsaw's population on about 2.4 per cent of the city's area. The Germans set a food ration for the Warsaw ghetto of 181 calories per day. In practice smuggling raised the average that people received to about 1,125 calories, and even that was starvation. Tens of thousands died in 1941 of hunger and disease, particularly typhus, before any mass killing began in Warsaw. The ghetto was a policy of death by neglect as well as a holding place; historians still argue about how far it was intended to kill.

The Judenräte faced impossible choices, which historians still debate (see the next chapter). In Warsaw **Adam Czerniaków**, chairman of the Jewish council, kept a diary and tried to save what he could by negotiation. When the Germans began the deportations on 22 July 1942 and told him to produce a daily quota, he took his own life the next day, leaving a note. In Łódź **Mordechai Chaim Rumkowski**, the Elder of the Jews, argued that work could save the ghetto, since a ghetto of productive workers would be useful to the Germans, and he made himself the ghetto's master; his policy kept the ghetto alive until 1944 but it also meant choosing who would be sent away. These two leaders are the standard examples of how differently men acted under impossible pressure.

### The Madagascar Plan

After France fell in June 1940 officials in the Foreign Ministry, led by Franz Rademacher, revived an older antisemitic idea: to deport Europe's Jews to the French colony of Madagascar. A memorandum of July 1940 was drawn up and Hitler discussed it, but the plan required control of the seas and Britain had not been defeated; it was shelved. The plan shows that in 1940 Nazi officials were still thinking about mass expulsion, in conditions that would have killed many; it is also used by historians who argue that no plan to murder the Jews existed in 1940.

### Aktion T4: the murder of disabled people

In October 1939 Hitler signed an authorisation, backdated to **1 September 1939** so that it looked like a wartime measure, empowering doctors to grant a "mercy death" to patients judged incurable. The programme was run from a villa at Tiergartenstrasse 4 in Berlin, hence its later name, **Aktion T4**. Doctors decided from questionnaires who would die; patients were taken to six centres (Brandenburg, Grafeneck, Bernburg, Sonnenstein, Hartheim and Hadamar) and killed in gas chambers using carbon monoxide gas. By August 1941, T4 itself recorded **70,273** victims. Protests grew when relatives noticed and the public learned; on 3 August 1941 Bishop Clemens von Galen of Münster preached against the killings, and Hitler ordered the centralised programme halted in late August 1941. Killing then continued in hospitals by starvation and overdoses, and the historians' estimate for all the victims is about **250,000** people.

T4 matters for the Holocaust in two ways. First, its technology and its staff: gas chambers disguised as showers, the use of doctors to select victims, the cremation of bodies and the secrecy around them. Second, its personnel: reliable T4 staff were transferred to Operation Reinhard in 1941–42, among them Christian Wirth, who ran Bełżec, and Franz Stangl, who later commanded Sobibór and Treblinka. Historians such as Henry Friedlander (*The Origins of Nazi Genocide*, 1995) have argued that T4 was the first stage of genocide; others warn that disabled people were killed for different reasons (the Nazi idea of "unworthy life" and the "burden" of their care) and that the two programmes should not be run together.

## The war in the East and the turn to mass murder, 1941

The invasion of the Soviet Union on **22 June 1941** (Operation Barbarossa) was planned as a war of annihilation, and it began the most deadly phase of the persecution. The key question for historians is how and when mass shootings became a plan to murder every Jew in Europe. This section tells the sequence, and the dating is left to the next chapter.

### The Einsatzgruppen

:::def Einsatzgruppen
"Task forces" of the Security Police and the SD (the SS security service): four groups, A, B, C and D, of about 3,000 men in all, that followed the German armies into the USSR in 1941. They were joined by Order Police battalions, Waffen-SS units and local auxiliaries, and helped by the Wehrmacht, and they shot Jews, Communist officials and others.
:::

At first the Einsatzgruppen killed mainly Jewish men of military age and Communist officials. From the late summer of 1941, in many places from August, they shot women and children too, which meant that they were killing whole communities. Local people took part in some places: in Lithuania, Latvia and Ukraine, nationalists carried out pogroms and joined auxiliary police units; in Romania, an ally of Germany, soldiers and civilians murdered thousands of Jews in the city of Iași at the end of June 1941. The Lithuanian report of an Einsatzkommando officer, Karl Jäger, listed a total of **137,346** people shot up to 1 December 1941, most of them Jews.

The best-known single massacre is **Babi Yar**, a ravine at Kyiv. The Germans took Kyiv on 19 September 1941. On 29 and 30 September, 99 days after the invasion began, they marched the city's Jews to the ravine and shot them. The Einsatzgruppen's own Event Report lists **33,771** Jews killed in two days, helped by Ukrainian auxiliaries. At least 1.5 million Jewish and other people were killed by shooting and in gas vans in the Soviet Union, and about one third of all Jewish victims of the Holocaust died in shooting actions. The shootings took place in public, in front of villages, and so thousands of ordinary soldiers, policemen and civilians saw them.

### The sequence of decisions, summer–autumn 1941

The facts, in order, are:
- **31 July 1941.** Göring wrote to Heydrich, instructing him to make the necessary preparations for a "complete solution" (*Gesamtlösung*) of the Jewish question in the German sphere of influence in Europe. Historians read it as the start of planning, though it does not mention killing.
- **September 1941.** From 19 September all Jews in the Reich over six had to wear a yellow star. Hitler allowed the deportation of Jews from the Reich to the East.
- **15 October 1941.** The first trains left Vienna, Prague, Luxembourg and Berlin for the ghetto of Łódź and other places in the East. On **23 October** the regime banned Jewish emigration from the Reich altogether. The "emigration policy" was over.
- **September–November 1941.** In September the SS tested Zyklon B on Soviet prisoners at Auschwitz. In the autumn it began to build Bełżec, the first of the stationary killing sites of Operation Reinhard, and prepared the site at Chełmno.
- **8 December 1941.** Killing in gas vans began at **Chełmno** (Kulmhof) in the Warthegau, an annexed part of Poland. At least 152,000 people were murdered there, nearly all Jews.
- **11–12 December 1941.** After Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, Germany declared war on the USA on 11 December. On 12 December Hitler told senior Nazi leaders in Berlin that the "prophecy" of January 1939 was now to be fulfilled; Goebbels's diary records it.

Historians disagree about which of these steps was "the decision". Some see a sequence of decisions through 1941; some date a decision in the summer, some in the autumn, some in December. All agree that by the end of 1941 the regime was murdering Jews on a large scale and was preparing to murder all those in its reach.

### The Wannsee Conference, 20 January 1942

The conference was planned for 9 December 1941 and was postponed. It took place on **20 January 1942** at a villa on the Wannsee lake in Berlin, chaired by **Heydrich**, with Adolf **Eichmann** (head of the Gestapo's Jewish section) keeping the minutes. Fifteen men attended, from the SS, the Gestapo, the Foreign Ministry, the Ministries of the Interior and Justice, the Reich Chancellery, the Four-Year Plan, the Party and the General Government. Heydrich told them that he had been given charge of the "Final Solution of the Jewish question" and that the ministries must cooperate. The protocol lists Jews country by country, from Germany and France to Britain, Ireland and the neutral states, and reaches a total of over **11 million**. It contained euphemisms, because it was a "top secret" document in 30 copies; only the sixteenth survives, found in 1947 in German Foreign Ministry papers.

Wannsee did not **decide** the murder of the Jews: Chełmno had begun killing 43 days earlier, the Einsatzgruppen had been shooting for seven months and deportations were under way. What it did was to bring the state's departments together under Heydrich's authority, show the plan reaching all Europe, and discuss the fate of the *Mischlinge* and of Jews married to non-Jews. Historians use it in different ways, as the next chapter shows.

### Operation Reinhard

:::def Operation Reinhard
The SS plan to murder the Jews of the General Government in Poland, named after Reinhard Heydrich (assassinated in Prague in June 1942). It was directed by SS General Odilo Globocnik at Lublin and ran three extermination camps, **Bełżec** (opened March 1942), **Sobibór** (May 1942) and **Treblinka** (July 1942), staffed by a few dozen Germans and Austrians at each camp, many from T4, and by auxiliaries, often former Soviet prisoners trained at Trawniki.
:::

These camps were built for one purpose: to murder. Trains brought Jews from ghettos, who were told that they were being "resettled", forced out and gassed within hours; a small group of prisoners was kept alive to sort belongings and bury or burn the bodies. The Jews of the General Government were killed in great waves in 1942. In Warsaw the **Great Deportation** from **22 July to 21 September 1942** sent about **265,000** people to Treblinka, a daily quota that Czerniaków could not accept. Operation Reinhard ended in 1943 after the revolts at Treblinka (2 August 1943) and Sobibór (14 October 1943), and after Operation Harvest Festival (*Erntefest*) on 3–4 November 1943, in which about 43,000 Jews were shot in the Lublin district. The camps were then dismantled and the sites planted over.

### Auschwitz-Birkenau

Auschwitz began in 1940 as a concentration camp for Polish political prisoners. From 1941 the SS built a vast second camp, **Birkenau**, and by March 1942 it was working as a concentration camp and killing centre. Jews arrived from every country in occupied Europe, were "selected" on the ramp, and the majority, those judged unfit to work, were gassed using the pesticide Zyklon B within hours; others were worked to death, including at the IG Farben factory at Monowitz. At least **1.3 million** people were deported there, and about **1.1 million** were murdered, of whom about **1 million** were Jews; the others were Poles, Roma, Soviet prisoners of war and others. In 1944 the largest single deportation was of Hungary's Jews: from 15 May to early July, more than 400,000 were deported, nearly all to Auschwitz. In January 1945, as the Red Army approached, the SS marched prisoners westward on death marches. Soviet troops entered the camp on **27 January 1945** and found about 7,000 prisoners alive.

### The "Final Solution" in the rest of Europe

German policy depended on others. In occupied western Europe the Germans used the local police and civil service. In France the Vichy regime passed its own anti-Jewish statute in October 1940, and on 16–17 July 1942 French police arrested about 13,000 Jews in Paris in the round-up that is named after the Vélodrome d'Hiver; about 4,000 were children. Dutch, Belgian, Slovak and Greek Jews were deported by train. Germany's allies Romania and Croatia carried out their own massacres; Hungary's gendarmes helped to deport its Jews in 1944. In Denmark in October 1943, warned in advance, ordinary Danes ferried nearly all the country's Jews, about 7,000, to neutral Sweden. In Bulgaria the government deported Jews from the territories it had occupied (about 11,000) but protected its citizen Jews after public protests. The variety of outcomes is important evidence for the debate about the role of local conditions and of choice.

The **Roma and Sinti** were persecuted under the racial laws and treated as "asocial" or "racially inferior"; their genocide is called the *Porajmos* by some Romani people. It was recognised by the West German government only in 1982. The main reason for treating the Jews as a distinct case is that the Nazi leadership aimed to murder every Jewish person in Europe, wherever they lived, however old they were and whatever they did, because they were Jews; for no other group was the aim the same. Historians argue about whether this justifies the narrow definition of "the Holocaust" or whether the broader term should be used (the next chapter).

:::warn Do not mix up the numbers
Six million is the approximate number of **Jewish** victims. Eleven million is the number of Jews Heydrich counted in Europe at Wannsee, not the number killed. A figure of eleven million is sometimes quoted for all victims of Nazism, but historians do not use it, and it blurs the Nazis' distinct aim to murder every Jew. Always give a source and say "about": the USHMM, for example, says "about six million".
:::

## Jewish responses

The syllabus asks how far Jews resisted, and the answer depends on how resistance is defined. Here are the main forms.

- **Emigration and flight**, until 1941, and by Jews who escaped across borders later.
- **Survival in the ghettos.** Jews organised soup kitchens, schools, medical care and religious services in hunger and typhus, often in secret. Children and young men and women smuggled food through the ghetto walls, and the smuggling kept tens of thousands alive.
- **Recording.** In Warsaw the historian **Emanuel Ringelblum** organised the *Oyneg Shabes* ("joy of the Sabbath") secret archive, which collected diaries, documents and reports on the ghetto and the killings; in 1942–43 it was buried in milk cans and metal boxes and two parts were found after the war. Ringelblum was killed in 1944.
- **Hiding.** Anne Frank, aged 13, went into hiding in Amsterdam with her family in July 1942; they were betrayed in August 1944, and she died in Bergen-Belsen in early 1945. Many thousands of Jews survived in hiding with the help of non-Jews.
- **Moral and spiritual resistance.** Janusz Korczak, a Polish-Jewish doctor and teacher, ran an orphanage in the Warsaw ghetto, and in August 1942 walked with the children to the deportation point and went with them to Treblinka.
- **Armed resistance.** In the **Warsaw ghetto uprising** (19 April to 16 May 1943) about 700 fighters of the Jewish Combat Organization (ŻOB) and others resisted the SS and police, when the Germans came to deport the last of the ghetto; the fighters, led by Mordechai Anielewicz, knew they could not win. About 42,000 people were captured and sent to labour camps and Majdanek, at least 7,000 died in the ghetto, and another 7,000 were sent to Treblinka. The uprising lasted nearly four weeks. Prisoners also rose at Treblinka (2 August 1943), **Sobibór** (14 October 1943), where Leon Feldhendler and the Soviet-Jewish officer Alexander Pechersky led the revolt and about 300 escaped (about 50 survived the war), and at Auschwitz-Birkenau (7 October 1944). Jewish partisans fought in the forests of Belarus and Lithuania; the Bielski brothers' group sheltered over a thousand people.

Why was armed resistance rare? The reasons include hunger and exhaustion; the lack of weapons and of outside help; the policy of collective punishment, in which the Germans killed many for the actions of one; the Germans' deception, which told victims they were being resettled; the hostile or indifferent population outside the ghettos; and, for many, their care for families that they would not leave. Historians therefore warn against the question "Why didn't they resist?" and ask instead what resistance could mean (the next chapter).

## Contemporary reactions

:::def Bystander
In Holocaust history, a person, government or organisation that knew of the persecution and killing and neither carried it out nor was a victim. The word includes neighbours, churches, neutral states and the Allies, and historians argue over how much each could have done.
:::

**What was known.** The killings of 1941 and 1942 were not secret for long. Soldiers on leave described the shootings, and Polish and Jewish underground groups sent reports to London. In June 1942 the British press and the BBC reported, from a Jewish socialist (Bund) report from Poland, that 700,000 Jews had been murdered. On **8 August 1942** **Gerhart Riegner**, of the World Jewish Congress in Geneva, sent a telegram to London and Washington reporting that a plan to kill Europe's Jews, using gas, had been discussed in Hitler's headquarters. The US State Department, doubtful about the report, held it back from the head of the American Jewish Congress, Rabbi Stephen Wise, who received it by another route on 28 August; the State Department confirmed it in November 1942. The Polish courier **Jan Karski**, who had seen the Warsaw ghetto and a transit camp, told Allied leaders in 1942–43, including President Roosevelt in July 1943.

**What the Allies did.** On **17 December 1942** the Allied governments issued a declaration that condemned the "extermination" of the Jews and promised punishment of the guilty; in the House of Commons, Foreign Secretary Anthony Eden read it, and the members stood in silence. At the **Bermuda Conference** (19–30 April 1943) British and American officials discussed refugees but changed no policy; the USA kept its immigration quotas, and Britain kept limits on Jewish entry to Palestine. In **January 1944** (22 January) Roosevelt created the **War Refugee Board**, after officials in the Treasury, led by Henry Morgenthau's staff, wrote a report that accused the State Department of obstructing rescue. The Board helped to fund the work of Raoul Wallenberg, a Swedish diplomat, in Budapest in 1944. In the summer of 1944 Jewish leaders asked the Allies to bomb Auschwitz or the railways leading to it, after two prisoners who had escaped in April 1944 gave details; the US War Department replied on 4 July 1944 that the diversion of aircraft was impracticable and might provoke worse. American bombers struck the IG Farben works at Monowitz, a few miles from Birkenau, on 20 August 1944. Historians continue to argue about whether bombing could have saved many (see the next chapter).

**Others.** The Catholic Church and Pope Pius XII did not condemn the killing of the Jews by name, and historians still debate why. Some churches and individuals acted: in Poland the underground Council for Aid to Jews (*Żegota*) was founded in December 1942 although helping Jews carried the death penalty; in France the villagers of Le Chambon-sur-Lignon sheltered many Jewish refugees, including children. Many neighbours were indifferent, some profited from Jewish property, and some denounced Jews in hiding.

:::key What contemporary reactions show
1. **Knowledge came early but disbelief and caution slowed action.** By the end of 1942 the Allied governments had the facts and had said so.
2. **Governments' priorities were military.** The Allies argued that victory was the best rescue; critics replied that this was an excuse for doing nothing that cost anything.
3. **Immigration policy, not just war, closed doors before 1941.** Évian and the quotas of the 1930s limited who could leave.
4. **Individuals could act.** Denmark, Le Chambon, *Żegota* and Wallenberg show what was possible, in some places, at some times.
5. **For Paper 3.** The bystander debate (Wyman, Wasserstein, Rubinstein) is about whether more could have been done; an extract on this subject will turn on how it answers that.
:::

## Three documents

Paper 3 extracts rest on a few famous documents. You do not need to quote them, but you do need to know what each is, who made it, and how a historian would use it. Each is given with its provenance, in the board's style. Where words are quoted they are the document's own, from the translation named.

**Document 1: the Nuremberg Laws.** *Adapted from the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour and the Reich Citizenship Law, 15 September 1935; Reichsgesetzblatt 1935, part I, pp. 1146–47, in English translation.*

> "Marriages between Jews and nationals of German or kindred blood are forbidden. … Extramarital intercourse between Jews and nationals of German or kindred blood is forbidden."

*Nature and origin.* A law of the Reich, published in the official gazette, so it is an authoritative statement of state policy. *Purpose.* To define who counted as a German by blood and to separate the groups. *What it shows.* The racial definition of Jewishness and the state's role in persecution. *Limits.* A law does not show how it was applied or what people thought. *Used by.* Intentionalists, who see in it the Nazis' racial programme in law; and by those who stress that it was drafted in haste to satisfy party radicals and left many questions to later decrees.

**Document 2: the Wannsee Protocol.** *Adapted from the minutes of the Wannsee Conference, 20 January 1942, drawn up by Adolf Eichmann and edited by Reinhard Heydrich; copy 16 of 30, found in German Foreign Ministry files in 1947; English translation in the Nazi Conspiracy and Aggression volumes (Nuremberg) and on the Avalon Project, Yale.*

> "Another possible solution of the problem has now taken the place of emigration, i.e. the evacuation of the Jews to the East … Able-bodied Jews, separated according to sex, will be taken in large work columns to these areas for work on roads, in the course of which action doubtless a large portion will be eliminated by natural causes. … The possible final remnant will, since it will undoubtedly consist of the most resistant portion, have to be treated accordingly …"

*Nature and origin.* A secret, edited record of a meeting by the men who ran the policy. *Purpose.* To record Heydrich's authority and the cooperation of the ministries; it was written in euphemism ("evacuation", "eliminated by natural causes", "treated accordingly") because it was a document circulated to officials. *What it shows.* That by January 1942 the policy aimed at the Jews of all Europe, 11 million by its count, and that it involved forced labour as a path to death. *Limits.* Euphemism means that the document does not say "kill" in plain words; it is not a record of a decision, since it was held after killing had begun. *Used by.* Those who see a plan (and who use it to argue that the aim was extermination); and those who argue that the decision was taken earlier or later and that Wannsee shows administration and coordination.

**Document 3: Himmler's Posen speech.** *Adapted from a speech by Heinrich Himmler to SS Gruppenführer (generals) at Posen, 4 October 1943; Nuremberg document 1919-PS (Nazi Conspiracy and Aggression, vol. IV); modern English translation by Stephane Bruchfeld, Gordon McFee and Ulrich Rössler, published by the Nizkor Project and the Jewish Virtual Library.*

> "I am talking about the evacuation of the Jews, the extermination of the Jewish people. … Most of you will know what it means when 100 bodies lie together, when 500 are there or when there are 1000."

*Nature and origin.* A recorded speech, to an audience of senior SS officers, by the man who led the SS. *Purpose.* Among other things, to bind the audience to the crime by making them share the secret. *What it shows.* That the Reichsführer-SS spoke openly of "extermination" to a private audience, and that he regarded it as policy; it is one of the clearest statements and was used at Nuremberg. *Limits.* A speech, not an order; it says nothing about who decided or when; Himmler spoke to flatter and to bind his hearers, and it is an SS audience. *Used by.* Everyone, since it ends any argument about whether the leadership knew what was happening; but intentionalists, and functionalists, differ about what it shows of Hitler's role.

:::key How to use a document in an answer
Say **what it is** (nature and origin), **what it shows**, **what a historian does with it**, and **what limits it** (euphemism, audience, date, who wrote it and why). Never use a document as a decoration; every time you quote one, tie it to a claim in the extract.
:::

## Evidence, and why denial is not an interpretation

The Holocaust is among the best-documented events in history. The evidence comes from many independent kinds of source.

- **The perpetrators' own documents:** orders, reports of the Einsatzgruppen, railway timetables, the Jäger report, the Wannsee protocol, Himmler's speeches, company records of the firms that built the ovens and supplied the gas.
- **Testimony:** the confessions of Nazi leaders and camp staff at Nuremberg (1945–46), including Höss; the trial of Adolf Eichmann in Jerusalem in 1961; the German trials of Auschwitz staff in Frankfurt (1963–65); the testimony of thousands of survivors, and diaries such as those of Klemperer, Czerniaków, Ringelblum and Anne Frank.
- **Physical evidence:** the camps, the mass graves, the photographs taken by the Germans and by the liberators in 1945, the aerial photographs.
- **Records of the victims' countries:** deportation lists and census records, which show who was missing.

Historians argue about **interpretation**: why, how, who and when. They do not argue about whether it happened, because the evidence rules that out. **Holocaust denial** is a political claim that the murder of the Jews did not take place, or was far smaller, and it fails the tests of evidence. In 2000 a British High Court judge, Mr Justice Gray, in the case of *Irving v Penguin Books and Lipstadt*, found that the historian David Irving had deliberately distorted the historical record to deny the Holocaust and to defend Hitler. You can read about the case at the Emory University site *Holocaust Denial on Trial*, which is in the links at the end. For Paper 3 the point is this: an extract is always an interpretation of events that happened, and the question tests whether you can see what it claims, not whether you accept that the events occurred.

## Using this knowledge on Paper 3

Half the marks (Table B, AO1) go for knowledge that is accurate, detailed and relevant. A good knowledge paragraph begins with a claim in the extract and uses facts to explain it, test it or place it. The four moves of Section A work here too.

:::key Four moves for knowledge in Section B
1. **Name the event, date and number exactly:** "On 20 January 1942 fifteen officials met at Wannsee, where Heydrich told them he had been given charge of the 'Final Solution'."
2. **Say what the extract claims it shows:** "The historian reads Wannsee as the point where a plan was settled."
3. **Test it with a second fact:** "But Chełmno had begun killing on 8 December 1941, and the Einsatzgruppen had been shooting women and children since the summer, so the policy of murder existed before the meeting."
4. **Say what the choice of facts reveals about the approach:** "By putting Wannsee at the centre, the extract shows a historian looking for a single moment of decision and for the central direction of policy."
:::

:::eg Table B: a claim tested with knowledge, level by level
**The claim (written for practice; not a historian's words).** "The persecution of the Jews between 1933 and 1939 was a planned road to extermination."

**What the levels want (Table B).** Level 3 (9–11) is generally accurate and relevant; Level 4 (12–14) is mostly relevant and accurate; Level 6 (18–20) is detailed, accurate and entirely relevant: every fact is doing a job.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Nazis hated the Jews. After Hitler came to power in 1933 there was a boycott and the Nuremberg Laws and then Kristallnacht, when many Jews were killed. Then the war began and the Jews were put in ghettos and then in camps like Auschwitz and six million were murdered. So the Nazis were always going to murder the Jews and this was their plan."

*Commentary.* Accurate and about the right events, but a list that jumps from 1938 to 1945 and ends by repeating the claim. No dates, no numbers and no test. Level 3.

**A Level 4 paragraph (adds explanation).** "The claim is supported by what Hitler wrote and said. In 1920 the party's programme said that no Jew could be a citizen, and *Mein Kampf* described the Jews as a poison. The Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935 took citizenship from Jews and banned marriage with 'Germans', and after Kristallnacht on 9–10 November 1938, when about 26,000 Jewish men were sent to camps, the regime drove the Jews from the economy. On 30 January 1939 Hitler said that a new world war would bring the 'annihilation of the Jewish race in Europe'. These facts show a steady direction."

*Commentary.* Detailed and accurate, and each fact explains why the claim might be true. But the paragraph only supports the claim, and the claim says 'planned' and 'extermination'.

**A Level 6 paragraph (explains, tests and links to the approach).** "The claim is partly supported. Hitler's ideas were fixed early: his letter of September 1919 spoke of removing Jews' rights and, in the end, removing the Jews, the 25 points of 1920 denied Jews citizenship, and the Nuremberg Laws and the decrees of 1938 followed a consistent direction. The prophecy of 30 January 1939 shows that Hitler could imagine annihilation. But the claim that this was a *plan* is hard to sustain for the period to 1939. The declared aim was emigration: over half of Germany's Jews had left by 1939, Eichmann's office in Vienna was organised to force them out, and the Madagascar plan of 1940 was an expulsion scheme. The pace of persecution varied with party pressure, as in the summer of 1935 and in November 1938, and with foreign opinion, as at the Berlin Olympics. No document before 1941 orders killing Jews, and the first mass killing programme, T4, was aimed at disabled Germans. The deportations began only on 15 October 1941 and emigration was banned on 23 October. A historian who writes 'road to extermination' is therefore reading 1933–39 backwards from 1941–45, and an extract that does so shows an approach that looks for Hitler's intentions and the unity of the whole; one that stresses variation and radicalisation points to the competing agencies of the Nazi state."

*Commentary.* Every sentence of knowledge does a job: the early texts support the claim, the emigration figures and the Madagascar plan test it, and the last sentence says what the choice of facts shows about the approach. Level 6.
:::

:::eg How the Kristallnacht, the Einsatzgruppen and Wannsee can each be used to test an interpretation
**Question.** For each event, say what interpretation it can support, what interpretation it can weaken, and what a historian would need to say to make it fit.

**Answer.**
- **Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938).** *Supports* the claim that violence and law worked together and that the regime could unleash terror to push Jews out; it also supports an argument that Goebbels and the party radicals, not Hitler's long plan, set the pace, since Goebbels's speech seems to have set off the pogrom and Göring and others were angry about the economic damage. *Weakens* the claim of a smooth plan, since the regime's own officials disagreed about it. *To make it fit,* a historian must say whether the pogrom was a decision of the leadership or an escalation from below.
- **The Einsatzgruppen (from 22 June 1941).** *Supports* the claim that the killing grew out of the war in the East and the radical measures of the SS on the ground: the extension from men to women and children in the late summer of 1941, with local initiative by commanders. *Weakens* the claim that the policy of murder was only decided at the centre in Berlin in the winter. *To make it fit,* a historian must say whether commanders were carrying out an order, anticipating one, or driving policy.
- **Wannsee (20 January 1942).** *Supports* the claim that central direction brought the departments together to organise murder across Europe. *Weakens* the claim that this was the decision, since killing had begun already at Chełmno. *To make it fit,* a historian must say what the meeting was for: decision, coordination or Heydrich's assertion of authority.
- **The point.** The same event supports one reading and complicates another; the examiner is looking for the sentence that says which.
:::

:::eg Explain the significance of the Wannsee Conference for the history of the Holocaust
**What the levels want.** A short significance answer scores on knowledge and on explaining what the fact changed. A good one says what the event was, what it changed, and what limits the claim.

**Answer (about 190 words).** "The Wannsee Conference took place on 20 January 1942, at a villa in Berlin. Reinhard Heydrich, head of the Reich Security Main Office, chaired it and Adolf Eichmann took the minutes. Fifteen officials from the SS and the Reich ministries attended. Heydrich told them that he was in charge of the 'Final Solution of the Jewish question' and that the ministries must cooperate, and the minutes list over 11 million Jews across Europe, from Germany and France to Britain and the neutral states. It was significant because it shows the plan reaching the whole continent and the state's civil service sharing in it, and because the language of 'evacuation to the East' and 'natural reduction' hid the killing. But its significance is limited. Killing had begun at Chełmno on 8 December 1941, Operation Reinhard was being built, and the Einsatzgruppen had shot hundreds of thousands. Wannsee was therefore not the decision to murder the Jews, but a meeting that organised and coordinated a policy already under way, and confirmed Heydrich's authority."

*Commentary.* It gives the date, the people and the numbers; it explains what the meeting did and what it did not; it uses another fact (Chełmno) to place it. The question is a significance question, so it has an argument.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
An extract argues that the decision to murder the Jews was taken at Wannsee. A student wrote this about the knowledge that tests it:

"The Wannsee Conference was in January 1942 and it was about the Jews. The Nazis killed six million Jews in the Holocaust, mostly in Auschwitz. Hitler hated the Jews and wrote about it in *Mein Kampf*. So the Holocaust was decided at Wannsee because the Nazis planned to kill all the Jews."

**The level.** Table B **Level 2** (5–8): there is some accurate knowledge (the date of Wannsee, six million, *Mein Kampf*) but it is general and some is wrong: most of the six million were not killed at Auschwitz (about a million were), and the paragraph does not use any fact to test the claim that Wannsee was the decision. The last sentence repeats the claim.

**The fix.** "The extract claims that Wannsee, on 20 January 1942, was the moment of decision. The conference, chaired by Heydrich with Eichmann taking minutes, did make the 'Final Solution' a matter for the whole state: fifteen officials were told to cooperate and the minutes counted over 11 million Jews. But the killing was already happening. The Einsatzgruppen had shot 33,771 Jews at Babi Yar in September 1941 and women and children in many places; deportations from the Reich had begun on 15 October; and gas vans had begun murdering Jews at Chełmno on 8 December 1941, 43 days before the conference. A claim that Wannsee was the decision therefore fits a historian who looks for a single moment and central direction, and it passes over the evidence that policy developed in stages." The fix gives a date for each fact and uses it to test the claim.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name three ideas or events before 1933 that historians use to explain Nazi antisemitism.
A: Any three: the long Christian tradition of hostility; Marr and the word "antisemitism" (1879) and Treitschke's slogan; racial theory (Gobineau, Chamberlain 1899), Social Darwinism and eugenics; the pogroms in Russia and the *Protocols* (1903); the Dreyfus affair; the stab-in-the-back legend after 1918; the 25-point programme (1920) and *Mein Kampf* (1925–26).
Q: What did the Nuremberg Laws of 15 September 1935 do?
A: The Reich Citizenship Law made Jews "subjects", not citizens; the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour banned marriage and sexual relations between Jews and "Germans"; a decree of 14 November 1935 defined a Jew by grandparents.
Q: What happened on 9–10 November 1938, and what followed?
A: Nazi leaders organised a pogrom after the death of Ernst vom Rath: over 1,400 synagogues were burned or destroyed, shops smashed, hundreds of Jews died, and about 26,000 Jewish men were sent to Dachau, Buchenwald and Sachsenhausen. Afterwards Jews were fined 1 billion Reichsmarks and excluded from the economy.
Q: Why did the Évian Conference fail?
A: Delegates from 32 countries met in July 1938, but nearly all refused to raise their quotas, citing unemployment and fear of immigration; only the Dominican Republic offered to take significant numbers.
Q: What was Aktion T4 and why does it matter for the Holocaust?
A: The programme of "euthanasia" authorised by Hitler in October 1939 (backdated to 1 September), which killed disabled patients with carbon monoxide in six centres; 70,273 had been killed by August 1941; it matters because it trained the staff and developed the methods of gassing used in Operation Reinhard.
Q: What were the Einsatzgruppen, and what happened at Babi Yar?
A: Mobile killing squads of about 3,000 men that followed the army into the USSR from 22 June 1941; at Babi Yar, near Kyiv, on 29–30 September 1941 they and local auxiliaries shot 33,771 Jews (their own report's figure).
Q: Put in order: Wannsee Conference; Chełmno begins killing; the Great Deportation from Warsaw; Hitler becomes Chancellor; the Nuremberg Laws.
A: Hitler becomes Chancellor (30 January 1933); the Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935); Chełmno begins killing (8 December 1941); the Wannsee Conference (20 January 1942); the Great Deportation from Warsaw (22 July 1942).
Q: What were the Judenräte, and why are they controversial?
A: Jewish councils that the Germans ordered to run ghettos and carry out German orders, including supplying labour and, later, lists for deportation; they kept ghettos going and tried to save lives, but their cooperation helped the Germans, and historians disagree about how far leaders such as Czerniaków and Rumkowski should be judged.
Q: Give three facts about the Warsaw ghetto uprising.
A: It began on 19 April 1943; about 700 fighters of the ŻOB and others, led by Mordechai Anielewicz, resisted the SS for nearly four weeks, until about 16 May; the ghetto was destroyed, about 42,000 were captured and sent to labour camps and Majdanek.
Q: What did the Allies do after the Riegner telegram, and what did they not do?
A: They confirmed the report in November 1942 and issued a declaration on 17 December 1942 condemning the "extermination"; but at Bermuda (April 1943) they changed no refugee policy, the War Refugee Board was set up only in January 1944, and they did not bomb Auschwitz or the rail lines.
Q: Why is Holocaust denial not a historical interpretation?
A: Because it denies events that are proved by the perpetrators' documents, testimony, physical evidence and records; a historical interpretation is an argument about why and how events happened, and must fit the evidence.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain the significance of the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 for the persecution of the Jews.
A: Indicative content: what they were (15 September 1935, Reich Citizenship Law and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour; the decree of 14 November defining who was a Jew); what they changed (citizenship removed; marriage and sexual relations banned; descent, not religion, defined Jewishness); what they led to (a legal base for Aryanisation and later decrees); limits (they did not themselves lead to murder; emigration remained the aim). A top answer links the laws to the idea of racial antisemitism and says what a historian who stresses a plan, and one who stresses the party's pressure in 1935, would each make of them.
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the war with the Soviet Union, from June 1941, changed Nazi policy towards the Jews.
A: Indicative content: the invasion of 22 June 1941 was a war of annihilation; Einsatzgruppen (about 3,000 men) followed the armies; shooting extended from men to women and children from the late summer; millions more Jews were under German rule; the Jäger report (137,346) and Babi Yar (33,771) show scale; Göring's letter of 31 July; deportations from the Reich from 15 October and the ban on emigration on 23 October. A top answer explains that the war made killing seem possible and a "military necessity", and notes that historians date the decisions differently.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Show how the Wannsee Conference of 20 January 1942 can be used both to support and to challenge the view that the "Final Solution" was a plan decided by the leadership in Berlin.
A: Support: Heydrich, in charge, told 15 officials the Jews of all Europe (over 11 million) were to be dealt with; the ministries were brought in; the protocol speaks of "evacuation to the East" and "treated accordingly"; it shows central direction. Challenge: it was held after Chełmno (8 December 1941) and months after the first mass shootings; policy had developed through local initiatives, the war and the SS's own actions; the protocol is euphemistic and does not record a decision by Hitler. A top answer says which view is better supported, and what the choice reveals about the historian's approach.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Explain how Jewish responses to persecution in the ghettos can be understood as resistance.
A: Indicative content: survival work (soup kitchens, schools, medicine, secret religious life); smuggling in Warsaw; the *Oyneg Shabes* archive; Korczak's orphanage; armed resistance (Warsaw ghetto uprising 19 April–16 May 1943, Sobibór on 14 October 1943, partisans); the Judenräte's dilemmas (Czerniaków, Rumkowski). A top answer notes the debate on defining resistance (armed only, or any act of defiance) and the constraints (hunger, collective punishment, deception, no weapons or allies).
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice; not a historian's words.) "The persecution of the Jews between 1933 and 1938 was driven more by pressure from the Nazi Party than by a plan of Hitler's." Use your knowledge of 1933–38 to explain and test this claim.
H: Which events show party or SA pressure? (April 1933 boycott, summer 1935, November 1938.)
H: Which show the state and Hitler setting direction? (The Civil Service Law, the Nuremberg Laws, Göring's decrees.)
H: What did the persecution have in common throughout?
A: Support: the boycott of 1 April 1933 was organised by the party and called off; street violence in the summer of 1935 preceded the Nuremberg Laws, which Hitler had officials draft in a few days; Kristallnacht was set off by Goebbels's speech on 9 November and angered Göring who feared the economic damage; the regime paused for the Olympics and for the economy. Test: Hitler's ideas were consistent; each wave ended in law made by the state (7 April 1933, 15 September 1935, 12 November 1938); Göring's Four-Year Plan drove Aryanisation; the aim of forced emigration was constant; Hitler himself announced the Nuremberg Laws and made the prophecy of January 1939. A top answer concludes that the evidence supports a mixture, with party pressure setting the timing and the state setting the framework, and says what an extract that stresses only one reveals about its approach.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice.) "Without the Second World War there would have been no Holocaust." Use your knowledge to explain and test this claim.
H: What changed in 1939–41: territory, numbers, methods, secrecy?
H: What did Nazi policy look like before 1939?
H: What evidence suggests intentions earlier than the war?
A: Support: before 1939 the policy was forced emigration; the war brought 3.3 million Polish Jews and later millions more under German rule; ghettos and shootings began in conditions of war; the killing of Soviet Jews in 1941 and the gassing at Chełmno (8 December 1941) came after Barbarossa; T4 was masked as a wartime measure; Hitler's January 1939 prophecy was tied to a new world war. Test: Hitler's antisemitism and the Nuremberg Laws predate the war; the T4 authorisation and ideas of "unworthy life" show willingness to kill earlier; the prophecy of 30 January 1939 shows Hitler could think of annihilation; the war was started by the same regime. A top answer judges that the war was a necessary condition for the form and scale of the murder, but that the intentions and the structures of racial persecution came before; and says that a historian who stresses contingency and one who stresses intention would each use the evidence in different ways.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice.) "The Allies could have done much more to save Europe's Jews." Use your knowledge to explain and test this claim.
H: What did the Allies know and when?
H: What actions were considered: immigration, bombing, the War Refugee Board?
H: What limited the Allies?
A: Support: the Riegner telegram (8 August 1942) and Karski's reports; the declaration of 17 December 1942 was not followed by action; Bermuda in April 1943 changed nothing; US quotas stayed filled below limits; the War Refugee Board was created only in January 1944; requests to bomb Auschwitz in 1944 were refused on 4 July, though the IG Farben works at Monowitz was bombed on 20 August; Denmark and Wallenberg show what was possible. Test: Europe was under German control; the Allies believed that victory was the best rescue; Britain feared for Palestine and the USA for jobs; bombing would have killed prisoners; cases such as Hungary 1944 show late and limited success. A top answer assesses what was possible at each date, and notes that the question is the subject of the Wyman/Rubinstein argument.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ (A statement written for practice.) "The ghettos were created to hold the Jews until a final policy was decided, not to kill them." Use your knowledge of the Warsaw and Łódź ghettos to explain and test this claim, and say what the claim would show about an historian's approach.
H: What did the Germans intend in 1939–40: concentration, or murder?
H: What conditions killed people in the ghettos (rations, typhus)?
H: What happened to the ghettos in 1942?
A: Support: Heydrich's order of 21 September 1939 concentrated Jews in cities near railways; the ghettos were first holding places (Łódź from April 1940, Warsaw from November 1940); the Madagascar plan of 1940 shows that deportation was still the idea; Jewish councils and workshops (Rumkowski) kept some ghettos alive until 1944; the Germans used Jewish labour. Test: the official ration in Warsaw was 181 calories per day, and tens of thousands died of hunger and disease before any deportation; the Great Deportation from Warsaw (22 July–21 September 1942) sent about 265,000 people to Treblinka, and the Reinhard camps were built to empty the ghettos of the General Government; so the ghettos can be seen both as an interim solution and as a stage toward killing. A top answer says that the evidence supports both "holding" and "killing by neglect", and that the historian who sees them only as a holding stage is stressing circumstance and improvisation over intention.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Explain how knowledge of the Einsatzgruppen in 1941 could be used to support a functionalist reading of the origins of the Holocaust, and a more intentionalist reading, using the same facts.
H: A functionalist stresses local initiative and the pressure of war; an intentionalist stresses orders from the centre.
H: What do the dates 22 June, 31 July and the late summer show?
A: Functionalist: the shift from killing men to killing women and children happened in the field, in different places at different times, as commanders such as those of Einsatzgruppen and Order Police units took initiatives; the war's brutality and the shortage of food made the killing seem practical; the rapid escalation can be seen as radicalisation driven by circumstance. Intentionalist: the Einsatzgruppen were created in advance, with the Commissar Order and Heydrich's instructions; Göring's letter of 31 July shows that planning continued at the centre; Himmler visited the East in August 1941 and ordered the extension; the killing of Jewish women and children was consistent with Hitler's ideas. A top answer uses the same facts for both and shows that the difference is in the emphasis and in the question asked (how it happened, or what was intended).

:::recap
- **Before 1933.** Religious hostility → emancipation in Germany by 1871 → racial antisemitism from the 1870s (Marr 1879, Chamberlain 1899), Social Darwinism and eugenics → the stab-in-the-back legend after 1918. Antisemitism was European, not only German; the new Nazi element was a state policy of racial removal.
- **Nazi ideas.** 25 points (24 February 1920), *Mein Kampf* (1925–26); Jews as a racial enemy and as a world conspiracy.
- **1933–39.** About 500,000 Jews in Germany. Boycott (1 April 1933), Civil Service Law (7 April 1933), Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935), Aryanisation, Anschluss (March 1938), Évian (July 1938), Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938; about 26,000 men sent to camps; 1 billion Reichsmark fine). The aim until 1941 was emigration.
- **War.** Poland (1 September 1939), 3.3 million Polish Jews; ghettos (Łódź April 1940, Warsaw November 1940); Judenräte; Madagascar plan (1940); T4 (October 1939; 70,273 killed by August 1941; about 250,000 in all).
- **Killing.** Einsatzgruppen from 22 June 1941; Babi Yar 29–30 September 1941 (33,771); deportations from 15 October; Chełmno 8 December 1941; Wannsee 20 January 1942 (over 11 million Jews counted); Reinhard camps 1942; Auschwitz. About six million Jews were murdered, about a third by shooting. Other victims: Roma at least 250,000; disabled about 250,000–300,000; Soviet POWs about 3.3 million; Polish civilians about 1.8 million.
- **Responses.** Judenräte; Czerniaków and Rumkowski; Warsaw ghetto uprising (19 April–16 May 1943); Sobibór (14 October 1943); partisans.
- **Reactions.** Riegner telegram (8 August 1942); declaration (17 December 1942); Bermuda (April 1943); War Refugee Board (22 January 1944); bombing refused (4 July 1944).
- **Use.** A knowledge paragraph begins with the extract's claim, names events and numbers exactly, tests with a second fact, and says what the choice shows about the approach. Denial is not an interpretation.
