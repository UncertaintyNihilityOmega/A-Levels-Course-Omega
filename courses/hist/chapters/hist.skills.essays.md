:::why
Most students who lose marks in History essays do not lack knowledge. They tell the story instead of answering the question. An essay is an argument: a claim, reasons, evidence, and a verdict that weighs one reason against another. This chapter teaches that argument as a set of habits you can practise, the same habits for AS Paper 2 and A Level Paper 4. Once you have them, any topic chapter in this course gives you the facts to use.
:::

:::try Warm-up
Q: In a PEEL paragraph, which step is the one that narrative answers leave out? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))
A: Explain: saying how the evidence proves the point. Evidence does not argue for itself; "interest was 318 million livres" becomes analysis only when you add what it shows.
Q: What is the difference between a long-term cause, a short-term cause and a trigger? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))
A: A long-term cause builds the conditions over years; a short-term cause makes a crisis likely in the months before; a trigger is the immediate event that sets things off.
Q: Why can a source be strong evidence for one question and weak for another? (from [Skills: reading and comparing sources](#hist.skills.sources))
A: Because strength is judged for a particular question, using the source's nature, origin, purpose and context. In the same way, a factor is important or unimportant only in relation to the outcome the question asks about.

## What the examiner is asking

Every essay question has a **command word** (what you must do) and a **focus** (what you must do it to). If you misread either, even a brilliant answer scores low.

:::def Command word
The word or phrase in a question that tells you what kind of answer to write: for example Explain, Assess, Evaluate, "To what extent" or "How far".
:::

| Command | What the examiner wants | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Explain why** | Say why something happened: set out the reasons, make the relationships between them clear, and support each with relevant evidence | Paper 2 (a) |
| **To what extent … ? / How far do you agree …? / How far was … ?** | A reasoned **judgement**: consider the view in the question, consider alternatives, and say which is stronger | Paper 2 (b) |
| **How successful / How important was …?** | Judge against **criteria** (successful at what? compared with what?) and give a verdict | Paper 2 (b) |
| **Assess …** | Make an informed judgement about the importance, extent or significance of something, weighing several factors | Paper 4 |
| **Evaluate** (usually "Evaluate this view") | Judge the quality, importance or value of the view in the quotation | Paper 4 |

The two that confuse students most:
- **Explain** wants **causes and links**, not a verdict. It is the question for which a story is most tempting and least rewarded.
- **How far / To what extent / Assess / Evaluate** want a **judgement that you have argued for**. "There were many factors" is not a judgement.

:::def Judgement
A reasoned conclusion that says how far, or how much, or which is more important, and gives the reason for it. A judgement can be partial ("largely", "only in the short term"), but it must commit to a position that the evidence supports.
:::

:::def Criterion
A standard against which you decide: "most important" as the one without which the outcome would not have happened, or as the one with the widest effects. Naming the criterion makes your judgement visible and testable.
:::

## Unpack the question

Before you plan, spend a minute taking the question apart. Underline four things.

1. **The command** (Explain? How far? Assess? Evaluate?).
2. **The topic** (which event, person, policy, period).
3. **The focus**: the concept the question asks about (causes, success, importance, change).
4. **The limits**: dates, "main", "only", "mainly", "key", "the most". These tell you how strong the claim is.

:::eg Unpacking a question
'Nationalism was the main reason for the revolutions of 1848 in Germany.' How far do you agree? [20]

- **Command**: How far do you agree (a judgement on a view).
- **Topic**: the revolutions in the German states in 1848.
- **Focus**: reasons for the outbreak.
- **Limit**: "the **main** reason". You are not asked whether nationalism mattered (it did), but whether it mattered **more than the other causes**. A student who writes everything they know about nationalism has answered an easier question than the one set.

**What a one-sided answer would ignore.** Other causes: liberal demands for constitutions, civil rights and a free press; hunger and hardship after the potato blight and the bad harvests of 1846–47, and the slump that followed; the news of the revolution in Paris in February 1848, which set off the unrest in the German states. These are the "alternative perspectives" that must be weighed against nationalism. The question word "main" means the answer must **rank** them.
:::

:::warn Misreadings that cost whole levels
1. **Answering a different question**: "Why did X happen?" for "How far was X the main reason?". Narrow the knowledge to the claim.
2. **Ignoring the limits**: a question about 1861–65 does not need 1870.
3. **Writing about the topic** instead of the focus: all you know about Napoleon, not the question about his rise.
4. **Treating "How far do you agree" as "Do you agree?"**: a Yes/No, with no weighing.
5. **Missing a word**: "main", "only", "most", "failed", "successful". They set the strength of the claim.
:::

## Planning in three to five minutes

A plan takes 3 to 5 minutes and makes the next forty easier. It is where the **argument** is decided; the writing only carries it out. Without it, students write the first thing they know, run out of time and conclude with "so there were many reasons".

:::steps
1. **Unpack** the question (above).
2. **List the factors.** For a part (b) or Paper 4 essay: the view in the question, and at least two alternatives. For a part (a): two or three causes.
3. **Add evidence** to each factor: a date, a name, a number, an event. Two or three items each are enough.
4. **Rank** the factors by a **criterion** (see "Weighing and the criteria for judgement" below).
5. **Decide your judgement** before you start, and write it as one sentence.
6. **Note the links** between factors: which made another possible, or worse, or came first.
:::

## Paper 2 part (a): "Explain why …" [10]

**The task.** Explain why something happened. You are not asked for a judgement; you are asked for **causes** (usually two or three), each **explained** with relevant knowledge, and for the **connections** between them. The best answers also end with a reasoned conclusion.

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 4 | 9–10 | **Connects** factors to reach a reasoned conclusion: a range of factors explained with relevant information, clear understanding of how the causes link, a supported conclusion |
| 3 | 6–8 | **Explains** factor(s) with good knowledge and understanding, supported by relevant information |
| 2 | 3–5 | **Describes** factor(s): some knowledge, may address causation but may be entirely descriptive |
| 1 | 1–2 | Describes the topic, with no reference to causation |

:::key What moves a part (a) answer up the levels
- **Level 2 to 3**: stop describing and start **explaining**. Each paragraph names a cause and says **how** it produced the result: use "because", "which meant", "so that".
- **Level 3 to 4**: **connect** the causes. Say how one made another possible, worse or more likely, and which was most important. This is what the descriptor calls "connections between causes".
:::

Four kinds of link are worth knowing:
- **Enabling**: A made B possible (a victory in one place allowed an advance in another).
- **Reinforcing**: A made B worse (hunger made a political crisis more dangerous).
- **Sequence**: A led to B led to C, a chain.
- **Trigger and background**: a long-term cause made the situation unstable; a trigger set it off.

A part (a) answer needs about 350 to 450 words in 15 minutes: three short paragraphs and a conclusion of one or two sentences. No introduction beyond a sentence that names your factors.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Prussia defeated Austria in the Austro-Prussian War of 1866.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors; Level 4 (9–10) connects them and concludes.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Prussia had a new gun called the needle gun. The two armies fought at Königgrätz on 3 July 1866 and Prussia won. Bismarck had made an alliance with Italy. Moltke used the railways to move the army. Austria lost and the war ended with the Treaty of Prague."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but a list of facts. No sentence says **why** any of them produced a Prussian victory. The word "so" or "because" appears nowhere. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8 marks): explain each factor.** "One reason was Prussian military strength. The army had been enlarged and reorganised under Roon after 1860, and its infantry had the needle gun, a breech-loading rifle that could be loaded lying down and fired much faster than the muzzle-loading rifles of the Austrians. Moltke used the railways and the telegraph to bring three armies to Bohemia at once, so that at Königgrätz on 3 July the Prussians had about as many men as the Austrians but fired faster and arrived on the flank. A second reason was Bismarck's diplomacy. At Biarritz in October 1865 he won a vague promise of French neutrality, and in April 1866 an alliance with Italy, which forced Austria to keep an army in the south."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise detail (Roon, needle gun, Moltke, Biarritz, the Italian alliance). But the factors sit side by side and there is no conclusion. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10 marks): connect and conclude.**

"Prussia won mainly because Bismarck's diplomacy and Prussia's military reforms worked together. The first reason was military. The army had been enlarged and reorganised under Roon after 1860, its infantry had the needle gun, which could be loaded lying down and fired much faster than the Austrians' muzzle-loaders, and Moltke used the railways and the telegraph to bring three armies to Bohemia at once, so that on 3 July at Königgrätz the Prussians arrived on the Austrian flank and won in a day. The second reason was diplomatic. At Biarritz in October 1865 Bismarck won a vague promise of French neutrality, and in April 1866 an alliance with Italy that forced Austria to keep an army in the south. A third reason lay in Austria's own position. Its commander, Benedek, had to fight at Königgrätz with a river at his back, and its German allies were beaten or tied down within weeks, so Austria had no reserves to correct the mistake.

The factors are linked. The Italian alliance and French neutrality did not win the war, but they decided where it was fought: Austria had to divide its forces between Bohemia and Italy, and could not rely on any other power, so the war would be settled by one army in one place. That was the kind of war Prussia had prepared for, since Roon's reform gave Moltke an army he could move quickly by rail and equip with a faster-firing rifle. Diplomacy created the opportunity; the military superiority took it. If Prussia's army had been no better than Austria's, isolating Austria would not have won the war, and if Austria had had allies, Prussian speed would not have been enough. The decisive factor was therefore the army, but only because the diplomacy had made a short war possible."

*Commentary.* Three factors (military, diplomatic, Austrian weakness), explained with precise evidence, **connected** (diplomacy fixed the kind of war; the army won it) and ending with a supported conclusion about which mattered most. This is what the Level 4 descriptor means: "connects factors to reach a reasoned conclusion". About 380 words.
:::

:::warn Five ways to lose marks in part (a)
1. **Telling the story** of the war or event instead of explaining why it happened.
2. **One factor only**, however well explained. Two or three, connected, is the target.
3. **No evidence**: "Bismarck's diplomacy helped" with no Biarritz, no Italian alliance.
4. **A list of factors** with no links and no view about which mattered most.
5. **Writing a (b)-style judgement** on a view that was not in the question: (a) wants reasons, not "how far".
:::

## Paper 2 part (b): alternative perspectives and a comparative judgement [20]

**The task.** The question puts forward a view or a claim ("'…' How far do you agree?", "To what extent …?", "How far was … the main reason?"). You must explain the case **for** it, explain the **alternative perspectives** (the case against, or other factors), and judge which is stronger.

| Level | Marks | What the answer does |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 17–20 | A **comparative judgement** based on analysis of alternative perspectives: clear analysis that supports and challenges, consistently supported by relevant detailed evidence, and a clear judgement of the **relative strength** of the perspectives |
| 4 | 13–16 | Explains alternative perspectives, supporting and challenging with detailed evidence, but may end with a conclusion that does not clearly compare them |
| 3 | 9–12 | Explains a **single** perspective; the others are described, not explained |
| 2 | 5–8 | Aspects of the question, relevant description, few explicit links |
| 1 | 1–4 | General information loosely linked to the question |

The three steps up are all about the same thing: **how many perspectives you explain** (one, then two or more) and **whether you compare them** (a conclusion that says which is stronger and why).

:::key What each level step needs in part (b)
- **Level 2 to 3**: choose a side and **explain** it with detailed evidence, with an argument from beginning to end.
- **Level 3 to 4**: explain a second perspective **properly**, not in one line at the end.
- **Level 4 to 5**: **compare**. Say which side is stronger and why, using a criterion, and let that judgement run through the answer, not just the last paragraph.
:::

### Weighing and the criteria for judgement

Words such as "main", "key", "most important" or "successful" cannot be judged until you say **by what standard**. State a criterion and test the factors against it.

| Criterion | The question it asks | Example of use |
|---|---|---|
| **Necessary** | Without this, would the outcome still have happened? (the counterfactual test) | Bankruptcy was necessary for calling the Estates-General: without it the king would not have summoned it |
| **Decisive** | Which factor turned the situation from one outcome to another? | The factor that changed a crisis into a revolution |
| **Widest in effect** | Which touched the most people, regions or other factors? | A policy that changed the whole economy against one that helped a region |
| **Most lasting** | Whose effects survived? | A law that lasted for decades against an event of one year |
| **Root cause and symptom** | Does this explain the others, or does something else explain it? | Does the weakness of an institution explain a series of failures? |
| **Success against aims** (for "How successful") | Did it do what it set out to do, and at what cost? | Compare results with the stated aims, the short and the long term |

:::key Language that weighs
Use words that **compare**, and give the reason: "more important than … because", "outweighed by", "only possible because", "made worse by", "necessary but not sufficient", "in the short term … in the long term". A paragraph that never says one thing mattered more than another has described, not weighed.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The League of Nations was weak from the start because the USA did not join.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** of which is stronger.

**Plan (about 4 minutes).**
- For: the Senate rejected the treaty on 19 November 1919 (39 to 55) and again in March 1920; no American power behind sanctions; Germany excluded until 1926 and the USSR until 1934.
- Against 1, design: no army; sanctions depended on members; unanimity.
- Against 2, will: Britain and France, which led the League, would not act alone (Vilna 1920, Corfu 1923).
- Against 3, "from the start": early successes: Aaland Islands 1921, Upper Silesia 1921, Greece and Bulgaria 1925.
- Criterion: **root cause**, which weakness explains the others? Judgement: the USA's absence mattered, but the League's weakness came mainly from its design and the unwillingness of Britain and France to use it; the view is **partly right**.

**A Level 3 answer (explains one side).** "The League of Nations was weak because the USA did not join. The Senate voted against the Treaty of Versailles on 19 November 1919, by 55 votes to 39, so the USA never became a member, even though it was the idea of the American President, Woodrow Wilson. The USA was the richest country in the world and could have supplied money, trade and ships to support sanctions. Without it, the League depended on Britain and France, which had been weakened by the war. Germany and Russia were not members either. This shows that the League was weak from the start because the USA did not join."

*Commentary.* A well-explained single perspective with accurate evidence. Other factors are not considered, and the last sentence only repeats the claim. Level 3 (9–12).

**A Level 4 answer (two perspectives, no clear comparison).** Adds a paragraph: "However, the League had other weaknesses. It had no army, and under the Covenant its members were only to apply economic sanctions, with military action merely recommended. All important decisions needed the agreement of every member of the Council. When Italy occupied Corfu in 1923 after the murder of an Italian general in Greece, the League could do little, and the matter was settled by the Conference of Ambassadors. Conclusion: So there were many reasons why the League was weak, including the USA's absence and its own structure."

*Commentary.* A second perspective explained with detailed evidence (Covenant, unanimity, Corfu). But the conclusion lists reasons; it does not say which is stronger. Level 4 (13–16).

**Level 5 paragraphs: the alternative perspective, then the comparative judgement.**

"It would be wrong to rest the weakness of the League on one absence, however. The League's most serious weaknesses were in its design and in the attitude of the two members who led it. The Covenant gave it no army: Article 16 let members decide how far to apply economic sanctions, and military action was only a recommendation. Decisions of the Council needed agreement, so a single Great Power could block action. And Britain and France, the leaders, would not act alone or against each other's interests. At Vilna in 1920, France would not press Poland, its ally, to give up the city after General Zeligowski seized it; in 1923, when Italy bombarded and occupied Corfu, Britain and France would not back the League against Mussolini, the matter was settled by the Conference of Ambassadors, and Greece had to pay Italy 50 million lire. In both cases the USA's membership would have changed nothing, because the problem was that the League's own leaders did not want to use it against a Great Power.

Nor was the League weak 'from the start' in every respect. It settled the Aaland Islands dispute in 1921 by awarding them to Finland with guarantees for the Swedish-speaking islanders; it divided Upper Silesia in 1921; and in 1925, when Greek troops entered Bulgaria after a border shooting, the Council ordered them to stop and Greece obeyed. These were small states and small disputes, and that is the point: the League worked when no Great Power's interest was at stake.

Overall, the absence of the USA made the League weaker, but it was not the main reason for its weakness. If the criterion is which weakness explains the others, the answer is the League's dependence on the will of Britain and France, because with the USA absent, the other two had to carry the League alone, and they were unwilling to do so. The USA's absence deepened that problem but did not create it: with the USA in, unanimity and the lack of an army would still have been there, and Britain and France might still have preferred their own interests. The view is therefore partly right: the USA's absence was an important handicap from the start, but not the main reason."

*Commentary.* The alternative perspective is explained with precise evidence, linked to the question (the USA would have made no difference at Vilna or Corfu), and the conclusion **compares** by a stated criterion (which weakness explains the others) and says why the given reason is weaker. With an opening paragraph on the case for the view, this is Level 5 (19–20).
:::

:::warn Mistakes that keep (b) answers at Level 3 or 4
1. **Seven lines on the case for the view and one line on the rest** (or the opposite). Both perspectives must be explained.
2. **Describing the alternative** ("Another factor was the army") without explaining how it matters.
3. **A fence-sitting conclusion**: "there were many factors". Say which was stronger.
4. **Conclusion with no criterion**: "I think the USA was more important" without saying by what standard.
5. **Ignoring the limit** in the question ("from the start"; "main"; "only").
6. **Equal-length paragraphs** just for balance. Weigh them: the stronger side usually deserves more.
:::

## Paper 4 essays [30] (A Level)

**The task.** Paper 4 asks for the same argument as Paper 2 (b), but at greater depth and on a bigger canvas. Each essay is marked on two tables of 15 marks each.

| Level | Marks | Table A: analysis and judgement (AO2) | Table B: supporting knowledge (AO1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 13–15 | Full understanding; valid, wide-ranging **criteria**; consistently analytical; focused, balanced argument with sustained reasoning; clear, sustained, **supported judgement** | High level of relevant detail: carefully selected, wide-ranging, consistently precise and accurate |
| 4 | 10–12 | Good understanding, mostly analytical; valid criteria; analysis may be uneven; balance may lack coherence in places; supported judgement | Good level: appropriately selected, mostly focused, uneven depth, mostly precise |
| 3 | 7–9 | Some analysis, but the argument lacks balance; attempts at criteria; begins to form a judgement with weak substantiation | Adequate: mostly appropriate, descriptive in places, narrow range, occasionally imprecise |
| 2 | 4–6 | Some understanding, descriptive; implicit criteria; assertion rather than judgement | Some relevant detail: narrative, limited range and depth, frequently imprecise |
| 1 | 1–3 | Addresses the topic, not the question; descriptive | Limited knowledge, limited relevance |

Four differences from Paper 2 (b):
1. **Criteria** are named and used throughout, and should be valid and **wide-ranging**: not one test applied to every factor, but several (necessary, decisive, widest, most lasting), used where they fit.
2. **Sustained argument**: the line of argument in your introduction is carried through every paragraph, and every paragraph ends by saying what it adds to the judgement.
3. **Precision** is half the marks (Table B): dates, names, figures and terms, chosen to prove the point, not to fill space.
4. **Length**: about 50 minutes and 900 to 1,200 words, and half of each essay's marks are for knowledge. Plans for three or four paragraphs usually work best.

"**Assess**" asks you to make an informed judgement about importance, extent or significance (a typical question: "Assess the impact of …", "Assess the reasons for …"). "**Evaluate this view**" asks you to judge the quality of a quoted claim: put the claim to the test of the evidence, and say how far it holds. The skills are the same; the difference is that "Evaluate" starts from a view that you must examine.

:::eg Paper 4 [30] (A Level): 'Propaganda was more important than terror in maintaining Nazi rule in the 1930s.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 5 (Table A 13–15, Table B 13–15): valid, wide-ranging criteria; sustained balanced argument; a supported judgement; precise, carefully selected knowledge.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- **Line of argument**: the view is **partly right**. Propaganda and the Hitler myth did more to win the active support of ordinary Germans after 1934; but terror was the condition that made them work, because it removed organised opposition in 1933–34 and silenced doubters.
- **Criteria**: (1) *necessary*: which was needed for the regime to survive at all? (2) *widest in effect*: which reached most Germans? (3) *most lasting*: which still operated in 1939?
- **Paragraphs**: (1) propaganda (for); (2) terror (against); (3) the third factor, economic recovery, and the interaction of the three; (4) conclusion. Evidence: Ministry of Propaganda 1933; 'people's receiver'; Nuremberg rallies; 1936 Olympics; plebiscite of 19 August 1934 (about 90 per cent); Dachau (March 1933); Gestapo; block wardens; Night of the Long Knives (30 June–2 July 1934, at least 85 killed); the Enabling Act (23 March 1933); unemployment 1933–38.

**Level 5 introduction.** "Nazi rule survived because most Germans either supported it or did not resist, and the question is which of two methods did more to secure that. I will argue that the view is only partly right. Propaganda, together with the successes it advertised, did more than terror to win the consent of ordinary Germans after 1934, but terror was the necessary condition for propaganda to work, since it destroyed organised opposition in 1933–34 and made open criticism dangerous. I shall judge by three criteria: which was necessary for the regime's survival, which reached the widest number of Germans, and which lasted longest."

**Level 5 paragraphs.**

"The case for propaganda is strong on the criterion of reach. The Ministry of Popular Enlightenment and Propaganda, set up under Goebbels in March 1933, controlled the press, radio, film and the arts; cheap 'people's receiver' radios carried Hitler's voice into the homes of ordinary families, and by 1939 about 70 per cent of households had a radio; mass rallies at Nuremberg and the Berlin Olympics of 1936 showed a united people under a strong leader. Its greatest success was the Hitler myth, the picture of a leader above the squabbles of party politics, and it had real results to point to: the Rhineland was reoccupied in March 1936 and Austria was united with Germany in 1938 without a shot. When Hitler combined the offices of Chancellor and President in August 1934, about 90 per cent of voters said yes. That vote was held in a state of terror and under pressure, but the willingness of so many to cheer the regime in the later 1930s suggests that propaganda had changed what Germans wanted to believe, not just what they dared to say.

Terror was nevertheless the necessary condition. After the Reichstag Fire of February 1933, the Communist leaders were arrested; Dachau opened in March; the Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 was passed with the SA in the chamber; by July Germany was a one-party state, and the Night of the Long Knives in June 1934 showed that even insiders could be killed. The parties and trade unions that might have organised opposition were destroyed within eighteen months, before Goebbels's methods had time to work. The terror also continued: the SS and the Gestapo, together with block wardens and informers, made an ordinary remark in a pub dangerous, and historians such as Robert Gellately have shown that the Gestapo depended on denunciations from ordinary Germans, so that fear and consent were not separate. Terror was less visible after 1934 only because it had worked.

The choice between them is therefore a false one, which is why the criteria matter. On the test of what was necessary for survival, terror comes first: without it, the Communists, Social Democrats, unions and Churches would have had room to organise, and no amount of propaganda would have removed them. On the test of reach and lasting effect among the majority, propaganda comes first, and it was helped by a third factor which neither phrase in the question covers: unemployment fell from about six million in 1933 to under one million by 1938, which gave many Germans a reason to accept the regime that no broadcast could have supplied. Yet that recovery made the propaganda believable; it did not replace it.

I therefore judge that the view is partly right. Propaganda, supported by recovery and success, was more important in maintaining the loyalty of the majority; terror was more important in removing opposition and in keeping the minority silent. For the regime's **survival**, the decisive factor was the combination: terror created the silence in which propaganda could do its work, and for that reason, if one must be chosen, terror was the more fundamental. The statement is right about what held the loyalty of most Germans, and wrong about what held the regime in place."

*Commentary.* The introduction states a line of argument and three criteria; the paragraphs use them (reach, necessity, lasting effect); each paragraph ends with a link to the judgement; the evidence is specific and well chosen (dates, numbers, terms, a named historian with a work); and the conclusion distinguishes between **loyalty of the majority** and **regime survival**. This is Level 5 on both tables.
:::

:::eg A short plan for an "Assess" question (A Level)
Question: Assess the importance of the Lateran Treaties of 1929 for Mussolini's regime. [30]

**What "Assess" asks.** Make an informed judgement about **importance**: how much the Treaties mattered, to what, and compared with what.

**Plan.**
- **What the Treaties did** (precise AO1): signed 11 February 1929; Italy recognised the Vatican City as an independent state; Catholicism was the state religion; the Church was paid 750 million lire in cash and 1,000 million in bonds for the loss of the Papal States.
- **Importance for the regime**: it ended the quarrel between Church and state that had lasted since 1870; it gained the support, or at least the acquiescence, of Catholics; the Church's backing helped the regime in the plebiscite of March 1929 (more than 98 per cent voted yes under pressure).
- **Limits**: tension continued over Catholic Action and youth organisations (1931); the Church kept an independent voice; Mussolini's ideas of the all-controlling state clashed with the Pope's.
- **Criteria**: effect on popular support (short term); effect on the regime's control of society (long term); compared with other sources of legitimacy (the monarchy, the army, economic policies).
- **Judgement**: the Treaties were an important success, perhaps the greatest diplomatic triumph of the regime for domestic support, but they did not give the regime control of the people it wanted, and the Church remained the one institution that could say no.

## Introductions, paragraphs and conclusions

### The introduction: two to four sentences

An introduction sets out your **line of argument**. It does not tell the story, repeat the question or announce "in this essay I will". It should say: what the question turns on (a key term, if it needs defining), your answer in one sentence, and how you will judge it (the criterion).

:::eg A weak and a strong introduction
Question: 'The League of Nations was weak from the start because the USA did not join.' How far do you agree?

**Weak.** "The League of Nations was set up after the First World War by President Wilson. It was designed to keep peace by getting countries to work together. The USA did not join. In this essay I will look at the reasons why the League was weak and then decide whether the USA's absence was the most important."

*Why it is weak.* A story and a plan, no argument. The reader has no idea what the writer thinks, and nothing is committed.

**Strong.** "The USA's refusal to join left the League without its richest member, and that mattered. But the League's weakness came mainly from its design and from the unwillingness of Britain and France to use it against a Great Power; the American absence made those worse without causing them. I shall judge by which weakness explains the others."

*Why it works.* In three sentences: concedes what is true in the view, answers the question ("mainly from its design"), and names the criterion. Every later paragraph now has a job.
:::

### The paragraph: point, evidence, explanation, link

[History from zero](#hist.f.basics) taught PEEL. For essays, add two things.

:::key The argumentative paragraph
1. **Point**: the first sentence is a claim that answers the question, not a topic ("The USA's absence mattered because …", not "Next I will discuss the USA").
2. **Evidence**: precise knowledge: a date, name, number, event.
3. **Explain**: say how the evidence proves the point. Use "because", "so", "which meant".
4. **Link**: end by tying the paragraph back to the **question** and to the other factors: "so this weakened the League, but, as at Corfu, it does not explain …". Say how important this factor is compared with another.
:::

Test every paragraph with two questions. **Could the first sentence be the answer to the question?** If it is only a topic or an event, it is narrative. **Does the last sentence say what the paragraph adds to the judgement?** If it only repeats the first, the paragraph is not yet arguing.

### The conclusion: weigh, do not repeat

A conclusion is the place where the judgement is made. It must **compare** the factors and apply the criterion; it must not summarise paragraph by paragraph, introduce new evidence or sit on the fence.

:::eg A weak and a strong conclusion
**Weak.** "In conclusion, there were many reasons why the League was weak, such as the USA, the lack of an army and Britain and France. All of these were important and they all played a part."

**Strong.** "The USA's absence was an important handicap, but not the main reason for the League's weakness. By the criterion of which weakness explains the others, the decisive one was the unwillingness of Britain and France to act against a Great Power, shown at Vilna and Corfu, which the USA's membership would not have changed. The view is therefore partly right, but it mistakes a symptom for a cause."

*Why it works.* It answers "how far" ("partly right"), names the criterion, makes a comparison, and gives a reason for it.
:::

:::exam Technique for essays
- **Start with the question.** The first sentence of every paragraph should be a claim that answers it.
- **Argue, don't narrate.** If you find yourself writing "then … next … after that", stop and write the point.
- **Use precise evidence.** A date, name or figure shows knowledge; "a lot" and "many" do not.
- **Compare as you go**: use "more than", "unlike", "whereas", "only possible because".
- **Finish.** Leave 3 minutes for a conclusion. An essay without a judgement cannot reach the top level, however good the paragraphs.
- **Do not teach yourself a formula.** PEEL, plans and criteria are tools. The question decides the argument; your structure serves it.
:::

## Spot the weak paragraph

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: 'The Scramble for Africa was caused mainly by economic motives.' How far do you agree? [20]

A student wrote:

"The Berlin Conference was held in 1884 and 1885. It was organised by Bismarck and was attended by fourteen countries. They met to decide how Africa should be divided up. They agreed that a country could only claim land in Africa if it had control of it, which was called effective occupation. King Leopold II of Belgium was allowed to keep the Congo, which became the Congo Free State. After the conference, the European powers took much more land in Africa very quickly."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but a **narrative**: it tells what happened at the conference without saying what it shows about **why** the Scramble happened. It never mentions economic motives, so it does not address the question. Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description, few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, and use the detail as evidence for it. "The Berlin Conference shows that the Scramble was driven at least as much by rivalry between the powers as by economic motives. The conference was not called to open trade but to settle claims, and its main rule, that a claim needed effective occupation, set off a race to take control of land on the ground before a rival could. That is a political motive, fear of being shut out, and it explains why the pace of annexation increased after 1885. Economic motives still mattered, since the Congo's ivory and, later, rubber drew Leopold II and the powers wanted markets, but the Conference suggests that the competition for status was the force which turned commercial interest into annexation." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What is the difference between the command "Explain" and the command "How far do you agree"?
A: "Explain" asks for causes and the links between them, supported by evidence; it does not need a verdict. "How far do you agree" asks for a reasoned judgement that weighs the view against alternatives.
Q: Name three things you underline when you unpack an essay question.
A: The command word; the topic; the focus (the concept); and the limits (dates, "main", "only", "most").
Q: What moves a Paper 2 (a) answer from Level 3 to Level 4?
A: Connecting the factors (saying how one made another possible, worse or more likely) and reaching a reasoned, supported conclusion about which mattered most.
Q: What moves a Paper 2 (b) answer from Level 4 to Level 5?
A: A clear comparative judgement of the relative strength of the perspectives, based on analysis of both and (ideally) a stated criterion; not a list of "many factors".
Q: Give three criteria you could use to decide which factor was "most important".
A: Necessary (without it the outcome would not have happened); decisive (it turned the situation); widest in effect; most lasting; root cause (it explains the others).
Q: What should the first sentence of an argumentative paragraph be?
A: A claim that answers the question (a point), not a topic or an event.
Q: Why is "there were many factors" a poor conclusion?
A: It makes no judgement. A conclusion must weigh the factors by a criterion and say which was stronger and why.
Q: How do Paper 4 essays differ from Paper 2 (b) answers?
A: Same argument, but with valid and wide-ranging criteria, a sustained line of argument throughout, much more precise and selected knowledge (half the marks), and about 50 minutes per essay.
:::

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: ★ Write a line of argument (one sentence) for the question 'The revolutions of 1848–49 in Germany failed mainly because the liberals were divided.' How far do you agree? State the criterion you would use.
A: A good line of argument takes a position and shows the criterion, for example: "The divisions among liberals were an important cause, but the revolutions failed mainly because the princes kept control of the armies, which is the factor without which the liberal programme could have been enforced." (Criterion: necessary, the counterfactual test.) Examiners reward a line that can be argued with evidence; "there were many reasons" does not qualify.
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Nicholas II issued the October Manifesto in 1905.
A: Indicative content: the general strike of October 1905, which began among railway workers and halted the railways, telegraphs and much of industry; the formation of the St Petersburg Soviet of workers' deputies in mid-October; defeat in the war with Japan, which had weakened the government's prestige and left the army tired; the advice of Sergei Witte, who warned that without concessions the choice was a military dictatorship, which the Tsar's advisers doubted the army could impose; and the hope of dividing the opposition by satisfying the liberals and the middle classes. Level 4 (9–10) connects these: the strike was the pressure and the war the weakness, which made Witte's choice of concession look wiser, and concludes which mattered most (the general strike).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Wall Street Crash occurred in October 1929.
A: Indicative content: speculation, with share prices rising far beyond company profits in the 1920s; buying on the margin (paying a small part of the price and borrowing the rest), which made the market depend on credit; overproduction and unequal incomes, so that the profits that justified high prices were uncertain; the Federal Reserve's higher interest rates in 1929; the loss of confidence, as the first falls led to calls for repayment and to panic selling on 24 and 29 October (Black Thursday and Black Tuesday). Level 4 connects: margin buying made speculation fragile, and the first fall turned fragility into panic; it ends with a conclusion on whether the underlying weakness of the economy or the speculative bubble mattered more.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The revolutions of 1848–49 in Germany failed mainly because the liberals were divided.' How far do you agree?
A: For: moderates against radicals (the Baden risings led by Hecker and Struve in 1848); the Frankfurt Parliament divided over a "greater Germany" including Austria and a "smaller Germany" led by Prussia; the liberals were unable to agree on social and economic reform. Against: the liberals had no army, and depended on the princes' troops (Prussian and Austrian troops helped put down the Frankfurt riots of September 1848); Austria recovered in 1848 (Prague in June, Vienna in October); Frederick William IV refused the crown in April 1849; the risings of May 1849 were put down by the Prussian army. Level 5 uses a criterion (necessary), for example that the revolutions could not succeed without control of the armies, which divisions alone do not explain, and judges that the liberals' divisions were important but not the main reason.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Munich Agreement was the result of Britain's military weakness.' How far do you agree with this view?
H: Name the "view" in your plan: which kind of weakness? Army, air force, defences, allies?
H: Alternative factors: Chamberlain's beliefs, public opinion, the Dominions, France, the case of the Sudeten Germans.
A: For: the Chiefs of Staff advised that Britain could not save Czechoslovakia by force; the RAF and the air defences were not ready, and fear of bombing of cities was strong; Britain was in the middle of rearmament. Against: Chamberlain's belief in appeasement and in the justice of some German grievances, public opinion that wanted to avoid another war, the opposition of the Dominions, the weakness of France, the strength of Czechoslovakia's own defences, and the fact that Britain's position was to improve in 1939 (which suggests weakness was not the sole factor). Level 5 judges, for example, that weakness shaped the options but belief in appeasement determined the choice.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Reconstruction failed mainly because of white Southern violence.' How far do you agree?
H: Set your criterion: what does "failed" mean here, and which factor ended the changes?
H: Think about the North as well as the South.
H: Do not forget the Supreme Court and the economy.
A: For: the Ku Klux Klan and other groups used terror against Black voters and Republican officials; the Enforcement Acts of 1870–71 reduced the Klan but violence returned (the White League; Mississippi 1875); violence led to Democratic victories in the elections of 1874 and 1876. Against: Northern commitment weakened: the Panic of 1873, war-weariness and divisions among Republicans reduced support; the Supreme Court read the Fourteenth Amendment narrowly (the Slaughter-House cases 1873; Cruikshank 1876); the Compromise of 1877 withdrew the last federal troops from the South. Level 5 uses a criterion such as "necessary" or "decisive", for example: violence was the method that overthrew the Southern Republican governments, but the retreat of the North made that violence successful, so Northern withdrawal was the decisive factor.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ (A Level) Assess the reasons why Mussolini invaded Abyssinia in 1935.
H: Sort the reasons into domestic and foreign, and into long-term and short-term.
H: Choose two or three criteria: which reason was necessary, which was decisive, which was most lasting?
A: Reasons: prestige and the wish to revive the Roman empire; revenge for the defeat at Adowa in 1896; the need to boost popularity at a time of economic difficulty after the Depression; land and resources for settlers and the economy; the weakness of the League and the apparent tolerance of Britain and France (the Stresa Front of April 1935); the Wal Wal incident of December 1934 as a pretext; Mussolini's own ideas of conquest. Level 5 uses precise evidence for each and argues which mattered most, for example that prestige and domestic popularity were decisive and the international situation merely made the moment possible.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ (A Level) Assess the impact of the Montgomery bus boycott on the civil rights movement.
H: Identify the aims of the boycott and its outcome first.
H: Look for impact on leaders, methods, the law, and later events.
A: The boycott began after Rosa Parks was arrested on 1 December 1955 and lasted 381 days to December 1956. Impact: it produced a leader, Martin Luther King, and showed the power of mass nonviolent action; Browder v. Gayle (the Supreme Court upheld the ruling against bus segregation in November 1956) gave a legal victory; it led to the founding of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference in 1957; it gave encouragement to later protest. Limits: segregation in the rest of the South remained in place; many of the real gains came later (the sit-ins of 1960, Birmingham 1963, the Civil Rights Act 1964); the legal case was already being fought by the NAACP. Level 5 uses criteria (immediate results, effect on methods, long-term significance) and judges that the boycott was important chiefly for what it began, not what it achieved in Montgomery.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] (A Level) Evaluate the view that Mussolini's Corporate State was a failure.
H: What were its aims? Compare the aims with the results.
H: Whose interests did it serve? Distinguish between the regime's aims and the effect on workers and employers.
A: Aims: to end class conflict and strikes, to organise the economy in the national interest, and to claim a "Third Way" between capitalism and socialism. Support for "failure": real wages fell in the 1930s; workers lost independent trade unions (the Vidoni Palace Pact of 1925, the Rocco Law of 1926, the Charter of Labour of 1927); in practice the system left employers in control; the corporations were slow and bureaucratic; the Chamber of Fasci and Corporations did not come into being until 1939. Challenge: strikes and class conflict were suppressed, which was a main aim; it gave the regime control of economic organisation; it was popular with employers. A* judgement: against its aim of controlling conflict it succeeded; against its claim to create a new economy it failed, because the system was a political device and not an economic one.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent was the Wall Street Crash the cause of the Great Depression in the USA?
H: Does the question distinguish "a cause" from "the cause"? Which criterion shows the difference: necessary or sufficient?
H: Think about weaknesses in the economy before 1929, the banking system and government policy after 1929.
A: For: the Crash destroyed paper wealth and confidence, caused businesses and banks to fail and cut spending and investment. Against: the underlying weaknesses (overproduction, uneven distribution of income, weak farming, an unstable banking system, debts) existed before October 1929; the Depression deepened after 1930 with bank failures and the collapse of world trade; government policy (the Hawley-Smoot Tariff of 1930, a slow response by the Federal Reserve) made it worse. Level 5 uses the idea of "necessary but not sufficient": the Crash was the trigger and did great damage, but it did not by itself cause a depression of this depth, which came from weaknesses already present and from what was done afterwards.
:::

:::recap
- **Command words**: Explain wants causes and links (Paper 2 (a), 10 marks); How far, To what extent, Assess and Evaluate want a judgement that weighs (Paper 2 (b), 20 marks; Paper 4, 30 marks).
- **Unpack** the question: command, topic, focus, limits ("main", "only").
- **Plan** for 3–5 minutes: factors for and against, evidence, ranking by a criterion, a judgement, the links.
- **Part (a)**: two or three factors, each explained; Level 4 connects them and concludes.
- **Part (b)**: Level 3 explains one perspective; Level 4 explains alternatives; Level 5 compares them with a clear judgement.
- **Criteria**: necessary, decisive, widest, most lasting, root cause; "successful" means measured against aims.
- **Paper 4 (A Level)**: Table A (AO2) 15 + Table B (AO1) 15; valid, wide-ranging criteria, sustained argument, precise knowledge.
- **Writing**: introduction with a line of argument; paragraphs that start with a point and end with a link; a conclusion that weighs, never "there were many factors".
- **Time (a guide)**: Paper 2 (a) about 15 minutes, (b) about 35; Paper 4 about 50 per essay.
