## What "transform" meant: the totalitarian claim

:::def Totalitarian
A system in which the state tries to control not just politics but every part of life: work, leisure, education, family and belief. The word was first used against Fascism by its critics in the early 1920s, and Mussolini took it up as a boast.
:::

:::note Evidence: Mussolini's own words
"Everything within the State, nothing outside the State, nothing against the State."

*Adapted from Mussolini's speech at La Scala, Milan, 28 October 1925; a standard English translation, which varies slightly between books.*
:::

Mussolini said he wanted to make Italians into new men and women: obedient, disciplined, patriotic, and ready for war. His regime tried many ways: laws about marriage and births, schools and uniforms, mass leisure, deals with the Church, and a campaign to change daily manners. To judge how far it worked, this chapter uses four tests:
1. **Reach:** how many people did the policy touch?
2. **Behaviour:** did people change what they did?
3. **Belief:** did they change what they thought?
4. **Limits:** who or what stayed outside the regime's control, such as the Church, the monarchy, the family and the south?

The key point to hold on to is that Mussolini could demand outward conformity more easily than inner belief. Many historians, among them Philip Morgan (*Italian Fascism, 1915–1945*, 1995), describe a regime that was strong in organisation and weak in conviction. Do not assume Italy was totalitarian just because Mussolini said so.

## Women and the family

### Fascist attitudes towards women

Fascist ideology placed women in the home. A woman's duty was to be a wife and mother, and to bear and raise sons for the nation and the army. Mussolini linked a rising population to national strength: a large population meant a large army, more workers and a claim to empire. Fascist writers contrasted the healthy, fertile, rural mother with the thin, fashionable, city "crisis woman" who, they said, would not have children.

At the same time, Fascism did not simply send women home. It gave them roles in its own organisations: the *Fasci Femminili* (women's groups), the *Piccole Italiane* and *Giovani Italiane* (girls' sections of the youth movement), and the *Massaie Rurali* (rural housewives, from 1933). It needed women as workers in textile and food factories and in the fields, where they were cheap. Victoria de Grazia, in *How Fascism Ruled Women* (1992), argued that the regime pulled women in opposite directions: it wanted them to be traditional mothers and also to be disciplined, modern members of its mass organisations. The result was control, not the return to the past that Fascist speeches promised.

### Aims to increase the birth rate: the Battle for Births

:::def Battle for Births
The campaign, announced by Mussolini in the Ascension Day speech of 26 May 1927, to raise the population of Italy from about 40 million to 60 million by 1950, through propaganda, tax and welfare incentives, and bans on contraception and abortion.
:::

Mussolini believed that numbers meant power. In his Ascension Day speech he said that Italy must grow to 60 million people, half as many again as in 1927. Falling birth rates alarmed him, for the rate had been dropping since the early 1900s. The campaign worked in three ways: **propaganda**, **incentives** and **bans**.

*Propaganda.* The press, schools and cinema praised large families. From 1933 there was a "Day of the Mother and Child" on 24 December, when mothers of many children were honoured by Party leaders in ceremonies. Families with many children received medals and public prizes.

*Incentives.*
- **Tax on bachelors** (from 1927): unmarried men between 25 and 65 paid an extra tax. The regime also gave priority in state jobs to married men with children.
- **Family help:** the ONMI (1925) offered clinics and support for mothers and babies; family allowances came in 1934 for industrial workers; from 1937 couples could get **marriage loans**, partly cancelled as they had children.
- **Prizes and tax breaks** for large families, with preference in public jobs and housing.

*Bans and penalties.*
- The public security law of 1926 forbade propaganda for contraceptives.
- The Penal Code of 1930 (the Rocco Code, in force from 1931) made abortion a crime "against the integrity and health of the race", with heavy penalties.
- **Divorce** was never legal in Italy before 1970, so the regime had no divorce to ban. What it did was to strengthen marriage: the Lateran Treaties of 1929 gave Church weddings civil effect, and the state gave tax and job benefits to married couples. Nor did it make it easier to leave a husband.

### Outcome: did the birth rate rise?

No. Births fell through most of the period, and the campaign did not reverse the long-term trend. The official birth rate figures:

The rate fell from 30.8 births per 1,000 people in 1922 to 22.4 in 1936, a fall of more than a quarter. It rose a little between 1936 and 1940, to 23.5, which may reflect the marriage loans and the end of the Depression, but it never again reached the level of the early 1920s. The number of births each year went down from about 1.13 million (1922) to about 1.05 million (1940), even though the population was larger. The chart shows five years only, so it cannot tell you what happened in the years between, and it does not show *why* the rate fell: the long-term causes were the growth of towns, later marriage and the Depression, which the regime could not control. Mussolini's target of 60 million by 1950 was not reached.

### Limits on women's employment

The regime also tried to push women out of paid work, partly to make jobs for men in the Depression and partly because it believed that work took them away from home and children.
- In 1926 women were barred from teaching some subjects, such as history and philosophy, in the top secondary schools.
- A decree of 1933 (turned into law in 1934) allowed state offices to bar women from competitions for posts.
- A decree of 1938 limited women to about 10 per cent of posts in public offices and in larger private firms, except in jobs thought "suitable" for women.
- Women were paid much less than men for similar work.

These measures hurt educated women in the professions and public service. But the overall effect on women's employment was limited. Industry and farming relied on women's labour, especially in the textile mills, in the rice fields of the Po Valley and in the south, and the regime did not touch it. The measures also came during the Depression, when many women lost jobs anyway.

:::key Judging the Battle for Births
**Success:** the campaign gave the regime a wide-reaching programme of welfare for mothers and children (ONMI, allowances, loans); it brought in strict laws on abortion and contraception; a small rise in births after 1936.
**Failure:** the birth rate fell from 30.8 to 22.4 per thousand (1922–36) and the 60 million target was not reached; bans did not stop contraception or illegal abortion; incentives were too small to change family choices; women's work continued where the economy needed it.
**Judgement to defend:** the campaign **failed in its main aim** but succeeded in extending state control over reproduction and reinforcing traditional ideas about women.
:::

## Young people: education and the youth movements

### Education: the Gentile reform and its aftermath

**Giovanni Gentile**, a leading philosopher and minister of education from 1922 to 1924, carried out the Fascist regime's first big reform of schools in 1923. Mussolini called it "the most Fascist of reforms". It:
- made the classical high school (*liceo*) the main route to university, so that a classical education was reserved for a small elite, while other pupils took technical or vocational courses that did not lead to university;
- raised the school-leaving age to 14 on paper, though many children in the poor south left much earlier and enforcement was weak;
- brought back religious instruction in elementary schools, which was a favour to the Church;
- introduced state examinations at the end of each stage.

After Gentile left office in 1924 the regime turned education into indoctrination.
- **Curriculum and textbooks.** History was rewritten to praise Rome, the Risorgimento and Mussolini. From 1929 the regime decided that elementary schools would use one **single state textbook** (*il libro unico di Stato*), in use from the early 1930s, so that the same Fascist version of history, geography and reading reached every child. Teachers' own choice of books ended.
- **Teachers.** Teachers were pressed to join the Party, to wear uniform and to lead their pupils in Fascist ceremonies. From 1929 teachers had to swear loyalty to the regime, and from 1933 a Party card was needed for any state post.
- **Universities.** Professors had to swear an oath of loyalty to the regime in 1931. Out of about 1,200, only about a dozen refused, and they lost their posts. The rest took the oath, some of them after anguished debate. Students were organised in the **GUF** (Fascist University Groups), and from 1934 in the *Littoriali* competitions in sport, art and writing. Other students used these to argue in private against the regime, and a few of the future leaders of anti-Fascism and the post-war republic came out of the GUF.

*Results.* Illiteracy fell, from about 27 per cent of people over six in 1921 to about 21 per cent in 1931 (census figures), but this was a continuation of an older trend. The schools reached most children, especially in the north, and gave the regime a chance to shape what they learnt. But in the south many children never went to school regularly, many teachers were poorly paid, and teachers often followed the new rules without enthusiasm.

### The ONB and the GIL

:::def Opera Nazionale Balilla (ONB)
The Fascist youth organisation created by the law of 3 April 1926 to organise boys and girls outside school. It was named after Balilla, a Genoese boy who started an uprising against Austrian troops in 1746. In 1937 it was merged into the Gioventù Italiana del Littorio (GIL), run directly by the Party.
:::

The ONB had four aims: to give children a Fascist education, to train boys for the army, to prepare girls to be mothers, and to separate young people from the Church and family. It provided uniforms, drill and gymnastics, summer camps and sport, mixed with lessons in Fascist beliefs and the cult of Mussolini. The slogan "Book and musket make the perfect Fascist" (*Libro e moschetto, fascista perfetto*) summed up its approach. Boys practised drill with small rifles, and older boys took part in military-style camps. Girls learned gymnastics, first aid and home skills.

| Age | Boys | Girls |
|---|---|---|
| 6–8 | *Figli della Lupa* (Sons of the She-Wolf) | *Figlie della Lupa* |
| 8–14 | *Balilla* | *Piccole Italiane* (Little Italian Girls) |
| 14–18 | *Avanguardisti* (vanguardists) | *Giovani Italiane* (Young Italian Girls) |
| 18–21 | *Giovani Fascisti* (Young Fascists) | *Giovani Fasciste* |

*Organisation.* The ONB was first under the Ministry of National Education, and from 1937 the GIL was under the Party and the Party secretary, Achille Starace. The regime added "Fascist Saturday" (*Sabato fascista*) in 1935: Saturday afternoons were taken for drill, sport and Party activities, for adults as well as children. Membership was voluntary at first, but the pressure to join grew through the 1930s, and enrolment became compulsory for schoolchildren by the end of the decade (sources differ on the exact date).

*Outcomes.* The numbers look impressive. An Italian historical source gives the ONB about 5.6 million members in 1936 and the GIL about 7.5 million in 1937. But these are totals of those *enrolled*, including children signed up at school, not those who took an active part. Estimates suggest that a large share of young people, perhaps a third or more, never joined, and the share was higher in the south and in the countryside, where there were fewer clubs and instructors. The historian Tracy Koon (*Believe, Obey, Fight*, 1985) concluded that the regime reached a very large number of young people but that its youth organisations were too poorly run, too short of money and too dependent on the cooperation of schools and the Church to produce the committed Fascists it wanted. Parents often saw the ONB as an opportunity: cheap holidays, sport and a uniform. The Church also kept its own youth groups, which led to the clash of 1931 (see below).

:::key Judging the effect on young people
**Success:** schools and the ONB reached most young Italians; textbooks, ceremonies and drill filled their time; the GUF organised university students; teachers and professors almost all took the oath; young people were the group the regime most hoped to win.
**Limits:** membership was patchy in the south and countryside; many joined for practical reasons, not belief; the Church and family kept their influence; the Littoriali and the GUF produced some critics; teachers and professors conformed in public.
**Judgement to defend:** Fascism **organised** the young far more than it **converted** them. Outward conformity was nearly universal; inner belief was patchy.
:::

## Religion: the Church and the regime

### Mussolini's early anti-clericalism and changing policy

Mussolini began as a hostile critic of the Church. As a young Socialist he wrote against priests, and the first Fascist programme of 1919 called for the seizure of Church property. But he saw that most Italians were Catholics, and that the Church could give him support. By 1921 he told parliament that the tradition of Rome was Catholic. In power, he made a series of gestures:
- he put the crucifix back in schools and law courts (1923);
- the Gentile reform of 1923 restored religious teaching in elementary schools;
- he helped the Banco di Roma, a bank linked to the Vatican, when it was in difficulties (1923);
- he paid for the repair of churches, appointed chaplains to the Fascist militia and, in a private matter that was also public, had his own children baptised and married his wife in church;
- the Catholic People's Party (PPI) was pushed out of politics, which suited a Pope who preferred to deal with the government directly.

Pope Pius XI, elected in February 1922, wanted to end the quarrel between Church and state, which had lasted since Italy took Rome in 1870 (the "Roman Question"). He believed a strong government would protect the Church from socialism and secularism.

### The Lateran Treaties, 1929

:::def Lateran Treaties
Three agreements signed on 11 February 1929 by Mussolini and Cardinal Pietro Gasparri, the Pope's Secretary of State, that ended the quarrel between the Italian state and the Papacy: a treaty, a financial convention and a concordat.
:::

| Part | What it did |
|---|---|
| **Treaty** | Recognised the Pope's sovereignty over the Vatican City, a state of about 44 hectares in Rome. The Holy See recognised the Kingdom of Italy with Rome as its capital. |
| **Financial convention** | Italy paid the Church compensation for the loss of the Papal States: 750 million lire in cash and 1,000 million lire in 5 per cent government bonds, 1,750 million lire in all. |
| **Concordat** | Catholicism was declared the religion of the state. Church marriages had civil effect. Religious teaching was extended to secondary schools. Bishops swore an oath to the king and priests were barred from joining political parties. Catholic Action, the Church's lay organisation, was allowed, provided it kept out of politics. |

*Impact and significance.* The treaties ended a break of 59 years between Italy and the Papacy. For Mussolini they were a huge propaganda gain: they gave his regime a respectability no Liberal government had won, brought Catholics behind it, and weakened any Catholic opposition. Pius XI praised Mussolini as a man whom Providence had sent. In a plebiscite on 24 March 1929, with ballots for "yes" printed in the national colours and those for "no" plain, over 98 per cent of the votes were for the Fascist list, and priests urged their congregations to vote yes. The Church gained too: its sovereign territory, its money, Catholic marriage, religious teaching in schools and a legal place for Catholic Action. It was no longer a victim of the state, but a partner.

### Church support, and tension

*Examples of support.* Many bishops and priests supported the regime. Priests urged yes votes in the 1929 plebiscite. In 1935–36 many bishops backed the invasion of Abyssinia, some blessing troops and some giving gold to the state, and Catholic newspapers presented the war as a mission of civilisation. Italian clergy used their sermons to support national ceremonies. The Church saw Fascism as a bulwark against communism.

*Growing tensions in the 1930s.* The Church and the regime were rivals as well as partners, because both wanted to educate the young.
- **Catholic Action, 1931.** In spring 1931 Mussolini, who feared a rival youth movement, had Catholic Action's local clubs and youth groups attacked and closed. On 29 June 1931 Pius XI answered with the encyclical *Non abbiamo bisogno* ("we have no need"), which protested at the persecution of Catholic Action and said that a regime that educated the young "for the exclusive advantage of a party" amounted to "a true, a real pagan worship of the State". After talks, an agreement in September 1931 let Catholic Action continue but kept it out of sport and politics.
- **Racial laws, 1938.** The antisemitic laws of 1938 forbade marriage between Jews and non-Jews, including Catholics. The Church saw this as a violation of the Concordat, which gave it control over Catholic marriage, and Pius XI protested publicly. But the Church did not condemn the persecution of Jews as a whole, and the protest had little effect.

:::note Evidence: the Pope on the Fascist education of the young
"…for the exclusive advantage of a party and of a regime based on an ideology which clearly resolves itself into a true, a real pagan worship of the State…"

*Adapted from the encyclical Non abbiamo bisogno, by Pope Pius XI, 29 June 1931, in the Vatican's English translation. Shortened; the phrases are the document's own.*
:::

:::key Judging the Lateran Treaties
**Gains for the regime:** ended the Roman Question; legitimacy and Catholic support; the plebiscite result; weakened Catholic opposition; international prestige.
**Gains for the Church:** sovereignty, money, Catholic marriage, religious teaching, Catholic Action.
**Limits:** the Church kept an independent organisation, which clashed with the regime in 1931 and 1938; many Catholics, especially the young, were not "Fascist"; the treaties did not make Italy more religious.
**Judgement to defend:** a **lasting political success** for Mussolini that bought support but not control.
:::

## Fascist organisations

### The Dopolavoro (OND)

:::def Opera Nazionale Dopolavoro (OND)
The "National After-Work Organisation", founded in May 1925 to organise workers' leisure time. It ran sports, outings, theatre, cinema, radio and cheap holidays, and was brought under the Party in 1927.
:::

The OND began in 1925 as a body created by the Fascist unions and Mussolini, partly modelled on social clubs abroad. The regime's aims were:
- to replace the leisure clubs of the Socialists and the Church with Fascist ones;
- to draw workers into the regime through pleasure rather than force;
- to show that Fascism cared about ordinary Italians;
- to keep workers busy and under the eyes of the Party.

*Organisation.* From 1927 the OND was run by the Party, and in the 1930s under Starace it concentrated on sport, excursions and entertainment. It had local clubs in towns, factories and villages, which offered a bar, a radio, football, bowls, band concerts, film shows and travelling theatre (the *Carro di Tespi*). It ran cheap holidays, at the seaside and in the mountains, and "popular trains" with cheap tickets. The ordinary worker's leisure was thus partly financed by the state.

*Outcomes.* The OND was the most popular of the mass organisations. One estimate says that nearly 40 per cent of the industrial workforce had joined by 1939, and it had the largest membership of all the Fascist bodies. Victoria de Grazia (*The Culture of Consent*, 1981) argued that it was a real success in organising leisure on a mass scale, bringing sport, cinema and the radio to people who had little access to them and replacing independent worker clubs with state ones. But the OND did not turn workers into committed Fascists. They used the facilities, such as the cheap trains and the sports clubs, much as they would have used any other, and it was far smaller in scale than the German *Kraft durch Freude* (Strength through Joy). Historians such as Adrian Lyttelton noted that attempts at political education usually failed because the organisers did not speak the language of their audience. In the south, with few clubs and little money, it hardly reached the peasants.

### The Reform of Customs, 1938

:::def Reform of Customs (*Riforma del costume*)
A campaign led by the Party in 1937–38 to change Italians' everyday manners to make them more "Fascist": ending the handshake, replacing the polite "Lei" by the "Voi", and teaching the Roman step.
:::

In the late 1930s, the Party secretary Achille Starace pressed a campaign to remake Italians' behaviour:
- the **handshake** was condemned as bourgeois and foreign, and the **Roman salute** (the outstretched arm) was ordered in its place;
- the polite form of address *Lei* was banned in the press and in official use, and the **Voi**, which was regarded as more Roman, was ordered instead;
- the **Roman step** (*passo romano*, a goose-step-like march) was introduced in the army and Party parades;
- Party officials and some civil servants were told to wear uniforms, and there were campaigns against foreign words and foreign fashions.

*Impact.* The Reform of Customs was widely ridiculed. Many Italians found the Roman step comic, the Voi awkward and Starace silly, and they quietly kept saying Lei in private. Mussolini dropped Starace as Party secretary in October 1939. As a test of totalitarian control, the campaign shows both the regime's ambition and its limits: it could order a change in public behaviour, but it could not make Italians believe in it, and it pushed Italians back to private life.

## Opposition

### How much opposition was there?

Open opposition was small because it was very dangerous. After the attempts on Mussolini's life of 1925–26 the regime passed the exceptional laws of November 1926: opposition parties were banned, a **Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State** was set up, the death penalty was restored for attacks on the head of government, and critics could be sent into internal exile (*confino*) on remote islands. A secret police, the OVRA, followed in 1927 (see [Mussolini's Italy (2)](#hist.d.italy.2)). Opposition then either went into exile or went underground.

### Strikes and demonstrations

Before 1926 workers still struck: the Fascist unions could not at first prevent them. But the Palazzo Vidoni Pact and the Rocco Law (see [Mussolini's Italy (3)](#hist.d.italy.3)) made strikes crimes, ended the free unions, and with them the usual means of protest. After 1926 there were isolated acts of protest and local grievances over pay and hours, but the regime faced no mass strike movement until March 1943, which is after the period of this key question. In this sense, the economic and the political control of workers were connected.

### The Communist Party

The Communist Party (PCI), founded in 1921, was the best organised of the opposition groups, and for that reason it was a main target. Its leader **Antonio Gramsci** was arrested on 8 November 1926, despite his parliamentary immunity, tried in 1928 and sentenced to more than 20 years in prison. He was released, in very poor health, in April 1937 and died on 27 April 1937. In prison he wrote the *Prison Notebooks*, one of the most influential works of political theory of the century, which were not published until after the war. Other leaders, such as Palmiro Togliatti, worked from exile (mostly in Moscow). In Italy the Party survived underground in small cells, with only a few thousand active members, repeatedly broken up by the police. Its position was weakened by Moscow's changing line and by the arrest of its leaders.

### Giustizia e Libertà

*Giustizia e Libertà* (Justice and Liberty) was founded in Paris in 1929 by **Carlo Rosselli**, a Socialist who had escaped from internal exile on the island of Lipari in July 1929. It was not a communist group but a movement of liberals and democratic socialists that aimed at action: it published newspapers, smuggled in propaganda and in 1930 dropped leaflets from an aeroplane over Milan. When the Spanish Civil War began in 1936 Rosselli organised Italian volunteers to fight Franco, with the slogan "today in Spain, tomorrow in Italy". On 9 June 1937 Carlo and his brother Nello were murdered at Bagnoles-de-l'Orne in Normandy by French fascist gunmen of the Cagoule, with help from Italian military intelligence. The movement was small and based abroad, but it kept alive the idea of an alternative Italy, and its supporters formed the Action Party in 1942.

### Non-conformity

Many Italians did not oppose the regime openly but did not follow it either. Examples:
- The philosopher **Benedetto Croce** wrote an anti-Fascist manifesto in 1925 in reply to the one by Fascist intellectuals, and stayed in Italy, publishing historical works that were read as quiet criticism.
- The conductor **Arturo Toscanini** refused to play the Fascist anthem at Bologna in May 1931 and was slapped by Blackshirts. He later left Italy.
- Ordinary Italians ignored the Roman step, said Lei, told jokes about Mussolini and the Party, listened to foreign radio and enjoyed jazz.
- Catholic groups and many peasant families kept their own loyalties.
- Some university students used the GUF and the *Littoriali* to argue for change.

This everyday dissent rarely threatened the regime. But it shows the limits of its control over private opinion.

### Assassination attempts

| Date | Attacker | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| 4 November 1925 | Tito Zaniboni, a Socialist | Planned to shoot Mussolini from a hotel window in Rome; arrested before the attempt |
| 7 April 1926 | Violet Gibson, an Irishwoman | Shot Mussolini in Rome; the bullet grazed his nose; she was judged insane and sent to England |
| 11 September 1926 | Gino Lucetti, an anarchist | Threw a bomb at Mussolini's car in Rome; it bounced off and hurt bystanders |
| 31 October 1926 | Anteo Zamboni, aged 15 | Fired at Mussolini's car in Bologna; he was seized and lynched by the crowd |

The attempts produced sympathy for Mussolini, a wave of public support, and a pretext. After the Zamboni attempt, the regime passed the exceptional laws of November 1926 and made the dictatorship complete. It is a classic example of repression that was prepared as a reaction. After 1926 there were no serious attempts that came close.

:::key Extent of opposition: the evidence
**Why it was weak:** a police state (OVRA, Special Tribunal, *confino*); the end of free unions and strikes; opposition parties banned; leaders in prison or in exile; the Church and the monarchy did not oppose; and a degree of real consent for a regime that seemed to succeed.
**What it shows:** Gramsci's imprisonment and writings; the Communist cells; the Rosselli brothers and Giustizia e Libertà; Croce and Toscanini; everyday non-conformity.
**Judgement to defend:** opposition was **small, divided and mostly in exile**, and the regime did not fall because of it. But silence does not mean consent, and the limits of Fascism's hold on opinion show up in private behaviour.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch)
- **Victoria de Grazia**, *The Culture of Consent* (1981) and *How Fascism Ruled Women* (1992), argues that the regime built mass institutions, especially the OND, that organised ordinary people's lives, and that its policies on women were full of contradictions.
- **Tracy Koon**, *Believe, Obey, Fight* (1985), studies the political education of the young and finds that the regime reached many but converted few.
- **R. J. B. Bosworth**, *Mussolini's Italy* (2005), looks at everyday life and stresses how many Italians accommodated the regime without being convinced by it.
- **Philip Morgan**, *Italian Fascism, 1915–1945* (1995), describes a regime that was strong in organisation and weak in conviction.

The most defensible overall verdict is that Fascism **changed the surface of Italian life** (uniforms, ceremonies, organisations, the Church settlement) **more than its depths** (family size, belief, private habits, the south). Mussolini's own word, totalitarian, was an aim, not a description.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg Evaluate the view that the Lateran Treaties were Mussolini's greatest domestic success [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, sustained balanced argument, clear supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** Criteria: (1) political value to the regime; (2) lasting effect; (3) extent to which the regime gained control, not only support. Other candidates for "greatest success": the destruction of opposition (exceptional laws, 1926); the IRI and economic rescue; the OND; the propaganda and cult of the Duce. For: ended the Roman Question; the plebiscite; Catholic support. Against: the Church kept independence (Catholic Action, 1931; racial laws, 1938); the Church gained as much; the other successes were more important to Mussolini's power. Judgement: the Lateran Treaties were among the greatest political successes, but not the greatest.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Lateran Treaties were signed on 11 February 1929. Mussolini and Cardinal Gasparri agreed that the Vatican City would be independent and that the Church would get 1,750 million lire. The Concordat made Catholicism the state religion and allowed religious teaching in schools. Pius XI praised Mussolini. A plebiscite in March 1929 gave the Fascists over 98 per cent of the vote. So the Lateran Treaties were Mussolini's greatest success."

*Commentary.* Accurate and precise, so Table B Level 3 or higher, but the paragraph is **narrative**, and the last sentence asserts "greatest" without comparing with anything. No criteria, no counter-argument. Table A Level 2–3.

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and compare.** "The Lateran Treaties were a major political success because they ended a quarrel that had divided Italy since 1870. In the 1920s the Church was the one institution that could mobilise millions of Italians against the regime, and in 1929 it became, for the first time, its partner: Pius XI called Mussolini a man whom Providence had sent, and priests urged their congregations to vote for the Fascist list in the plebiscite of 24 March 1929. This gave the regime a legitimacy that Liberal governments had never achieved. However, it is not clear that this was greater than the destruction of political opposition in 1925–26, which made Mussolini's rule secure."

*Commentary.* The knowledge now serves a criterion (legitimacy and security of rule) and a comparison. This is the analytical standard of Level 4. It still needs a second criterion and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The Lateran Treaties of 11 February 1929 were among the most important successes of Mussolini's domestic policy, but they were not the greatest. To judge them, I will use three criteria: how much they added to the regime's legitimacy, how lasting their effect was, and how far they gave Mussolini control, not just support. On the first two the treaties score very highly. On the third they score much less, and this is why other achievements, above all the creation of the dictatorship itself, mattered more.

On legitimacy, the case is strong. Since Italy took Rome in 1870, the Papacy had refused to recognise the Italian state, and many Catholics stayed out of politics or voted for the Catholic party. By settling the Roman Question, Mussolini won what no Liberal prime minister had. The treaty recognised the Kingdom of Italy with Rome as its capital, the financial convention paid the Church 1,750 million lire, and the Concordat made Catholicism the religion of the state, gave Church weddings civil effect and extended religious teaching to secondary schools. Pius XI repaid him with public praise, calling him a man sent by Providence, and the plebiscite of 24 March 1929, in which over 98 per cent voted for the Fascist list, was helped by priests. A regime that had begun with anticlerical rhetoric now had the Church's blessing. The effect was lasting too: the settlement survived until a new Concordat of 1984, long after the regime had gone, and the Church supported the Abyssinian war in 1935–36.

Yet the third criterion, control, shows the limits. The Church gained a great deal from the treaties: sovereign territory, money, state support for religious teaching and Catholic marriage, and a legal place for Catholic Action. It kept an independent organisation with its own network of clubs and youth groups, and it used them. In 1931 Mussolini, who could not tolerate a rival for the loyalty of the young, closed Catholic Action's clubs, and Pius XI answered with the encyclical *Non abbiamo bisogno* of 29 June, denouncing 'a true, a real pagan worship of the State'. Mussolini had to compromise in September 1931 and let Catholic Action survive. The racial laws of 1938 led to another quarrel because they banned marriages between Jews and Catholics, which the Church saw as a breach of the Concordat. A totalitarian regime would not have had to bargain with a rival institution in this way, and this suggests that the treaties bought support, not control.

Other candidates for the 'greatest' success outrank the Lateran Treaties on the criterion of control. The exceptional laws of November 1926, which created the Special Tribunal, banned opposition parties and restored the death penalty, ended organised political opposition inside Italy, and the Rocco Law of April 1926 removed strikes. These gave Mussolini what the Church settlement did not: security against being overthrown. The IRI (1933), which saved the banks, was a greater economic success, although it was less important to the regime's survival. On the other hand, the Lateran Treaties were more important than the Dopolavoro or the Reform of Customs, which affected daily life but not the foundations of power.

Overall, the Lateran Treaties were Mussolini's greatest success in winning *consent* from a Catholic nation, and they were the most lasting. They were not his greatest in securing *control*, since the Church kept its independence and the dictatorship rested on repression and the removal of opposition in 1925–26. The statement is therefore partly justified: the treaties were the regime's greatest triumph in legitimacy, but not its greatest domestic success overall."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): the judgement is built on three explicit criteria, the argument is balanced (the case for legitimacy against the limits of control), alternative candidates are weighed, and the conclusion is qualified and supported. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise dates, figures and names (11 February 1929, 1,750 million lire, 24 March 1929, over 98 per cent, 29 June 1931, September 1931), all selected for the argument. Total about 27–28 out of 30. The answer does not describe the treaties twice; each paragraph argues one point. About 800–900 words, 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the success of the Battle for Births [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. An "Assess" answer makes an informed judgement on how successful, so it needs a clear measure of success and a verdict.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) did it meet its main aim (raise the birth rate and reach 60 million); (2) did it change behaviour (bans and incentives); (3) did it achieve other aims (control, ideology). For: propaganda, welfare, laws; small rise after 1936. Against: birth rate fell from 30.8 to 22.4 per thousand; target missed; contraception and abortion continued; incentives were small. Judgement: a failure on its own terms, a success only at extending control.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "The Battle for Births was announced by Mussolini in 1927. He wanted Italy to have 60 million people by 1950. He taxed bachelors, gave prizes to big families and banned abortion and contraception. He also set up the ONMI. But the birth rate went down, so the campaign did not work."

*Commentary.* Relevant, mostly accurate, and with a judgement at the end, but the judgement rests on one short sentence with no figure, and the knowledge is a list. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2–3.

**Moving to Level 4: measure the outcome with figures and explain it.** "By its own measure the Battle for Births failed. Mussolini's target was 60 million people by 1950, but the birth rate fell from 30.8 births per 1,000 people in 1922 to 22.4 in 1936. A small rise to 23.5 by 1940 may owe something to the marriage loans introduced in 1937, but it did not restore the early 1920s level. The causes of the fall were beyond the regime's control: towns were growing, marriages were later, and the Depression made large families a burden. The incentives were also small compared with the cost of a child: the bachelor tax and the prizes could not change the way families planned."

**A Level 5 conclusion.**

"Judged by its main aim, the Battle for Births failed. Judged by the regime's wider purposes, it was more successful. It did not stop the birth rate falling, because the causes were social and economic and the regime had no answer to them. But the campaign extended the state's reach into the family: the Penal Code of 1930 made abortion a crime against 'the race', public security law banned the promotion of contraception, the ONMI, family allowances and marriage loans gave the state a role in mothers' lives, and the ceremonies for fertile mothers made motherhood a public duty. These were real changes, and the historian Victoria de Grazia argued that they reflected the regime's wish to control women, not only to raise numbers. So the campaign was a failure as demography and a partial success as control, which is why it was a typical Fascist 'battle': better at making the regime look strong than at changing society."

*Commentary.* The conclusion applies two criteria (the aim, the wider purpose), uses figures and a named historian, and reaches a judgement that distinguishes between types of success. With paragraphs on bans and incentives and on the limits on women's employment, this is Level 5.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess how far Fascist policies towards young people succeeded in changing Italian society." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Fascists set up the ONB in 1926, which had Balilla for boys and Piccole Italiane for girls. The ONB organised drill, sport and summer camps. In 1937 it became the GIL. Gentile's reform in 1923 changed schools and there was a single textbook from 1929. Teachers had to swear an oath. The youth movements had millions of members. This shows that Fascism greatly changed Italian society."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the paragraph describes the youth organisations and education policy, but the last sentence asserts "greatly changed" without testing it. There are no criteria, no counter-argument and nothing on the limits of the movement. Table B Level 3 (7–9): correct and relevant detail, but narrow, with no figures and no consideration of the south, the Church or the family.

**The fix.** Use the same facts to prove an argument. "Fascism succeeded in organising young people but not in converting them. The ONB, created in 1926, and the GIL of 1937 reported millions of members (about 5.6 million in the ONB in 1936), and schools used one state textbook from 1929 while teachers and professors swore oaths. Yet these figures count enrolment, not belief: a large share of young people, especially in the south, never joined, and the Church kept its own youth groups, which led to the clash of 1931. The historian Tracy Koon concluded that the regime reached many young Italians but lacked the money, the staff and the control of the Church needed to produce committed Fascists. The policies changed the appearance of Italian childhood more than its content." Now every fact is evidence for a judgement.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What were the aims of the Battle for Births, and what happened to the birth rate?
A: Announced on 26 May 1927, it aimed to raise Italy's population from about 40 million to 60 million by 1950 through propaganda, incentives (bachelor tax, family allowances, marriage loans) and bans on contraception and abortion. The birth rate fell from 30.8 per 1,000 in 1922 to 22.4 in 1936 and rose only to 23.5 in 1940, so the aim was not met.
Q: What limits did the regime place on women's employment?
A: Women were barred from teaching some subjects in top secondary schools (1926); a decree of 1933 let state offices bar women from competitions; a decree of 1938 limited women to about 10 per cent of posts in public offices and larger firms. The effect was limited because industry and farming needed women's work.
Q: What was the Gentile reform of 1923?
A: A reform by the minister of education Giovanni Gentile. It made the classical liceo the route to university for an elite, raised the school-leaving age to 14 on paper, brought back religious teaching in elementary schools and introduced state examinations. Mussolini called it the most Fascist of reforms.
Q: How did the regime control teachers and universities?
A: Teachers had to swear loyalty (from 1929) and, from 1933, hold a Party card for state posts; the single state textbook was decided in 1929. University professors had to swear an oath in 1931, and only about a dozen out of about 1,200 refused. Students were organised in the GUF.
Q: Describe the organisation of the ONB.
A: Created by the law of 3 April 1926. Boys went through Figli della Lupa (6–8), Balilla (8–14), Avanguardisti (14–18) and Giovani Fascisti (18–21); girls through the Piccole Italiane and Giovani Italiane; university students joined the GUF. It was merged into the GIL in 1937 under the Party. Fascist Saturday began in 1935.
Q: What did the Lateran Treaties give each side?
A: The Church got the sovereign Vatican City, 1,750 million lire (750 million in cash and 1,000 million in bonds), Catholicism as state religion, Church weddings with civil effect, religious teaching in secondary schools and a legal place for Catholic Action. The regime got an end to the Roman Question, Catholic support and legitimacy, shown in the plebiscite of 24 March 1929.
Q: Why did Church and regime clash in 1931 and 1938?
A: In 1931 Mussolini closed Catholic Action's clubs and youth groups because he wanted no rival for the young, and Pius XI answered with *Non abbiamo bisogno* on 29 June. In 1938 the racial laws banned marriage between Jews and Catholics, which the Church said broke the Concordat.
Q: What was the OND and what did it do?
A: The Opera Nazionale Dopolavoro, founded in May 1925 to organise workers' leisure: sports, outings, cinema, radio, theatre and cheap holidays and trains. It was run by the Party from 1927 and was the most popular Fascist organisation, but it did not make workers committed Fascists.
Q: What was the Reform of Customs?
A: A Party campaign of 1937–38 under Achille Starace to change manners: the Roman salute instead of the handshake, the Voi instead of the Lei, and the Roman step. It was widely ridiculed, and Starace was dropped in October 1939.
Q: Name four assassination attempts on Mussolini and say what followed them.
A: Zaniboni (November 1925, uncovered), Violet Gibson (7 April 1926, nose grazed), Gino Lucetti (11 September 1926, bomb) and Anteo Zamboni (31 October 1926, Bologna). After the last, the regime passed the exceptional laws of November 1926: opposition banned, a Special Tribunal, the death penalty restored, and *confino*.

:::recap
- **Totalitarian**: Mussolini's own word and aim ("everything within the State", 28 October 1925). Test it: reach, behaviour, belief, limits.
- **Battle for Births**: 26 May 1927, target 60 million by 1950; bachelor tax (1927), ONMI (1925), allowances (1934), marriage loans (1937); bans on contraception propaganda (1926) and abortion (Penal Code 1930). Birth rate fell from 30.8 to 22.4 per 1,000 (1922–36); target missed.
- **Women's work**: restrictions in 1926, 1933 and 1938 (about 10 per cent of posts); limited effect because the economy needed women.
- **Education**: Gentile reform 1923; single textbook from 1929; teachers' oath; professors' oath of 1931 (about 12 of about 1,200 refused); GUF.
- **ONB**: law of 3 April 1926; Figli della Lupa, Balilla, Avanguardisti; GIL in 1937; Fascist Saturday 1935. Large enrolment, patchy commitment, weaker in the south.
- **Church**: early anti-clericalism, then gestures; Lateran Treaties 11 February 1929 (1,750 million lire, Vatican City, Concordat); plebiscite 24 March 1929 (over 98 per cent); tensions in 1931 (Catholic Action, *Non abbiamo bisogno*, 29 June) and 1938 (racial laws).
- **OND** (May 1925): sport, outings, cheap holidays; the most popular body, but support was not belief. **Reform of Customs** (1937–38): Roman salute, Voi, Roman step; ridiculed.
- **Opposition**: exceptional laws of November 1926 after the attempts (Zaniboni, Gibson, Lucetti, Zamboni); Gramsci (arrested 8 November 1926, died 27 April 1937); Giustizia e Libertà (1929), Rosselli brothers murdered June 1937; no mass strike until 1943.
- **Judgement**: Fascism changed the surface of Italian life more than its depths; the Lateran Treaties bought support, not control.
:::
