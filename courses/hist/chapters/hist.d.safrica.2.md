## What is the question really asking?

The key question has two halves. **How did resistance grow?** asks you to trace a movement through five stages: the pass campaigns of 1960, the turn to armed struggle, the years of silence after Rivonia, the rise of Black Consciousness and the Soweto revolt. **How did the authorities respond?** asks about the state's weapons (emergencies, bans, detention without trial, the police, the courts and the Bantustan policy) and about its relations with the world outside. Four tests help to weigh each event:
1. **How many people did it involve?** A massacre of 69 and a revolt of tens of thousands are different kinds of event.
2. **What did it change?** Laws, the form of resistance, the state's methods, or outside opinion?
3. **How did the state react?** An event matters more when it forces the state to change course.
4. **How long did the effect last?** A shock that fades is a smaller event than one that shapes the next decade.

The chapter tells the story in order, then comes back to these in "How far did resistance and repression change South Africa?"

:::def Umkhonto we Sizwe (MK)
"Spear of the Nation", the armed wing of the ANC, founded in 1961 (with members of the South African Communist Party) under Nelson Mandela and Walter Sisulu. Its first campaign (1961–63) was sabotage of government buildings and installations, with instructions to avoid loss of life.
:::

:::def Black Consciousness
A philosophy led by Steve Biko in the late 1960s and 1970s that urged Black people, a term it used for Africans, Coloureds and Indians together, to reject the idea that they were inferior, to take pride in themselves and to build their own organisations free of white leadership. It was a movement of mind and organisation before it was a movement of mass action.
:::

## The Year of the Pass, 1960

### Two rival campaigns

By 1959 the African opposition had split. The **ANC**, led by Albert Luthuli, was committed to the Freedom Charter and to working with Indian, Coloured and white allies. The **Pan Africanist Congress (PAC)**, founded on 6 April 1959 under **Robert Sobukwe**, a lecturer in African languages at the University of the Witwatersrand, wanted a movement of Africans alone and aimed to overtake the ANC with a dramatic action. Both turned to the pass laws, which, now extended to women, were hated in every township. The ANC planned an anti-pass campaign to begin on 31 March 1960. Sobukwe pre-empted it by announcing a PAC campaign for **21 March**: on that day Africans were to leave their reference books at home, go to the nearest police station and offer to be arrested, to fill the jails and make the pass system unworkable. He called on them to be peaceful.

### Sharpeville and Langa, 21 March 1960

Response was patchy, but in two places it was large.
- **Sharpeville**, a township near Vereeniging in the Transvaal. A crowd gathered outside the police station. Estimates of its size range from about 5,000 to 20,000, and witnesses, including a photographer there, described the mood as noisy but not violent. Fewer than 20 police were there at first; about 130 more arrived with four armoured cars. At about 1.30 pm police opened fire without any warning, firing more than 1,300 rounds in about two minutes, and reports from the time and later said that many of the victims were shot as they ran away.
- **Langa**, a township near Cape Town. Police fired on a crowd there on the same day and killed two people (some accounts say three).

The official count was **69 dead and 180 wounded**, and this is the number textbooks give. Later research found at least 91 dead and 238 injured. In an essay, say that the figure is "69 by the police count, and at least 91 by later research" and you will show that you know the sources differ.

### The state's reaction

The crisis grew over the next days.
- On **26 March** Albert Luthuli burned his pass in public, and the government briefly suspended arrests under the pass laws. On **30 March**, at Langa and Nyanga, between 30,000 and 50,000 people (estimates go higher) marched on Cape Town led by a young PAC leader, **Philip Kgosana**; the march dispersed after the police promised talks with a minister, and Kgosana was arrested soon afterwards.
- On **30 March** the government declared a **state of emergency** and detained thousands of people without trial (estimates range from about 2,000 to more than 18,000), among them Mandela. The emergency lasted until 31 August 1960.
- On **8 April** the **Unlawful Organizations Act** banned both the ANC and the PAC. Neither could legally operate again until 1990.
- **Sobukwe** was arrested on 21 March and sentenced in May 1960 to three years for incitement. When his sentence ended in 1963, a special law, the "Sobukwe clause" in the General Law Amendment Act, allowed the government to keep him in detention for a further six years on Robben Island, and he was then restricted until his death in 1978.
- On **9 April** a white farmer, David Pratt, shot and wounded Prime Minister Verwoerd at a show in Johannesburg. The shooting convinced many whites that the government should act firmly, and it made it easier for Verwoerd to continue.

### Why Sharpeville mattered

:::key Reasons why Sharpeville mattered
1. **It showed the world what apartheid meant.** Photographs of bodies in the street appeared in newspapers across the world, and the UN Security Council discussed apartheid for the first time on 1 April 1960 (Resolution 134).
2. **It frightened the government's investors.** Foreign capital left South Africa and share prices fell in 1960–61; Verwoerd's government had to act to restore confidence, and once order returned the economy grew fast for the rest of the decade.
3. **It ended the era of legal protest.** With the ANC and PAC banned, there was no legal way for them to organise, and leaders had to choose between exile, silence and underground resistance.
4. **It strengthened the state.** The emergency and the bans gave the police new powers and a precedent, and the government's response to criticism was to tighten laws and to leave the Commonwealth.
:::

:::note Evidence: how reliable is the number "69"?
The figure of 69 killed comes from the police and the government of the day, which had a reason to keep it low. Later research found at least 91. The police say that they feared the crowd; photographers and others present said that the crowd was not attacking. When a source is the very body whose conduct is in question, ask what it gained by its version. This is the kind of reasoning that earns Table A marks when you weigh evidence on a Paper 4 essay.
:::

Sharpeville was a defeat for the opposition in one sense, since it was banned and leaderless, and a victory in another, since it made the world pay attention. The way to a Level 5 answer is to say which kind of event it was, and for whom.

## From protest to armed struggle, 1961–64

### The decision to use force

After the ban the ANC debated its future. Luthuli, a believer in non-violence, was confined to his home area and in 1961 received the **Nobel Peace Prize** for 1960. Nelson Mandela, who had been acquitted in the Treason Trial in March 1961, went underground, organised a three-day "stay-at-home" in May 1961 that failed to move the government, and, with Walter Sisulu and others, persuaded the ANC to allow a military wing. On **16 December 1961**, the anniversary of the Boer victory at Blood River (celebrated as the Day of the Covenant), bombs exploded at government buildings in Johannesburg, Port Elizabeth and Durban, and leaflets announced **Umkhonto we Sizwe**. In the next eighteen months MK carried out about 200 acts of sabotage, mostly against power pylons, post offices, pass offices and government buildings. Its leaders said that they chose targets that would not cost lives.

The PAC's armed wing, **Poqo** ("pure" or "alone"), took a different path, attacking chiefs, police and some white civilians in the Transkei and the Cape in 1962–63. Its attacks, including one at Paarl in November 1962, led to hundreds of arrests, and by 1964 it was crushed.

### The state's tools

The government answered with a series of laws that gave the police powers they had never had.
- The **General Law Amendment (Sabotage) Act of 1962** defined sabotage so widely that it could cover strikes and demonstrations and made it punishable by death.
- The **General Law Amendment Act of 1963** allowed police to detain a suspect for **90 days** without trial, without access to a lawyer, and to arrest the person again on release. Detainees were held in solitary confinement and many were tortured.
- The **Terrorism Act of 1967** went further: it allowed **indefinite** detention without trial, with no access to family or lawyer, and defined "terrorism" so broadly that it included acts that might "endanger the maintenance of law and order".

### Mandela's arrest and the Rivonia Trial

After a tour of African states in 1962, Mandela was arrested near Howick, Natal, on **5 August 1962**, and on 7 November sentenced to five years for inciting a strike and leaving the country illegally. On **11 July 1963** the police raided **Liliesleaf Farm** at Rivonia, near Johannesburg, a hiding place for the ANC's underground leaders, and arrested most of the leadership of MK, who were found with documents about a plan for guerrilla war ("Operation Mayibuye"). Mandela, already in prison, was added to the case.

The **Rivonia Trial** began in October 1963 in Pretoria, before Judge Quartus de Wet. Ten men stood trial, charged with sabotage and with preparing guerrilla warfare, charges that carried the death penalty. The defence team included **Bram Fischer** (a leading advocate who later joined the underground and was himself jailed for life), Vernon Berrangé, Joel Joffe and George Bizos. The prosecutor was Percy Yutar. The accused did not deny that they had planned sabotage. On **20 April 1964** Mandela made a statement from the dock instead of giving evidence, in which he set out why the ANC had turned to force.

:::note Evidence: Mandela's statement from the dock, 20 April 1964
*From Nelson Mandela, statement from the dock at the opening of the defence case, Rivonia Trial, Pretoria Supreme Court, 20 April 1964 (text as published by the United Nations). Shortened; every cut is marked with dots, and the words are Mandela's own.*

"I, and the others who started the organization, did so for two reasons. Firstly, we believed that as a result of Government policy, violence by the African people had become inevitable, … Secondly, we felt that without violence there would be no way open to the African people … Sabotage did not involve loss of life, and it offered the best hope for future race relations. … strict instructions were given to its members right from the start, that on no account were they to injure or kill people … in planning or carrying out operations."

"Africans want to be paid a living wage. … Africans want to be allowed to live where they obtain work, and not be endorsed out of an area because they were not born there. … Africans want a just share in the whole of South Africa; they want security and a stake in society."

"During my lifetime I have dedicated myself to this struggle of the African people. I have fought against white domination, and I have fought against black domination. I have cherished the ideal of a democratic and free society in which all persons live together in harmony and with equal opportunities. It is an ideal which I hope to live for and to achieve. But if needs be, it is an ideal for which I am prepared to die."

*Use.* As evidence it shows the ANC's own account of why it chose sabotage and how it presented itself to the court and the world: as non-racial, as forced into violence by government policy and as limiting harm. Its **purpose** matters: it was a speech to a court that might sentence him to death, and to the press, so it presents the case at its strongest. A historian checks it against the Rivonia documents, which included a plan for guerrilla war, and against the later and fuller account in Mandela's memoir, *Long Walk to Freedom* (1994), which was written thirty years later and in the light of what he had become.
:::

On **11 June 1964** the court found eight of the accused guilty and on **12 June** sentenced them to **life imprisonment**: Mandela, Walter Sisulu, Govan Mbeki, Raymond Mhlaba, Ahmed Kathrada, Elias Motsoaledi, Andrew Mlangeni and Denis Goldberg. One of the ten, Lionel Bernstein, was acquitted, and another, James Kantor, was discharged. Bob Hepple, named in the original charges, escaped abroad. The judge did not impose the death penalty, a decision usually put down to the world's attention: the UN and many governments had called for the accused not to be executed. Mandela and the other black prisoners were sent to **Robben Island**, an island prison off Cape Town; Goldberg, who was white, was held in Pretoria.

:::key What the Rivonia Trial meant
1. **It destroyed the ANC's internal leadership.** With the leaders in prison for life and the movement banned, organised resistance inside South Africa almost disappeared for several years.
2. **It made Mandela a symbol.** His statement from the dock and, later, his long imprisonment gave the movement a figure who stood for it, which the Anti-Apartheid Movement used for a quarter of a century.
3. **It showed the limits of the world's help.** International protest probably prevented the executions, but no state used force or sanctions against South Africa.
4. **It pushed the ANC into exile.** Oliver Tambo, who had left the country in 1960, led the ANC abroad from Lusaka and Dar es Salaam, with military camps in friendly African states.
:::

## The National Party in power: separate development

### Verwoerd and Vorster

The 1960s were the National Party's strongest years. The whites-only electorate returned it with larger majorities in 1961 and 1966, and a referendum of white voters on 5 October 1960 approved a **republic** by a narrow margin; it came into being on 31 May 1961, after the Commonwealth had made clear that South Africa's policies were unacceptable. The economy boomed. Foreign investment, which had fled in 1960, returned in the middle of the decade, and growth in the 1960s was fast (more than 5 per cent a year by one estimate), fed by cheap labour and high returns on capital. Many white South Africans were richer and more secure than ever.

Two Prime Ministers led the state.
- **Hendrik Verwoerd** (Prime Minister 1958–66) was the system's chief theorist. He called his policy **separate development**: the African "nations" would each develop in their own "homelands", and in time become self-governing. He was assassinated on **6 September 1966**, stabbed in the House of Assembly by a parliamentary messenger, Dimitri Tsafendas.
- **B. J. Vorster** (Prime Minister 1966–78) had been Minister of Justice from 1961 and was the man who built the security laws. He kept Verwoerd's policy, which he renamed "multi-national development", and was ready to adjust the way it dealt with the outside world, for example by seeking contacts with some African states (a policy called "outward movement" or détente, in the 1970s). At home he strengthened the police, the courts and the security services.

### Separate development and the Bantustans

:::def Separate development
The policy, associated with Hendrik Verwoerd, that Africans were members of separate ethnic "nations" with their own homelands, in which they would exercise their political rights, while white South Africa would keep control of the rest of the country, about 87 per cent of the land, the cities, the mines and the industries. It replaced the earlier "baasskap" language with the language of self-determination, but did not change who had power.
:::

The **Promotion of Bantu Self-government Act of 1959** was the foundation. It divided the African reserves into national units (eight at first, later ten) based on language groups, ended African representation in Parliament, and promised each unit a path to self-government. Verwoerd and Vorster took the policy forward in stages.
1. **Self-government.** Transkei, the home of the Xhosa in the Eastern Cape, was the first to receive it under the Transkei Constitution Act of 1963, with an elected assembly and a chief minister, **Kaiser Matanzima**, who supported the policy. The others followed in the 1970s.
2. **Citizenship.** The **Bantu Homelands Citizenship Act of 1970** gave every African a citizenship in one of the homelands, whether or not they had ever lived there, so that in law they were foreigners in white South Africa. The aim, as ministers said openly, was that in the long run no African would be a citizen of the Republic.
3. **"Independence."** Transkei was declared independent on **26 October 1976**, Bophuthatswana on 6 December 1977, Venda in 1979 and Ciskei in 1981. No government except South Africa's recognised them.

### Life in the Bantustans

The reality was very different from the plan.
- **Poverty and overcrowding.** The ten homelands held roughly 13 per cent of the land for most of the African population. The land was poor, often eroded, and there was little industry. Farming could not feed the people, so families depended on wages sent home by migrant workers, or on men who commuted long distances each day to work in white South Africa.
- **Forced removals.** To make the "white" areas white, the state moved people into the homelands in what became one of the largest programmes of forced removal in the twentieth century. The Surplus People Project estimated that about **3.5 million** people were moved between 1960 and 1983 (some sources give 1960–82), from "black spots" (African-owned farmland in white areas), from farms where labour tenancy was ended, from towns under the Group Areas Act and from areas needed for white use. Many were dumped in **resettlement camps** with little housing and few jobs, such as Dimbaza in the Ciskei, set up in 1967, and the conditions there were shown to the world in a secretly filmed documentary, *The Last Grave at Dimbaza* (1974).
- **Dependence.** The homeland governments depended on grants from Pretoria for most of their money, and their leaders, whom the state helped to power, were often accused of corruption and authoritarianism. Critics said that the leaders of the "independent" homelands were puppets.
- **Citizenship.** Africans from the independent homelands lost their South African citizenship, which meant that they could be treated as foreign migrant workers in white South Africa.
- **Resistance.** Some chiefs and politicians, including Buthelezi, used the homelands as a platform to oppose apartheid, and the ANC and the Black Consciousness Movement said that the homelands were a fraud.

:::key Why the government pursued separate development
1. **To keep white control of the country while claiming to give Africans self-determination,** which answered critics abroad.
2. **To remove the political threat of an African majority,** by making Africans citizens of homelands, so that there would be no African majority in "South Africa".
3. **To keep cheap African labour** while avoiding the cost of settling families in white areas.
4. **To divide Africans by ethnic group** and to make use of chiefs and homeland politicians who would cooperate.
:::

## Black Consciousness, 1968–76

### Why a new movement?

By 1968 the old organisations were banned or in exile, the leaders were on Robben Island, and fear and the new laws kept people quiet. The new energy came from young people in the universities and the townships. African students in the multi-racial but white-led **National Union of South African Students (NUSAS)** felt that white liberals spoke for them and set the agenda. In 1968 a group of Black students, among them **Steve Biko**, a medical student at the University of Natal's Black section, broke away; in **July 1969** they founded the **South African Students' Organisation (SASO)**, which was open only to Black students, with Biko as its first president.

### The ideas of Black Consciousness

Biko and his colleagues argued that apartheid worked on the mind as well as on the body. Black people had been taught to see themselves as inferior and to look to whites for leadership, and so the first task was psychological liberation: pride, solidarity and self-reliance. "Black" meant the political identity of all the groups that apartheid oppressed: Africans, Coloureds and Indians. The slogan "Black is beautiful" meant just that. Black Consciousness also said that white liberals, however well-meaning, should not lead the struggle.

:::note Evidence: Steve Biko on the mind of the oppressed
*From Steve Biko, essays collected in* I Write What I Like *(1978); the sentence appears in two of the essays, "Black Consciousness and the Quest for a True Humanity" and "White Racism and Black Consciousness". One sentence only, the words are Biko's own.*

"The most potent weapon in the hands of the oppressor is the mind of the oppressed."

*Use.* The sentence states the movement's central idea: that apartheid depended on Black acceptance of inferiority, so liberation began with self-respect. It does not tell you how many people shared the idea, or what Biko thought of armed struggle (he did not make it a part of the movement's programme). The book was published after his death by his friends, so you cannot know how far it is a full record of his views.
:::

### Organisation and growth

Biko and his colleagues built a network of organisations.
- The **Black People's Convention (BPC)**, launched in July 1972, was a political movement for adult Blacks.
- **Black Community Programmes (BCP)** ran practical projects, such as clinics, literacy classes and a health centre at Zanempilo near King William's Town, which showed the idea of self-help.
- The **South African Students' Movement (SASM)** brought the ideas to school pupils in Soweto and other townships.
- **Trade union and church groups** took up the language of Black Consciousness, and the Durban strikes of January–March 1973, in which about 60,000 workers stopped work for higher pay, showed that African workers were ready to organise even without political parties.

The state noticed. In **March 1973** Biko and other SASO and BPC leaders were banned, which restricted Biko to the King William's Town district and forbade him to speak in public or to be quoted. He went on working through the BCP. In **September 1974** SASO and the BPC called rallies to celebrate the independence of **Mozambique** under Frelimo. The government banned them, many people were arrested, and nine of the leaders were tried under the Terrorism Act and sent to Robben Island for several years. The trial gave the movement more publicity and a story of martyrdom.

### How significant was Black Consciousness?

:::key The significance of Black Consciousness
1. **It restored a sense of pride and identity.** It reached a generation that had grown up under Bantu Education and had no experience of the old parties.
2. **It filled the gap left by the banning of the ANC and PAC.** Between 1964 and 1976 it was the main source of new political ideas inside South Africa.
3. **It inspired the Soweto students.** The students' leaders were members of SASM, and the language of the 1976 revolt (the dignity of Black people) was that of Black Consciousness, though it did not plan the revolt.
4. **It provoked the state's heaviest response** after the revolt: the banning of its organisations in October 1977 and the death of Biko.
5. **Its limits.** It was small (a movement of students and professionals, rather than of workers), it did not have an army, it was weakened by the bans, and in the 1980s the ANC and the United Democratic Front, which accepted white allies and the Freedom Charter, took over much of the ground.
:::

## The Soweto uprising, 1976

### Causes

Soweto ("South Western Townships") was the biggest African township in the country, with hundreds of thousands of people (about a million by some counts), on the edge of Johannesburg. Its schools were crowded and poor, and three kinds of cause came together.
- **The long-term cause: Bantu Education.** Black pupils were taught in overfull classes by poorly trained teachers, and many left school without qualifications. A generation of angry young people had grown up.
- **The immediate cause: language.** In 1974 the Department of Bantu Education issued the **Afrikaans Medium Decree**, which required Afrikaans to be used with English as a language of instruction in African schools, for mathematics and social studies, from 1975. Most African teachers and pupils had little Afrikaans, and the language was widely seen as that of the oppressor. School boards, teachers and parents asked for the decree to be dropped; when the deputy minister, Punt Janson, was asked about consulting Africans, he is quoted as replying that he had consulted the Constitution, which made English and Afrikaans the two official languages.
- **The context: Black Consciousness and a changing region.** The ideas of Biko, the example of Mozambique and Angola (see below) and the economic slow-down of the mid-1970s all fed the mood.

### The events

On **16 June 1976** thousands of school pupils (estimates range from about 3,000 to 20,000) marched from several Soweto schools towards the Orlando stadium, organised by an Action Committee of the SASM, which became the **Soweto Students' Representative Council** (SSRC) under Tsietsi Mashinini. They carried placards against Afrikaans. At Orlando West the police stopped them, and then fired. A 12-year-old boy, **Hector Pieterson**, was among those killed, and the photograph by Sam Nzima of another boy, Mbuyisa Makhubo, carrying him with Hector's sister beside them became the best-known image of the uprising.

The crowds then burned beer halls, liquor stores and government buildings, which they saw as symbols of the system that exploited them. On 17 June about 1,500 armed police were in Soweto and the army was on standby. The unrest spread to other townships on the Rand and to the Cape Town, Port Elizabeth and the Eastern Cape; there were strikes (stay-aways) by workers in August and September, and school boycotts went on into 1977. The government withdrew the Afrikaans requirement in July 1976. By the end of 1976 hundreds of people had been killed and many arrested, and thousands of young people left the country, many to join the ANC's military camps.

Be careful with the numbers. The government first said that 23 had died; the figure commonly given for the first days is 176; a government commission, chaired by Judge Cillié, estimated 575 deaths from violence by the end of February 1977; and the *Times* of London reported more than 700 across the country by the end of 1976. They count different things over different periods, so say which you mean.

### The government's response

The government met the revolt with force and with a few concessions. The police used tear gas, dogs and live ammunition and made mass arrests, among them many leaders of SASM, SSRC and the BPC; the Minister of Justice, **Jimmy Kruger**, led the response. Vorster's government appointed a commission under Judge Cillié, whose estimate of 575 deaths up to the end of February 1977 is the highest official figure. The government dropped the Afrikaans requirement and made small concessions, but it offered no political change. It also pressed ahead with the "independence" of Transkei, declared on 26 October 1976, in the middle of the crisis.

### Why Soweto mattered

:::key Why Soweto mattered
1. **It was a revolt by the young,** born under apartheid, who were no longer afraid of the state, and it showed that Bantu Education had produced the opposite of its aim.
2. **It spread across the country,** which Sharpeville had not, and drew in workers and, later, parents and townships.
3. **It brought a new generation into the movement.** Thousands left for exile and joined the ANC's MK, which gave it fresh recruits after years of weakness.
4. **It changed opinion abroad.** UN Security Council Resolution 392 (19 June 1976) condemned the South African government; the rand fell and the economy was shaken; foreign banks and companies became more careful.
5. **It was not the end of apartheid.** The state survived, banned the BC organisations in 1977 and reasserted control; most of the changes came only after 1978 under P. W. Botha.
:::

## The state's reaction to resistance

Sort the state's responses of 1960–77 under four headings, because they make good paragraphs in an essay.
1. **Emergency powers and bans (1960).** The state of emergency of 30 March 1960, the banning of the ANC and PAC (8 April 1960) and the use of banning orders against individuals.
2. **Security law (1962–67).** The Sabotage Act (1962), the 90-day law (1963) and the Terrorism Act (1967), with detention without trial and solitary confinement.
3. **The security apparatus.** Under Vorster, the **Bureau of State Security (BOSS)** was set up in 1969 under Hendrik van den Bergh, to coordinate intelligence at home and abroad; the Special Branch of the police grew; the defence force was expanded; and informers and agents infiltrated opposition groups. Deaths in detention occurred, among them that of Ahmed Timol in October 1971, whom the police said had killed himself; in 2017 a High Court judge ruled that he had been murdered.
4. **Persecution of the Black Consciousness leaders.** Banning orders (March 1973), the trial of the nine in 1975–76, detention of leaders after Soweto, and the events of 1977 below.

### The death of Steve Biko

Biko was arrested on **18 August 1977** at a police roadblock, for breaking his banning order by travelling out of his district, and held without trial under the Terrorism Act in Port Elizabeth. He was badly injured by the security police during interrogation in early September, and driven, naked and in the back of a police vehicle, more than 1,000 kilometres to a prison hospital in Pretoria, where he died on **12 September 1977**, aged 30. An autopsy found extensive brain injury. The Minister of Justice, Jimmy Kruger, first implied that Biko had died after a hunger strike, a claim that was later dropped. An inquest the same year found that he had died of head injuries but that nobody was to blame, and no one was prosecuted. In 1998 the Truth and Reconciliation Commission refused amnesty to five former policemen who applied for it, and in 2003 the Justice Department announced that they would not be prosecuted. About 20,000 people attended his funeral.

On **19 October 1977** the government banned 18 Black Consciousness organisations, including the BPC, SASO, SASM and Black Community Programmes, and two newspapers, *The World* and *Weekend World*, and detained many leaders; the day is called "Black Wednesday".

:::key How effective was the state's response?
1. **In the short term it was very effective.** After 1960 and 1964 open resistance stopped for years; most of the leaders were in prison or exile; the economy boomed.
2. **It could not stop new resistance.** Black Consciousness arose in the gap, and Soweto showed that repression could not prevent a new generation from organising.
3. **It cost the state its international standing.** Sharpeville, Rivonia, Soweto and Biko's death each led to new criticism abroad, and the arms embargo of 1977 followed directly from the violence of 1976–77.
4. **It was a response to events, not a plan.** The state was surprised by Sharpeville and by Soweto, and its reforms (the dropping of Afrikaans) were small.
:::

## International opposition, 1960–77

### Reaction to Sharpeville

Sharpeville was the first event that made apartheid a world issue. There were demonstrations in many countries; the **UN Security Council** debated South Africa's policies for the first time and passed Resolution 134 on 1 April 1960, which called on South Africa to abandon apartheid (Britain and France abstained). Foreign money flowed out of South Africa, and the government found that foreign opinion could affect the economy. South Africa also left the Commonwealth: at the Commonwealth conference of March 1961 the African and Asian members' criticism led Verwoerd to withdraw South Africa's application to stay a member as a republic.

### The Anti-Apartheid Movement

The **Anti-Apartheid Movement (AAM)** was founded in London in 1959 as the Boycott Movement and was renamed in 1960. Its leaders included exiled South Africans and British supporters. Its main activities were campaigns to boycott South African goods (the oranges and fruit sold in British shops, for example), to pressure banks and companies to stop investing in South Africa, to support sporting boycotts and to campaign for the release of political prisoners, especially, after 1964, for Nelson Mandela. Similar movements developed in the Netherlands, Scandinavia, the USA and elsewhere. They brought apartheid into schools, churches, trade unions and local councils, and they kept the issue alive in years when governments did little.

### Sport and the boycott

Sport was the area in which the boycott was most effective in the 1960s. South Africa was excluded from the 1964 Tokyo Olympics and expelled from the International Olympic Committee in 1970; the football body FIFA suspended it in 1961 and expelled it in 1976. The **D'Oliveira affair** of 1968 made the issue clear. Basil D'Oliveira, a Coloured cricketer from Cape Town who had moved to England, was left out of the England team chosen for the winter tour of South Africa, and was then picked as a replacement for an injured player. Vorster declared that the team had been chosen by the anti-apartheid movement, and the English cricket authorities cancelled the tour. In 1969–70 protests disrupted the Springbok rugby tour of Britain, and in 1970 a South African cricket tour of England was cancelled.

### The United Nations and the divisions over sanctions

The UN was where the issue was most debated, and where its limits were clearest.
- The **General Assembly** passed **Resolution 1761 on 6 November 1962**, by 67 votes to 16 with 23 abstentions: it condemned apartheid, asked member states to break off diplomatic relations, to boycott South African goods and to stop selling arms, and set up a **Special Committee against Apartheid**.
- The **Security Council** was more cautious, because three of its permanent members with a veto (the UK, France and the USA) did not want economic sanctions. In 1963 it called for a **voluntary** arms embargo (Resolution 181) and in 1964 it appealed for the Rivonia accused to be spared. In October 1974 Britain, France and the USA vetoed a resolution to expel South Africa from the UN.
- **Why the divisions?** Britain was South Africa's biggest trading partner and investor; the USA and Western Europe saw South Africa as an anti-communist partner and as a source of gold and strategic minerals, and it controlled the sea route round the Cape; they feared that sanctions would hurt their own economies and that the alternatives to white rule might be pro-Soviet. African and Asian states, and the Soviet bloc, wanted sanctions and support for the liberation movements. So the UN's resolutions were strong in words and weak in effect.

### The arms embargo of 1977

The turning point came after Soweto and Biko's death. Many Western governments and public opinion were shaken. On **4 November 1977** the Security Council passed **Resolution 418**, unanimously, imposing a **mandatory** arms embargo on South Africa, which, unlike the voluntary measure of 1963, bound all member states. This was the first time that the Council had imposed mandatory measures on a member state. The embargo was important as a symbol and had an effect on supplies of weapons and spare parts, but South Africa built its own arms industry (Armscor, created in 1968), and the embargo did not touch trade in other goods.

### Political changes in southern Africa and the OAU

The 1974 revolution in Portugal changed the region. In 1975 the Portuguese colonies of **Mozambique** (25 June 1975, under Frelimo) and **Angola** (11 November 1975, with a civil war, in which South African forces invaded to support the anti-Marxist side and Cuban troops arrived to support the MPLA) became independent. South Africa lost the buffer of white-ruled and friendly states around it. The only white-ruled neighbours left were Rhodesia, under siege by guerrillas (see [End of minority rule (4): the end of white minority rule in Zimbabwe, 1965–80](#hist.d.safrica.4)), and South Africa's own mandated territory of South West Africa (Namibia).

The **Organisation of African Unity (OAU)**, founded in May 1963, gave the liberation movements both political and practical support. Its African Liberation Committee, based in Dar es Salaam, channelled money and training to the ANC, the PAC and other movements. African states let the ANC and PAC set up offices and military bases: the ANC had its headquarters in Lusaka (Zambia) and training camps in Tanzania, and, after 1975, in Angola and later elsewhere; the PAC was based in Dar es Salaam and Lusaka. In 1967 MK fighters entered Rhodesia in the **Wankie campaign**, alongside Zimbabwean allies, which failed but marked the ANC's first effort at guerrilla warfare.

:::key How far did international opposition affect apartheid by 1977?
1. **It isolated South Africa culturally and in sport,** from the Olympics (1964) to cricket tours (1968, 1970), which hurt white opinion in a country that loved sport.
2. **It kept the issue alive and supported the exiles,** through the AAM and the OAU.
3. **It gave the ANC a base and a Mandela to rally behind,** which made the movement outside the country, as well as inside it.
4. **It did not reduce the state's power.** The Western powers vetoed sanctions, trade and investment grew in the 1960s and early 1970s, and the arms embargo came late.
5. **It was changed by internal events.** Each of the main steps (Resolution 134, 1761, the 1977 embargo) followed violence in South Africa; foreign pressure grew when South African resistance gave it a cause.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: what changed in 1960 and 1976?)
Tom Lodge's *Black Politics in South Africa since 1945* (1983) is the standard detailed account of the ANC, the PAC and the other organisations of this period, and shows how repression and exile shaped them. Leonard Thompson (*A History of South Africa*, 1990) and Nigel Worden (*The Making of Modern South Africa*, 1994) place Sharpeville and Soweto within the longer history of white power and of African protest. Mandela's memoir, *Long Walk to Freedom* (1994), is a source to evaluate rather than only to quote: it was written decades later by a participant, who shaped the story as the leader of the movement that triumphed. A good essay uses these differences to make a point: historians who stress the **state's strength** see 1960–76 as a period of consolidation, and those who stress the **growth of resistance** see it as the preparation for the 1980s. Both are right, about different aspects.
:::

## How far did resistance and repression change South Africa?

Apply the four tests.

| Event | How many people? | What did it change? | How did the state react? | How long did the effect last? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sharpeville and the pass campaign (1960) | Thousands; 69 (or at least 91) killed | Ended legal opposition; ANC and PAC to exile; the world's attention | Emergency, bans, new laws | Long: the state's powers and the opposition's turn to force |
| MK and Rivonia (1961–64) | A few hundred active; ten on trial | Armed struggle; the ANC leadership imprisoned; Mandela as symbol | Sabotage Act, 90-day law, the trial | Long: symbol and strategy, but a short-term defeat |
| Black Consciousness (1968–77) | Thousands (students and professionals) | New pride and ideas; the gap filled | Banning, trials; bans of 1977 | Medium: ideas lasted, the organisations did not |
| Soweto (1976) | Tens of thousands; hundreds killed | Spread; a new generation; exile recruits; world opinion | Force, a few concessions | Long: it started the cycle of the 1980s |
| International pressure (1960–77) | Governments and peoples | Isolation in sport, the 1977 embargo | Defiance, Armscor | Growing but limited before 1977 |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Resistance **grew in size and changed its form**: from legal protest (1950s) to sabotage (1961), to a movement of ideas (Black Consciousness) and then to a mass revolt (Soweto). The state's response, **repression**, was very effective in the short term, in 1960–64 above all, and left the country calm for ten years. But repression also **created** the conditions for the next stage: it drove the ANC abroad and into armed struggle, and it made the Soweto generation. Outside pressure grew, but it was **not strong enough** to change the state before 1977, because the Western governments protected their interests. The most important change of the period was not the end of the state's power, which remained intact, but the end of its **legitimacy**.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'Sharpeville was a turning point in the struggle against apartheid.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained and balanced argument, a clear and supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Turning point" needs a test: did something change direction, and did it last? Criteria: (1) the form of resistance; (2) the strength of the state; (3) international opinion; (4) what happened later (1973–76). Support: 69 (at least 91) killed; the ANC and PAC banned on 8 April 1960; Mandela and MK turn to sabotage on 16 December 1961; the exile of Tambo; UN Resolution 134 (1 April 1960); capital flight; the Commonwealth exit; the laws of 1962–67. Challenge: the state grew stronger (the boom, the Rivonia convictions of 1964, ten years of quiet); the Western powers vetoed sanctions; resistance revived in 1973–76 with Black Consciousness, strikes and Soweto, and the arms embargo of 1977 followed Soweto and Biko, not Sharpeville. Judgement: a turning point in the *form* and the *geography* of the struggle, but not in the balance of power.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Sharpeville was a turning point because after it the ANC and PAC were banned and Mandela started Umkhonto we Sizwe. Sixty-nine people were killed and the world was shocked. The government declared a state of emergency. The United Nations also criticised South Africa. Later there was the Rivonia Trial where Mandela went to prison. All of this was because of Sharpeville, so it was a turning point."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, with a range of consequences, so Table B is Level 3. But it is a **chain of events** ("after it … later …") with the claim that all of it was "because of Sharpeville", and no test of what a turning point is or how far later events were caused by something else. No challenge is considered. Table A Level 3 at best (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If a turning point means a change in the *form* of the struggle, Sharpeville qualifies. Before 1960 the ANC used petitions, strikes and the Defiance Campaign; after the banning of the ANC and PAC on 8 April 1960 there was no legal way to organise, and on 16 December 1961 Mandela and others launched Umkhonto we Sizwe to begin sabotage. But if it means a change in the *balance of power*, the evidence is weaker. The state declared a state of emergency on 30 March 1960, banned both parties and, within four years, had convicted Mandela and the Rivonia leaders. Foreign capital left in 1960 but returned in the middle of the decade, and the economy grew fast."

*Commentary.* Two criteria are applied and tested against accurate detail. Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs the international criterion, a test of how far later events (Soweto, 1977) changed the picture, and a sustained judgement.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is partly valid. Sharpeville was a turning point in how the struggle was fought and where, but not in who held power, and the events that did shake the state came later, in 1976–77. I shall test it with four criteria: the form of resistance, the strength of the state, international opinion and what followed.

On the **form of resistance**, Sharpeville was decisive. In the 1950s the ANC had used petitions, the Defiance Campaign of 1952 and the Freedom Charter of 1955, all legal and non-violent. After police fired on the PAC's pass campaign on 21 March 1960 and the Unlawful Organizations Act of 8 April banned both the ANC and the PAC, there was no legal channel left. Mandela, Sisulu and others turned to force, and on 16 December 1961 Umkhonto we Sizwe began its sabotage campaign, which Mandela justified in 1964 by saying that without violence there would be 'no way open to the African people'. The leadership moved abroad under Oliver Tambo. On this criterion the event changed the course of the movement for thirty years.

On the **strength of the state**, however, the evidence is the other way. Verwoerd's government met the crisis with the emergency of 30 March 1960, which detained thousands, and then with the Sabotage Act of 1962, the 90-day law of 1963 and, in 1967, the Terrorism Act. By June 1964 Mandela and seven others had been sentenced to life imprisonment at Rivonia, and open resistance almost disappeared. Foreign capital fled in 1960 but returned in the middle of the decade, and the economy grew fast. The government's majority in the whites-only electorate grew in 1961 and 1966. Far from being weakened by 1960, the state was stronger in 1966 than in 1959.

On **international opinion**, Sharpeville was a beginning. The Security Council debated apartheid for the first time and passed Resolution 134 on 1 April 1960, and South Africa left the Commonwealth in 1961. The General Assembly asked members to break off relations in November 1962 (Resolution 1761, 67 votes to 16). But Britain, France and the USA, South Africa's main partners, did not support sanctions, and the mandatory arms embargo of 4 November 1977 followed Soweto and the death of Biko and not Sharpeville. So the shock of 1960 produced words more than action.

Finally, the argument about **what followed** matters. The revival of resistance came from different places: the Black Consciousness Movement of the late 1960s, the Durban strikes of 1973 and the Soweto uprising of 1976. They involved more people than the pass campaign of 1960, spread through the whole country and brought thousands of recruits to the ANC in exile. If a turning point is the moment after which the state could never again govern as before, Soweto has a better claim.

Overall, Sharpeville was a turning point in the *form* of the struggle, which became underground, armed and international, and in the way the world looked at South Africa. It was not a turning point in the *balance of power*, for the state remained strong for another decade, and it was Soweto and Biko's death that began the process of the state's loss of legitimacy. The fairest verdict is that Sharpeville ended one phase of resistance and began another, but that the real change of direction came sixteen years later."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): four explicit criteria, a balanced argument in which each side is tested against the other and a sustained judgement that separates the form of the struggle from the balance of power. Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise and selected detail (21 March and 8 April 1960; 16 December 1961; the laws of 1962, 1963 and 1967; Resolutions 134 and 1761, 67 to 16; 4 November 1977). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 700 words, 35 to 40 minutes.
:::

:::eg Assess the significance of the Black Consciousness Movement. [30]
**What the levels want.** The same two tables. "Assess the significance" asks how much the movement mattered, and so it needs criteria (for example its effect on ideas, on organisation, on the Soweto uprising and on the state) and a judgement about the **extent** of its importance.

**Plan.** Criteria: (1) ideas and identity; (2) organisation; (3) the link to Soweto; (4) the state's reaction; (5) its limits. Evidence: SASO (July 1969) and Biko; BPC (July 1972) and the Black Community Programmes; "Black is beautiful" and the definition of Black; the Durban strikes (1973); the Viva Frelimo rallies (September 1974) and the trial of nine; SASM and the Soweto SSRC; Biko's banning (March 1973) and death (12 September 1977); the bans of 19 October 1977. Limits: small numbers; no army; the ANC and the UDF took over in the 1980s; Black Consciousness leaders did not plan Soweto. Judgement: very significant for ideas and for the mood of 1976, less significant as an organisation.

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "The Black Consciousness Movement was very significant. It was started by Steve Biko in 1969 and it said that black people should be proud of themselves. Biko was killed by the police in 1977 which made it very famous. The movement also helped the Soweto uprising. This shows that the Black Consciousness Movement was very important in the fight against apartheid."

*Commentary.* True but general. The movement is described and not analysed, and "helped the Soweto uprising" and "very famous" are asserted. There is no mention of SASO, the BPC or the state's reaction in 1973–77, and no judgement of how far. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 (4–6).

**Moving to Level 4: judge the effect against a criterion.** "The Black Consciousness Movement mattered most for ideas. Biko and SASO, founded in July 1969 when Black students left the white-led NUSAS, argued that apartheid worked on the mind as well as on the body, so that the first task was to end the sense of inferiority. 'Black' meant Africans, Coloureds and Indians together, and the slogan 'Black is beautiful' gave the idea a form that young people could share. It filled a gap: after Rivonia the ANC and PAC were banned or in exile, and from 1969 to 1976 Black Consciousness was the main source of new political ideas inside the country. Yet it remained small, a movement of students and professionals, and it had no way of making the state change."

*Commentary.* A criterion (ideas) is applied with accurate detail and tested against its limits. Level 4. It still needs the other criteria (organisation, Soweto, the state's reaction) and a judgement on the **extent** of significance.

**A Level 5 conclusion** (after paragraphs on ideas, on organisation and Soweto, and on the state's reaction).

"Overall, the Black Consciousness Movement was highly significant, but its significance lay in *what it changed in South African politics* more than in *what it achieved as an organisation*. As an organisation it was weak: it had at most a few thousand active members, no army and no mass base among workers, and by 19 October 1977 it was banned. In ideas it was very strong: it gave a generation that had grown up under Bantu Education a language of dignity and self-reliance, and it was the only movement that was growing inside South Africa while the ANC and PAC were in exile. Its link to Soweto is the best test. The SASM, whose Action Committee organised the march of 16 June 1976, was influenced by Black Consciousness, and the students' placards and their rejection of a language that symbolised white power came from its ideas, though the movement's leaders did not plan the march. And the state's reaction shows that it took the movement seriously: the banning orders of 1973, the trial of nine leaders in 1975–76, the death of Biko in detention on 12 September 1977 and the banning of 18 organisations on 19 October 1977. I conclude that Black Consciousness was a necessary condition of the Soweto uprising, since without its ideas the revolt would have lacked its language and many of its leaders, but not a sufficient one, since without Bantu Education and the Afrikaans decree there would have been no spark."

*Commentary.* A clear judgement on the **extent** of significance, with a distinction (ideas and organisation, necessary and sufficient) that the paragraphs have earned. Table A Level 5; Table B depends on the detail in the paragraphs before it.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the reasons for the Soweto uprising of 1976." [30]

A student wrote:

"The Soweto uprising happened because the government made schools teach in Afrikaans. The students did not like Afrikaans because it was the language of the people who ran apartheid, so they marched on 16 June and the police shot at them. Many students were killed, such as Hector Pieterson. After that there were riots all over South Africa. So the main reason was the Afrikaans rule."

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): one reason is named and explained, the rest of the argument is a narrative of the day, and "so the main reason was the Afrikaans rule" is asserted, not tested against other reasons. Table B Level 2 (4–6): accurate but thin: the Afrikaans Medium Decree is not dated (1974), nothing is said of Bantu Education, Black Consciousness, SASM or the wider context (Mozambique and Angola in 1975), and the second half describes the events of the day and their results, which the question does not ask for.

**The fix.** Separate the long-term from the immediate causes and rank them. "The Afrikaans Medium Decree of 1974 was the immediate cause, because it forced pupils to learn mathematics and social studies in a language that most of them and their teachers hardly knew, and the SASM's Action Committee organised the march of 16 June against it. But it was a trigger, not the underlying cause. Bantu Education had produced crowded, poorly funded classrooms and a generation that saw no future, and Black Consciousness had given those pupils a language of pride and a movement, the SASM, that was ready to organise. Without the decree the revolt might have waited, but without the other causes the decree would have led to complaints, not an uprising of this size." Now each fact is evidence for a ranked argument, and the paragraph is on its way to Level 4 or 5.

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What happened at Sharpeville and Langa on 21 March 1960, and how many died at Sharpeville?
A: The PAC's anti-pass campaign: Africans were to leave their reference books at home and offer themselves for arrest. At Sharpeville police fired on a crowd without warning (about 1,300 rounds in about two minutes); the police count was 69 killed and 180 wounded, and later research found at least 91 killed and 238 injured. At Langa, near Cape Town, police fired on a crowd and killed two (some accounts say three).
Q: How did the government respond to Sharpeville?
A: A state of emergency on 30 March 1960 (detaining thousands, among them Mandela, and lasting until 31 August); the banning of the ANC and PAC on 8 April under the Unlawful Organizations Act; the arrest and imprisonment of Sobukwe; new security laws in 1962–67.
Q: What were Umkhonto we Sizwe and Poqo?
A: MK, "Spear of the Nation", was the ANC's armed wing, launched on 16 December 1961 with sabotage of government buildings and orders not to injure people. Poqo was the PAC's armed wing, which attacked chiefs, police and some white civilians in 1962–63 and was crushed by 1964.
Q: What did the 90-day law (1963) and the Terrorism Act (1967) allow?
A: The 1963 law allowed police to detain a person for 90 days without trial or a lawyer and to re-arrest them on release; the 1967 Act allowed indefinite detention without trial, in solitary confinement and with no access to family or lawyer, for a very broad range of "terrorist" acts.
Q: What happened at the Rivonia Trial, and what did Mandela say on 20 April 1964?
A: Ten men stood trial for sabotage after the police raided Liliesleaf Farm on 11 July 1963. Mandela's statement from the dock said that violence had become inevitable because of government policy and that sabotage was chosen to avoid loss of life, and ended that he was "prepared to die" for a democratic and free society "if needs be". On 12 June 1964 eight men, among them Mandela, Sisulu and Mbeki, were sentenced to life imprisonment; Bernstein was acquitted and Kantor discharged.
Q: What was Black Consciousness, and who founded SASO?
A: A philosophy of Black pride and self-reliance led by Steve Biko, who with other Black students founded the South African Students' Organisation in July 1969 after breaking from the white-led NUSAS. "Black" meant Africans, Coloureds and Indians. It spread through the BPC (July 1972), the Black Community Programmes and SASM.
Q: What caused the Soweto uprising of 16 June 1976, and what do estimates of the dead range between?
A: Bantu Education, the Afrikaans Medium Decree (1974) and the ideas of Black Consciousness. Estimates run from 23 (the first government figure) and 176 (the usual figure for 16–18 June) to 575 (the Cillié Commission up to 28 February 1977) and more than 700 (The Times, to the end of 1976).
Q: What was separate development, and what were its main stages by 1977?
A: The policy of treating Africans as members of ethnic "nations" with homelands. The Promotion of Bantu Self-government Act (1959); self-government for Transkei (1963); the Bantu Homelands Citizenship Act (1970), which gave every African citizenship of a homeland; "independence" for Transkei (26 October 1976) and, later, Bophuthatswana (1977), Venda (1979) and Ciskei (1981). About 3.5 million people were removed between 1960 and 1983, according to the Surplus People Project.
Q: What happened to Steve Biko, and how did the state respond to his death?
A: Biko was banned in March 1973, arrested on 18 August 1977 and died in police detention on 12 September 1977 of head injuries. The Minister of Justice, Kruger, first implied a hunger strike, an inquest found that nobody was to blame, and on 19 October 1977 the government banned 18 Black Consciousness organisations and two newspapers.
Q: What did the Security Council do in 1960 and in 1977, and why was the second more important?
A: In 1960 (Resolution 134, 1 April) it discussed apartheid for the first time and asked South Africa to abandon it. On 4 November 1977 (Resolution 418) it imposed a mandatory arms embargo, binding on all members, which was the first mandatory measure of that kind against a member state; Western powers had vetoed stronger measures before.
Q: How did events in southern Africa change South Africa's position in 1974–75?
A: The Portuguese revolution (25 April 1974) led to independence for Mozambique (25 June 1975) and Angola (11 November 1975), so that South Africa lost the buffer of white-ruled and friendly states and faced guerrilla war on its borders; the ANC could use bases in the region.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why the ANC and the PAC were banned in 1960.
A: Indicative content. The PAC's pass campaign and the events at Sharpeville and Langa (21 March 1960); the march of 30 March; the government's fear of a general revolt; the Unlawful Organizations Act of 8 April 1960 after the state of emergency of 30 March; the government's long-held aim to remove the ANC (the Treason Trial; the Suppression of Communism Act); the ANC's own planned pass campaign (31 March) and the fear that the two parties would lead a general revolt. Level 5 uses criteria (immediate trigger, long-term policy, political advantage) and judges that Sharpeville gave the government the excuse for a plan it had held for years.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'The Rivonia Trial destroyed the ANC.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Support: the arrest of the MK leadership on 11 July 1963; life sentences for eight men (12 June 1964); the movement banned and its leaders on Robben Island or in exile; almost no organised resistance inside the country for several years. Challenge: Mandela's statement of 20 April 1964 and the trial made him a symbol; the exile leadership under Tambo remained; the Anti-Apartheid Movement grew; Soweto brought new recruits; the ANC revived in the 1980s. Level 5 judges that it destroyed the ANC inside South Africa for a decade but not the movement, and that it helped to make a symbol.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the significance of the Soweto uprising of 1976.
A: Indicative content. A revolt by pupils, which spread across the country; death toll estimates (23, 176, 575, more than 700); thousands of young people left to join MK; UN Security Council Resolution 392 (19 June 1976); the rand fell; Transkei "independence" in October; the Afrikaans requirement withdrawn; the bans of 1977; arms embargo (4 November 1977). Limits: the state survived and reasserted control; most change came after 1978. Level 5 uses criteria (effect on resistance, the state, the outside world) and judges that Soweto's main significance was to begin the loss of the state's legitimacy.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that Steve Biko was the most important opponent of apartheid between 1968 and 1977.
A: Indicative content. Support: SASO (1969), the BPC (1972), the Black Community Programmes; the idea of Black pride; the state's fear of him (banned 1973; died 12 September 1977); his influence on Soweto. Challenge: Mandela, Tambo and the ANC in exile; Luthuli's moral leadership until 1967; Sobukwe; workers and the Durban strikes (1973); the SASM students; international movements. Level 5 sets a criterion (influence inside South Africa in those years) and judges that Biko was the most important figure inside the country but that the movement, not one leader, made the changes.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'International opposition was the main reason why South Africa's government changed its policies after 1976.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which policies changed after 1976, and who pressed for the change (the economy, Soweto, other states)?
H: Separate the events inside South Africa from the pressures outside.
A: Indicative content. Support: the UN arms embargo (4 November 1977); the isolation in sport and the UN debates; the pressure from the Anti-Apartheid Movement; the effect of Mozambique and Angola; the fall of the rand in 1976. Challenge: the state changed little until after 1978; the Western powers had vetoed sanctions in 1974; the pressure that mattered was internal (Soweto, the strikes); Armscor reduced the effect of the embargo; the mines and business wanted reform for economic reasons. Level 5 judges that international opposition was a necessary pressure but not the main cause, which was internal resistance.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which the Bantustans solved the National Party's problems in the period 1960–77.
H: List the aims of separate development (white control, labour, legitimacy), and ask whether each was met.
H: Consider the removals, the poverty and the response of the world.
A: Indicative content. Success: it gave a legal reason to deny Africans rights in white South Africa; the Bantu Homelands Citizenship Act (1970); the "independence" of Transkei (26 October 1976) and Bophuthatswana (1977); cheap labour continued; some chiefs and politicians cooperated. Limits: no country recognised the "independent" homelands; they were poor and dependent; about 3.5 million forced removals created resentment; most Africans stayed in white South Africa; the ANC and BCM rejected the homelands; Buthelezi refused independence. Level 5 judges that the Bantustans solved the problem of legitimacy in the National Party's own eyes but not in the eyes of the world or of Africans.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the effectiveness of the state's response to resistance in the period 1960–77.
H: Which period was the state most effective in, and which least?
H: Compare 1960–64 with 1976–77.
A: Indicative content. Effective: the state of emergency, the bans of 1960; the laws of 1962–67; Rivonia; BOSS (1969); the quiet of 1964–72; the bans of 19 October 1977. Ineffective: it could not stop Black Consciousness or Soweto; deaths in custody (Timol, 1971; Biko, 1977) and the killings of 1976 damaged it abroad; exile and armed struggle grew out of repression. Level 5 judges that the state's methods worked in the short term and failed in the long term, because they created the conditions for the next stage.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The apartheid state was stronger in 1977 than in 1960.' Evaluate this view.
H: Measure strength by several tests: control of the population, the economy, allies and legitimacy.
H: Compare the state's position before and after the loss of the Portuguese colonies.
A: Support: the economy grew fast in the 1960s and early 1970s; the security laws and BOSS; Armscor made it less dependent on imported arms; the ANC and PAC were weak inside the country; the Bantustans were advanced (Transkei "independent" 1976); whites still supported the National Party. Challenge: Soweto showed a new generation was unafraid; Mozambique and Angola removed the buffer states; the arms embargo (1977); the rand fell in 1976; the opposition of the world was greater than in 1960. A* judgement: the state was stronger in coercive terms (police, army, laws) and weaker in legitimacy, at home and abroad, so that the answer depends on what is meant by strength; the later history suggests that legitimacy mattered more.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Violence did more than non-violence to advance the struggle against apartheid in the period 1960–77.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which events were violent (by whom) and which were non-violent, and what did each change?
H: Consider the roles of MK, Poqo, Black Consciousness and the state's own violence.
A: Support: MK's sabotage and Rivonia made Mandela a symbol and led to world attention; Soweto, a violent confrontation with the state, spread across the country and brought recruits and the 1977 embargo. Challenge: MK's campaign was crushed by 1964 and did little damage; Poqo failed; Black Consciousness was non-violent and had more lasting effect on ideas; Sharpeville began as a non-violent protest and the state's violence changed opinion; the strikes of 1973 were non-violent. A* judgement: in this period it was the state's violence against non-violent protest (Sharpeville, Soweto, Biko) that changed opinion most, and the ANC's violence mattered more as a symbol than in its effects.
:::
