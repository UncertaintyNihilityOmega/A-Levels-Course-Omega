## What counts as success?

"How successful?" is a question about a standard. Before you write, decide which standard you will use, because the same facts can look like victory or defeat depending on the test. Four tests work for the Progressive Era.

:::key Four tests of success
1. **Was it passed?** Did the reformers get their laws and amendments?
2. **Was it enforced?** Did the law have inspectors, money and the support of the courts, or did it stay on paper?
3. **Did it work?** Did the problem (adulterated food, child labour, the power of a few banks) actually get better?
4. **For whom?** Who gained, and who was left out?
Most Progressive reforms score well on the first test, less well on the second and third, and very unevenly on the fourth. Good answers say which test they are using and why.
:::

Two other standards are useful. Compare the achievement with the movement's *own aims* ([Gilded Age (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3): honest government, democracy, regulation of business, protection of the weak). And compare it with *what had been done before*, since by 1920 the Federal Government did things that in 1890 almost no one thought it could do.

## The role of the Presidents

### The changing role of the presidency

In the Gilded Age most Presidents saw themselves as administrators who carried out the laws that Congress made. Congress and the party bosses were stronger; the President of 1880 had few staff and little power over the economy. This changed in three steps between 1901 and 1920.

:::def Stewardship theory
Theodore Roosevelt's view of the presidency, which he explained in his *Autobiography* (1913). The President is the "steward" of the people, and may do anything the needs of the nation require unless the Constitution or a law forbids it. In his words, he "declined to adopt the view that what was imperatively necessary for the Nation could not be done by the President unless he could find some specific authorization to do it".
:::

**Roosevelt** (1901–09) used the presidency as what he called a "bully pulpit", a position from which to lead public opinion. He sued trusts without waiting for Congress, settled a strike by his own authority, protected public land by executive order, and appealed over the heads of senators to the voters. **Taft** (1909–13), a lawyer who had been a judge, held the opposite view: a President can exercise only the powers that can be traced to the Constitution or a law. **Wilson** (1913–21), a former professor of politics, saw the President as the leader of a party and a legislative programme. On 8 April 1913 he went to the Capitol and delivered a message to Congress in person, the first President to do so since John Adams in 1800, and he worked with congressional leaders to pass his bills. By 1920 the President was the main source of national policy. That is the "changing role of the presidency" the syllabus names.

### Theodore Roosevelt and the Square Deal, 1901–09

Roosevelt was the youngest man to become President (he was 42) when William McKinley died on 14 September 1901, eight days after being shot by an anarchist at Buffalo. He was a Republican, a wealthy New Yorker who had been Governor of New York and Civil Service Commissioner, and he was a conservative reformer: he believed in capitalism but wanted a strong state to keep it fair and to keep social conflict from growing into revolution.

:::def Square Deal
Roosevelt's name for his approach to domestic policy: a fair chance for everyone, whether a worker or a business man, and a government that acted as an umpire between them, not as the servant of either. He used it in the 1902 coal strike and made it the motto of his 1904 campaign.
:::

**The coal strike, 1902.** On 12 May 1902 about 150,000 anthracite coal miners in Pennsylvania, led by John Mitchell of the United Mine Workers, struck for higher pay, shorter hours and recognition of their union. The mine owners, led by George Baer of the Reading Railroad, refused to negotiate. As winter came and coal prices rose, Roosevelt feared a fuel shortage in the cities. He called both sides to the White House and, when the owners refused, threatened to send the army to run the mines. J. P. Morgan and Secretary of War Elihu Root persuaded the owners to accept arbitration by a commission. The strike ended on 23 October 1902, after more than five months. The commission's award gave the miners a 10 per cent rise and a nine-hour day, but did not require the owners to recognise the union. This was new. Earlier Presidents had sent troops against strikers in 1877 and 1894. Roosevelt treated the union as a party with rights and himself as an arbitrator.

**Business and the trusts.** Roosevelt did not oppose big business as such. He distinguished "good" trusts, which were efficient and behaved fairly, from "bad" ones, which used their power to crush competitors or to cheat the public, and he thought that the Federal Government should supervise them. His main actions:
- **The Bureau of Corporations (1903)**, in the new Department of Commerce and Labor, gathered information about large companies. It was a way of using publicity that matched Progressive faith in facts.
- **The Elkins Act (1903)** made it a crime for railroads to give, or shippers to take, secret rebates.
- **Northern Securities.** In 1902 the Justice Department sued the Northern Securities Company, a holding company (a company that owns the shares of other companies and so controls them) created in 1901 by J. P. Morgan, J. J. Hill and E. H. Harriman to control the main railroads of the north-west. On 14 March 1904 the Supreme Court, by 5 votes to 4, ordered it dissolved. This showed that the Sherman Act could be used against the largest combinations, and Wall Street was shocked.
- **Other suits.** The government began a number of other suits, including against the "Beef Trust" of meat packers, Standard Oil (filed in 1906) and the American Tobacco Company (1907). Sources differ on the count; about 44 suits were begun in his years.
- **The railroads.** The **Hepburn Act (29 June 1906)** let the Interstate Commerce Commission set maximum railroad rates when shippers complained, extended its powers to pipelines and express companies, and made its orders binding unless a court overturned them.
- **Food and drugs.** The **Meat Inspection Act** and **Pure Food and Drug Act** of 30 June 1906 (see below).
- **Conservation.** Roosevelt protected about 230 million acres of public land, including 150 national forests, and the **Antiquities Act (1906)** let Presidents declare national monuments (see below).

Roosevelt also had limits and failures. He allowed U.S. Steel to buy the Tennessee Coal and Iron Company during the Panic of 1907, which seemed to strengthen a monopoly. He did little for tariff reform. He treated reform as something done to business rather than with workers. And on race, as you will see, his record was mixed: in 1906, after a night of shooting in Brownsville, Texas, he dismissed 167 Black soldiers without trial, on thin evidence.

### William Howard Taft, 1909–13

Roosevelt kept his promise not to stand for a third term and chose **William Howard Taft**, his Secretary of War, as his successor. Taft won easily in 1908. He was a large, genial man and a judge by temperament. Taft was more active against trusts than Roosevelt had been, but he lost the support of Progressives, partly because of the way he conducted his affairs.
- **Trust-busting.** His Justice Department brought nearly 90 anti-trust suits in four years, about twice as many as Roosevelt's in eight. The Supreme Court ordered **Standard Oil** broken up on 15 May 1911 into more than thirty companies, and ordered the American Tobacco Company reorganised. But the Court's ruling in the Standard Oil case set the **rule of reason**: only "unreasonable" restraints of trade were illegal, so a court could decide that a combination was acceptable. Taft's suit against U.S. Steel in 1911 outraged Roosevelt, whose approval of the Tennessee Coal and Iron purchase was criticised in the complaint; the Court later (1920) decided in the company's favour.
- **Regulation.** The **Mann–Elkins Act (1910)** put telephone, telegraph and cable companies under the Interstate Commerce Commission and gave it power to suspend new rates. Taft also supported postal savings banks (1910), the Children's Bureau (1912), an eight-hour day for workers on federal contracts (1912) and the creation of the Department of Labor, which he signed on his last day in office, 4 March 1913.
- **The income tax.** In 1909 Taft asked Congress to send a constitutional amendment on the income tax to the states. It was the 16th Amendment.

His difficulties were a matter of politics. In 1909 he signed the **Payne–Aldrich Tariff**, which cut some duties but raised others, and called it "the best tariff bill that the Republican party ever passed"; Progressive Republicans such as La Follette thought it was a betrayal of a promise to cut duties. In the **Ballinger–Pinchot affair** (1909–10) Gifford Pinchot, the head of the Forest Service and Roosevelt's friend, accused Taft's Secretary of the Interior, Richard Ballinger, of helping private interests to claim coal lands in Alaska. Taft backed Ballinger and dismissed Pinchot in January 1910. To Progressives this seemed to show that Taft had abandoned conservation. Taft also sided with the old guard in the House when the insurgents revolted against Speaker Cannon in 1910. By the autumn of 1910 Roosevelt, who had returned from a long tour of Africa and Europe, was publicly at odds with his successor.

### The election of 1912

Roosevelt spoke at Osawatomie, Kansas, on 31 August 1910 and set out a programme he called the **New Nationalism**: a strong national government, supervision of corporations, protection of workers, and the idea that "the national need" must come before "sectional or personal advantage". When he lost the Republican nomination to Taft in June 1912, he formed the Progressive Party. The Democrats chose **Woodrow Wilson**, the Governor of New Jersey, who promised a **New Freedom**: to break the power of trusts and restore competition, rather than supervise big business as Roosevelt proposed.

### Woodrow Wilson and the New Freedom, 1913–21

Wilson, a Democrat, was the first Southerner elected President since Zachary Taylor in 1848. He had been a professor of politics and then president of Princeton University before becoming Governor of New Jersey in 1911. His first term was the most productive legislative period of the Progressive Era. He called Congress into special session in the spring of 1913 and put forward three bills.
- **Tariff and income tax: the Underwood–Simmons Act (3 October 1913)** lowered duties on hundreds of goods and introduced the first regular federal income tax under the new 16th Amendment: 1 per cent on incomes above $3,000 for a single person ($4,000 for a married couple), with extra tax on larger incomes up to 7 per cent in total.
- **Banking: the Federal Reserve Act (23 December 1913)** set up twelve regional Federal Reserve banks, supervised by a Federal Reserve Board in Washington whose members the President appointed, to issue a flexible currency and lend to banks in a crisis. It was a compromise between bankers who wanted a private system and Progressives who wanted public control.
- **Trusts: the Federal Trade Commission Act (26 September 1914)** and the **Clayton Act (15 October 1914)**. The FTC could investigate "unfair methods of competition" and order them stopped. The Clayton Act listed practices that were illegal when they reduced competition (price discrimination, buying a competitor's shares, interlocking directorates, the same men sitting on the boards of directors of competing firms), and it said that "the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce", limiting the use of injunctions against strikes.

After 1914 Wilson, who had believed that the New Freedom was complete, moved toward Roosevelt's ideas of positive government, partly because he needed Progressive voters to be re-elected in 1916. In 1916 his administration and Congress passed the **Federal Farm Loan Act** (cheap credit for farmers), the **Keating–Owen Child Labor Act** (a ban on interstate shipping of goods made by children), the **Adamson Act** (an eight-hour day for railroad workers, passed after a threatened strike), a workers' compensation law for federal employees, and the appointment of Louis Brandeis to the Supreme Court. Wilson also supported women's suffrage after 1918.

His record has a darker side. Wilson's administration extended racial segregation in federal offices in 1913, and he defended it. He condemned mob violence in public only in July 1918, and Congress passed no law against lynching. He doubted the case for a federal suffrage amendment until 1918. And after the United States entered the First World War in April 1917, the government's priorities moved to the war, with reform energy turned into mobilisation and then, in 1919–20, into fear of radicals in the Red Scare.

## The growth of regulation by city, state and federal governments

Regulation grew at three levels at once, and the states and cities often went first. For each area below, ask the four tests: passed, enforced, worked, for whom.

### Transport

Railroads were the first business regulated, and the story shows how a weak law became a strong one by stages. The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 (see [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2)) had set up the Commission but the courts had taken away its power to set rates. The Progressives rebuilt it.
- The **Elkins Act (1903)** made rebates illegal for both the railroad and the shipper.
- The **Hepburn Act (1906)** allowed the Commission to set a maximum rate once a shipper complained, widened its authority to pipelines, express companies and sleeping-car companies, and required railroads to keep standard accounts. Railroads could appeal to the courts, but the burden was now on them.
- The **Mann–Elkins Act (1910)** added telephone, telegraph and cable companies and let the Commission suspend a rate rise while it investigated.
- The **Adamson Act (1916)** fixed an eight-hour day for railroad workers on interstate lines, after the four brotherhoods (the main railroad workers' unions) had threatened a national strike. The Supreme Court upheld it in 1917.
- The **Federal Aid Road Act (1916)** gave states federal money for roads on a matching basis, the first national road programme.
- States and cities set up commissions to regulate streetcar fares, gas and electricity: Wisconsin and New York created public service commissions in 1907.

The result was a real change. By 1916 the railroads' freedom to set any price was over. But the regulators needed skill and money to do the job, and by the war the railroads' financial position had weakened so far that the Federal Government took them over in December 1917.

### Food and drugs

[Gilded Age (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3) described how *The Jungle* and Adams's articles led to two laws on 30 June 1906. Their provisions are:
- **The Pure Food and Drug Act** banned the interstate sale of adulterated or falsely labelled food and drugs, and required labels to show the amount of alcohol and of habit-forming drugs such as morphine and cocaine. It was enforced by the Bureau of Chemistry under **Harvey Wiley**, whose "Poison Squad" of volunteer young men (1902–04) had tested the effects of food preservatives on their own bodies.
- **The Meat Inspection Act** required federal inspection of animals before and after slaughter in every plant that sold meat across state lines, and set sanitary standards for the plants. Congress refused to make the packers pay for the inspectors, and the taxpayers did.

Their limits are as important. The Supreme Court held in 1911 (*United States v. Johnson*) that the Act did not cover false claims about a medicine's power to cure, and Congress had to pass the Sherley Amendment (1912) to close the gap. The Gould Amendment (1913) required the net weight on packages. Both laws covered only products that crossed state lines. And although the meat was cleaner, the conditions of the people who worked in the packing plants changed far less, which was the point of Sinclair's book. Many states passed their own food laws, which were often stronger, and cities passed pure milk laws (Chicago required pasteurisation of most milk in 1908) and began to chlorinate water (Jersey City in 1908).

### Environmental protection

:::def Conservation
The Progressive policy of using natural resources (forests, water, minerals, grazing land) wisely and under public control, so that they would last, rather than leaving them to be used up by private companies. Its leader was Gifford Pinchot, head of the Forest Service from 1905. It differed from *preservation*, the aim of John Muir and the Sierra Club, which was to keep wild places untouched.
:::

Conservation was Roosevelt's most lasting achievement and the part of his presidency that was least controversial in the Northeast.
- The **Reclamation Act (1902)** used money from public land sales to build irrigation dams in the arid West, such as the Roosevelt Dam in Arizona.
- The **Forest Service (1905)** took control of the forest reserves under Pinchot, who ran them for "wise use" (grazing and logging were allowed, under licence).
- Roosevelt protected about **230 million acres**, including 150 national forests, five national parks, many bird reserves (the first, Pelican Island in Florida, in 1903) and, using the **Antiquities Act of 1906**, national monuments, of which the first was Devils Tower in Wyoming. In March 1907, just before a law limiting his power to create forests in six western states took effect, he signed proclamations for twenty-one new national forests covering about 16 million acres. Westerners complained that the East was locking up their land. In May 1908 he held a White House conference of state governors on conservation.
- **Taft** added to the forests in the East under the Weeks Act (1911), but his dismissal of Pinchot in 1910 gave conservationists a reason to distrust him.
- **Wilson** approved the flooding of the Hetch Hetchy valley in Yosemite National Park to supply San Francisco's water in 1913, a defeat for Muir's preservationists; but in August 1916 he signed the act that created the **National Park Service**.

Conservation was an achievement for the Federal Government because the lands stayed public. But it was a policy of compromise: it allowed commercial use, and the supporters of preservation lost the argument about Hetch Hetchy.

### Working and living conditions

The main work remained with the states, as [Gilded Age (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3) showed. The Federal Government added what the Constitution allowed.
- **Federal action:** safety rules for the railroads (the Safety Appliance Acts); a Federal Employers' Liability Act (1906, re-enacted in 1908 after the first was struck down) for railroad workers; the **Bureau of Mines (1910)**; the **Children's Bureau (1912)** under Julia Lathrop, to study child welfare; the **Department of Labor (1913)**; the **Seamen's Act (1915)**; the **Adamson Act (1916)**; and the **Keating–Owen Act (1 September 1916)**, which banned the interstate shipment of goods made in factories by children under fourteen, or in mines by children under sixteen. On 3 June 1918, in *Hammer v. Dagenhart*, the Supreme Court struck it down by 5 votes to 4, holding that the manufacture of goods was not commerce, and that the Act intruded on the power of the states. Congress's second attempt, a tax on goods made with child labour (1919), was also struck down, in 1922. The Child Labor Amendment of 1924 was never ratified.
- **State and city action:** workers' compensation (most states by 1920); mothers' pensions; hours laws; New York's **Tenement House Act of 1901**, drafted by Lawrence Veiller, which required windows, ventilation, running water and a toilet in every new apartment; New York's zoning resolution of 1916, the first comprehensive one in the country; playgrounds, school nurses (Lillian Wald placed the first in New York in 1902), public baths and smoke laws.

How well did it work? Some measures of living standards improved. The share of children aged 10 to 15 recorded as at work fell from about 18 per cent in 1900 to about 8 per cent in 1920, though the census changed its methods, so the exact figures need care. Life expectancy at birth rose from about 47 years in 1900 to about 54 in 1920. But reform was not the only cause: medical science, cheaper food and higher incomes mattered too. Slums remained. The tenement law applied to new buildings. Millions of workers in small firms, agriculture and domestic service were outside the factory laws. The courts made reform patchy: hours laws, in particular, were struck down or upheld case by case.

### Banks, businesses, the stock market, monopolies and trusts

Here the Federal Government's role grew most. See the presidents' sections for the laws; the picture is this.
- **Banks.** The Federal Reserve system gave the United States a central banking system for the first time since the 1830s. It ended the old pattern of panics caused by a shortage of cash in the autumn, and it worked, in the sense of surviving, to the present. Its limits: it was run by a board in which bankers had a strong voice and the system was weak against the speculative boom of the 1920s.
- **Trusts and monopolies.** Roosevelt and Taft won the breakup of Northern Securities, Standard Oil and American Tobacco, but the "rule of reason" (1911) meant that size alone was no crime, and in 1920 the Court refused to break up U.S. Steel. The Clayton Act's wording ("where the effect may be to substantially lessen competition") gave the courts wide discretion, and the Federal Trade Commission, though important, was often restrained by the courts and then, in the 1920s, was led by men friendly to business. Concentration continued: a new wave of mergers followed in the 1920s.
- **The stock market.** There was no federal law. The New York Stock Exchange regulated itself and the main protection for investors was the state "blue sky laws", which varied in strength. The gap lasted until the 1930s.

### Anti-corruption measures

Since honest government was the first Progressive goal, here is a summary of what was done.
- **Campaign money.** The **Tillman Act (1907)** forbade corporations and national banks to contribute to federal election campaigns; the **Publicity Act (1910)**, amended in 1911, required party committees to report their spending. Both were weak: contributions could be made in other forms and the penalties were rarely used. Many states passed "corrupt practices" acts.
- **Structure.** The secret ballot, primaries, the recall and the 17th Amendment reduced the chances of buying nominations or seats; commission and manager governments, and civil service examinations, reduced the number of jobs that bosses could hand out.
- **Prosecution.** Local prosecutors such as Joseph Folk in St Louis and Francis Heney in San Francisco (who prosecuted the city's graft ring after 1906) showed that bribery could be punished.

The results were real but partial. Cities such as Cleveland, Toledo and Galveston were governed more honestly. Machines survived in New York, Chicago, Kansas City and Philadelphia, and some, like Tammany Hall, adapted by supporting factory reforms. The most important change was probably in standards: after 1900 open bribery of legislatures became harder, though the influence of money on politics did not disappear.

## The amendments to the Constitution

Four constitutional amendments were ratified between 1913 and 1920, more than in the previous 40 years. They mark the peak of the Progressive Movement. Passed and ratified dates differ by months or years.

**The 16th Amendment.** The case for it was that the Federal Government depended on tariffs and excise duties, which fell hardest on poor consumers. (An excise is a tax on one kind of good, such as alcohol or tobacco.) The Constitution said that a "direct" tax had to be apportioned: divided among the states according to their population, whatever their wealth. In *Pollock v. Farmers' Loan and Trust Co.* (1895) the Supreme Court ruled that the 1894 income tax was a direct tax, so it was unconstitutional because it was not apportioned. A tax on incomes could not be shared out by population (each state would have to raise the same sum per head, so the rate would have to be much higher in poor states than in rich ones), so the Court's ruling made a federal income tax impossible. The 16th Amendment removed the apportionment rule for taxes on incomes. An income tax would fall on those best able to pay and would make the government less dependent on the tariff. After Congress passed the amendment in 1909, many conservatives expected the states to reject it, but by 3 February 1913 the necessary 36 states had approved. The **Revenue Act of 1913** put it into effect with a tax of 1 per cent on incomes above $3,000 and a higher rate for the richest. It was small: fewer than 400,000 people filed a return in 1914, well under 1 per cent of the population. But it gave the Federal Government a source of revenue that could grow with the economy, and in the First World War the top rate rose to 77 per cent (1918). This amendment more than any other made possible a large federal government.

**The 17th Amendment.** Its effect was to make Senators answerable to voters rather than to state legislatures. It did not abolish Senate millionaires or the influence of money, and historians doubt how much it changed what the Senate did, but it removed one of the clearest examples of corruption and it ended deadlocks in the legislatures.

**The 18th Amendment.** The reasons it was passed are the answer to a common question, and the best answers connect several of them.
- **The long campaign.** The WCTU (1874) and the Anti-Saloon League (1893) built a mass movement of Protestant women and churches. By 1917 more than twenty states had gone dry.
- **Pressure group method.** The League, under Wayne Wheeler, worked through the churches and rewarded or punished candidates, whatever their party. Legislators, particularly in rural states with over-represented country districts, could see which way votes would go.
- **Progressive belief in law.** Many Progressives believed that the state could improve citizens' conduct, and linked drink to poverty, crime, machine politics and the exploitation of workers. The saloon was the bosses' base.
- **The income tax.** Before 1913, taxes on alcohol had provided a large share of federal revenue, which made prohibition seem impractical. The 16th Amendment removed that objection.
- **The First World War.** The Lever Act (1917) limited the use of grain for drink, so as to save food; the need for sacrifice made drinking seem unpatriotic; and the leading brewers had German names (Pabst, Schlitz, Anheuser-Busch), so anti-German feeling turned against them. Congress approved the amendment in December 1917, and ratification took only thirteen months. The **Volstead Act** of October 1919, passed over Wilson's veto, defined an "intoxicating liquor" as one with more than 0.5 per cent alcohol.

Prohibition took effect on 17 January 1920. Consumption fell at first, but illegal supply by bootleggers grew and brought organised crime, and it divided Americans between "dry" countryside and "wet" cities. It was repealed by the 21st Amendment in 1933. It was a victory for the pressure groups and a failure as policy: a reminder that "passed" is only the first test.

**The 19th Amendment** is covered in the next section, because to explain why it passed you must explain the movement behind it.

## The impact of pressure groups and the role played by women

Pressure groups are organisations that try to influence governments without taking office. The Progressive Era was a golden age of them, and most of the amendments and laws in this chapter owed their passage to one.

:::key Why pressure groups succeeded when they did
1. **A single, clear aim** that a politician could vote for (prohibition, the vote, a child labour law).
2. **Organisation and money:** a mass membership, paid lobbyists and a publishing operation (the ASL, NAWSA).
3. **Publicity and evidence:** Hine's photographs, the Brandeis brief, the Poison Squad: facts in a form that voters could understand.
4. **Allies in office:** a President or senators who could deliver (Roosevelt on conservation, Wilson on the Federal Reserve, the Senators who sponsored the suffrage amendment).
5. **Timing:** the First World War gave the suffrage and prohibition causes a patriotic argument that they had lacked in 1914.
:::

### The votes-for-women campaign

The National American Woman Suffrage Association was formed in 1890 by the merger of two older groups. For some twenty years it won little: between 1896 and 1910 no new state granted women the vote. The revival came after 1910, when Washington State (1910), California (1911) and Oregon, Arizona and Kansas (1912) voted for it, and a younger generation turned to parades, open-air meetings and lobbying. By 1914 women could vote in eleven western states.

Two leaders divided the work. **Carrie Chapman Catt**, who led NAWSA again from 1915, followed a "Winning Plan" (1916) of winning state campaigns where possible while keeping up pressure for a federal amendment. NAWSA was the larger, more cautious body. **Alice Paul** had learned militant methods from the British suffragettes. In 1913 she organised a parade of thousands in Washington on 3 March, the day before Wilson's inauguration; the marchers were attacked by crowds and the police did little. She formed the Congressional Union, which became the **National Woman's Party** in 1916, and held that the party in power (the Democrats) should be held responsible for failing to pass the amendment. From 10 January 1917 NWP members stood daily at the White House gates with banners, the "Silent Sentinels". After the United States entered the war, the pickets were arrested, and some women who were jailed at the Occoquan Workhouse went on hunger strike and were force-fed. The public sympathy this produced helped the cause.

The war was decisive in three ways. Women's work in factories, hospitals and the Red Cross was visible and made the argument that they were citizens easier. Wilson, who had avoided the question by saying that the issue belonged to the states, changed his mind: in January 1918 he supported the amendment, and in an address to the Senate on 30 September 1918 he called it "vitally essential to the successful prosecution of the great war of humanity in which we are engaged". The House passed it on 10 January 1918 with exactly the two-thirds needed; the Senate rejected it in October 1918 and February 1919; and after the elections of November 1918 had strengthened the supporters, it passed the House on 21 May and the Senate on 4 June 1919 (56 to 25). Ratification took 14 months. The last battle was in Tennessee, where on 18 August 1920 the legislature ratified by a margin of one vote after a young member, Harry Burn, changed his vote. Tennessee was the 36th state.

The campaign had opponents: Southern politicians who feared that Black women would vote and who defended "states' rights"; brewers who feared that women would vote dry; machine politicians; and a body of women who believed that the vote was not for women, in the National Association Opposed to Woman Suffrage (1911). It also had a racial shadow. NAWSA leaders in the South accepted segregated organisations and sometimes argued that the vote for educated white women would strengthen white control. At the 1913 parade, Black women were asked to march at the back of the procession, and Ida B. Wells joined her Illinois delegation instead. And after 1920, Black women in the South were kept from voting by the same poll taxes and literacy tests as Black men.

### Women's role beyond the vote

Women's contribution to the Progressive Movement was more than the suffrage campaign. They ran the settlements, the National Consumers' League, the Women's Trade Union League and the clubs (see [Gilded Age (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3)). They held posts in government: Julia Lathrop headed the Children's Bureau from 1912, and Florence Kelley and Frances Perkins (who watched the Triangle fire and then worked on New York's factory laws) were among those who moved from campaigning into official work. In 1916 Jeannette Rankin of Montana became the first woman elected to the House of Representatives, four years before the 19th Amendment. After 1920 the League of Women Voters, formed by NAWSA in 1920, kept this political activity going.

## The remaining problems of the Progressive Movement

The syllabus lists four. They are not a list of failures to be learned beside the successes: they are the other half of every "how successful?" judgement.

### The nature and extent of the legislation

- **A patchwork.** The Constitution divided power between the Federal Government and 48 states, and most Progressive laws were passed by the states. A law on hours in New York did not apply in Georgia, and an employer could move to the state with the weakest law. National laws existed only where Congress could find a power over interstate commerce, taxation or the mails.
- **The courts.** *E.C. Knight* (1895), *Lochner* (1905), *Loewe v. Lawlor* (1908) and *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918) show a Supreme Court that often read the Constitution narrowly. The Court also approved much reform (*Muller*, *Bunting*, *Northern Securities*, *Standard Oil*), so a good answer says that it was an obstacle but not a wall.
- **Weak enforcement.** The Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act were enforced by small bureaus; the Interstate Commerce Commission had few staff; child labour laws had many exceptions and few inspectors.
- **Compromise with business.** Many laws took the form business could live with. Kolko argued (1963) that this was the pattern, and that regulation often stabilised big business more than it limited it. The 1911 "rule of reason" and the pro-business Federal Trade Commission of the 1920s support him.
- **Limited redistribution.** The income tax in 1913 fell on very few people. The Progressives mostly accepted the market economy and private property. They did not support universal pensions, health insurance or unemployment benefits, which Europe was beginning to adopt (Germany since the 1880s, Britain since 1908–11), though the 1912 platform called for "social insurance" and a few states passed mothers' pensions.
- **The end of the movement.** The war turned energy and money to mobilisation, and the Red Scare of 1919–20 made reform look radical. The Republicans, who won the presidency in 1920 with Warren Harding's promise of "normalcy", were not interested in new regulation. The Progressive impulse did not die: its ideas returned in the New Deal of the 1930s (see [Crash, Depression and New Deal (1)](#hist.us.depression.1)).

### Minorities

For African Americans, who in 1900 were more than one in nine Americans and lived mainly in the South, the Progressive Era was in many ways worse than what came before.
- **Segregation and disfranchisement became law.** *Plessy v. Ferguson* (18 May 1896) upheld "separate but equal" facilities, and the South added segregation laws on streetcars, in schools, in parks and in public buildings. Across the South, constitutional conventions between 1890 and 1908 wrote poll taxes, literacy tests and grandfather clauses into state constitutions (see [Gilded Age (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3)).
- **Violence.** Lynching continued in the South and elsewhere, and white mobs attacked Black communities: Springfield, Illinois (August 1908), East St Louis (July 1917) and many cities in the "Red Summer" of 1919.
- **The Federal Government and the Presidents.** Roosevelt invited Booker T. Washington to dinner at the White House in October 1901, which angered white Southerners, but dismissed the Black soldiers at Brownsville in 1906. Taft followed a policy of appeasing the white South. In 1912 the Progressive Party, to win Southern white votes, refused to seat Black delegates from some Southern states and said little about civil rights. In 1913 Wilson's administration segregated work areas, restrooms and lunchrooms in federal departments such as the Treasury and the Post Office. When the Black editor William Monroe Trotter protested in the White House in November 1914, Wilson defended the policy, and the exchange ended badly.
- **Black responses.** Booker T. Washington's "Atlanta Compromise" speech of 1895 had advised Black Southerners to accept social separation and concentrate on education and work. **W. E. B. Du Bois**, in *The Souls of Black Folk* (1903), and the Niagara Movement (1905) demanded full civil and political rights. The riot at Springfield in 1908, in Lincoln's home town, led white and Black reformers (Du Bois, Ida B. Wells, Mary White Ovington, Oswald Garrison Villard, Moorfield Storey) to found the **NAACP** in 1909, and Du Bois edited its magazine **The Crisis** from November 1910, which grew to a circulation of about 100,000 by 1918. The NAACP won some legal cases: *Guinn v. United States* (1915) struck down Oklahoma's grandfather clause, and *Buchanan v. Warley* (1917) struck down a Louisville ordinance that enforced residential segregation. Many Black Americans also voted with their feet: hundreds of thousands moved north in the Great Migration that began in 1916, looking for jobs and a freer life.
- **Other groups.** The Immigration Act of 1917 introduced a literacy test for immigrants (passed over Wilson's veto) and barred most Asian immigrants. Native Americans were not full citizens until 1924. Some Progressives ran "Americanisation" programmes for immigrants, which taught English and civics but also pressed them to give up their languages and customs.

### Gender inequality

The 19th Amendment gave women the vote, but equality was a long way off. Most Progressive laws for women were *protective*: they limited their hours or banned night work because women were seen as weaker or as mothers (*Muller v. Oregon*, 1908). Those laws helped many women but also excluded them from some jobs and kept women's pay below men's. Women were barred from most professions and from jury service in many states; married women's legal rights over property and wages varied from state to state; and birth control, publicised by Margaret Sanger, who opened a clinic in Brooklyn in 1916, was illegal under the Comstock laws (federal and state laws that banned sending birth-control information through the mail). The women's movement split in the 1920s when the National Woman's Party proposed an Equal Rights Amendment (1923) that many reformers who had backed protective laws opposed. Black women in the South were still denied the vote. So the suffrage victory was real and lasting, but it was the end of one movement and the start of another.

### Rural deprivation

Even in 1920 almost half of Americans (48.8 per cent, by the census) still lived in the countryside, and the Progressive Movement was mainly urban. Farmers had won the 1890s Populist argument over the money question, since gold discoveries raised prices, and the years from 1900 to 1914 are sometimes called the "golden age" of American agriculture, when prices were good. But country people lacked many things that city reformers took for granted: electricity, running water, good roads, doctors, schools and credit. Tenant farming grew from about a quarter of farms in 1880 to about 38 per cent in 1920, especially among Black sharecroppers in the South. In 1908 Roosevelt appointed a **Country Life Commission** under the horticulturalist Liberty Hyde Bailey, which reported in 1909 that rural life was in crisis and recommended better schools, roads, mail and farm advice. Action followed: Rural Free Delivery spread, parcel post began in 1913, the **Smith–Lever Act (1914)** paid for county agents and farm demonstration, the **Federal Farm Loan Act (1916)** set up land banks that lent at low rates, and the **Federal Aid Road Act (1916)** funded rural roads. These were real gains, but the Progressives did not tackle tenancy and low rural incomes, and the farm depression after 1920 showed that the problems remained. Farmers had their own protest movements rather than a place in the city-led reform movement.

## How successful was the Progressive Movement?

### A scorecard

| Group or aim | What was gained | What was not |
|---|---|---|
| Voters and the political system | Secret ballot, primaries, direct election of Senators, initiative and recall in many states, women's suffrage | Black disfranchisement; machines survived; money kept its influence |
| Industrial workers | Workers' compensation, some hours laws, factory inspection, Adamson Act, partial legal recognition of unions (Clayton Act) | Courts struck down hours laws; no minimum wage for men; unions still fought injunctions; no pensions or unemployment pay |
| Children | Child labour fell; Children's Bureau; school attendance rose | Keating–Owen struck down (1918); no federal ban until 1938 |
| Consumers | Pure Food and Drug Act, Meat Inspection Act, state milk and water laws | Enforcement thin; patent medicines survived; workers' side of The Jungle untouched |
| Women | The vote (1920); public roles and government posts | Equal pay and legal equality; Black women excluded in the South |
| African Americans | The NAACP and The Crisis; first court wins; the Great Migration | Segregation spread, disfranchisement, lynching; federal discrimination under Wilson |
| Immigrants | Settlement help; some city services | Literacy test (1917); Americanisation pressure |
| Farmers | Credit, extension, roads, mail | Tenancy rose; rural poverty remained |
| Economy and business | Federal Reserve; FTC; Hepburn Act; the Standard Oil and Northern Securities cases | Concentration continued; no stock market regulation |
| The environment | National forests, monuments, parks, Park Service | Hetch Hetchy; limits on preservation |
| The federal role | Income tax; central bank; regulatory commissions; a stronger presidency | War brought many controls, but a retreat after 1920 |

:::key Weighing success: a judgement you can defend
1. **Passed:** very successful. In twenty years, four constitutional amendments and some of the largest laws in American history.
2. **Enforced and effective:** partly successful. Where the law created an institution that lasted (the Federal Reserve, the Forest Service, the income tax, the FTC), it worked. Where it relied on courts, small bureaus or voluntary compliance, results were uneven.
3. **For whom:** unevenly. White, urban, middle-class and many working-class Americans gained; Black Americans, many immigrants and rural families gained little or lost ground.
4. **Lasting:** the biggest changes (the new Federal role, the 16th, 17th and 19th Amendments, regulation as a principle) lasted. The 18th Amendment was repealed in 1933 and *Hammer v. Dagenhart* stood until 1941.
Overall verdict: a movement that succeeded in changing what Americans expected from government, much more than in solving the particular problems it named.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **Richard Hofstadter**, *The Age of Reform* (1955), judged the movement partly a success in practical reforms but saw it as limited by the moral anxieties of its middle-class leaders, whom he thought were fighting to protect their own status.
- **Gabriel Kolko**, *The Triumph of Conservatism* (1963), argued that the Progressive Era was a triumph for business, which used regulation to protect itself, so the movement succeeded for the reasons conservatives wanted.
- **Michael McGerr**, *A Fierce Discontent* (2003), argued that Progressives were trying to change the way Americans lived, to replace the individualism of the Gilded Age with a social ideal, and that they failed to carry the people with them: the movement ended when its demand for control (Prohibition, immigration restriction, the war) alienated the public.
Use these only to show that "success" depends on what you think the movement was trying to do.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the 18th Amendment was passed.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The 18th Amendment banned alcohol in 1919. There was a temperance movement with the WCTU and the Anti-Saloon League. Many people thought drink caused crime and poverty. America also joined the First World War in 1917. The amendment was passed in December 1917 and was ratified in 1919."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence states a fact. None says how it made the amendment pass; the link between the war and the amendment is not explained. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The main reason was the long campaign by pressure groups. The WCTU, founded in 1874, and the Anti-Saloon League, founded in 1893, built a mass movement among Protestant churches and women. The League worked across party lines, backing any candidate who would vote for prohibition, so legislators learned that to oppose it would cost votes. By 1917 more than twenty states had gone dry, which made a national law seem less of a leap. A second reason was the First World War. After the United States entered the war in April 1917, the Lever Act limited the use of grain for drink and drinking was said to be unpatriotic. The leading brewers, Pabst, Schlitz and Anheuser-Busch, had German names, and anti-German feeling turned against them."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add a third factor and show how they work together.

"A third reason was the Progressive belief that law could improve people. Reformers linked drink to poverty, crime and violence in families, and the saloon to the machine politicians who bought votes there, so prohibition seemed part of making government honest and protecting the weak. The income tax helped, too: the 16th Amendment in 1913 meant that the government no longer depended on taxes on alcohol, which had made prohibition look unaffordable.

These factors were linked. The long campaign made the idea respectable and gave the League its organisation; the Progressive argument gave it supporters outside the churches; and the war supplied the moment, because it turned the argument about health and morals into one about patriotism and food. Congress passed the amendment in December 1917 and the states ratified it within thirteen months, much faster than the 16th, which had taken over three years. The long campaign was therefore the necessary cause, but the war was probably the decisive one, because it removed the arguments of the brewers and the fear of lost revenue and turned a bitter dispute into a wartime duty."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (campaign, belief, moment), and a supported conclusion distinguishing necessary from decisive causes. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Theodore Roosevelt was the most successful Progressive president.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: the coal strike (1902) made the President an arbitrator; Northern Securities (1904); Hepburn Act and Elkins Act; the 1906 food and meat laws; 230 million acres of conservation; he changed what a President could do (stewardship theory).
- Against 1, Wilson: Underwood–Simmons Act with the income tax, the Federal Reserve, the FTC and the Clayton Act, Adamson and Keating–Owen; the legislation lasted longer.
- Against 2, Taft: about 90 suits to Roosevelt's 44, the Standard Oil judgement, the Department of Labor.
- Against 3, Roosevelt's own limits: US Steel in 1907, "good trusts", little on race (Brownsville), few permanent institutions.
- Judgement: Roosevelt changed the office and began reform; Wilson wrote the laws. Criterion: lasting institutions and change in the role of the Federal Government.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Theodore Roosevelt was the most successful Progressive president. He settled the coal strike of 1902 by setting up a commission, and the miners won a 10 per cent rise and a nine-hour day. He brought the Northern Securities case, which the Supreme Court decided in 1904 by ordering the railroad holding company to be dissolved. In 1906 he signed the Hepburn Act, the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act. He also protected 230 million acres of land. So he did more than any other president."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided; the last sentence asserts. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with Wilson and Taft merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet in terms of laws passed and institutions created, Wilson has the stronger claim. In 1913–14 his administration put through the Underwood–Simmons Act, which introduced the first regular federal income tax under the 16th Amendment, the Federal Reserve Act, the Federal Trade Commission Act and the Clayton Act, followed in 1916 by the Adamson Act and the Keating–Owen Act. The Federal Reserve and the income tax were not just regulations, they changed the structure of the economy, and both still exist. Roosevelt's greatest successes, by contrast, often rested on his own decisions, which his successors could reverse, and some were symbolic. Northern Securities did not stop the merger movement, and in 1907 he let U.S. Steel buy the Tennessee Coal and Iron Company, which strengthened a monopoly. Taft also deserves credit: his Justice Department brought about 90 suits against Roosevelt's 44, and the Standard Oil judgement of 1911 was the largest break-up of a company in the period, though it came with the 'rule of reason'.

However, a president's success should also be measured by what he made possible. Roosevelt turned the presidency from the office of an administrator into one of leadership. By mediating in the coal strike, he made the Federal Government an arbitrator between capital and labour and not an ally of employers. By using the Bureau of Corporations, the Antiquities Act and the Forest Service he showed that the executive could act without Congress, and he made the Progressive agenda national through the 1904 Square Deal campaign and the 1910 New Nationalism speech. Wilson's laws of 1913–14 depended on this change in what Americans thought possible. Wilson himself moved in 1916 to Roosevelt's idea of an active state, passing laws such as Keating–Owen that his New Freedom had rejected.

Overall, Wilson was the most successful Progressive president in terms of the laws and institutions that lasted, while Roosevelt was the most successful in changing the presidency and winning public support for reform. Since the question asks about success and not influence, and since the lasting changes of the era (the income tax and the Federal Reserve) came under Wilson, the statement is only partly true. Roosevelt was the most important Progressive president, but not the most successful."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (lasting institutions) and distinguishes "most important" from "most successful". With an opening paragraph on the case for Roosevelt, this is Level 5 (18–20).
:::

Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is by Roosevelt, who says he wants the rules changed so there is more equality of opportunity. Source B is by Wilson, who says that his work is a work of restoration. So Roosevelt wants change and Wilson wants to restore things. Both were Presidents, so they are reliable."

*Commentary.* One difference, loosely supported. No similarity. The claim about reliability is generic and wrong in fact: Roosevelt was a former President in 1910. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources have common ground. Both speak for a government that must act to change the economy. Roosevelt in Source A wants the 'rules changed', and Wilson in Source B says that the 'economic system' will be 'modified'. Both also appeal to the ordinary honest citizen against special interests: Roosevelt puts 'the national need' before 'sectional or personal advantage', and Wilson summons 'all honest men'. Both claim fairness as their goal, Roosevelt through 'a more substantial equality of opportunity' and Wilson through 'restoration'.

They differ in tone and in how far they are prepared to go. Roosevelt in Source A is bold: the 'rules of the game' themselves are to be changed, and he calls his programme the 'New Nationalism', a national policy with a strong central government. Wilson in Source B is more cautious: he will work with the economic system 'as it is' and will modify it, and his work is 'restoration', as if returning to a healthier past and not building something new. Roosevelt asks for a new kind of government; Wilson speaks as if he were repairing an old one.

The context explains the difference. Roosevelt spoke at Osawatomie in 1910 as a former President who had just returned from abroad and was in open conflict with Taft, whom he thought had betrayed Progressive causes. The speech was given at a park dedicated to John Brown, and he wanted to make a radical impression on a Progressive audience and to prepare his return to politics, which is why he chooses strong words. Wilson spoke in March 1913 as a new President elected by only 41.8 per cent of the popular vote, with a divided opposition, who needed to reassure business and Southern Democrats while promising reform. An inaugural address is addressed to the whole nation, which encourages moderation. The sources should not be taken as the full measure of their authors' policies: Wilson's actual programme of 1913–14 (the income tax, the Federal Reserve and the FTC) was in practice as ambitious as anything Roosevelt proposed, so Source B is better evidence of the rhetoric a new President chose than of what he did."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (a former President campaigning; a new minority President) and each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. **Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (a permanent power for the Federal Government to tax incomes); B (a permanent extension of the vote); D (documents a problem the movement set out to change, at its start). Challenge or qualify: C (the Court struck down the national child labour law, so one major reform did not last); D (a photograph from 1910 shows the problem and not the cure); A and B (they state powers and rights, not what was done with them).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A and B**: texts of constitutional amendments, the most authoritative form of evidence for what changed in law, and neither was ever repealed. But they are evidence of rights and powers and not of effects. A tax of 1 per cent on incomes above $3,000 in 1913 touched fewer than 400,000 people; B did not stop the Southern states from keeping Black women from voting.
- **C**: a headnote, which is a summary written by a court official and not the Court's own words, but it states the majority's reasoning accurately. It is strong evidence that a major reform was struck down, by 5 votes to 4, which shows the closeness of the decision. It is not evidence that all of the Court's rulings went against reform (it upheld the Adamson Act and *Muller*).
- **D**: a photograph made by Hine for a pressure group, the National Child Labor Committee, to persuade the public; its subject was chosen, and one image cannot show how typical the girl was. But Hine's photographs were widely used by reformers and the census confirms that about 18 per cent of children aged 10 to 15 were working in 1900. It dates from 1910, so it shows the problem before the federal law of 1916 and is no evidence about how far things then changed.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A and B strongly support the view that the movement brought lasting change: the 16th and 19th Amendments are still part of the Constitution, and the first gave the Federal Government a means of raising revenue that grew with the economy, while the second gave women the vote. Source C qualifies this. In *Hammer v. *Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and context; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify, and uses own knowledge (the Federal Reserve, the 18th Amendment, the census figures); the judgement answers "how far" directly. :::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How successful was the Progressive Movement in improving living and working conditions?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Progressive Movement was very successful in improving living and working conditions. In 1906 Congress passed the Pure Food and Drug Act and the Meat Inspection Act. In 1911 Wisconsin passed workers' compensation, and in 1916 Congress passed the Keating–Owen Act to stop child labour and the Adamson Act for an eight-hour day on the railroads. New York passed the Tenement House Act in 1901. The Children's Bureau was set up in 1912. So the Progressives did a lot to improve living and working conditions, and the movement was very successful."

**The level.** The facts are accurate and well chosen, but the paragraph is **a list of laws**: it equates passing a law with improving conditions, and it never tests whether any law worked or compares success with failure. The conclusion repeats the question. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use a test of success, and bring in the counter-evidence. "The movement was successful at passing laws on conditions, but only partly at improving them. The best evidence of real improvement is child labour, which fell from about 18 per cent of children aged 10 to 15 in 1900 to about 8 per cent in 1920, and the creation of workers' compensation in most states by 1920. But the Keating–Owen Act was struck down in 1918, the tenement law of 1901 applied only to new buildings, and the food laws covered only goods that crossed state lines. Success was therefore greatest where state governments acted and the courts accepted it, and least where reform depended on national action." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement about how far conditions improved.

Q: What was Roosevelt's "Square Deal", and which event first made it famous?
A: A fair chance for everyone, with the government as an umpire between labour and capital, not the servant of either. It was first used in the anthracite coal strike of 1902, when he set up a commission instead of sending troops against the miners; the commission gave a 10 per cent rise and a nine-hour day, but no recognition of the union.
Q: Name three pieces of railroad regulation between 1903 and 1916 and say what each did.
A: Elkins Act (1903): banned rebates. Hepburn Act (1906): the Interstate Commerce Commission could set maximum rates. Mann–Elkins Act (1910): extended its power to telephones and telegraph and let it suspend rate rises. (Also the Adamson Act, 1916: an eight-hour day for railroad workers.)
Q: What did the Supreme Court decide in the Standard Oil case of 1911, and what was the "rule of reason"?
A: It ordered Standard Oil broken up into more than thirty companies. In doing so, it ruled that the Sherman Act forbade only "unreasonable" restraints of trade, so size alone was not illegal. This made later anti-trust cases harder.
Q: Give two reasons why Progressive Republicans lost faith in Taft.
A: He signed the Payne–Aldrich Tariff (1909), which did not cut duties as promised, and called it the best the Republican party had passed. He dismissed Gifford Pinchot (January 1910) in the Ballinger–Pinchot affair, which seemed to abandon conservation. (Also his support for Speaker Cannon and the U.S. Steel suit.)
Q: What were the New Nationalism and the New Freedom?
A: Roosevelt's programme (1910–12): a strong national government to supervise big business. Wilson's programme (1912): break up monopolies and restore competition. In office, Wilson's laws of 1913–16 moved closer to Roosevelt's approach.
Q: What did the Federal Reserve Act (1913) create?
A: A central banking system of twelve regional Federal Reserve banks, supervised by a Federal Reserve Board appointed by the President, able to issue a flexible currency and to lend to banks in a crisis.
Q: Why was the Keating–Owen Act struck down, and when?
A: In *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (3 June 1918), by 5 votes to 4, the Supreme Court held that manufacture is not commerce and that the Act invaded the States' power over local trade. It was not replaced by a federal ban until 1938.
Q: Which amendment introduced the federal income tax, and which amendment gave women the vote? Give the ratification dates.
A: The 16th Amendment (ratified 3 February 1913) and the 19th Amendment (ratified 18 August 1920).
Q: Why did the NAACP come into being in 1909?
A: The Springfield, Illinois, race riot of August 1908 and the spread of segregation, disfranchisement and lynching led white and Black reformers (including Du Bois, Wells, Ovington, Villard and Storey) to found an organisation for civil rights. Du Bois edited its magazine *The Crisis* from 1910.
Q: Give two measures that benefited farmers in this period.
A: The Smith–Lever Act (1914), which paid for county agents and farm demonstration; the Federal Farm Loan Act (1916), which set up land banks; the Federal Aid Road Act (1916); Rural Free Delivery and parcel post (1913).

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the Federal Reserve Act was passed in 1913.
A: Indicative content: the repeated panics (1873, 1893 and especially 1907) showed that the United States lacked a central bank or a lender of last resort; J. P. Morgan's rescue of 1907 showed how much power lay with one private banker; the National Monetary Commission (1908) and the Pujo Committee (1912–13) argued that credit was controlled by a "money trust"; the election of Wilson and a Democratic Congress in 1912 gave a President who wanted to reform banking; the Act was a compromise between bankers who wanted a private system and Progressives who wanted public control. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects the economic cause (panics) with the political one (distrust of concentrated financial power) and judges which was the more important.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the 19th Amendment was ratified in 1920 and not earlier.
A: Indicative content: the campaign needed more than 40 years to win a majority; between 1896 and 1910 no new state granted the vote; opposition from Southern politicians who feared Black women's votes, brewers who feared they would vote dry, and machine politicians; NAWSA's cautious state-by-state strategy was slow until Catt's 1916 plan; Alice Paul's militancy (1913 parade; pickets from January 1917) increased pressure; the First World War made women's war work visible; Wilson changed his stand in January 1918; the amendment passed Congress only in June 1919 after the 1918 elections, and ratification took 14 months. A Level 4 answer links the delay (opposition, strategy) with the timing (the war and Wilson) and judges the decisive factor.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Taft lost the support of many Progressives between 1909 and 1912.
A: Indicative content: the Payne–Aldrich Tariff (1909) and his praise of it; the Ballinger–Pinchot affair and the dismissal of Pinchot (January 1910); his support for Speaker Cannon until the revolt of 1910; the suit against U.S. Steel (1911), which attacked Roosevelt's decision of 1907; a legal temperament that preferred the courts to the "bully pulpit"; Roosevelt's return and his speech at Osawatomie (1910). A Level 4 answer notes that Taft's record on anti-trust suits was in fact stronger than Roosevelt's, so the loss of support was about trust and style as much as policy, and judges the main reason.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Progressive Movement did little for African Americans.' How far do you agree?
A: For: segregation and disfranchisement spread (Plessy 1896; Southern constitutions 1890–1908); lynching and riots (Springfield 1908, East St Louis 1917, 1919); Roosevelt's Brownsville decision (1906); the Progressive Party's "lily-white" policy (1912: it refused to seat Black delegates from some Southern states); Wilson's segregation of federal offices (1913); many Southern Progressives supported disfranchisement as a reform. Against: the NAACP (1909) and *The Crisis* (1910) became permanent institutions; court wins (*Guinn* 1915, *Buchanan* 1917); Progressive organisations such as the National Association of Colored Women worked for Black communities; settlement and educational work; the Great Migration offered more freedom and better wages. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that the movement was a failure for African Americans as a national policy, but that Black Americans created their own reform institutions, and distinguishes between what white Progressives did and what Black reformers did.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Woodrow Wilson did more than Theodore Roosevelt to increase the power of the Federal Government.' How far do you agree?

H: List Wilson's laws of 1913–16 and Roosevelt's actions of 1901–09, and ask which lasted.
A: For Wilson: the income tax (1913) and large revenue after 1916; the Federal Reserve; the FTC and the Clayton Act; the Adamson Act (the first federal law fixing hours for private employees); the Federal Farm Loan Act; the National Park Service; war powers in 1917–18 (railroads, food, industry). For Roosevelt: he made the President a leader of policy (stewardship theory), settled the coal strike, extended the Interstate Commerce Commission's power (Hepburn Act), began Federal conservation (Forest Service, 230 million acres), created the Bureau of Corporations, the food and meat laws. Challenge: Roosevelt's actions often relied on executive discretion and could be reversed; Wilson's were statutes. Level 5 judges, e.g., that Wilson did more in law and institutions, but Roosevelt made it possible by changing what Americans expected of the presidency.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about how African Americans should respond to racial inequality.
A: Similarities: both speak of the position of Black Americans in a society where race prejudice is strong; both see a role for publicity and argument (Washington's speech to a mixed audience, Du Bois's magazine); neither calls for violence. Differences: Washington accepts social separation ("as separate as the fingers") and stresses cooperation ("one as the hand") on economic matters; Du Bois sets out to expose "the danger of race prejudice" and to campaign against it. Washington's focus is on mutual progress; Du Bois's is on a danger. Explanation: Washington spoke in 1895 at the Atlanta Exposition to a white and Black audience, in the year before *Plessy*, and needed to reassure white Southerners whose support Tuskegee depended on; Du Bois wrote fifteen years later as the editor of the organisation formed after the Springfield riot of 1908, writing for a Black and liberal white readership, after the Niagara Movement (1905) had rejected Washington's approach. Level 4 explains the difference through purpose, audience and the years between, and judges each source's value.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources suggest that the reforms of the Progressive Era benefited all Americans equally?
H: Which sources show a reform benefiting a group (C? D?) and which show a group that was left out (A, B)?
H: Use your own knowledge: who benefited from the 18th and 19th Amendments, who was excluded, and what was Southern practice?
H: Evaluate each source: C is a legal text; D is a President's speech with a purpose; A is a pressure group's founding statement.
A: Support for equal benefit: C (a law applying to all in its terms); D (the argument for women to share "privilege and of right"). Challenge: A (a major group argues that prejudice endangers it); B (Washington accepts a separate position); the 19th Amendment's wording was equal, but Southern states kept Black women from voting; Prohibition's costs fell most on the immigrant working class and city-dwellers who drank; Keating–Owen was struck down. Evaluation: C is the text of the amendment and says nothing of effects; D is a persuasive speech made to win votes in the Senate and to serve the war effort; A is the statement of an organisation that exists to campaign against inequality, so it stresses prejudice; B is a speech made to reassure an audience, and Washington himself worked privately against some segregation. Level 5 judges, e.g., that the reforms did not benefit all equally: they were passed by majorities and tended to benefit white, urban and middle-class Americans most, and the sources suggest that groups such as African Americans were left out or had to wait.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent were pressure groups the main reason for the success of the Progressive Movement?
H: List the groups and what each won (ASL, NAWSA, NCLC, NAACP, AFL, conservationists).
H: Compare with Presidents, courts and wartime conditions as reasons for success.
A: For: the ASL won the 18th Amendment; NAWSA and the NWP won the 19th; the NCLC won child labour laws and the Keating–Owen Act; the National Consumers' League won the *Muller* decision; the AFL won the Clayton Act; conservationists won national parks; the pressure groups supplied evidence, organisation and votes. Against: Presidents made policy (Roosevelt's Square Deal, Wilson's programme of 1913–14); the courts decided what survived; the muckrakers prepared opinion; the First World War produced the 18th and 19th Amendments' final passage; the 16th and 17th Amendments owed as much to politicians' calculations and the Populists' earlier campaign. Level 5 judges, e.g., that pressure groups were the main reason for reforms in which a single cause won public support, but that they succeeded only when a President, a crisis or a war gave them a way through.

Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Michael McGerr argued in *A Fierce Discontent* (2003) that the Progressive Movement collapsed because its supporters tried to control too much of people's lives. How far does the evidence of the years 1917–20 support this view?
H: Which Progressive measures after 1917 were about controlling behaviour (Prohibition, immigration restriction, wartime controls)?
H: What other explanations are there for the end of the movement: the war, the Red Scare, success, division?
A: Support: the 18th Amendment, the Volstead Act and the literacy test (1917) were attempts to remake behaviour and the people; the Espionage and Sedition Acts and wartime propaganda ended the movement's tolerance of dissent; Prohibition split the coalition between "dry" rural and "wet" urban Progressives, and the Red Scare associated reform with radicalism. Challenge: the movement had also achieved many of its goals (the amendments, the Federal Reserve, the FTC), so fewer remained; the war diverted energy and money; the economic boom and the Republican victory of 1920 changed the mood; Progressive ideas returned in the New Deal. Q: [25 marks] ★★★ How far was the First World War responsible for the success of the 18th and 19th Amendments, and for the end of the Progressive Era?

A: The 18th: the Lever Act, anti-German feeling and the patriotic need for grain made prohibition easier; the amendment passed within a year of the war's start, and ratification took thirteen months. But the Anti-Saloon League had built a majority in the states and in Congress before 1917. A* judgement: the war was an accelerator of the final reforms and a cause of the movement's decline, but the long-term causes of both lay in the movement's own history.

- **Regulation:** railroads (Elkins, Hepburn, Mann–Elkins); food and drugs (1906, with the Sherley and Gould amendments); conservation (Reclamation Act 1902, Forest Service 1905, Antiquities Act 1906, Park Service 1916); working and living conditions (state laws, Tenement Act 1901, Children's Bureau 1912, Department of Labor 1913); banks and trusts; anti-corruption (Tillman Act 1907, Publicity Act 1910).
- **Amendments (ratified):** 16th, 3 February 1913 (income tax); 17th, 8 April 1913 (Senators); 18th, 16 January 1919 (Prohibition, in force 17 January 1920); 19th, 18 August 1920 (votes for women).
- **Pressure groups and women:** the Anti-Saloon League; NAWSA (Catt) and the National Woman's Party (Paul); the NCLC; the National Consumers' League; the NAACP; women in government (Lathrop, Kelley, Rankin).
- **Remaining problems:** a patchwork of laws with weak enforcement and court defeats (*Hammer v. Dagenhart*); segregation and disfranchisement; gender inequality; rural poverty and rising tenancy.
- **Judgement:** the movement changed what Americans expected of government more than it solved the problems it named, and it did so unevenly.
