## What the Progressive Movement was

Historians use "progressivism" for a wave of reform that ran from the 1890s to about 1920. It had no founder, no single organisation and no single programme. That is the first thing an examiner wants you to know.

:::def Progressive Movement
The wide range of reform efforts, between the 1890s and the First World War, by which Americans in cities, states and the Federal Government tried to deal with the problems of industrial society: corruption, poverty, dangerous work, the power of trusts, and the exclusion of women from public life. It was many movements, not one party.
:::

:::def Progressive Party
The political party formed in August 1912 to run Theodore Roosevelt for President. It was nicknamed the Bull Moose Party after Roosevelt's remark that he felt "as strong as a bull moose". It was only one part of progressivism: most Progressives belonged to the Republican and Democratic parties, and some to the Socialist Party.
:::

Four political leaders appear in this chapter. **Theodore Roosevelt**, a Republican, was Vice-President when President McKinley was shot (6 September 1901; he died on 14 September), and so became President; he served until March 1909. **William Howard Taft**, also a Republican, was his chosen successor and President from 1909 to 1913. **Woodrow Wilson**, a Democrat, was President from 1913 to 1921; the programme on which he won in 1912 was called the New Freedom (explained in [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4)). **Eugene Debs** was the leader and presidential candidate of the Socialist Party, which wanted the economy owned in common and not by private owners.

Three ideas were common to most Progressives, even when they disagreed about everything else.
- **Industrial society had created problems that old rules could not solve.** Slums, adulterated food, child labour and trusts were not the fault of individuals who had failed. They needed new laws.
- **Government should act.** In the Gilded Age most Americans, courts included, believed in *laissez-faire* (see [Gilded Age (1)](#hist.us.gilded.1)). Progressives wanted state and Federal governments to regulate business, inspect factories and protect the weak. But they also wanted to make government honest and democratic first, so that it could be trusted with that power.
- **Facts and experts would lead to better answers.** They gathered statistics, ran surveys and set up commissions staffed by trained people: economists, engineers, doctors, social workers. Publicity, they believed, was the great remedy.

:::warn Progressivism was not a party, and "Progressive" was not always "progressive"
Do not write "the Progressive Party passed the Pure Food and Drug Act". It was passed in 1906 by a Republican President and a Republican Congress, six years before the Progressive Party existed. Nor were all Progressive causes liberal by modern standards: some Progressives backed prohibition, immigration restriction and the exclusion of Black voters in the South as "reforms". Write "Progressives" or "the Progressive Movement" for the whole wave, and "the Progressive Party of 1912" for the party.
:::

### The work of the muckrakers

In the 1890s American magazines changed. New monthly magazines such as *McClure's*, *Everybody's*, *Cosmopolitan* and *Collier's* sold at 10 or 15 cents, where older literary magazines had cost 35 cents, and they paid for themselves with the advertising that a mass readership attracts. They needed stories that readers would talk about. Some editors found that the best story was how the country was actually run.

:::def Muckrakers
Investigative journalists and writers, mostly between about 1902 and 1912, who exposed corruption, business abuses and social conditions in magazines and books. Theodore Roosevelt gave them the name in a speech of 14 April 1906, taking it from a character in John Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress*.
:::

The founding moment is a single issue. In January 1903, *McClure's Magazine* printed three articles that, without any plan, told the same story: **Ida Tarbell** on how the Standard Oil Company had destroyed its competitors (part of a series that began in November 1902 and became *The History of the Standard Oil Company* in 1904); **Lincoln Steffens** on the corruption of the city government of Minneapolis (the second of his series on American cities, which began in October 1902 with St Louis); and **Ray Stannard Baker** on the coal strike and the violence of both sides. The editor, S. S. McClure, told readers that the coincidence showed how much lawlessness there was across the country: business men, workers, politicians and ordinary citizens were all breaking the law.

| Muckraker | Best-known work | What it exposed |
|---|---|---|
| Ida Tarbell | *The History of the Standard Oil Company* (serialised in *McClure's* from 1902; book 1904) | How Rockefeller used rebates and secret deals with railroads to destroy rivals |
| Lincoln Steffens | *The Shame of the Cities* (articles in *McClure's* from 1902; book 1904) | Bribery in city governments: St Louis, Minneapolis, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, Chicago, New York |
| Ray Stannard Baker | *Following the Color Line* (1908) | Racial segregation and violence in the North and the South |
| David Graham Phillips | "The Treason of the Senate" (*Cosmopolitan*, 1906) | Senators who he said served the railroads and trusts, not the voters |
| Samuel Hopkins Adams | "The Great American Fraud" (*Collier's*, from 1905) | Patent medicines containing alcohol and drugs, sold with false claims |
| Upton Sinclair | *The Jungle* (1906) | Filth and danger in the Chicago meatpacking industry, and the lives of its immigrant workers |
| Jacob Riis (earlier) | *How the Other Half Lives* (1890) | The tenements of New York (see [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2)) |

What made their work powerful was the method. Tarbell, whose father had been an oil producer pushed aside by Standard Oil's deals with the railroads, spent about two years in documents, court records and interviews, and she let the facts speak. Steffens travelled to the cities and found the bribers as well as the bribed. His conclusion was uncomfortable: the guilty were not just the bosses. In the introduction to *The Shame of the Cities* (1904) he wrote that "the people are not innocent", and that "the typical business man is a bad citizen; he is busy". Respectable business men paid the bribes and enjoyed the franchises that the bribes bought; ordinary voters sold their votes or did not vote. For Steffens the cure was an honest and active citizenry, which is why his articles were read as a call for reform and not only as scandal.

**The Jungle** shows both the strength and the limits of muckraking. Upton Sinclair, a Socialist, spent about seven weeks in Chicago's Packingtown in 1904 and wrote a novel about a Lithuanian immigrant family ruined by the stockyards. He wanted to show the exploitation of workers. Readers in 1906 noticed instead what the book said about the food. Sinclair is reported to have said later that he "aimed at the public's heart, and by accident" hit it "in the stomach". President Roosevelt, who was suspicious of Sinclair's politics, sent two investigators, Charles Neill and James Reynolds, to Chicago. Their report confirmed that conditions were filthy. On 30 June 1906 Roosevelt signed the **Meat Inspection Act** and the **Pure Food and Drug Act**. The workers' conditions, the heart of the book, changed far less.

**Roosevelt's response.** On 14 April 1906 Roosevelt used the laying of the cornerstone of a new House of Representatives office building to speak about journalists. He recalled the man in *Pilgrim's Progress* "who could look no way but downward, with the muck-rake in his hand". He agreed that "there is filth on the floor, and it must be scraped up with the muck-rake", and that the men with the muck-rakes were "often indispensable to the well-being of society", but only if they knew "when to stop raking the muck". He warned that "hysterical sensationalism is the very poorest weapon wherewith to fight for lasting righteousness". Roosevelt feared that attacks on every business man would feed the Socialists. The name stuck, though the writers themselves disliked it. By 1912 the golden age was over: advertisers and bankers put pressure on editors, readers tired of exposures, and the leading writers had moved on.

:::key Why the muckrakers mattered, and the limits of their role
1. **They gave reformers facts and a mass audience.** Magazines reached millions of middle-class readers in every state at 10 or 15 cents.
2. **They shifted opinion.** The idea that business and politics were corrupt, once Populist or Socialist, became respectable and middle-class.
3. **They fed particular laws:** the Meat Inspection and Pure Food and Drug Acts (1906), insurance regulation, and the case against the Senate that led to direct election.
4. **But they were not the whole cause.** They exposed problems that others had already described (Riis in 1890, the Populists in 1892). Reform needed organisers, politicians and laws, and they did not supply those. The Meat Inspection Act also passed because the big packers wanted foreign buyers to trust American meat.
:::

### Temperance

:::def Temperance movement
The campaign, led mainly by Protestant women and churches, to persuade or force Americans to drink less alcohol or none, which developed into a demand for prohibition by law.
:::

Temperance was the oldest reform cause in the story, and the one that taught reformers how to organise. The **Woman's Christian Temperance Union (WCTU)** was founded at Cleveland in November 1874. From 1879 its president was **Frances Willard**, who widened its aim with the motto "Do Everything": the WCTU campaigned not only against alcohol but also for the vote for women, for kindergartens, for prison reform and for laws on child labour. Willard argued that a woman who wanted to protect her home needed the ballot to do it. By the 1890s the WCTU was one of the largest women's organisations in the country, with well over 100,000 members.

The **Anti-Saloon League (ASL)** was a different kind of body. Founded at Oberlin, Ohio, in 1893 and organised nationally in 1895, it was a **pressure group** with a single aim. It worked through Protestant churches, put money and organisers into campaigns and backed whichever candidate, of whichever party, promised to vote for laws against drink. Its lobbyist **Wayne Wheeler** later became famous for the skill with which the League rewarded friends and punished enemies. This method, a single-issue pressure group that works across party lines, became the model for later Progressive causes.

Why was drink a political question, and not only a moral one?
- **The saloon was a political institution.** In immigrant wards the saloon was a social club, an employment office and a place to cash a paycheck; it was also where machine politicians and ward bosses met voters, and where votes were bought. To many reformers the saloon and the machine were one problem.
- **Drink was blamed for poverty and violence in the family.** Wives had few legal rights over their husbands' wages, and domestic violence was less protected by the law than it is today. Temperance gave women a respectable reason to enter public life.
- **Brewers' money in politics.** The brewers and distillers were rich and organised, and gave money to candidates, which turned the issue into one of "the interests" against the people.
- **Religion and nativism.** Prohibition was backed mostly by Protestants in the countryside and small towns and opposed by many Catholic and Jewish immigrants and many Germans. Some temperance supporters saw drink as an immigrant vice, which mixed a moral cause with prejudice.

By 1917 more than twenty states had gone dry by their own laws, before the national amendment. Temperance mattered to Progressivism because it trained a generation of women and church leaders in lobbying, because it linked home and politics ("home protection") and because it shows the Progressives' belief that law could improve people's behaviour. It also shows a limit: this was the cause that other Progressives, especially in the cities, most disliked. [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4) explains how it ended in the 18th Amendment.

### Banking "panics" and the concentration of wealth

The syllabus names the frequency of banking "panics". The word is the period's own.

:::def Panic
A sudden financial crisis in which depositors and investors rush to withdraw their money or sell their shares, banks and businesses fail and credit dries up. The United States had panics in 1873, 1884, 1893 and 1907.
:::

The United States in these years had no central bank to lend to failing banks. (A central bank is a public bank that holds the other banks' reserves and lends them cash in a crisis; it is the "lender of last resort".) Gold-backed currency, thousands of small banks and the habit of banks keeping their reserves in New York banks made each panic spread quickly. [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2) described the Panics of 1873 and 1893. The one that mattered most for Progressivism came in October 1907. The Knickerbocker Trust Company of New York failed on 22 October after a run, share prices collapsed, and for a few weeks it seemed that the whole financial system might fail. There was no public body strong enough to stop the panic. **J. P. Morgan**, the most powerful banker in the country, organised the rescue himself, gathering bankers in his library, with some Treasury money to help. The lesson was plain to many: a private banker had acted as the country's central bank, and the country had been lucky that he was willing. A system that depended on one man was no system. Congress passed the Aldrich–Vreeland Act in 1908 as a stopgap and set up a National Monetary Commission to study the problem, which led to the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 (see [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4)).

The second part of this cause is wealth. The merger wave of 1897–1904, which produced U.S. Steel in 1901 (see [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2)), put much of American industry in the hands of a few corporations and the bankers who financed them. In 1896 the economist **Charles B. Spahr** estimated, from probate records, that the richest 1 per cent of families owned more than the other 99 per cent together. His method was rough and his figure should be treated as a sign of how the problem was understood, not as an exact measure, but it was widely quoted. In 1912–13 a House committee chaired by Representative **Arsène Pujo** of Louisiana investigated the "**money trust**". It reported that a small group of bankers around J. P. Morgan held about 340 directorships in over a hundred companies. The lawyer **Louis Brandeis** used the findings in a series of articles collected as *Other People's Money and How the Bankers Use It* (1914).

:::def Money trust
The name used in 1912–13 for the small group of New York bankers and financiers, led by J. P. Morgan, who the Pujo Committee said controlled credit and many large companies through shared directorships.
:::

Why did this make people reform rather than revolt? Because it was an argument that Americans of all classes understood. Farmers had said it in 1892 and small businessmen said it in 1912: concentrated economic power threatened competition and democracy together. The Progressives' answer was not to abolish business, but to regulate it and to restore competition. That is why this cause leads straight to the regulation of banks and trusts, later in this chapter.

### Rapid and uncontrolled urbanisation, and endemic corruption

The scale of change was enormous. In 1870 about 26 per cent of Americans lived in towns and cities; by 1920 the census showed that more than half did.

The chart shows steady growth, with the 1920 census recording an urban majority for the first time. It does not show the speed of change in particular places or the conditions inside the cities, which [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2) describes (tenements, disease, child workers). Cities were growing faster than their governments could provide water, sewers, police, schools and housing, and the services were often supplied through contracts and franchises. A franchise (a licence from the city) to build streetcars, supply gas or lay pipes could be worth millions, and the councillors who granted it could be bribed.

This is the "endemic corruption" of the syllabus: not an occasional scandal, but a system. The **political machines** (see [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2)) of Tammany Hall in New York and similar organisations in other cities won the votes of immigrants and used city jobs and contracts to reward their own. Steffens' articles showed that the system was not limited to New York. In St Louis the "boodle" scandal came to light when the circuit attorney, **Joseph Folk**, prosecuted councillors who had taken bribes to sell city franchises; Folk was later elected Governor of Missouri. In Minneapolis the mayor's administration sold protection to gamblers and criminals. In Philadelphia a Republican machine controlled the city so completely that Steffens called the city "corrupt and contented". Corruption was not limited to cities. State legislatures chose United States Senators, and critics said that railroads and trusts had bought many of the legislatures that did so. Phillips' articles on the "treason" of the Senate (1906) made that charge to a national audience.

The Progressives' phrase for it was the "**invisible government**": the alliance between corrupt business and corrupt politics that, the Progressive Party platform of 1912 said, had to be destroyed. This is why so many of their first reforms were about politics rather than economics. If voters could not control the legislature, no economic law would be passed or enforced. Reformers called the opening of government to the voters "the key to all other reforms".

### The demand by women's groups for radical social change, and their participation in society and government

Women supplied much of the energy of the movement, and they did so through organisations that middle-class women had built since the Civil War.

:::def Settlement house
A community centre in a poor, usually immigrant, urban district, where educated, mostly middle-class, volunteers lived and offered clubs, classes, nurseries, health care and advice, and investigated and campaigned on local conditions.
:::

The most famous is **Hull-House** in Chicago. **Jane Addams** and her friend **Ellen Gates Starr** opened it in September 1889 in an old mansion in a district of Italian, Irish, Greek, Jewish and Bohemian immigrants. They began with a kindergarten and clubs, and went on to a day nursery, a gymnasium, public baths, a playground, a labour museum and a cooperative residence for working girls. Addams said that a settlement's motive was "to make social intercourse express the growing sense of the economic unity of society", and she wrote that one of the forces behind it was "the desire to interpret democracy in social terms" (*Twenty Years at Hull-House*, 1910). The settlement was also a research station and a lobbying centre. The women of Hull-House mapped the neighbourhood's wages and nationalities (*Hull-House Maps and Papers*, 1895), and Addams herself served as the ward's garbage inspector in 1895, because the rubbish of the district was a cause of disease. **Florence Kelley**, a resident, became the chief factory inspector of Illinois under a state law of 1893 that limited women's hours and banned work under the age of fourteen. **Alice Hamilton** studied industrial poisons, and **Julia Lathrop** became the first head of the federal Children's Bureau in 1912. By 1910 there were roughly 400 settlements in American cities, including **Lillian Wald's** Henry Street Settlement in New York, founded in 1893, which began a visiting nurse service.

Women's organisations multiplied beyond the settlements.
- The **General Federation of Women's Clubs** (1890) joined thousands of local clubs of middle-class women, who started by reading literature and ended by campaigning for pure food, clean streets, libraries, playgrounds and child-labour laws.
- The **National Association of Colored Women** (1896), with Mary Church Terrell as first president and the motto "Lifting as we climb", did the same for Black communities, and had to do so separately, because white clubs often excluded Black women.
- The **National Consumers' League** (1899), with Florence Kelley as its secretary, asked shoppers to buy only from firms with decent conditions (the "white label"), and in 1908 gave Brandeis the research for his argument in *Muller v. Oregon*.
- The **Women's Trade Union League** (1903) joined working women and middle-class "allies" to organise women workers and support their strikes, such as the garment workers' strike of 1909–10 in New York.
- The **National American Woman Suffrage Association** (1890) campaigned for the vote.

The common idea was "**municipal housekeeping**": the city was an enlarged home, and women, who had always been responsible for health, food, children and cleanliness, had a right and a duty to take part in running it.

:::def Municipal housekeeping
The idea, common among Progressive women, that the work women did in the home (health, food, cleanliness, the care of children) should be extended to the city and the state, so that women had a duty, and therefore a right, to take part in public life.
:::

Women's groups wanted more than charity. They demanded **radical social change** in the laws governing work, children and housing, and in who could vote. Their argument for the vote shifted. The older argument was one of justice: women were citizens. The newer Progressive argument was one of use: women's votes would clean up politics and protect homes. Both were used, and the second was more persuasive to men who feared radicalism. The limits of the movement are also clear: most of its leaders were white, educated and middle-class, and relations with immigrant women and Black women were often unequal, though Addams worked with Black reformers such as Ida B. Wells and W. E. B. Du Bois on some causes.

:::key How women's groups helped to create the Progressive Movement
1. **Organisation:** thousands of clubs, leagues and settlements gave the movement trained, unpaid workers in every city.
2. **Evidence:** settlements gathered the facts about slums, wages and child labour that became the basis of laws.
3. **Moral authority:** "municipal housekeeping" allowed women to claim a public role without challenging the idea that their place was the home.
4. **Direct influence:** women such as Kelley and Lathrop moved from campaigning into government posts.
:::

### Other forces behind the movement

The syllabus names five reasons, but the best answers also notice three more.
- **Religion.** The **Social Gospel** movement taught that the Christian duty was to reform society and not only to save individual souls. **Washington Gladden** and **Walter Rauschenbusch**, whose *Christianity and the Social Crisis* (1907) was widely read, turned many Protestant churches into centres of reform.
- **Ideas and experts.** Universities trained economists, sociologists and lawyers who thought about solutions: Richard Ely and John R. Commons at Wisconsin, Herbert Croly, whose *The Promise of American Life* (1909) argued for a strong national government and influenced Roosevelt. The idea of efficiency, shown by Frederick Taylor's *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911), appealed to Progressives who wanted government to work like a well-run business.
- **The legacy of the Populists.** The People's Party had been defeated in 1896, but many of its demands became Progressive policies: direct election of Senators, a graduated income tax, the secret ballot and direct legislation by initiative and referendum. The farmers and workers of [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2) had asked first. The Progressives, mostly middle-class and urban, made the demands acceptable and got them enacted.

- **Richard Hofstadter**, *The Age of Reform* (1955), argued that the Progressives were mainly middle-class people, especially old-stock Protestant professionals and clergy, who felt their social standing threatened by the new rich and by immigrants. This "status anxiety" explained their moral tone.
- **Robert Wiebe**, *The Search for Order, 1877–1920* (1967), saw the Progressives as a new middle class of professionals and managers who wanted a more organised, bureaucratic society to replace the small-town world that industrialisation had dissolved.

- **Michael McGerr**, *A Fierce Discontent* (2003), argued that Progressives were united by an attempt to remake individualist America into a more cooperative, social one, and that the movement collapsed when its confidence faded after 1917.

## Reforms in cities, states, Congress, the Federal Government and the parties

The first Progressive goal was honest and democratic government. The reforms came at every level, and the order matters: most began in cities and states, which were closer to the voters and easier to change, and were copied by others. "Laboratories of democracy" is a phrase historians use for the states.

### Reform in the cities

Reformers attacked the machines in two ways. The first was to put honest and energetic mayors in office. **Hazen Pingree** in Detroit (mayor 1890–97) forced down streetcar fares and gas prices and gave unemployed families plots of land for vegetables in the depression of the 1890s. **Samuel "Golden Rule" Jones** in Toledo (mayor 1897–1904) and **Tom L. Johnson** in Cleveland (mayor 1901–09) fought the franchise-holders and backed public ownership of utilities. The second was to change the structure of city government, so that no machine could capture it.

:::def Commission government and the city manager
Two Progressive plans for city government. In commission government, begun at Galveston, Texas, in 1901, a small elected commission governs a city, each commissioner heading a department. In the city-manager plan, begun at Staunton, Virginia, in 1908, a small elected council hires a trained professional to run the city. Both replaced a large council elected by wards with a few officials elected by the whole city.
:::

Galveston adopted commission government after the hurricane of 8 September 1900 destroyed much of the city and killed thousands of people (estimates run from about 6,000 to 12,000). The old council had proved unable to rebuild, and a group of business leaders persuaded the state to let five commissioners take charge. The "Des Moines plan" of 1907 added non-partisan elections, the initiative, the referendum and the recall. **Dayton**, Ohio, adopted a city manager after a great flood in 1913. By 1914 hundreds of cities had commission governments. Other reforms included non-partisan elections (the party label was removed from the ballot), "home rule" charters that let a city write its own rules without the legislature's interference, and the municipal ownership of water, gas and streetcars.

These reforms were typical of the Progressives' faith in efficiency and expertise, and typical of their weakness. Electing officials city-wide, and not by wards, made it harder for the machines to deliver the votes of single immigrant districts, but it also reduced the share of seats that working-class and immigrant neighbourhoods could win. Many machines, including Tammany Hall, survived. Reform of cities could also be reform against the poor.

### Reform in the states: the Wisconsin Idea

:::def Initiative, referendum and recall
Three ways of "direct democracy" that Progressives added to state constitutions. The **initiative** lets voters propose a law (or constitutional amendment) by petition and vote on it. The **referendum** lets voters approve or reject a law passed by the legislature. The **recall** lets voters remove an elected official before his term ends. South Dakota adopted the initiative and referendum in 1898 and Oregon in 1902 (the recall followed in 1908). By about 1920 around twenty states had some form of them.
:::

The model state was **Wisconsin**. **Robert M. La Follette**, a Republican known as "Fighting Bob", was elected governor in 1900 and served from January 1901 to 1906, after which he sat in the Senate. His programme in the state is called the **Wisconsin Idea**:
- **Honest politics:** a statewide **direct primary** law (passed in 1903 and approved by the voters in 1904), so that party members, and not the bosses, chose the candidates; limits on lobbyists and on campaign spending.
- **Control of the railroads and taxes:** a railroad commission with the power to set rates (1905), and a tax commission to make the owners of large property pay their share.
- **Expertise:** a legislative reference library to draft bills, and a close partnership between the state government and the University of Wisconsin, whose professors served on commissions. Later laws included the first effective workers' compensation law (1911) and a state income tax (1911).

Other states had their own leaders. In Oregon, the lawyer William U'Ren campaigned for the "Oregon System" of the initiative, referendum, direct primary and recall. In California, Governor **Hiram Johnson** (1911–17) broke the hold of the Southern Pacific Railroad over the legislature and won the recall, initiative, referendum and, in October 1911, votes for women. In New York, Governor **Charles Evans Hughes** (1907–10), who had made his name as the lawyer in the insurance investigation, created Public Service Commissions to regulate utilities. Southern Progressives like Governor Hoke Smith of Georgia reformed railroad rates and schools, and their reform of elections, as you will see, was for whites only.

### Widening the franchise and reforming elections

:::def Australian ballot (secret ballot)
A ballot printed and issued by the state, marked in private, in place of the party-printed ticket that voters had handed in publicly. Massachusetts adopted it in 1888, and by the mid-1890s most states had.
:::

Reformers wanted to take elections out of the hands of party managers. The **secret ballot** cut vote-buying: a boss could no longer check that a bought voter had kept his side of the bargain. **Registration laws** made it harder to vote more than once and to use "repeaters". The **direct primary** (see below), the initiative, referendum and recall, and the direct election of Senators gave voters a say in choosing officials and making law. The **vote for women** was the largest widening of all. Wyoming had given women the vote while it was still a territory (1869); by 1913 about nine states, nearly all in the West, had done so, and the number rose quickly after 1910 (see [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4)).

But the history of the franchise (here the word means the right to vote, not the city licence met earlier) in these years has a dark side, and you must not leave it out. In the South, the same decades saw white-run state governments cut the Black vote almost to nothing. Mississippi's constitution of 1890 introduced a poll tax and a literacy test; Louisiana in 1898 added a "grandfather clause", which exempted men whose grandfathers had voted. In Louisiana the number of Black registered voters fell from over 130,000 in 1896 to about 1,300 by 1904. Many white Southern politicians called these measures "reforms" because they ended election fraud and the "corrupt" Black vote. Some Northern Progressives accepted that argument. Literacy tests and residence requirements were also used to reduce the immigrant vote in cities. So "widening the franchise" was true for white women in the West and false for Black men in the South.

### Primary elections and the direct election of Senators

Before the primary, party candidates were chosen at conventions run by party managers and bosses. A **direct primary** lets ordinary party members vote for the candidate. Wisconsin's was the first comprehensive statewide system; by 1917 nearly all states used primaries for at least some offices. In 1912 about a dozen states held presidential primaries, and Roosevelt won most of them, though the party conventions, controlled by the old guard, renominated Taft.

The **Constitution** originally gave the choice of each state's two Senators to its legislature. By 1900 many people thought that this was a recipe for corruption. Legislatures sometimes failed to agree and left a seat empty (Delaware's was vacant for several years around 1900), and rich men seemed to buy their way into the Senate. Phillips' "The Treason of the Senate" (1906) accused senators of serving the trusts. Several states found ways round the rule. Oregon in the early 1900s let voters choose the candidate and pressed the legislature to elect whoever won; by 1912 about 29 states in effect chose their Senators by popular vote. In 1912 the Senate unseated Senator William Lorimer of Illinois, whose election in 1909 had been tainted by bribery. In the same year Congress sent the **17th Amendment** to the states, and it was ratified in April 1913.

### Civil service reform

:::def Spoils system and civil service reform
The "spoils system" was the practice of giving government jobs to loyal party workers as a reward for helping to win an election ("to the victor belong the spoils"). Civil service reform was the movement to fill government posts by competitive examination and to protect the holders of those posts from being dismissed for political reasons.
:::

This was the oldest Progressive reform, begun before the word "Progressive" existed. Under the spoils system, each new President faced a stream of office-seekers, and those jobs funded the parties: officeholders were expected to pay a share of their salaries to party funds. The problem came to a head on 2 July 1881 when **Charles Guiteau**, a disappointed office-seeker who had not been given a post, shot President James Garfield at a railroad station in Washington. Garfield died on 19 September. Public anger helped the reformers to pass the **Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act** on 16 January 1883, about eighteen months after the shooting. The Act:
- created a **Civil Service Commission** of three members;
- required competitive examinations for the posts it covered (about 10 per cent of the federal service at first);
- forbade the dismissal or demand for political payments from the people in those jobs;
- let Presidents extend the list by order.

Presidents extended it steadily, and by 1900 close to half of federal civilian posts were covered. Theodore Roosevelt, a member of the Commission from 1889 to 1895, was a strong supporter. States and cities followed (New York in 1883 and Massachusetts in 1884 were first), though the highest posts remained political appointments and the machines in the large cities kept control of local jobs. Progressives saw civil service reform as the base of honest government and as an example of their belief that technical skill should count for more than party loyalty.

### Reform in Congress and the parties

Reform reached Congress and the national parties from outside. In 1909, a group of "insurgent" Republican Senators (Progressive Republicans who defied their own party's leaders), led by La Follette, voted against the Payne–Aldrich tariff (the 1909 law on import duties), which their party leaders supported. In March 1910 insurgent Republicans, led by Representative **George Norris** of Nebraska, joined the Democrats to take control of the Rules Committee away from the Speaker, **Joseph Cannon**. The Speaker is the chairman of the House of Representatives, and the Rules Committee decided which bills reached a vote; Cannon had used these powers to block progressive bills. In January 1911 La Follette and his allies formed the **National Progressive Republican League**. The movement's break with the party came in 1912, when Roosevelt, who had lost the Republican nomination at Chicago in June to Taft, left and accepted the nomination of a new party in August. The platform that the **Progressive Party** adopted in 1912 is the best summary of the movement's programme. It called for the "prohibition of child labor", "an eight hour day for women and young persons", "minimum wage standards for working women", "a system of social insurance adapted to American use", "direct primaries for the nomination of State and National officers", "the initiative, referendum and recall" and equal suffrage for men and women. Jane Addams seconded Roosevelt's nomination.

## Regulation of banking, business and industry

Honest government was the means. The end was the control of a system that, in Progressive eyes, ran for the benefit of the few. The syllabus names the recognition of unions, working conditions, and new controls over banks, insurance companies, trusts and the Stock Market. Notice that in this key question you are asked about *aims and policies*, so explain what was tried and why. [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4) asks how well it worked.

### The recognition of labor unions

:::def Recognition of unions
The acceptance by employers, courts or governments that workers have a right to organise, to bargain collectively and to strike. In 1900 neither the employers nor the courts accepted it: employers could dismiss union members, and courts often treated strikes and boycotts as conspiracies.
:::

Recognition was the most uneven Progressive aim. Union membership grew steadily: from about 0.9 million in 1900 to over 2 million in 1910 and about 5 million in 1920.

The Progressive position was mixed. Many middle-class Progressives sympathised with workers, and the Women's Trade Union League and the National Consumers' League worked with them. Others feared class conflict and wanted harmony. The American Federation of Labor under **Samuel Gompers** (see [Gilded Age (2)](#hist.us.gilded.2)) wanted a law that protected unions from the courts, and in 1906 presented a "Bill of Grievances" to Congress. The courts went the other way. In *Loewe v. Lawlor* (the Danbury Hatters' case, 1908) the Supreme Court held that a union boycott was a "combination in restraint of trade" under the Sherman Act, and the union members were fined heavily. Presidents did not take sides. The decisive moment was the anthracite coal strike of 1902, when Roosevelt, instead of sending troops against the strikers as earlier Presidents had done, set up a commission and treated the union as a party to talk to (see [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4)). The Clayton Act of 1914 declared that "the labor of a human being is not a commodity or article of commerce" and limited the use of injunctions against strikes, which Gompers called labor's "Magna Carta"; Congress also created a Department of Labor in 1913. Unions got what historians call partial recognition: legal toleration, not the right to be recognised by employers.

### Regulation of working conditions

Here the main action was in the states, and the main obstacle was the courts.

:::def Liberty of contract
The legal doctrine, used by the Supreme Court between the 1890s and the 1930s, that the 14th Amendment protected the right of employer and worker to agree terms of work freely, so that a state law fixing hours or wages could be struck down as an unconstitutional interference.
:::

Progressives used four approaches.
- **Hours.** In *Holden v. Hardy* (1898) the Supreme Court upheld an eight-hour day for miners, because mining was dangerous. In *Lochner v. New York* (17 April 1905), by 5 votes to 4, it struck down a New York law limiting bakers to ten hours a day or sixty hours a week, as a breach of liberty of contract. Justice Holmes dissented with a famous line: "The Fourteenth Amendment does not enact Mr. Herbert Spencer's Social Statics." In *Muller v. Oregon* (24 February 1908) the Court unanimously upheld a ten-hour day for women in laundries. Louis Brandeis, representing Oregon, filed a long "Brandeis brief", made up mainly of reports and statistics on the effects of long hours on women's health and only a few pages of law. The Court accepted the evidence, but its reasons were about women's special role as mothers, which allowed states to protect women while denying the same protection to men, and which many feminists later criticised. *Bunting v. Oregon* (1917) upheld a ten-hour day for men and women in factories.
- **Safety and compensation.** Factory inspection laws, safety rules for mines and railroads, and **workers' compensation**, which paid an injured worker without the need to prove the employer's fault. Wisconsin passed the first effective state law in 1911, and by 1920 most states had one.
- **Children.** The **National Child Labor Committee** (1904) campaigned for state laws and sent the photographer Lewis Hine round the mills and mines. Most northern states restricted child labour by 1914, but the laws had many exceptions.
- **Women and the poor.** Illinois passed the first "mothers' pension" law in 1911; Massachusetts passed the first minimum wage law for women in 1912, and Oregon followed.

The shock that moved legislatures most was the **Triangle Shirtwaist fire** in New York on 25 March 1911. A fire on the upper floors of the Asch Building, where the Triangle Waist Company employed about 500 mostly young immigrant women, killed 146 workers; exit doors had been locked, the fire escape collapsed and ladders could not reach the high floors. A **Factory Investigating Commission**, led by two Tammany Hall politicians, Robert Wagner and Alfred E. Smith, produced a series of laws on fire safety, hours and factory inspection in 1911–14. It is the best example of a disaster converted into reform by organised pressure.

### New controls over banks, insurance companies, trusts and the Stock Market

- **Banks.** After the Panic of 1907 the debate was about a central bank. The Aldrich–Vreeland Act (1908) allowed emergency currency and set up the National Monetary Commission. The Pujo investigation of 1912–13 convinced many that the "money trust" had to be broken, and the Federal Reserve Act of December 1913 was the result (see [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4)).
- **Insurance companies.** The life insurance companies held the savings of millions. In 1905 a committee of the New York legislature, the **Armstrong Committee**, with Charles Evans Hughes as counsel, showed that the heads of the Equitable, New York Life and Mutual had paid themselves huge salaries, used policyholders' money to speculate and to make political contributions, and kept their accounts secret. In 1906 New York passed laws limiting what insurers could spend, banning them from owning stocks and requiring open accounts. Other states followed. Hughes became governor in 1907.
- **Trusts.** The Sherman Act of 1890 was weak. Roosevelt created the Bureau of Corporations in 1903 to collect information on large companies and began a number of suits, the first of which was against the Northern Securities Company. State governments passed their own anti-trust laws. [Gilded Age (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4) covers the details.
- **The Stock Market.** In 1909 Governor Hughes appointed a committee to investigate speculation, and its report led to little action. The first successful controls were the "**blue sky laws**", so called because dishonest promoters sold shares "as worthless as so many feet of blue sky". Kansas passed the first in 1911, and by 1913 more than twenty states had similar laws. There was no federal regulation of the stock market until the 1930s, a gap which is part of the explanation for the crash of 1929.
- **Railroads.** The Hepburn Act (1906) allowed the Interstate Commerce Commission to set maximum railroad rates. States set up their own railroad commissions.

:::key The aims of the Progressives
1. **Honest government:** end bossism and bribery, through the secret ballot, primaries, direct election of Senators, city reform and civil service rules.
2. **Democratic government:** give voters direct power through the initiative, referendum and recall, and widen the vote to women.
3. **Control of business:** regulate the railroads, banks, insurers and trusts so that no few men could control the economy. Most Progressives wanted to regulate big business, not to destroy it.
4. **Protection of the weak:** laws on hours, safety, child labour, food and housing, backed by facts gathered by experts.
5. **Moral improvement:** the temperance and anti-vice causes, through law.
:::

## Why did the Progressive Movement develop? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type, then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | Rapid industrial growth and urbanisation; the concentration of wealth; the moral traditions of temperance and the Social Gospel | Created the problems and the habit of reform, but existed for decades without a national movement |
| Medium-term | Failures of the Populists and unions; panics of 1893 and 1907; corrupt city and state governments; growth of women's organisations | Showed that old methods had failed and gave reform its organisers and its issues |
| Short-term | The muckrakers (1902–10); the Meat and Food scandals (1906); the Triangle fire (1911) | Turned unease into outrage and into specific laws |
| Triggers | Panic of 1907; Roosevelt's leadership from 1901 | Gave the movement a President |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Corruption was the **common ground**: almost every group in the movement agreed that politics had to be cleaned up first. But a movement as large as this needed an **organisation** (the women's clubs, settlements and temperance leagues), a **channel** to the public (the cheap magazines and the muckrakers) and a **reason to think the government could do better** (the examples of Wisconsin and the cities). Corruption was therefore the necessary cause but not the sufficient one: it provided the grievance, while the muckrakers and women's groups provided the means of turning it into action. That is a better answer than choosing one cause.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the muckrakers were important to the Progressive Movement.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Muckrakers were journalists who wrote about corruption. Ida Tarbell wrote about Standard Oil and Lincoln Steffens wrote about the cities. Upton Sinclair wrote The Jungle about the meatpacking industry. Roosevelt called them muckrakers in 1906. They were important because they told people about problems in America."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but the answer lists names and works. "They told people about problems" is the only attempt at explaining importance and it is a general claim. No factor is developed. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The muckrakers were important first because they reached a mass audience. Magazines such as McClure's and Cosmopolitan sold at 10 or 15 cents and were read across the country, so when Steffens showed in 1902–04 that cities from St Louis to Philadelphia were run by bribery, millions of middle-class readers learned it in the same months. Before this, complaints about corruption came from Populists and Socialists whom many ignored. A second reason is that they led to specific laws. After Upton Sinclair's The Jungle in 1906 and a report by two investigators sent by Roosevelt, Congress passed the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act on 30 June 1906. Here exposure produced regulation within months."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support and tied to the question. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add a third factor and show how they work together.

"A third reason is that the muckrakers changed what respectable people believed. Steffens argued in 1904 that 'the people are not innocent': the voters and business men shared in the guilt. That made reform a duty for ordinary citizens and not only the aim of radicals. It helps to explain why the demands for direct election of Senators, the initiative and the regulation of railroads, once Populist, were taken up by the middle classes.

These reasons were linked. The magazines gave the facts a mass audience; the audience's anger gave politicians, notably Roosevelt, a reason to act; and the law gave the exposure a result. But the muckrakers did not do the work alone. Riis had exposed the tenements in 1890 with little effect, and the Meat Inspection Act also suited the big packers, who wanted foreign buyers to trust American meat. Roosevelt himself, in his 'Man with the Muck-Rake' speech of 14 April 1906, said that they were 'often indispensable to the well-being of society' but warned against 'hysterical sensationalism'. Their most important contribution was therefore to turn a widely felt unease into a public demand at a time when politicians were ready to respond."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (audience, anger, law), a limit that shows the judgement is not one-sided, and a supported conclusion. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'The Progressive Movement was a middle-class movement.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: leaders and members (Addams, Brandeis, Steffens, Tarbell, clergy of the Social Gospel); the settlement houses and women's clubs were middle-class; the reforms were expert-led (commissions, city managers, the Wisconsin professors); Hofstadter and Wiebe.
- Against 1, business: George Perkins (a Morgan partner) funded Roosevelt's party in 1912; big firms shaped regulation (Kolko).
- Against 2, workers: AFL and the Women's Trade Union League backed the labour laws; the Tammany politicians Wagner and Smith led the New York factory commission after Triangle; Socialists won 6 per cent in 1912.
- Against 3, others: farmers' demands, Black reformers (Du Bois, Wells, the National Association of Colored Women); the movement took the Populists' proposals.
- Judgement: the leadership, organisations and ideas were middle-class, but the movement needed, and often got, support from other classes. Criterion: who designed the reforms against who supported them.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "The Progressive Movement was a middle-class movement. Its leaders, such as Jane Addams, Louis Brandeis and Lincoln Steffens, were educated people from the middle class. Jane Addams, who set up Hull-House in 1889, and the women of the General Federation of Women's Clubs were middle-class women who campaigned on child labour and pure food. The muckrakers wrote for middle-class magazines and the reformers wanted experts to run cities, as in the city manager plan. So the movement was clearly middle class."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than weighs. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other classes merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet the movement was not only middle-class, and the evidence of who backed particular reforms shows this. Workers' organisations were part of it: the American Federation of Labor supported laws on hours and compensation, and after the Triangle fire killed 146 workers in March 1911 the New York Factory Investigating Commission was led by Robert Wagner and Alfred Smith, who came from the Tammany Hall machine that the middle-class reformers had condemned for years. The machine politicians who had once opposed reform changed sides when they saw that the voters of their wards wanted safer factories. Business also played a role. George Perkins, a partner of J. P. Morgan, gave money to Roosevelt's Progressive Party in 1912, and some large firms favoured federal regulation, since national rules could stop smaller competitors cutting costs. Gabriel Kolko argued in 1963 that regulation was shaped by big business and was less a reform against business than one for it. And the movement inherited the demands of the Populists, a farmers' movement, for the direct election of Senators and a graduated income tax.

Even so, these other groups were allies and not the designers of the movement. The people who identified problems, drew up programmes and organised the campaigns were nearly all educated middle-class professionals: lawyers, journalists, social workers and academics. Workers' groups wanted particular laws, but many of the Progressive reforms, from commission government to civil service examinations, took power from the neighbourhoods where working-class voters lived and gave it to experts. Black Americans and immigrants were mostly the objects of reform, not its leaders, and some reforms, such as literacy tests and some temperance laws, were aimed at them.

Overall, the statement is largely right about who led the movement and about whose values shaped it, but wrong if it implies that only the middle class took part. The best description is that the middle class provided the leaders, the ideas and the organisation, while other groups supplied support on the particular issues that affected them. The movement succeeded when it won this wider support, as with factory safety after Triangle, and failed when it did not, as with the rights of Black Americans."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** with a clear criterion (leadership and design against support) and says where the movement succeeded and failed. With an opening paragraph on the case for the statement, this is Level 5 (18–20). Note that the strongest answers distinguish who *led* a movement from who *joined* it.

Question: "To what extent was corruption the main reason for the growth of the reform movement?" [20]

A student wrote:

"Corruption was the main reason for the growth of the Progressive Movement. In the Gilded Age there was a lot of corruption. In New York the Tweed Ring stole millions of dollars. Lincoln Steffens wrote The Shame of the Cities in 1904 about St Louis, Minneapolis, Pittsburgh, Philadelphia, Chicago and New York. In St Louis Joseph Folk prosecuted councillors who took bribes, and he became Governor of Missouri. The Senate was also corrupt, so in 1913 the 17th Amendment was passed. So corruption made people want reform."

**The level.** The facts are accurate and many, but the paragraph is **narrative and assertion**: it lists examples of corruption without explaining how corruption made people join a reform movement, and it never compares corruption with other causes. "So corruption made people want reform" repeats the question. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, choose the detail that proves it, then compare. "Corruption was the main reason because it was the one grievance shared by every group in the movement, whatever else they disagreed about. Steffens' articles of 1902–04 showed middle-class readers that the failings of city government were not confined to Tammany Hall, and the Progressives' first reforms, such as the primary, the recall and the 17th Amendment, were about making government honest before they regulated business. However, corruption alone does not explain why women's groups and settlements became so active; they were driven by conditions in slums and factories, which no honest government would have changed without new laws." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement, and the paragraph prepares the comparison with other causes.

Q: Who gave the muckrakers their name, when, and what did he mean by it?
A: Theodore Roosevelt, in a speech on 14 April 1906. He took the "man with the muck-rake" from Bunyan's *Pilgrim's Progress*. He agreed that exposure was "often indispensable" but warned that a writer who looked only at the filth, and wrote sensationally, would become a force for evil.
Q: Name three muckrakers and say what each exposed.
A: Ida Tarbell, Standard Oil's rebates and deals with railroads; Lincoln Steffens, bribery in the governments of American cities; Upton Sinclair, the filthy conditions and exploited workers of the Chicago meatpacking industry. (Also: Phillips on the Senate, Adams on patent medicines, Baker on race.)
Q: Why was the Anti-Saloon League a new kind of organisation?
A: It was a single-issue pressure group, founded in 1893, that worked across parties through the churches, backing whichever candidate promised to vote for prohibition. It became the model for later Progressive pressure groups.
Q: What did the Panic of 1907 teach reformers about the American banking system?
A: That it had no central bank or lender of last resort. J. P. Morgan had to organise the rescue himself, so many concluded that the country needed a public institution, which led to the Aldrich–Vreeland Act (1908), the National Monetary Commission and the Federal Reserve Act (1913).
Q: What was "municipal housekeeping"?
A: The Progressive women's idea that women's work in the home (health, food, cleanliness, children) should be extended to the city, so they had a right and a duty to take part in public life.
Q: Name the three Progressive "direct democracy" measures and say what each does.
A: The initiative (voters propose laws), the referendum (voters approve or reject laws passed by the legislature) and the recall (voters remove an official before his term ends). Oregon adopted them in 1902 and 1908.
Q: What was the Wisconsin Idea?
A: La Follette's programme as governor (1901–06) of honest, expert government: the direct primary, a railroad commission and a tax commission, and the use of University of Wisconsin professors as advisers.
Q: Why was the Pendleton Act passed in 1883, and what did it do?
A: President Garfield's assassination by a disappointed office-seeker (1881) turned opinion against the spoils system. The Act created a Civil Service Commission and competitive examinations for a minority of federal jobs (about 10 per cent at first).
Q: What was the "liberty of contract" doctrine, and which case shows it in use?
A: The doctrine that the 14th Amendment protects the freedom of employers and workers to bargain, so that states could not fix hours or wages. *Lochner v. New York* (1905) used it to strike down a sixty-hour limit for bakers. *Muller v. Oregon* (1908) upheld a ten-hour day for women.
Q: Why does "widening the franchise" need care as a description of Progressive reforms?
A: It was true for white women in the West, but Southern states cut the Black vote almost to nothing with poll taxes, literacy tests and grandfather clauses (Louisiana's Black registrations fell from over 130,000 in 1896 to about 1,300 by 1904), and some of these measures were presented as reforms.
Q: What happened after the Triangle Shirtwaist fire?
A: 146 workers died on 25 March 1911. New York set up a Factory Investigating Commission led by Robert Wagner and Alfred Smith, which led to a series of laws in 1911–14 on fire safety, hours and inspection.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why the settlement house movement grew in the 1890s.
A: Indicative content: the growth of immigrant districts in industrial cities, with slums, child labour and no public services (Chicago's population rose from about 300,000 in 1870 to about 1.7 million in 1900); the example of Hull-House (1889) and Henry Street (1893); the desire of educated, mostly women graduates for useful work, with limited careers open to them; the Social Gospel, which urged Christians to serve the poor; and the ability of settlements to gather facts (Hull-House Maps and Papers, 1895) and to lobby for laws such as the Illinois factory act (1893). A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects need (the cities), supply (educated women with few careers) and motive (religion and democracy), and says which was decisive.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Progressives wanted Senators to be elected directly by the voters.
A: Indicative content: legislatures chose Senators under the original Constitution; deadlocks left seats empty (Delaware, c.1899–1903); accusations that railroads and trusts bought legislatures and so the Senate (Phillips, 1906; the Lorimer case, 1909–12); Populists had demanded direct election in the Omaha Platform (1892); the Senate had blocked reforms such as railroad regulation; states already used primaries (Oregon from 1901; about 29 states in effect by 1912). A Level 4 answer links the charge of corruption to the wider Progressive aim of giving voters control, and judges the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why the Panic of 1907 strengthened the case for reform of the banking system.
A: Indicative content: the failure of the Knickerbocker Trust (22 October) and the run on New York banks showed that there was no central bank or lender of last resort; J. P. Morgan's personal rescue showed how much power lay with one private banker; it revived the existing criticism of the "money trust" (Pujo hearings 1912–13, Brandeis 1914); the Aldrich–Vreeland Act (1908) and the National Monetary Commission led to the Federal Reserve Act (1913). A Level 4 answer connects the technical failure (no central bank) to the political one (private concentration of power) and judges which mattered more to reformers.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent was the temperance movement important to the development of the Progressive Movement?
A: For: the WCTU (1874) trained a generation of women in public campaigning and, under Willard, linked temperance to suffrage, child labour and prison reform; the Anti-Saloon League (1893) was the model pressure group; the saloon and the machine were closely linked, so attacking one attacked the other; more than twenty states had gone dry by 1917. Against: many Progressives disliked prohibition as moralistic and anti-immigrant; the most active urban reformers (Addams, Steffens) were not mainly interested in it; the key causes of the movement were industrial and political, not moral; prohibition was achieved in 1919 by war and by a coalition that was partly outside Progressivism. Level 5 (17–20) judges, e.g., that temperance was important as a source of organisation, methods and women's participation, though less important for the movement's economic and political programme, and it distinguishes its role as a cause from its role as a result.

Knight (1895) narrowed the Sherman Act; Holmes's dissent in Lochner shows that the majority was making a choice. Against: the Court upheld state laws in Holden (1898, miners' eight-hour day), Muller (1908, women's ten-hour day) and Munn v. Illinois (1877); the Brandeis brief taught reformers how to win; later cases upheld hours laws; other obstacles were as important: the Senate, party bosses, business lobbying, employers' resistance, and divisions inside the movement; the 1906 food laws, the Hepburn Act and the 17th Amendment passed without court opposition. Level 5 judges, e.g., that courts blocked some important labour laws, particularly of the early 1900s, but that the Court's record was mixed and that politics was the larger obstacle.

:::recap
- **Progressivism** was many movements, not one party; the Progressive Party of 1912 was a part of it. Common beliefs: new problems needed new laws, government should act, and experts and publicity would find the answers.
- **Five pressures:** the muckrakers (McClure's, January 1903; Tarbell, Steffens, Sinclair; Roosevelt's speech of 14 April 1906); temperance (WCTU 1874, Anti-Saloon League 1893); panics (especially 1907) and the "money trust"; urbanisation and corruption; women's groups (Hull-House 1889, the clubs, municipal housekeeping).
- **Cities:** commission government (Galveston 1901), city managers (Staunton 1908, Dayton 1913), reform mayors (Pingree, Jones, Johnson).
- **States:** Wisconsin Idea (La Follette 1901–06); initiative, referendum and recall (Oregon 1902, 1908); direct primary; Hiram Johnson in California; the Southern "reforms" that disfranchised Black voters (Louisiana registrations from 130,000 in 1896 to about 1,300 in 1904).
- **Federal and parties:** Pendleton Act (16 January 1883); revolt against Speaker Cannon (March 1910); 17th Amendment (ratified 8 April 1913); Progressive Party platform (August 1912).
- **Regulation:** unions had partial recognition (Clayton Act 1914, Department of Labor 1913); courts: *Lochner* (1905) against *Muller* (1908); workers' compensation (Wisconsin 1911); Triangle fire (146 dead, 25 March 1911); Armstrong investigation and the laws of 1906; blue sky laws (Kansas 1911); Hepburn Act (1906).
- **Judgement:** corruption was the common ground, the necessary cause; the muckrakers and women's groups gave the movement its voice and its organisation.
:::
