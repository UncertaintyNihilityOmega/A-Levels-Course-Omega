Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.italy.4) **Section A: Mussolini's Italy, 1919–41.** 'The Lateran Treaties of 1929 brought more benefit to Mussolini's regime than to the Catholic Church.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section A, Mussolini's Italy.** Table A (AO2: analysis and judgement) out of 15 and Table B (AO1: knowledge) out of 15. Accept any other valid response that is relevant and supported.

**What the question asks.** The view is that the regime gained more than the Church. "Benefit" has to be defined, so the essay needs criteria: legitimacy and support, money and territory, freedom of action, and how long the benefit lasted. The command is to evaluate: compare what each side gained and decide.

**For the view (the regime gained more).**
- The treaties ended the "Roman Question", the quarrel between Italy and the Papacy since 1870, a break of 59 years that no Liberal government had solved. This gave the regime a respectability and prestige that it did not have before.
- Catholic support followed. Priests urged a yes vote in the plebiscite of 24 March 1929, in which over 98 per cent voted for the Fascist list. Pius XI praised Mussolini as a man sent by Providence. Many bishops later backed the invasion of Abyssinia in 1935–36.
- Catholic opposition was weakened: the Catholic People's Party had already been pushed out, priests were barred from joining political parties by the Concordat, and bishops swore an oath to the king.
- Catholic Action was allowed only if it kept out of politics. Mussolini had prepared the ground with gestures: the crucifix returned to schools and courts in 1923, religious teaching in elementary schools in 1923, and help for the Banco di Roma in 1923.

**Against (the Church gained as much or more).**
- Territory and money: sovereignty over the Vatican City (about 44 hectares) and compensation of 1,750 million lire (750 million in cash and 1,000 million in 5 per cent bonds). The three agreements were signed on 11 February 1929 by Mussolini and Cardinal Gasparri.
- Position and influence: Catholicism was declared the religion of the state; church marriages had civil effect; religious teaching was extended to secondary schools; Catholic Action had a legal place. The Church changed from victim to partner.
- Independence: the Church was not absorbed. In spring 1931 Mussolini closed Catholic Action's clubs and the Pope answered with the encyclical *Non abbiamo bisogno* of 29 June 1931, attacking a "pagan worship of the State"; the September 1931 agreement let Catholic Action continue, but kept it out of sport and politics. The racial laws of 1938 forbade marriage between Jews and non-Jews, including Catholics, in conflict with the Concordat, and Pius XI protested in public.
- Support was not control: many Catholics, especially young people, did not become Fascist, and the treaties did not make Italy more religious.

**Routes to a judgement.** (a) The regime gained more in the short term and in politics (legitimacy, consent), while the Church gained institutional security that outlasted the regime. (b) The benefits were of different kinds, so the comparison must be made against stated criteria. (c) The conflicts of 1931 and 1938 show that the Church's gain was real, because it could still resist.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines criteria, weighs both sides throughout, shows the connections (support bought, independence kept) and reaches a clear, supported judgement. *Level 3 (7–9):* lists the terms of the treaties with some analysis, argues one side ("Mussolini won everything"), and the judgement is weakly supported. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise detail, selected for the argument (11 February 1929, 1,750 million lire, the 98 per cent plebiscite, 29 June 1931, the 1938 laws). *Level 3 (7–9):* general statements ("the Pope got a state and money") with some inaccuracy.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.stalin.1) **Section A: Stalin's Russia, 1924–41.** Assess the reasons why Stalin gained control of the Soviet Union in the years 1924–29.

A: **Mark scheme: Section A, Stalin's Russia.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Reasons for one outcome over five years. A top essay gives a range of reasons, shows how they connect, and uses a criterion to decide which mattered most (for example whether a reason was necessary, or whether the outcome would have happened without it). It does not write as if the outcome was certain in 1924.

**Reasons (indicative content).**
- *The party machine.* Stalin became General Secretary on 3 April 1922 and sat on the Orgburo and Politburo. The Secretariat controlled appointments (the *nomenklatura*), so regional secretaries owed him their posts and delegates to congresses voted for his policies. The Lenin Enrolment brought about 240,000 workers into the party between January and May 1924 (membership about 446,000 on 1 January 1924, over a million by January 1926), and the new members followed the secretaries. His allies (Molotov, Kaganovich, Voroshilov, Ordzhonikidze) held key posts, and Kirov took over Leningrad from Zinoviev in 1926.
- *The weaknesses and divisions of his rivals.* Trotsky was a late Bolshevik, arrogant, with no base in the apparatus, and feared as a possible "Bonaparte"; he stayed silent at the Thirteenth Congress in May 1924 and missed Lenin's funeral. Zinoviev and Kamenev had opposed the October rising and Zinoviev was blamed for the failure of the German "October" of 1923. Bukharin was a popular theorist but a naive politician; the Right had the government, the trade unions and *Pravda* but no mass base. Each rival allied with Stalin against another, and the United Opposition of Trotsky, Zinoviev and Kamenev formed only in 1926.
- *Ideas and policy.* "Socialism in One Country" (December 1924) appealed to a party tired after the failure of revolutions abroad, against Trotsky's Permanent Revolution. Stalin first backed the NEP with Bukharin (1925) and then, after the grain crisis of 1927–28 (about two million tonnes less grain collected), adopted rapid industrialisation, the Left's programme, and beat the Right.
- *Lenin's legacy.* Stalin presented himself as Lenin's disciple at the funeral on 27 January 1924. The Testament, with its postscript of 4 January 1923 asking the party to remove him, was read to leading delegates at the Thirteenth Congress but not published, and Zinoviev and Kamenev argued that he should stay. The ban on factions (1921) was used to condemn each opposition.
- *Repression and propaganda.* Trotsky lost the War Commissariat in January 1925; opposition meetings were broken up and the OGPU arrested supporters; Trotsky and Zinoviev were expelled from the party on 12–14 November 1927; Trotsky was exiled to Alma-Ata in January 1928 and deported in February 1929; the Shakhty trial of 1928 and the celebration of Stalin's fiftieth birthday on 21 December 1929 began the cult.
- *Stalin himself.* Hard-working, patient, with a retentive memory, plain in manner and underestimated by every rival.

**Connections and judgement.** The machine turned every defeat of the opposition into a vote; the rivals' errors gave Stalin the time to build it; the ideas gave him a cause; repression finished the job. A strong judgement ranks the machine as the precondition (nobody else had one) and shows why the rivals' errors and the ideas were still needed: in 1928–29 the machine alone could not have beaten the Right without the grain crisis and a Central Committee majority. Level 5 argues which reason was necessary and why, not only which was important.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* a range of reasons, linked, with a supported ranking. *Level 3 (7–9):* two or three reasons explained, partly narrative, weak ranking. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise dates, posts and numbers chosen for the argument. *Level 3:* general ("Stalin was General Secretary and his rivals were weak") with few dates.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.hitler.1) **Section A: Hitler's Germany, 1929–41.** 'Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in January 1933 was the result of political intrigue, not of Nazi popular support.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section A, Hitler's Germany.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Two explanations are set against each other: intrigue (the deals and decisions of a few men) and popular support. Strong essays test the "not": they ask whether the two were separate, and how each worked.

**For intrigue.**
- Hitler was appointed, not elected: he never won a majority in a free election. In the election of 6 November 1932 the Nazi vote fell to 33.1 per cent (196 seats), down from 37.3 per cent and 230 seats in July; the party was short of funds and despondent.
- Hindenburg refused Hitler the Chancellorship on 13 August 1932 and offered the Vice-Chancellorship; the change came from the deals of January 1933. Papen, who was angry at Schleicher, met Hitler at the Cologne home of the banker Kurt von Schröder on 4 January 1933; he won over Hugenberg, Hindenburg's son Oskar, the head of the President's office Otto Meissner and, with landowners lobbying, Hindenburg himself.
- Schleicher fell on 28 January 1933 when Hindenburg refused him an emergency decree. Hitler took the oath on 30 January at the head of a coalition in which only three of the eleven ministers were Nazis (Hitler, Frick, Göring). Papen and his colleagues believed they had "boxed him in".
- Earlier intrigue had paved the way: Papen's lifting of the ban on the SA and SS (16 June 1932) and removal of the Prussian government (20 July 1932); Schleicher's failed Querfront and the loss of Gregor Strasser (8 December 1932).

**Against (popular support mattered).**
- Hitler took 36.8 per cent in the second round of the presidential election on 10 April 1932; in July 1932 the Nazis became the largest party with 37.3 per cent and 230 seats of 608, with about 13.7 million votes. The Depression and the failure of Müller's and Brüning's governments (rule by decree began on 30 March 1930) had produced this support: the Nazi vote rose from 18.3 per cent (107 seats) in September 1930 to 37.3 per cent in July 1932.
- The Nazis and Communists together held more than half of the Reichstag seats, so no government could have a majority without the Nazis, and the elite looked for a mass movement to give a conservative government support. Papen and Schleicher had failed because they had none.
- Hitler's own tactics depended on his popular strength: his refusal to take less than the Chancellorship in August 1932 was possible because he led the largest party and 13 million votes.
- The elite's fear of the Communists, and the SA's street power.

**Routes to a judgement.** The essay is best argued as "both, and the question's 'not' is wrong": popular support made Hitler the only possible leader of a mass-based government, and intrigue decided that he was appointed in January 1933 rather than not at all, at a moment when his vote had fallen. A top-level answer says which was more necessary (support, since without it there was no deal to make) and which decided the timing (intrigue).

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines both explanations, tests each, links them and reaches a clear supported judgement on the "not". *Level 3 (7–9):* narrates 1932–33 with some analysis; one side is described, not tested. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* election percentages and seats, names (Papen, Schleicher, Schröder, Hindenburg, Oskar Hindenburg, Meissner), dates (4, 28, 30 January) used precisely. *Level 3:* "Papen made a deal with Hitler" without dates or numbers, or confusion between the elections.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.usa45.4) **Section B: Truman, Eisenhower and post-war USA, 1945–61.** 'Eisenhower's foreign policy was a continuation of Truman's, not a change of direction.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section B, post-war USA, 1945–61.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Whether the two presidents' foreign policies were the same in substance. "Continuation" and "change of direction" need tests: aims, means, risks, results, or regions. The syllabus asks "a change in direction?" of Eisenhower, so a strong answer sets criteria and shows that the answer depends on them.

**For continuation.**
- The same aim: to contain Soviet power and Communism, and neither rolled it back by force. Eisenhower's platform had called containment "negative, futile and immoral" and promised the "liberation" of Eastern Europe, but he did nothing when Soviet tanks crushed the Hungarian rising on 4 November 1956.
- The same structures: NATO (4 April 1949), the alliances with Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, aid programmes and the CIA (set up in 1947) were Truman's; Eisenhower kept them and added West Germany to NATO in 1955, SEATO (8 September 1954) and the Baghdad Pact.
- Nuclear weapons and covert action: Truman approved the hydrogen bomb in January 1950 and authorised covert operations in 1948.
- Korea and Vietnam: the armistice of 27 July 1953 ended the war on terms that Truman had been ready to accept; Eisenhower inherited American aid to France in Vietnam (by 1954 most of the cost) and deepened the commitment, backing Diem and the refusal of the 1956 elections.
- Anti-Communism at home bound both.

**For change.**
- Means and cost: Truman after NSC-68 (approved in September 1950) raised defence spending from about 14 billion dollars in 1950 to over 50 billion in 1953. Eisenhower's New Look (NSC 162/2, 30 October 1953) relied on nuclear weapons, a smaller army and navy, allies' ground troops and the CIA, to balance the budget; Dulles announced massive retaliation on 12 January 1954.
- Use of force: no war after 1953; Eisenhower refused to act alone at Dien Bien Phu in 1954 (it fell on 7 May), and refused to fight over Hungary and Suez.
- Diplomacy with the USSR: Truman held no summit after Potsdam; Eisenhower made the "Chance for Peace" speech (16 April 1953), the Geneva Summit (18–23 July 1955) and hosted Khrushchev at Camp David in September 1959 (though the U-2 affair of 1 May 1960 and the failed Paris summit of 16 May ended the improvement).
- Regions: Eisenhower extended the Cold War to the Third World with covert action in Iran (August 1953) and Guatemala (18–27 June 1954), the Eisenhower Doctrine (5 January 1957), the Lebanon landing in July 1958 and the hostility to Castro that led to the break with Cuba on 3 January 1961.
- Style: brinkmanship (the Taiwan Strait crises of 1954–55 and 1958).

**Routes to a judgement.** The most defensible is that the strategic aim and structures stayed the same, while the means, the style and the places of action changed: "continuity in strategy, change in method". A Level 5 essay argues which test matters most (aims or means, since containment's purpose did not change but its cost and its geography did) and applies it to each region.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* explicit tests, balanced analysis across Europe, Asia, the Middle East and Latin America, a sustained supported judgement. *Level 3 (7–9):* lists what each president did with some comparison, argues one side. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* dates and names used precisely (NSC-68, NSC 162/2, Dien Bien Phu, Mossadegh, Árbenz). *Level 3:* general, "Eisenhower used nuclear weapons more and Truman fought in Korea", with errors on dates or confusion between the presidents.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.usa61.3) **Section B: A time of challenges: the USA, 1961–74.** Assess the importance of the federal government in the progress made by African Americans towards civil rights in the years 1961–68.

A: **Mark scheme: Section B, a time of challenges.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** The importance of one factor (the President, Congress, the courts and federal agencies) among others (the movement, local people, opposition). The date limits are 1961–68: Brown (1954) and the Montgomery boycott are outside them. The word "progress" needs a test: law, political power (the vote), daily life and reach (North and South).

**Where the federal government was important.**
- Law: the Civil Rights Act of 2 July 1964 (Title II on public places; Title VI, which cut federal money from discriminating bodies, so that the school districts of the South began to desegregate: in autumn 1964 only about 2.25 per cent of Black pupils in the South attended school with whites; Title VII and the EEOC); the Voting Rights Act of 6 August 1965 (a ban on literacy tests where fewer than half of eligible people were registered, federal examiners, preclearance); the Twenty-fourth Amendment (23 January 1964); the Fair Housing Act (11 April 1968).
- Results: Black registration in Mississippi rose from about 6.7 per cent in March 1965 to about 59.8 per cent in September 1967; the number of Black elected officials rose from about 1,500 in 1970 to about 3,500 in 1975.
- Only federal power could override the Southern states: Kennedy used marshals and troops to enrol James Meredith at the University of Mississippi on 30 September 1962 and federalised the Alabama National Guard in June 1963; the Supreme Court upheld the laws (*Heart of Atlanta Motel*, 14 December 1964; *South Carolina v. Katzenbach*, 7 March 1966) and struck down bans on interracial marriage (*Loving v. Virginia*, 12 June 1967). Executive Orders 10925 (6 March 1961) and 11246 (24 September 1965) introduced affirmative action. Johnson used his Senate skills and Kennedy's memory to pass the 1964 Act.

**Where other factors were as or more important.**
- The movement forced the federal government to act: the Freedom Riders (from 4 May 1961), Birmingham (April–May 1963, children, dogs and hoses on 2–3 May), Kennedy's television address of 11 June 1963, the March on Washington (28 August 1963), Freedom Summer 1964 (the murders of Chaney, Goodman and Schwerner on 21 June 1964) and Bloody Sunday at Selma (7 March 1965), which led within five months to the Voting Rights Act. Local organising by women and SNCC workers in Mississippi (as Charles Payne argues) prepared the ground.
- The federal government was reluctant before 1964: Kennedy sent no major bill until June 1963, appointed segregationist judges, and the FBI under Hoover seldom protected civil rights workers; Robert Kennedy authorised the wiretaps on King in October 1963. Its action was reaction to crises.
- Enforcement and reach were limited: housing enforcement was weak until 1988; Black unemployment stayed about twice the white rate; Black family income rose only from about 51 per cent of white income in 1959 to about 61 per cent in 1970; the riots of 1965–68 and the Kerner Commission's report of 29 February 1968 ("two societies, one black, one white") showed the North's problems untouched.

**Routes to a judgement.** The federal government was necessary and decisive for legal change, and the movement was necessary and decisive for making it act; the two were interdependent. Which mattered more depends on the test: law and the vote (federal, but prompted), daily life and economics (limited for both). A Level 5 essay chooses the test and applies it.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* a clear standard for "importance", sustained comparison of the federal and other factors, the interdependence shown. *Level 3 (7–9):* describes the Acts and the campaigns in sequence with some analysis, argues for one factor without weighing the other. *Table B, Level 5:* precise dates and measures (Title VI, Title VII, preclearance, the registration figures). *Level 3:* general ("Johnson passed the Civil Rights Act and the Voting Rights Act"), with few specifics.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.usa74.1) **Section B: The USA, 1974–93.** 'Reagan won the presidential election of 1980 mainly because of the failures of Carter.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section B, the USA, 1974–93.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Whether the cause of Reagan's win was negative (Carter's failures) or positive (Reagan's appeal, ideas and the New Right). Strong essays separate the two and test "mainly": a failure of the incumbent is only a cause if there is a winning alternative.

**For the view (Carter's failures).**
- Economic failure: stagflation, with inflation 13.5 per cent in 1980, a misery index of 20.6, interest rates over 20 per cent; Carter's approval fell from about 75 per cent in early 1977 to about 28 per cent in summer 1979.
- Style and leadership: poor relations with Congress (the "hit list", the Lance affair), the "malaise speech" of 15 July 1979 (the "crisis of confidence") followed by the Cabinet shake-up of 17 July, which looked like panic; his energy plans (the National Energy Plan of April 1977, the Energy Security Act of 30 June 1980) failed to calm the public.
- Foreign policy: the seizure of the Embassy in Tehran on 4 November 1979 (52 Americans held for 444 days; still in Iran on election day), the failed rescue of 24 April 1980 (Operation Eagle Claw; eight servicemen died and Secretary Vance resigned), and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979, which ended détente.
- A divided party: Edward Kennedy's challenge from 7 November 1979 left Carter weakened; Anderson took 6.6 per cent of the vote.

**Against (Reagan's strengths and other causes).**
- Personal appeal: optimism ("Let's make America great again"), television skill, a record as Governor of California (1967–75), a near-win against Ford in 1976 and the debate of 28 October 1980 in which he asked whether voters were better off than four years earlier.
- Policy: a clear alternative, a 30 per cent tax cut over three years (Kemp–Roth, Proposition 13), supply-side ideas and rebuilding defence; Bush's "voodoo economics" had not stopped it.
- The New Right: evangelicals (the Moral Majority of 1979), the anti-busing, anti-ERA and anti-abortion voters, and "Reagan Democrats"; the ending of the consensus on the state's role. Carter had won in 1976 (297 to 240 electoral votes) with Southern and Black votes; in 1980 he lost every Southern state except Georgia.
- The scale of the result: Reagan won 489 electoral votes to Carter's 49 (50.7 per cent to 41.0 per cent); the Republicans won the Senate for the first time since the 1950s. A protest vote against Carter might have been narrower; a landslide suggests that voters chose Reagan.

**Routes to a judgement.** The economy was probably the most important cause because it cut across groups, and it was a failure of Carter's; but a failure only counted because Reagan offered a credible answer. A strong answer argues that the two causes were linked: Carter's failures created the opening, and Reagan's appeal and the New Right turned it into a landslide. The hostages were one factor, not the whole.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* separates failure from appeal, weighs the economy, personality, Carter's record and the New Right with a clear criterion, and reaches a supported judgement on "mainly". *Level 3 (7–9):* lists reasons for Reagan's win with some analysis and a weakly supported view. *Table B, Level 5:* precise figures (489 to 49, 50.7 per cent to 41.0 per cent, 13.5 per cent), dates (4 November 1979, 24 April 1980) used for the argument. *Level 3:* general ("Carter had the hostages and inflation"), with errors.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.soviet.1) **Section C: The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953–91.** 'Soviet control of the satellite states in the years 1953–68 depended mainly on force.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section C, the Soviet Empire.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** The basis of Soviet control, 1953–68. "Force" can mean its use in crises, the threat of it, or the everyday machinery of repression; "mainly" asks for a ranking against other bases: the party system, economic ties, concessions and the loyalty of local leaders, and the passivity of the West. The date limits end with the Prague Spring and the Brezhnev Doctrine of 1968.

**For the view (force).**
- Force was used in each great crisis. In East Germany the rising of 16–17 June 1953 was ended by Soviet tanks, with at least 55 dead. In Hungary, after the rising began on 23 October 1956 and Nagy announced withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact on 1 November, Soviet forces attacked on 4 November (Operation Whirlwind: about 30,000 soldiers and over 1,000 tanks by one estimate); about 2,500 to 3,000 Hungarians and about 700 Soviet soldiers died, about 200,000 left the country, and Nagy was hanged on 16 June 1958. In Czechoslovakia the invasion of 20–21 August 1968 by the Soviet Union, Poland, Bulgaria and Hungary killed 137 Czechs and Slovaks and ended the Prague Spring; the Moscow Protocol was signed on 26 August and Husák replaced Dubček on 17 April 1969. The Brezhnev Doctrine (Pravda, 26 September 1968) made the threat permanent.
- Everyday force: secret police (the Stasi from 1950, the ÁVH, the StB), censorship, controls on churches (the arrest of Cardinal Wyszyński in September 1953, held for three years), closed frontiers (about 2.7 million left East Germany between 1949 and 1961, until the Berlin Wall on 13 August 1961).
- The Warsaw Pact (14 May 1955) put the satellite armies under a Joint Command in Moscow and gave a legal base for Soviet troops.

**Against (other bases of control).**
- The party monopoly and the *nomenklatura*: every career depended on the party, and local communist leaders (Ulbricht, Gomułka, Novotný, Kádár) were loyal and did much of the repression themselves; the executions and reprisals in Hungary in 1957–58 were carried out by Hungarians.
- Concessions: the "New Course" after Stalin's death (1953); the Polish October of 1956, when Moscow accepted Gomułka (21 October), collectivisation ended, the Church gained freedoms and Wyszyński was released (28 October); Kádár's change from "he who is not with us is against us" to "he who is not against us is with us" (1961), the amnesty of 1963 and rising living standards.
- Economic ties: Comecon (1949); and slow improvements in living standards from a low base (jobs, subsidised food, rent, health care) gave some acceptance, although the gap with the West grew.
- The West's passivity (no help for Hungary, which coincided with Suez, or for Prague) meant that Moscow had little to fear from force.

**Routes to a judgement.** Force underlay the system (control never rested on consent), but day-to-day control rested on the party, the local leaders and a mix of concessions, with force used in only three great crises in fifteen years (1953, 1956 and 1968), because after 1956 the threat was enough. A Level 5 essay separates crisis from routine and argues that force was the final guarantee, and not the main working method, or the reverse, with evidence.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines "force" and "mainly", weighs the bases of control against each other through the period, and judges. *Level 3 (7–9):* describes 1953, 1956 and 1968 with some analysis and a one-sided answer. *Table B, Level 5:* precise (dates, numbers, names, the Warsaw Pact, the Brezhnev Doctrine). *Level 3:* general, with confusions between 1956 and 1968.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.safrica.2) **Section C: The end of minority rule in South Africa and Zimbabwe, 1948–94.** 'The Sharpeville massacre of 1960 was a turning point in the history of apartheid.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section C, South Africa and Zimbabwe.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Whether 1960 changed the direction of apartheid's history. A "turning point" needs a before and after, and a test: for whom (the opposition, the state, the outside world) and in what (methods, power, international pressure). Strong essays compare Sharpeville with other possible turning points.

**For the view.**
- The event: on 21 March 1960, in the PAC's pass campaign, police opened fire without warning on a crowd at Sharpeville (estimates of its size run from about 5,000 to 20,000); 69 were killed by the police count, at least 91 by later research. Two died at Langa. Photographs shown across the world made it a symbol.
- Opposition: the state of emergency (30 March to 31 August 1960) and the banning of the ANC and PAC on 8 April ended legal protest; leaders chose exile, silence or underground work. Mandela and the ANC formed Umkhonto we Sizwe on 16 December 1961, and the turn to armed struggle followed (the Rivonia arrests of 11 July 1963; life sentences on 12 June 1964).
- International opinion: the UN Security Council discussed apartheid for the first time on 1 April 1960 (Resolution 134); South Africa left the Commonwealth and became a republic on 31 May 1961; the Anti-Apartheid Movement grew; foreign capital left and share prices fell in 1960–61.

**Against the view.**
- The state emerged stronger: it used the emergency and the bans to build new powers (the Sabotage Act of 1962, the 90-day law of 1963, the Terrorism Act of 1967); Verwoerd, wounded by an assassin on 9 April 1960, pressed on with separate development (Transkei self-government in 1963); once order returned the economy grew fast for the rest of the decade.
- International pressure was not decisive: the arms embargo of 1963 was voluntary and a mandatory one came only with Resolution 418 on 4 November 1977; Western trade and investment continued.
- Resistance did not begin in 1960: the Defiance Campaign (1952), the Freedom Charter (1955), the women's anti-pass march (1956) and the Treason Trials (from 1956) came before it.
- Later turning points were stronger: the Soweto uprising of 16 June 1976 and the Black Consciousness movement (SASO 1969; Biko's death on 12 September 1977), which began a new generation of resistance; the pressure of the 1980s and the changes of 1990. Sharpeville was a defeat for the opposition in one sense and a gain in another.

**Routes to a judgement.** Sharpeville was a turning point in the form of resistance (from legal protest to underground and armed struggle) and in the world's attention to apartheid, but not in the power or direction of the state, which grew stronger for fifteen years. The turning point is therefore partial: for the opposition and for opinion abroad, yes; for the apartheid state, no. A Level 5 essay says for whom, and compares it with Soweto.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* criteria for a turning point, balanced analysis of the state, the opposition and the world, a comparison with at least one other turning point, a supported judgement. *Level 3 (7–9):* describes Sharpeville and its aftermath with some analysis and a one-sided answer. *Table B, Level 5:* precise (the two death tolls and why they differ, 30 March, 8 April, Resolution 134, 31 May 1961, 16 December 1961, Rivonia). *Level 3:* general ("the ANC was banned and the world was shocked"), with confusions of dates and of ANC with PAC.
:::

:::try
Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.malaysia.4) **Section C: The route to independence: Malaysia and Indonesia, c.1909–67.** Assess the reasons why the Dutch failed to re-establish their control over Indonesia in the years 1945–49.

A: **Mark scheme: Section C, Malaysia and Indonesia.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** The reasons for one outcome in four years. The outcome ("failed to re-establish control") needs care: the Dutch won the military engagements and took the Republic's capital, yet lost. A top essay sorts the reasons into Indonesian, international and Dutch, shows how they worked together, and says which was decisive.

**Reasons (indicative content).**
- *Indonesian resistance and the survival of the Republic.* The proclamation of 17 August 1945 and the *pemuda*; an army that grew from Peta and local fighting groups, and could not be beaten in the open; the British landing and the battle of Surabaya (10 November 1945) showed the Republic's will to resist; the Dutch held the towns and plantations but not the villages. After the second police action (19 December 1948), when Sukarno and Hatta were captured, an Emergency Government carried on, the guerrilla war under Sudirman and Nasution continued, and the attack on Yogyakarta on 1 March 1949 showed that the Republic was alive. The Madiun affair (18 September 1948), in which the Republic suppressed a communist rising, won American respect.
- *International pressure.* The United Nations (the Committee of Good Offices) made it a world question; India and Australia raised it in 1947; Asian governments condemned the second police action. The strongest lever was the United States: after Madiun it saw the Republic as an anti-communist partner; on 22 December 1948 it suspended Marshall Plan aid to the Dutch East Indies, and in the spring of 1949 it let the Dutch know that aid to the Netherlands itself was at risk. Historians disagree about how much this threat decided the Dutch retreat (some argue that the Dutch were already changing course), so a strong answer weighs it rather than assumes it.
- *Dutch weakness.* An exhausted country after the German occupation; a costly army of over 100,000 men far from home; divided Dutch opinion; the failure of the Dutch federal policy, since the states they created had little support; the British had refused to fight for them and withdrew in November 1946; and the agreements (Linggadjati of 15 November 1946, signed 25 March 1947; the first police action of 21 July 1947; Renville of 17 January 1948) had not given a settlement.
- *The sequence.* The first police action (21 July 1947) and the second were military successes, but each brought international condemnation; after the second, the Roem–Van Roijen agreement (7 May 1949) and the Round Table Conference (23 August to 2 November 1949) led to the transfer of sovereignty on 27 December 1949.

**Connections and judgement.** The Republic's resistance gave the outside world something to support, and outside pressure raised the cost of continuing to a level the Dutch could not pay. The strongest answers argue that international pressure was decisive (it was the factor that ended the war), but that it could not have worked without the Republic's survival: the US would not have backed a Republic that had collapsed. Weak answers list the events of the revolution.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* a range of reasons, linked, with a criterion (decisive or necessary) and a supported ranking. *Level 3 (7–9):* narrates the police actions and the agreements with some explanation of why the Dutch failed. *Table B, Level 5:* precise dates (19 December 1948, 7 May 1949, 27 December 1949) and the Marshall Plan lever. *Level 3:* general, with confusion between Linggadjati and Renville or between the two police actions.
:::

## After the paper: mark it, then use the result

**1. Mark each essay twice.** Table A is analysis and judgement (AO2); Table B is knowledge (AO1). For Table A ask: did I use a standard of judgement and keep to it? Did I weigh both sides throughout, not only in the conclusion? Is there a supported judgement that answers the question as it was set? For Table B ask: was my detail precise (dates, names, numbers), chosen for the argument and accurate? Choose the best fit and, between two levels, say why. Do not mark a question you did not answer.

| Level | Table A (AO2): analysis and judgement | Marks | Table B (AO1): knowledge | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | full understanding; balanced and analytical; valid, wide-ranging criteria; sustained, supported judgement | 13–15 | a high level of relevant detail: selected, focused, wide-ranging, precise and accurate | 13–15 |
| 4 | good understanding; mostly analytical; valid criteria; some judgements partly substantiated | 10–12 | a good level: appropriately selected, mostly focused, mostly precise | 10–12 |
| 3 | understanding with some analysis; the argument lacks balance; begins to form a judgement, weakly substantiated | 7–9 | adequate: mostly appropriate, descriptive in places, narrow range | 7–9 |
| 2 | some understanding; descriptive; limited analysis; assertion rather than judgement | 4–6 | some relevant detail: narrative, frequently imprecise | 4–6 |
| 1 | addresses the topic, not the question; no analysis | 1–3 | limited knowledge; vague or inaccurate | 1–3 |

Each essay is out of 30 (15 and 15), and two essays make 60. The same level in both tables gives 26 to 30 at Level 5, 20 to 24 at Level 4, 14 to 18 at Level 3, 8 to 12 at Level 2 and 2 to 6 at Level 1. The course does not convert a total into a grade, because Cambridge sets grade boundaries after each series.

**2. Find the pattern across your two essays.** Did both essays reach a judgement, or did the second one trail off for lack of time? Did you tell the story in order (Level 2 and Level 3) or arrange the paragraphs by argument? Did your paragraphs each say whether they supported or challenged the view? Check the paper's pattern, because it repeats from essay to essay.

**3. Use your faults to choose your revision.** The chapter link under each question took you to the key question the essay needed. If a Level was lost on Table B, rebuild the dates, names and numbers for that topic. If it was lost on Table A, practise writing a plan with the criterion first, using the guide at [Paper 4: Depth study](#hist.exam.p4).
