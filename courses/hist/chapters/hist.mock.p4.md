Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.italy.4) **Section A: Mussolini's Italy, 1919–41.** 'The Lateran Treaties of 1929 brought more benefit to Mussolini's regime than to the Catholic Church.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section A, Mussolini's Italy.** Table A (AO2: analysis and judgement) out of 15 and Table B (AO1: knowledge) out of 15. Accept any other valid response that is relevant and supported.

**What the question asks.** The view is that the regime gained more than the Church. "Benefit" has to be defined, so the essay needs criteria: legitimacy and support, money and territory, freedom of action, and how long the benefit lasted. The command is to evaluate: compare what each side gained and decide.

**For the view (the regime gained more).**
- The treaties ended the "Roman Question", the quarrel between Italy and the Papacy since 1870, a break of 59 years that no Liberal government had solved. This gave the regime a respectability and prestige that it did not have before.
- Catholic support followed. Priests urged a yes vote in the plebiscite of 24 March 1929, in which over 98 per cent voted for the Fascist list. Pius XI praised Mussolini as a man sent by Providence. Many bishops later backed the invasion of Abyssinia in 1935–36.
- Catholic opposition was weakened: the Catholic People's Party had already been pushed out, priests were barred from joining political parties by the Concordat, and bishops swore an oath to the king.
- Catholic Action was allowed only if it kept out of politics. Mussolini had prepared the ground with gestures: the crucifix returned to schools and courts in 1923, religious teaching in elementary schools in 1923, and help for the Banco di Roma in 1923.

**Against (the Church gained as much or more).**
- Territory and money: sovereignty over the Vatican City (about 44 hectares) and compensation of 1,750 million lire (750 million in cash and 1,000 million in 5 per cent bonds). The three agreements were signed on 11 February 1929 by Mussolini and Cardinal Gasparri.
- Position and influence: Catholicism was declared the religion of the state; church marriages had civil effect; religious teaching was extended to secondary schools; Catholic Action had a legal place. The Church changed from victim to partner.
- Independence: the Church was not absorbed. In spring 1931 Mussolini closed Catholic Action's clubs and the Pope answered with the encyclical *Non abbiamo bisogno* of 29 June 1931, attacking a "pagan worship of the State"; the September 1931 agreement let Catholic Action continue, but kept it out of sport and politics. The racial laws of 1938 forbade marriage between Jews and non-Jews, including Catholics, in conflict with the Concordat, and Pius XI protested in public.
- Support was not control: many Catholics, especially young people, did not become Fascist, and the treaties did not make Italy more religious.

**Routes to a judgement.** (a) The regime gained more in the short term and in politics (legitimacy, consent), while the Church gained institutional security that outlasted the regime. (b) The benefits were of different kinds, so the comparison must be made against stated criteria. (c) The conflicts of 1931 and 1938 show that the Church's gain was real, because it could still resist.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines criteria, weighs both sides throughout, shows the connections (support bought, independence kept) and reaches a clear, supported judgement. *Level 3 (7–9):* lists the terms of the treaties with some analysis, argues one side ("Mussolini won everything"), and the judgement is weakly supported. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise detail, selected for the argument (11 February 1929, 1,750 million lire, the 98 per cent plebiscite, 29 June 1931, the 1938 laws). *Level 3 (7–9):* general statements ("the Pope got a state and money") with some inaccuracy.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.stalin.2) **Section A: Stalin's Russia, 1924–41.** 'The Soviet policy of collective security failed in the years 1933–39 mainly because Britain and France would not co-operate.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section A, Stalin's Russia.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Why a policy failed, and whether one cause ("Britain and France would not co-operate") outweighs the others. The policy has to be defined: from 1933–34 Litvinov sought agreements with Britain, France and others, and worked through the League, to deter Nazi Germany. "Failed" needs a date (the Nazi–Soviet Pact of 23 August 1939 ended it) and "mainly" needs a test: whose decisions were necessary for the failure, and when. A top essay compares the Western powers' conduct with the Soviet Union's own and with other causes (Poland and Romania, Hitler's offer), and does not treat the outcome as fixed in 1933.

**For the view (Britain and France).**
- Appeasement: Britain and France would not commit themselves to eastern Europe. After the Anschluss, Litvinov proposed (17 March 1938) a conference of the powers to check aggression, and Britain did not take it up. At Munich on 29–30 September 1938 Britain, France, Germany and Italy settled the fate of Czechoslovakia, an ally of both France and the USSR, without inviting the Soviet Union. Stalin concluded that the West wished to turn Hitler eastwards.
- Non-intervention: Britain and France set up the Non-Intervention Committee over Spain (1936) while Germany and Italy supplied Franco, and Franco won in March 1939. Collective security was shown to be one-sided.
- Weak commitments: the Franco-Soviet Treaty of 2 May 1935 was never given a military convention to make it work, and the Czech treaty of 16 May 1935 bound the USSR to help only if France did so first, which France did not do in 1938.
- 1939: after Hitler occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia (March), Britain and France gave Poland a guarantee on 31 March without any plan with Moscow. The Anglo-French military mission reached Moscow by sea on 11 August without authority to sign an agreement.

**Against (other causes).**
- Soviet policy was not trusted: the Comintern had attacked Social Democrats until 1933, and the Popular Front (Seventh Congress, 25 July to 20 August 1935) looked to Western governments like a tactic, not a change.
- The Terror damaged Soviet credibility: Tukhachevsky and seven generals were shot on 12 June 1937, and 13 of 15 army commanders and 3 of 5 marshals were killed; Western governments, and Hitler, took the Red Army to be weak (later research suggests that the damage to the leadership was still serious although fewer officers were removed than was once believed).
- Stalin's own choices: aid to Spain was limited, cautious and tied up with the NKVD's suppression of the POUM; the "chestnuts" speech to the Eighteenth Congress (March 1939) said that the USSR would not pull them out of the fire for others; he dismissed Litvinov on 3 May 1939 and signed the Pact on 23 August, with a secret protocol dividing eastern Europe. The Soviet Union then invaded eastern Poland on 17 September 1939. Stalin chose an agreement that gave him territory and time.
- Poland and Romania refused to let Soviet troops cross their land, the practical condition of any Soviet help, and the reason was fear of the USSR, which neither London nor Paris could remove. Hitler offered a deal that the West could not match.

**Routes to a judgement.** (a) Before Munich, Western reluctance was the main reason: Soviet policy was arguably sincere in this period, and it had no partner who would bind itself. (b) After Munich, Stalin's own decision (Litvinov's dismissal and the Pact) was the last link, although Munich had done most to make it. (c) Distrust was mutual: both sides' suspicion made an alliance unlikely. A Level 5 essay chooses a test (necessity, or the date at which the outcome became likely) and says which side's conduct was decisive at each stage.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines the policy and its failure, weighs Western and Soviet conduct and third parties through the period, distinguishes before and after Munich, and judges "mainly". *Level 3 (7–9):* narrates 1933–39 with some analysis, and either blames the West ("Munich") or Stalin ("the Pact") alone. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise (2 May 1935, 29–30 September 1938, 3 May 1939, 11 August 1939, 23 August 1939; the Czech treaty's condition; the transit refusals). *Level 3:* general ("Britain and France did not trust Stalin and he signed a pact with Hitler"), with errors of date, or Munich confused with the Pact.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.hitler.1) **Section A: Hitler's Germany, 1929–41.** 'Hitler's appointment as Chancellor in January 1933 was the result of political intrigue, not of Nazi popular support.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section A, Hitler's Germany.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Two explanations are set against each other: intrigue (the deals and decisions of a few men) and popular support. Strong essays test the "not": they ask whether the two were separate, and how each worked.

**For intrigue.**
- Hitler was appointed, not elected: he never won a majority in a free election. In the election of 6 November 1932 the Nazi vote fell to 33.1 per cent (196 seats), down from 37.3 per cent and 230 seats in July; the party was short of funds and despondent.
- Hindenburg refused Hitler the Chancellorship on 13 August 1932 and offered the Vice-Chancellorship; the change came from the deals of January 1933. Papen, who was angry at Schleicher, met Hitler at the Cologne home of the banker Kurt von Schröder on 4 January 1933; he won over Hugenberg, Hindenburg's son Oskar, the head of the President's office Otto Meissner and, with landowners lobbying, Hindenburg himself.
- Schleicher fell on 28 January 1933 when Hindenburg refused him an emergency decree. Hitler took the oath on 30 January at the head of a coalition in which only three of the eleven ministers were Nazis (Hitler, Frick, Göring). Papen and his colleagues believed they had "boxed him in".
- Earlier intrigue had paved the way: Papen's lifting of the ban on the SA and SS (16 June 1932) and removal of the Prussian government (20 July 1932); Schleicher's failed Querfront and the loss of Gregor Strasser (8 December 1932).

**Against (popular support mattered).**
- Hitler took 36.8 per cent in the second round of the presidential election on 10 April 1932; in July 1932 the Nazis became the largest party with 37.3 per cent and 230 seats of 608, with about 13.7 million votes. The Depression and the failure of Müller's and Brüning's governments (rule by decree began on 30 March 1930) had produced this support: the Nazi vote rose from 18.3 per cent (107 seats) in September 1930 to 37.3 per cent in July 1932.
- The Nazis and Communists together held more than half of the Reichstag seats, so no government could have a majority without the Nazis, and the elite looked for a mass movement to give a conservative government support. Papen and Schleicher had failed because they had none.
- Hitler's own tactics depended on his popular strength: his refusal to take less than the Chancellorship in August 1932 was possible because he led the largest party and 13 million votes.
- The elite's fear of the Communists, and the SA's street power.

**Routes to a judgement.** The essay is best argued as "both, and the question's 'not' is wrong": popular support made Hitler the only possible leader of a mass-based government, and intrigue decided that he was appointed in January 1933 rather than not at all, at a moment when his vote had fallen. A top-level answer says which was more necessary (support, since without it there was no deal to make) and which decided the timing (intrigue).

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines both explanations, tests each, links them and reaches a clear supported judgement on the "not". *Level 3 (7–9):* narrates 1932–33 with some analysis; one side is described, not tested. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* election percentages and seats, names (Papen, Schleicher, Schröder, Hindenburg, Oskar Hindenburg, Meissner), dates (4, 28, 30 January) used precisely. *Level 3:* "Papen made a deal with Hitler" without dates or numbers, or confusion between the elections.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.usa45.1) **Section B: Truman, Eisenhower and post-war USA, 1945–61.** 'Truman won the presidential election of 1948 mainly because of the mistakes of his opponent, Thomas Dewey.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section B, post-war USA, 1945–61.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Whether Truman's victory came from what Dewey did wrong or from what Truman and the voters did. "Mainly" asks for a ranking, and a good essay tests it: would Truman have won against a better campaign, and would Dewey have won against a better Truman? The result was close in three states, so the argument about "enough" matters. The date limit is the 1948 election; the Fair Deal and McCarthyism after it are outside the question.

**For the view (Dewey's mistakes).**
- Overconfidence: every poll gave Dewey a lead (Gallup's last poll, 15–25 October, had Dewey 49.5 per cent and Truman 44.5), Roper stopped polling in September, and Newsweek's October survey of fifty journalists forecast a Dewey win. Dewey, who had lost to Roosevelt in 1944 but was efficient and well organised, waged a cautious campaign of general speeches that avoided controversy.
- No answer to Truman's attacks: he did not defend the Republican 80th Congress, which he did not control but had to answer for, and did not attack Truman's record.
- Style: cold and remote to many voters, against Truman's plain talk.
- The margin was thin: Truman won Ohio (7,107 votes), California (17,865) and Illinois (33,612) by less than one point each, together about 58,600 votes (78 electoral votes); had Dewey won any two, Truman would have fallen short of the 266 needed and the House of Representatives would have chosen. The Republicans also assumed that the voters who had backed them in 1946 would stay.

**Against (what Truman and the voters did).**
- Strategy and effort: the Clifford memorandum of November 1947 planned to rebuild the New Deal coalition by running against a Republican Congress; the whistle-stop tours (over 300 speeches, 20,000 to 30,000 miles by different counts), the Democratic convention speech of 15 July, the "Turnip Day" session of Congress on 26 July and the attack on the "do-nothing" Congress gave him the initiative.
- The coalition: unions angered by Taft–Hartley, which Truman had vetoed; farmers, hit by falling grain prices and the lapse of the Commodity Credit Corporation's storage powers (Dexter, Iowa, on 18 September; he carried Iowa, Wisconsin, Minnesota and Ohio); Black voters in the northern cities, after the civil rights message of 2 February, the convention plank of 14 July (651½ to 582½) and Executive Order 9981 of 26 July; recognition of Israel on 14 May.
- The splits helped: the Dixiecrats (17 July; Thurmond won four states and 39 electoral votes) freed Truman to court Black voters, and Wallace's Progressives (about 2.4 per cent), tied to the Communists, let him answer the charge of softness.
- Cold War policy was popular: the Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan (passed April 1948) and the Berlin airlift (from June) left Dewey, who supported them, no foreign-policy issue.

**Routes to a judgement.** Dewey's mistakes were permissive: they left Truman room to campaign, but they did not give him the votes. Truman had to win the farmers, the unions and the Black voters in the key states, and he did. A strong essay uses the closeness of the result to argue both ways: a small change in Dewey's campaign could have flipped three states, so the mistakes were necessary to the result; but Truman's coalition had made it close enough for them to matter, so his campaign was the more important cause. The ranking has to be argued, with a test (necessary, or sufficient, or decisive in the closest states).

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines "mainly", weighs Dewey's errors against Truman's campaign, the coalition and the splits, uses the three close states, and reaches a supported judgement. *Level 3 (7–9):* lists reasons for Truman's win with some explanation, one-sided ("the polls were wrong") and a weakly supported judgement. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise (303 to 189 to 39 electoral votes, 26 July 1948, Ohio, California and Illinois, Dexter, the 266 needed, Executive Order 9981). *Level 3:* general ("Truman toured by train and Dewey was complacent"), with confusion of the Dixiecrats and Wallace, or of 1948 with 1944 and 1952.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.usa61.4) **Section B: A time of challenges: the USA, 1961–74.** Assess the extent to which the women's movement achieved its aims in the years 1963–74.

A: **Mark scheme: Section B, a time of challenges.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** How far a movement succeeded. "Its aims" must be defined, since the movement had two wings: liberal feminists (NOW, from 1966: equal rights in law, jobs and education) and women's liberation (from 1967–68: changes in family life, sexuality and culture). "The extent" asks for a measure, and strong essays use more than one: law, the economy, and attitudes, with opposition as a fourth. The dates are 1963 (*The Feminine Mystique*, the Equal Pay Act) to 1974; later events (the failure of the ERA to be ratified in 1982) are outside them, although the position by 1974 can be shown.

**Where it succeeded.**
- Law and equal opportunity: the Equal Pay Act (10 June 1963); Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which banned sex discrimination in employment after Howard Smith of Virginia proposed adding "sex" to it; Executive Order 11375 (1967), adding sex to the ban on discrimination by federal contractors; the Equal Employment Opportunity Act of 1972, giving the EEOC power to sue; Title IX of 23 June 1972, barring sex discrimination in federally funded education.
- The ERA: Congress passed it on 22 March 1972 by large majorities; 30 states had ratified by the end of 1973.
- Reproductive rights: *Roe v. Wade* (22 January 1973; seven votes to two, with *Doe v. Bolton* the same day) made abortion in the first three months a constitutional right in every state; the pill had been on sale since 1960.
- Change in attitudes and opportunities: women's labour-force participation rose from 37.7 per cent (1960) to 43.3 per cent (1970) and 45.7 per cent (1974); more women entered college and the professions; consciousness-raising, the Miss America protest (7 September 1968) and a mass of new groups made the personal political.

**Where it fell short.**
- Pay and jobs: women's median pay was about 60.7 per cent of men's in 1960 and about 59.4 per cent in 1970; most women still worked in low-paid clerical, teaching and nursing jobs; the EEOC was slow to enforce Title VII, which is why NOW was founded.
- The ERA stalled: Phyllis Schlafly's STOP ERA (1972) mobilised conservative and religious women, and by 1977 the ERA had 35 of the 38 states it needed.
- Divisions: over abortion (some members left NOW in 1968 to found the Women's Equity Action League), between liberal and liberationist wings, and between white middle-class leaders and Black and working-class women, who often had different priorities.
- Opposition: churches and the right-to-life movement after *Roe*; the backlash helped to shape the New Right after 1974.

**Routes to a judgement.** A strong essay says that the movement won most of its legal aims by 1974 and little of its economic or cultural aims, and that "extent" depends on whose aims are measured: the liberal feminists' (mostly achieved in law, stalled on the ERA) or the liberationists' (partly, in attitudes and personal life). It should also say that law changed faster than life. A Level 5 essay chooses its tests and applies them to each wing.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines the aims by wing and measures achievement by several tests, weighs success and failure throughout, and reaches a supported judgement on "the extent". *Level 3 (7–9):* describes the movement and its laws with some analysis, one-sided ("they won equality" or "nothing changed"), with a weakly supported conclusion. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise (10 June 1963, 22 March 1972, 22 January 1973, 37.7 to 45.7 per cent, 60.7 to 59.4 per cent, NOW 1966, Schlafly, 35 of 38 states). *Level 3:* general ("women got equal pay and abortion"), with the Equal Pay Act, Title VII and Title IX confused or Roe put in the wrong decade.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.usa74.3) **Section B: The USA, 1974–93.** 'Human rights were the guiding principle of President Carter's foreign policy in the years 1977–81.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section B, the USA, 1974–93.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Whether human rights were the main principle behind Carter's decisions across the world, or one aim among several (security, arms control, oil, the Cold War). "Guiding principle" needs a test: what did Carter do when rights conflicted with another interest? The dates run to the end of his term, so the shift after the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan counts. Strong essays use more than Latin America, and do not turn the question into "was Carter a failure?".

**For the view.**
- The principle was declared and organised: "our commitment to human rights must be absolute" (inaugural address, 20 January 1977); the Notre Dame speech of 22 May 1977 on freedom from an "inordinate fear of communism". A Bureau of Human Rights and Humanitarian Affairs was set up under Patricia Derian, with annual country reports, and Presidential Directive 30 (February 1978) tied aid to rights records.
- Action in Latin America: Argentina (arms sales and training banned by the Humphrey–Kennedy amendment of 1978; credit cut; the Inter-American Commission allowed to visit in 1979); Chile (pressure on Pinochet after the Letelier murder of 1976; loans opposed); Brazil (which rejected American military aid in 1977 after criticism); Nicaragua (aid made conditional, military aid to the National Guard cut in February 1979, and Somoza urged to go in June 1979 rather than propped up).
- The Panama Canal treaties (signed 7 September 1977; Senate 68–32 in 1978) were presented as an end to a colonial relic.

**Against the view.**
- Inconsistency: Carter toasted the Shah on 31 December 1977 as the leader of "an island of stability" while SAVAK tortured opponents; South Korea, the Philippines, Indonesia and Saudi Arabia (important for bases and oil) were not pressed; China was recognised (1 January 1979) with little reference to rights.
- Other principles drove major decisions: Camp David (5–17 September 1978) and the Egypt–Israel treaty (26 March 1979) were driven by the aim of peace, and the exclusion of the Soviet Union from the Middle East; SALT II (June 1979) by arms control; Brzezinski's anti-Soviet view carried more weight from 1978.
- After Afghanistan the Cold War took over: the grain embargo and Olympic boycott, the Carter Doctrine of January 1980 on the Persian Gulf, a Rapid Deployment Force and higher defence spending; Vance resigned in April 1980 after the Iran rescue attempt.
- Limits of leverage: Argentina turned to the Soviet Union for trade and refused the grain embargo; Pinochet stayed until 1990; in Nicaragua the policy neither saved a moderate outcome nor kept the Sandinistas friendly. Jeane Kirkpatrick (*Commentary*, November 1979) said that the policy damaged friendly authoritarian regimes.

**Routes to a judgement.** Human rights were a guiding principle where they were cheap to apply (weak states needing aid) and in the rhetoric and institutions of 1977–78, but when a conflict arose, security, oil and the Cold War won: the Shah, Saudi Arabia, China, and after December 1979 almost everywhere. A good essay says "one of several principles" and ranks them with the test of what Carter chose when they conflicted. A Level 5 essay can also argue that the principle outlived Carter, as a lasting subject in American policy.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines "guiding principle", tests it across regions and over time against competing aims, and judges. *Level 3 (7–9):* describes the human rights policy and a few cases with some analysis, one-sided ("he put rights first" or "it was hypocrisy"). *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise (20 January and 22 May 1977, PD-30, the Humphrey–Kennedy amendment, 31 December 1977, 1 January 1979, February and June 1979 for Nicaragua, the Carter Doctrine). *Level 3:* general, with Camp David or Iran treated as human rights policy, and errors on cases.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.soviet.3) **Section C: The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953–91.** Evaluate the view that Gorbachev's reforms made the crisis of the Soviet Union worse rather than better in the years 1985–91.

A: **Mark scheme: Section C, the Soviet Empire.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Whether, by 1991, Gorbachev's reforms had left the USSR in a worse position than the one he inherited. "The crisis" must be defined (an economy that was slowing, a system that depended on secrecy and force, a costly empire and arms race), and "worse or better" needs a test: for the economy, for the stability of the system, for the people, for foreign relations. The essay must also deal with the counterfactual: the crisis was there in 1985, so what would have happened without reform? The satellite states' revolutions of 1989 and the coup of August 1991 belong to the next key question, and are relevant here only as evidence of the effects.

**For the view (worse).**
- The economy: perestroika weakened the plan without putting a market in its place. The Law on State Enterprise (1987; in force from 1 January 1988) and the Law on Cooperatives (26 May 1988) left prices fixed and state orders in place, so wages rose faster than goods; official GNP fell by about 2 per cent in 1990 (a CIA estimate gives 4–5 per cent), and output in the first quarter of 1991 was about 8 per cent lower than a year earlier; coupons were used across the country for sugar, tea, soap and cigarettes in 1990–91.
- Fragmentation: regions held back food and the republics passed laws above the Union's in a "war of laws" in 1990; Russia declared sovereignty on 12 June 1990; Moscow could not collect the taxes that the Union needed, and the deficit grew.
- Self-inflicted losses: the anti-alcohol decree of 7 May 1985 cost the state 10 to 15 billion roubles a year in revenue; the hard-currency debt rose from about 26 billion dollars at the end of 1984 to between about 55 and 80 billion by 1990–91; Pavlov's withdrawal of 50 and 100 rouble notes (22 January 1991) and price rises (2 April 1991) were unpopular.
- Political damage: glasnost revealed the failings of the system and its past; Article 6 was repealed in March 1990, ending the party's monopoly; nationalists and Yeltsin (elected President of Russia in June 1991 with about 57 per cent) gained platforms against Gorbachev, who was chosen President by the Congress and never won a popular vote.

**Against (not worse, or not caused by the reforms).**
- The crisis came first: Soviet growth had slowed to about 1.8 per cent a year in 1980–85 from about 4.9 per cent in the 1960s; the economy relied on oil and gas, was in technological decline and had a hidden deficit. When the oil price fell by more than half in 1986 (from about 30 dollars to about 10), the shock was external. The Chernobyl accident (26 April 1986; one estimate puts the cost to the state at 18 billion roubles) showed the old system's failings.
- Gains for the people and the state: release of Sakharov (December 1986) and political prisoners; publication of banned books; the Law on the Press (June 1990); the competitive elections of 26 March 1989 for the Congress of People's Deputies; thousands of independent societies.
- Foreign relations improved: the INF Treaty (8 December 1987), the speech to the United Nations (7 December 1988) announcing troop cuts, the end of the Afghan war, and a lower arms burden.
- The old way had no future: Andropov's discipline had failed, and without change the gap with the West would have grown. Many of the new troubles (shortages, the republics) were produced by the freedom that reform allowed and not by bad policy.

**Routes to a judgement.** The economic record is the strongest for the view: on the standard measures, 1991 was worse than 1985. But on freedom, foreign relations and the end of the arms race, it was better. A good essay therefore says that the reforms exposed and accelerated a crisis they did not create, and chooses the test that matters most (the economy, or the survival of the Union). It may argue that the reforms were not the right ones, not that they were wrong in principle: a half-reformed economy (reform without a market) was worse than either the old plan or a clean break. A Level 5 essay takes the 1985 position as its baseline.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines the crisis, uses more than one test, deals with the counterfactual, and reaches a supported judgement. *Level 3 (7–9):* describes glasnost and perestroika and their results with some analysis, one-sided ("they failed" or "he had no choice"). *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise (the 1987 and 1988 laws, the 1990 output figures, 12 June 1990, March 1990, 8 December 1987, the oil price fall, the 1980–85 growth rate). *Level 3:* general ("Gorbachev introduced glasnost and perestroika and the USSR collapsed"), with dates confused or the causes of the 1991 collapse mixed in.

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.safrica.1) **Section C: The end of minority rule in South Africa and Zimbabwe, 1948–94.** 'The National Party won the general election of 1948 more because of the electoral system than because of the appeal of Afrikaner nationalism.' Evaluate this view.

A: **Mark scheme: Section C, South Africa and Zimbabwe.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** Which of two explanations of one result was the stronger. The two are compared, and the other reasons (the war, the campaign of the United Party) are weighed against them. The result is itself a puzzle: the party with fewer votes won more seats. A good essay uses a test, such as necessity (would the National Party have won under another system?) and sufficiency (could the electoral system alone have produced the win?). The date limit is the election of 26 May 1948; the apartheid laws that followed are not asked about.

**For the electoral system.**
- The result: the Reunited National Party (HNP) won 70 seats on 37.7 per cent of the vote and its ally, the Afrikaner Party, 9 seats on 3.9 per cent, so 79 of 150 seats on about 41.6 per cent; the United Party and Labour won 71 seats on about 51.7 per cent. The United Party had more votes than any other party.
- Why: the Constitution of 1910 let the boundary commission give rural seats up to 30 per cent fewer voters than urban ones. The UP and Labour needed about 7,800 votes for each seat, and the National Party and Afrikaner Party about 5,600. The UP piled up huge majorities in the cities; the National Party won small rural seats narrowly. One estimate says that with equal votes the UP would have won about 80 seats and its opponents about 60.
- Only whites could vote (about a fifth of the population), so the contest was among whites.

**For Afrikaner nationalism (and against the view).**
- Memory and grievance: the Boer War (about 26,000 Afrikaner women and children are thought to have died in the camps), anglicisation and the Voortrekker Centenary of 1938.
- Organisation: the Broederbond (1918), the churches, newspapers, the Federation of Afrikaans Cultural Societies (1929), Afrikaner banks and insurance companies (Volkskas, Sanlam), and the Economic Movement of 1939–40.
- Poor Afrikaners in the cities who feared competition from African workers; the split over the war (4 September 1939, 80 votes to 67); the reunion of Hertzog's supporters with Malan in 1940 and the pact with Havenga in 1947.
- The boundaries explain the size of the margin, not the direction of the swing: the rural seats were rural because of who lived there, and the National Party also did well among Afrikaans-speaking voters in the towns.

**Other reasons.**
- The war: urbanisation, the 1946 miners' strike (12 August), squatter camps and shortages; Smuts abroad for much of the war.
- The UP's campaign: divided, vague about race ("segregation had fallen on evil days"), an ageing leader who lost his own seat at Standerton, and the Fagan Report (February 1948), which accepted African urbanisation. The National Party offered the Sauer Report's "apartheid", a clear if not united answer.

**Routes to a judgement.** The electoral system was necessary for the victory (equal seats would probably have given the UP a majority) but nationalism made the National Party the largest party in the rural areas and gave it the base that the system rewarded: the two were not alternatives. A good essay argues that the system explains why the National Party won in 1948 rather than later, and nationalism explains why the party was able to win at all, and then says which is the better answer to "more". It can also bring in the UP's weakness as a third cause. A Level 5 essay uses a clear test.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* defines both explanations, tests each with the figures, connects them to the war and the campaign, and judges "more". *Level 3 (7–9):* lists reasons for the win with some analysis, one-sided ("nationalism won it"), with a weakly supported conclusion. *Table B, Level 5 (13–15):* precise (26 May 1948, 79 to 71 seats, about 41.6 and 51.7 per cent, the 30 per cent rural loading, 7,800 and 5,600 votes a seat, Malan, Havenga, Smuts at Standerton, the Broederbond, the Sauer and Fagan reports). *Level 3:* general ("the National Party was supported by Afrikaners and the system helped it"), with the seats and votes confused or apartheid laws described.
:::

Q: [30 marks] (#hist.d.malaysia.4) **Section C: The route to independence: Malaysia and Indonesia, c.1909–67.** Assess the reasons why the Dutch failed to re-establish their control over Indonesia in the years 1945–49.

A: **Mark scheme: Section C, Malaysia and Indonesia.** Table A (AO2) out of 15 and Table B (AO1) out of 15. Accept any other valid response.

**What the question asks.** The reasons for one outcome in four years. The outcome ("failed to re-establish control") needs care: the Dutch won the military engagements and took the Republic's capital, yet lost. A top essay sorts the reasons into Indonesian, international and Dutch, shows how they worked together, and says which was decisive.

**Reasons (indicative content).**
- *Indonesian resistance and the survival of the Republic.* The proclamation of 17 August 1945 and the *pemuda*; an army that grew from Peta and local fighting groups, and could not be beaten in the open; the British landing and the battle of Surabaya (10 November 1945) showed the Republic's will to resist; the Dutch held the towns and plantations but not the villages. After the second police action (19 December 1948), when Sukarno and Hatta were captured, an Emergency Government carried on, the guerrilla war under Sudirman and Nasution continued, and the attack on Yogyakarta on 1 March 1949 showed that the Republic was alive. The Madiun affair (18 September 1948), in which the Republic suppressed a communist rising, won American respect.
- *International pressure.* The United Nations (the Committee of Good Offices) made it a world question; India and Australia raised it in 1947; Asian governments condemned the second police action. The strongest lever was the United States: after Madiun it saw the Republic as an anti-communist partner; on 22 December 1948 it suspended Marshall Plan aid to the Dutch East Indies, and in the spring of 1949 it let the Dutch know that aid to the Netherlands itself was at risk. Historians disagree about how much this threat decided the Dutch retreat (some argue that the Dutch were already changing course), so a strong answer weighs it rather than assumes it.
- *Dutch weakness.* An exhausted country after the German occupation; a costly army of over 100,000 men far from home; divided Dutch opinion; the failure of the Dutch federal policy, since the states they created had little support; the British had refused to fight for them and withdrew in November 1946; and the agreements (Linggadjati of 15 November 1946, signed 25 March 1947; the first police action of 21 July 1947; Renville of 17 January 1948) had not given a settlement.
- *The sequence.* The first police action (21 July 1947) and the second were military successes, but each brought international condemnation; after the second, the Roem–Van Roijen agreement (7 May 1949) and the Round Table Conference (23 August to 2 November 1949) led to the transfer of sovereignty on 27 December 1949.

**Connections and judgement.** The Republic's resistance gave the outside world something to support, and outside pressure raised the cost of continuing to a level the Dutch could not pay. The strongest answers argue that international pressure was decisive (it was the factor that ended the war), but that it could not have worked without the Republic's survival: the US would not have backed a Republic that had collapsed. Weak answers list the events of the revolution.

**Levels.** *Table A, Level 5 (13–15):* a range of reasons, linked, with a criterion (decisive or necessary) and a supported ranking. *Level 3 (7–9):* narrates the police actions and the agreements with some explanation of why the Dutch failed. *Table B, Level 5:* precise dates (19 December 1948, 7 May 1949, 27 December 1949) and the Marshall Plan lever. *Level 3:* general, with confusion between Linggadjati and Renville or between the two police actions.
:::

## After the paper: mark it, then use the result

**1. Mark each essay twice.** Table A is analysis and judgement (AO2); Table B is knowledge (AO1). For Table A ask: did I use a standard of judgement and keep to it? Did I weigh both sides throughout, not only in the conclusion? Is there a supported judgement that answers the question as it was set? For Table B ask: was my detail precise (dates, names, numbers), chosen for the argument and accurate? Choose the best fit and, between two levels, say why. Do not mark a question you did not answer.

| Level | Table A (AO2): analysis and judgement | Marks | Table B (AO1): knowledge | Marks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | full understanding; balanced and analytical; valid, wide-ranging criteria; sustained, supported judgement | 13–15 | a high level of relevant detail: selected, focused, wide-ranging, precise and accurate | 13–15 |
| 4 | good understanding; mostly analytical; valid criteria; some judgements partly substantiated | 10–12 | a good level: appropriately selected, mostly focused, mostly precise | 10–12 |
| 3 | understanding with some analysis; the argument lacks balance; begins to form a judgement, weakly substantiated | 7–9 | adequate: mostly appropriate, descriptive in places, narrow range | 7–9 |
| 2 | some understanding; descriptive; limited analysis; assertion rather than judgement | 4–6 | some relevant detail: narrative, frequently imprecise | 4–6 |
| 1 | addresses the topic, not the question; no analysis | 1–3 | limited knowledge; vague or inaccurate | 1–3 |

Each essay is out of 30 (15 and 15), and two essays make 60. The same level in both tables gives 26 to 30 at Level 5, 20 to 24 at Level 4, 14 to 18 at Level 3, 8 to 12 at Level 2 and 2 to 6 at Level 1. The course does not convert a total into a grade, because Cambridge sets grade boundaries after each series.

**2. Find the pattern across your two essays.** Did both essays reach a judgement, or did the second one trail off for lack of time? Did you tell the story in order (Level 2 and Level 3) or arrange the paragraphs by argument? Did your paragraphs each say whether they supported or challenged the view? Check the paper's pattern, because it repeats from essay to essay.

**3. Use your faults to choose your revision.** The chapter link under each question took you to the key question the essay needed. If a Level was lost on Table B, rebuild the dates, names and numbers for that topic. If it was lost on Table A, practise writing a plan with the criterion first, using the guide at [Paper 4: Depth study](#hist.exam.p4).
