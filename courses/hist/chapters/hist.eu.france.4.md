A: The Civil Constitution of 1790 made priests elected, state-paid officials. Priests who refused the oath of loyalty were "refractory" and were persecuted, which divided the country (and fed the Vendée rising).

## Napoleon Bonaparte's military reputation and political ambitions

### The rise of a Corsican officer

Napoleon Bonaparte was born on **15 August 1769** at Ajaccio in Corsica, an island France had taken over only a year earlier. His family were minor nobles, and he trained as an artillery officer at the military school at Brienne and then in Paris. Before the Revolution he was an unknown lieutenant. The Revolution's need for officers, and the emigration of many nobles, opened careers to talent, and Bonaparte supported the Jacobins.

His reputation was made in a series of campaigns, each helped by a lucky break or a patron:
- **Toulon, December 1793.** He commanded the artillery that drove the British out of the port, and at 24 he became a brigadier general.
- **Vendémiaire, October 1795.** Barras gave him command of the guns that swept the royalist sections from the streets of Paris (see [France (3)](#hist.eu.france.3)). It won him the Directory's gratitude, and a command. In March 1796 he married Joséphine de Beauharnais, a widow with friends among the Directory's leaders.
- **Italy, 1796–97.** Given the poorest army, he beat the Austrians and Piedmontese in a series of quick victories (Lodi in May 1796, Arcole in November, Rivoli in January 1797). His army lived off the country and he sent money and art treasures to Paris. In October 1797 he negotiated the Treaty of **Campo Formio** with Austria largely on his own terms. Soldiers' love and public fame followed.
- **Egypt, 1798–99.** He took an army to Egypt to threaten British trade with India. It conquered Cairo (the battle of the Pyramids, July 1798), but the British fleet under Nelson destroyed the French ships at the Nile on 1–2 August 1798 and stranded the army. In August 1799, as the news from Europe became worse, he left his army secretly and sailed home. This was not the act of a hero, but his bulletins and newspapers presented it as a triumph.

:::def Propaganda
Information, images and slogans that a government spreads, and the tight control of everything else, to shape what the public believes about it. Napoleon was the first ruler to manage his image so thoroughly, from his bulletins of 1796 onwards.
:::

### Reputation: military skill and image-making

Part of Bonaparte's reputation was earned. He planned quickly, used artillery well and kept his troops moving faster than the enemy expected, and as a young man he showed real energy and talent. Part of it was made. His newspapers (the *Courrier de l'Armée d'Italie*) and bulletins gave the public a picture of the victor of Italy, and painters such as Antoine-Jean Gros made him the hero of Arcole, with a flag in his hand on the bridge. By 1799 he was the most famous soldier in France, at a time when the Directory had no heroes and the armies had just been beaten in Italy.

### Political ambitions

Bonaparte was a general of the Republic, but he acted like a politician. In Italy he made treaties and set up sister republics without waiting for the Directory's instructions. He had supported Fructidor in 1797 by sending General Augereau to Paris, and he saw how weak the Directory was. In 1799 his family and friends, among them his brother Lucien, president of the Council of Five Hundred, and Talleyrand, a former bishop and statesman, believed that he wanted power, and a group of politicians looking for a general found in him the man they needed. His own motives are debated. Some historians see long-planned ambition; others see an opportunist who took the chance that Sieyès offered. Either way, he had the reputation and the army that no politician had.

## The coup of 1799

### Why the Directory was ready to fall

By the autumn of 1799 the Directory had lost the confidence of nearly every group (see [France (3)](#hist.eu.france.3)). The Second Coalition had driven the French out of Italy, the Prairial coup in June had forced out three Directors, there were fears of a Jacobin revival in the Councils and of a royalist rising in the west, and the treasury was empty. **Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès**, a Director since May, wanted a new constitution with a stronger executive and a more limited electorate. He needed a general to supply the force. He first chose General Joubert, who was killed at Novi in August, and then Bonaparte, whose unexpected return from Egypt (he landed at Fréjus on 9 October 1799) was greeted with joy across France.

### The coup of Brumaire (9–10 November 1799)

The plotters were Sieyès, the Director Roger Ducos, Talleyrand, the minister of police Fouché, and the Bonaparte brothers, with General Murat and some of the Parisian garrison. The plan was legal in form. On **18 Brumaire (9 November)** the Council of Ancients, warned of a "Jacobin conspiracy", voted to move both councils to the palace of Saint-Cloud, outside Paris, and put Bonaparte in command of the Paris troops. Sieyès and Ducos resigned as Directors, Barras was persuaded to do the same and the two remaining Directors were held under guard. The aim was to make the two councils vote a new constitution under the protection of soldiers.

It nearly failed. On **19 Brumaire (10 November)** at Saint-Cloud Bonaparte went before the Ancients and made a muddled, nervous speech. He fared worse before the Council of Five Hundred, where many deputies, including Jacobins, shouted "Outlaw him!" and jostled him, and he was led out dazed. His brother Lucien, as president of the Council, refused to put the vote to outlaw him, left the chair, and told the guard that a group of deputies were threatening his brother with daggers. Murat's grenadiers cleared the hall. That evening a handful of deputies who remained voted to abolish the Directory and to appoint three provisional Consuls, **Bonaparte, Sieyès and Ducos**. The politicians had asked a general for help and now he commanded them.

:::key Why the coup succeeded (and nearly failed)
1. **Weariness**: the French were tired of coups, inflation and the war, and wanted order. Few would defend the Directory.
2. **A general**: Bonaparte's fame and the loyalty of the troops gave the plotters real force, as at Fructidor.
3. **Inside help**: Sieyès, Lucien Bonaparte, Talleyrand and Fouché were Directory insiders who knew how to use the law.
4. **Luck and nerve**: Bonaparte's performance at Saint-Cloud was poor, and Lucien's quick action saved the day. Brumaire was not a smooth military coup.
:::

:::warn Do not say Brumaire was a simple military takeover
It was a coup by politicians who needed a general, carried out in a legal form (the Ancients' decree) and almost wrecked by Bonaparte's own failure on 19 Brumaire. Sieyès expected to be the main author of the new constitution. Say that the coup succeeded because of weariness with the Directory, the plotters' organisation and the army, and add that Bonaparte quickly took over the new regime from Sieyès.
:::

## The Consulate, 1799–1804

### The Constitution of the Year VIII

Sieyès drafted the new constitution, but Bonaparte rewrote it to give himself the real power. It was published on 13 December 1799 and put into effect on 25 December, and then presented to the people in a **plebiscite** (a vote on a single proposal).

The key features were:
- **Three Consuls** appointed for ten years, of whom the **First Consul** (Bonaparte) held almost all power: he proposed laws, appointed ministers, officials, judges and diplomats, commanded the army and conducted foreign policy. The Second and Third Consuls, **Cambacérès** and **Lebrun**, only advised.
- **A Council of State**, appointed by the First Consul, which drafted laws and settled administrative disputes.
- **A Tribunate** of 100 members, which discussed the bills but could not vote, and a **Legislative Body** of 300, which voted but could not debate. Neither could propose or amend.
- **A Senate** of 80 members for life, which chose the members of the other two bodies and was supposed to guard the constitution.
- **Lists of notables.** All men over 21 had the vote, but only to draw up lists of candidates at the level of the commune, department and nation. This was "confidence from below, authority from above".

The constitution then went to a plebiscite. The official result was about 3.0 million votes for and only about 1,600 against. Historians believe that the figures were doctored: the interior minister, Lucien Bonaparte, added about a million extra "yes" votes, and the real number for was nearer to 1.5 million. Few voters were willing to say no.

### Napoleon's initiatives as First Consul

In its first years the Consulate settled, or seemed to settle, almost every question that the Directory had left open. The list is long. Learn it as five groups.

**Making peace.** Bonaparte crossed the Alps in the spring of 1800 and beat the Austrians at **Marengo** (14 June 1800). General Moreau's victory at Hohenlinden (December) led to the Treaty of **Lunéville** with Austria (9 February 1801), and the Peace of **Amiens** with Britain (25 March 1802) brought the first general peace in Europe for ten years. For most French people, peace was the First Consul's greatest gift, and it bought him loyalty.

**Healing divisions.** He ended the civil war in the west by a mixture of force, negotiation and concession (the Concordat, below). In April 1802 an **amnesty** allowed most émigrés to come home, and tens of thousands did, after promising loyalty; those who had bought national lands kept them. He gave posts to former Jacobins, royalists and Girondins alike, on condition that they served him. His slogan was national "fusion".

**Reorganising government.** In February 1800 he created **prefects**, one in each department, appointed by the First Consul and powerful (see below); in 1800 he reorganised the courts, and in 1802 he founded the **Legion of Honour**, a new order of merit that rewarded soldiers and civilians alike. The decision passed the Tribunate and Legislative Body only narrowly: many republicans saw it as a revival of titles.

**Money.** The Bank of France (February 1800), a professional tax-collecting service and the stable franc of 1803.

**Law, schools and religion.** The Civil Code, the lycées and the Concordat (below).

### The end of the Republic: Consul for life and Emperor

Bonaparte used his popularity to reach for more. After Amiens the Senate offered him the consulship for ten more years; he asked the people instead, in a plebiscite, whether he should be Consul for life. The official vote was about 3.57 million for and about 8,400 against, and on **2 August 1802** he became **Consul for life**, with the right to name his successor. The new constitution (the Constitution of the Year X) also gave him greater control of the Senate and reduced the Tribunate to 50 members; critics such as the writer Benjamin Constant had already been removed from it in 1802.

The step to Empire followed plots against his life: the explosion of a bomb in the rue Saint-Nicaise (December 1800) and a royalist conspiracy led by Georges Cadoudal in 1804. Napoleon's reaction in March 1804 was harsh. He had the Duc d'Enghien, a Bourbon prince, seized in Germany and shot at Vincennes on 21 March 1804 (the same day the Civil Code was promulgated). His advisers argued that a hereditary Empire would discourage plots, because killing one man could not end the regime. A Senate decree of 18 May 1804 proclaimed him **Emperor of the French**, and the plebiscite gave about 3.57 million for and about 2,600 against. On **2 December 1804** he was crowned at Notre-Dame in Paris in the presence of Pope Pius VII. Napoleon took the crown from the Pope's hands and put it on his own head. He was 35.

## Establishing authoritarian control

The syllabus asks for the *means* by which Napoleon built authoritarian control. Learn them as six headings.

:::key The means of control
1. **Ministries and the Council of State**: government by appointed officials, not elected politicians.
2. **Prefects and local government**: every department governed from Paris.
3. **Police and the judiciary**: Fouché's secret police and special courts.
4. **Propaganda and censorship**: control of newspapers, books, art and ceremony.
5. **Control of elections**: plebiscites and indirect election in which the voters could not choose.
6. **The Empire**: a hereditary monarchy and a new nobility tied to the regime.
:::

### New ministries and the Council of State

Napoleon governed through able, hardworking men, whom he chose for talent and who owed their careers to him. The ministers included Talleyrand (foreign affairs), Gaudin (finance, from 1799 to 1814) and Fouché (police), and he created new ministries for the public treasury (1801) and for religious affairs (under Portalis, 1804) and reorganised the others. The ministers answered to him, not to the Legislative Body. The **Council of State** was his main instrument. It met in his presence, often for many hours, and its members (lawyers, former revolutionaries and experts such as Cambacérès, Portalis and Tronchet) drafted the Civil Code and the main laws, heard administrative disputes and advised him. Napoleon took part in the debates and was often well informed.

### Prefects and local government

The law of **17 February 1800** (28 Pluviôse VIII) created a **prefect** in every department, appointed by the First Consul, with authority over the police, tax collection, conscription and public works. Beneath them came sub-prefects, and the mayors of towns and villages were appointed too, not elected. This was a system of centralised control that the Committee of Public Safety had tried to build in 1793–94 and that no earlier government had made to work. It also served the French state well, because prefects collected taxes, built roads, raised conscripts and reported on opinion. Many prefects were able administrators who held office for many years.

### Reform of the police force and the judiciary

- **Police.** Fouché, a former Jacobin and a survivor of the Terror, was Minister of Police in 1799–1802 and 1804–10. He ran a network of informers who sent reports on public opinion and opened letters. A Prefect of Police for Paris was created in 1800. The police were also given the task of watching former Jacobins and royalists. A decree of 1810 set up state prisons in which people could be held without trial.
- **Judiciary.** In 1800 the courts were reorganised, with a tribunal in each arrondissement and courts of appeal, and the judges were appointed by the government, not elected. Juries were retained for the most serious crimes but were selected by the prefects. Special tribunals without juries, set up in 1801, tried brigandage and some political crimes. Judges in ordinary cases were independent in practice; judges in political cases were not.

### Propaganda and censorship

- **Newspapers.** In January 1800 Bonaparte closed 60 of the 73 political papers of Paris, leaving 13; by a decree of 1811 only four survived in Paris, all of them under official supervision. The *Moniteur* became the official voice of the regime. Books and plays were censored, the number of printers was limited and a director of the press was appointed in 1810.
- **Bulletins and images.** The bulletins of the Grande Armée announced his victories to the whole country. The great painters of the age (David and his pupil Gros) made Napoleon a hero in huge canvases: David's *Napoleon Crossing the Alps* (1801) showed him on a rearing horse, although he had made the crossing on a mule. The coronation and the Empire's ceremonies were designed for effect.
- **Education and religion.** The **Imperial Catechism** (1806) was taught to every child in the Empire. It told Christians that they owed Napoleon "love, respect, obedience, fidelity" and military service, and a saint, **Saint Napoleon**, was given a feast day on 15 August 1806 (Napoleon's birthday, and a Catholic feast, the Assumption).
- **Cult of the leader.** Coins, medals, monuments (the Arc de Triomphe was begun in 1806) and the Legion of Honour made the regime's presence felt everywhere.

### Control of elections

Elections barely existed. Under the Year VIII constitution, voters only made lists of notables, and the Senate chose from the lists. The plebiscites of 1800, 1802 and 1804 asked the people to approve decisions already taken, in a vote where the voter signed a register in public, and the answers were counted by the government's own officials.

| Plebiscite | Question | Official "yes" | Official "no" |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1800 | The Constitution of the Year VIII | about 3.0 million | about 1,600 |
| 1802 | Napoleon Consul for life | about 3.57 million | about 8,400 |
| 1804 | A hereditary Empire | about 3.57 million | about 2,600 |

From 1802 the members of electoral colleges held office for life and were chosen from among the 600 highest taxpayers in each department, a system that gave power to the wealthy and loyal. The Tribunate was abolished in 1807.

### Emperor Napoleon, 1804

The Empire added a court, a new nobility and a hereditary succession. Napoleon's relatives became kings and princes, marshals became dukes and in 1808 he created an imperial nobility of titles that rewarded service to the state rather than birth. Exiles and new rich were tied to the regime. It was the opposite of the principle of equality, yet it held the support of the army and the wealthy.

:::key Authoritarian control: how it worked
- **Centralisation**: ministers and prefects made France a country run from Paris.
- **Fear**: police, special courts and prisons without trial made opposition dangerous.
- **Management of opinion**: censorship and propaganda shaped what the public could read and see.
- **Illusion of consent**: plebiscites with fixed outcomes and elections that chose nobody gave the regime a claim to popular approval.
- **Interest**: the army, the officials, the notables and the buyers of national lands each had a stake in his survival.
:::

## Napoleon's domestic reforms

### Legal: the Civil Code

Before the Revolution, France had hundreds of local legal systems. The Revolution began to unify the law, but it was Bonaparte who finished the work. A commission of four lawyers (Tronchet, Portalis, Bigot de Préameneu and Maleville), working with Cambacérès and the Council of State, produced the **Civil Code**, promulgated on **21 March 1804** (it was renamed the **Code Napoléon** in 1807). Napoleon himself attended many sessions. Four more codes followed (civil procedure 1806, commerce 1807, criminal procedure 1808, the Penal Code 1810).

:::def The Civil Code (Code Napoléon)
The single law code for all of France, promulgated on 21 March 1804. It confirmed the Revolution's principles of equality before the law, freedom of conscience and the protection of private property, ended feudal privilege, and gave a very strong authority to husbands and fathers.
:::

What the Code did:
- **Equality before the law**: one law for all citizens, with no legal privileges of birth, and an end to feudal dues and serfdom.
- **Property**: it protected private property absolutely, including the lands bought during the Revolution, and gave legal security to owners and contracts. This was a main reason why the propertied classes supported the regime.
- **Inheritance**: property had to be divided among all children (a fixed share for each), not left to the eldest son.
- **Civil marriage and divorce**: marriage became a civil contract. Divorce was allowed, but the Code treated the sexes differently: a husband could seek divorce for his wife's adultery, while a wife could do so only if the husband had kept his mistress in the family home.
- **Religion**: the state kept the registers of births, marriages and deaths, and freedom of belief was confirmed.
- **Workers**: employers' word was accepted in disputes over wages; combinations (trade unions and strikes) were banned, and workers were required to carry a work-book (*livret*) from 1803.

:::warn Do not call the Code liberal for women
Article 213 of the Civil Code states that "the husband owes protection to his wife, the wife obedience to her husband". Married women could not make contracts, sell property or go to court without their husband's permission, and fathers had strong powers over their children. The Code gave women less independence than the Revolution had, because it restored the head of the family. Say it confirmed legal equality for men, and made women legally dependent on their husbands.
:::

### Educational reform

The Revolution had planned a national school system but had not built one. Napoleon was interested in education as a tool for training servants of the state. The **law of 1 May 1802** created state secondary schools, the **lycées**, with strict military-style discipline (days run by the drum), a curriculum of Latin, mathematics and science, and scholarships for the sons of soldiers and officials. The **Imperial University** (created by law in 1806 and organised in 1808) set up a single national system under a Grand Master, with the baccalaureate exam (1808), teacher-training and a requirement that schools teach the Catholic religion and loyalty to the Emperor and his dynasty. The aim was to produce loyal officers, officials, lawyers and engineers. Primary schools were left to the communes and the Church, and girls' education was very limited: schools of the Legion of Honour, such as the one at Écouen, trained a few daughters of officers for a role as wives and mothers.

### Financial and economic reform

The new regime inherited bankruptcy; at once it made finance a priority, with Gaudin at the Ministry of Finance.
- **The Bank of France** (13 February 1800) was a private bank set up with the help of leading bankers, to lend to the state and discount bills. In 1803 it received the sole right to issue banknotes in Paris, and in 1806 its governor came under Napoleon's control. It gave the merchants and the state a source of credit and a currency people trusted.
- **The franc germinal** (law of 28 March 1803) fixed the franc at a set weight of silver, with gold coins at a fixed rate (the 20-franc *napoléon*). It was the first stable currency since 1789 and lasted until 1914.
- **Taxation.** Direct taxes (land, personal and door-and-window taxes) were assessed and collected by a professional service in the departments, building on what the Directory started, and a land survey (the *cadastre*) was begun in 1807. Napoleon avoided raising the direct taxes, which hit the landowners who backed him, and relied instead on **indirect taxes**: the *droits réunis* (1804), taxes on drink, tobacco and playing cards, and the revival of a salt tax in 1806, which people disliked because it recalled the gabelle. War also paid for war: the treasuries of conquered states were taxed or looted.
- **Bread prices.** Napoleon knew that bread riots had made the Revolution. He kept the price of bread in Paris stable by subsidising the bakers through a special fund (the *Caisse de service de la boulangerie*), by buying grain and building stores, and by sending police to deal with riots; people in the provinces were less protected. In the food crisis of 1811–12, after a poor harvest, he fixed grain prices again by law, as the Convention had done in 1793.
- **Trade and industry.** He encouraged new industry with exhibitions, roads, canals and ports, and protected French textile and ironworks from British competition (the cotton industry of Alsace and Normandy grew). Under the Continental System, however, the sea ports declined.

### Religious reform: the Concordat

The Revolution had left France divided over religion. Bonaparte aimed to settle the dispute, bring Catholics back to the regime and bring the Church under state control. After difficult negotiations with the Pope's envoy, Cardinal Consalvi, the **Concordat** was signed on **15 July 1801**, with Pope Pius VII.

:::def Concordat (1801)
The agreement of 15 July 1801 between Napoleon and Pope Pius VII. Catholicism was recognised as "the religion of the great majority of the French", bishops were nominated by the First Consul and given their authority by the Pope, the clergy were paid by the state, and the Church gave up its claim to the lands sold during the Revolution.
:::

What it did:
- It recognised the Catholic religion as that of most French people, without making it the state religion, and allowed public worship under police regulations. Protestants and Jews kept freedom of worship under separate legislation.
- Bishops were named by the First Consul and instituted by the Pope; the clergy were paid salaries by the state and swore loyalty.
- The Pope accepted the loss of Church lands, which was vital to the thousands of families who had bought them.
- In April 1802 Bonaparte added the **Organic Articles**, which he published without the Pope's agreement. They imposed state control: papal documents could not be published in France without government permission, seminaries and bishops were supervised, and the Church was brought into the administration.

The Concordat was published on Easter Sunday 1802 with a ceremony at Notre-Dame. Some generals and republicans disliked it; the Tribunate was lukewarm. It was a lasting success in settling the religious question (it lasted until 1905).

:::key Why did Napoleon sign the Concordat?
1. **To end religious division**, restore peace in the west and satisfy the Catholic majority.
2. **To undermine the royalists**: with the Church loyal to the regime, the Bourbons lost their greatest ally.
3. **To control the Church**: the First Consul chose the bishops and the state paid the priests.
4. **To protect the buyers of national land**: the Pope accepted the sale of Church property.
5. **To strengthen his image** at home and abroad as a peacemaker and the restorer of order.
:::

## How far was Napoleon's rule based on force or on consent?

This is the question behind most essay titles on the Consulate and Empire. Sort the evidence.

| Evidence of **force** | Evidence of **consent** |
|---|---|
| Police, informers, special courts, detention without trial | The Consulate ended instability: peace in 1801–02, order, a sound currency |
| Censorship (73 Paris papers cut to 13, then 4) | The Concordat and amnesty satisfied Catholics and returned émigrés |
| Doctored plebiscites (about a million votes added in 1800) | The Civil Code protected property and equality for the buyers of national land |
| Conscription enforced by gendarmes and fines on families | Careers open to talent: ordinary men could rise in the army and administration |
| The execution of the Duc d'Enghien (1804) | Victories made France proud (Marengo, Austerlitz, Jena) |
| The arrest of the Pope in 1809 | Real support from the notables, the army and those who feared the Bourbons |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Napoleon's rule rested on **both**. In the Consulate, consent was real among the propertied, the army and the Catholics who had gained from his settlement, and force was used mainly against the few who opposed him. As the wars grew, consent depended more and more on success, and force on conscription and censorship became heavier from about 1810. The best test is what happened in 1814: very few Frenchmen fought to save him, which suggests that the consent had been to victory and order, not to Napoleon himself.
:::

## Reasons for Napoleon's fall

The Empire at its height, in 1810–12, seemed unbeatable. Three years later it had collapsed. The syllabus lists the causes; learn them in three groups: the war with Britain, the problems at home and the failures abroad (Spain, Russia, Leipzig).

### The extent of the Empire by 1812

By 1812 Napoleon ruled or controlled most of Europe. This table is a schematic summary (not a precise map):

| Layer | Territories | Who ruled |
|---|---|---|
| **France itself** | 130 departments, more than 40 million people, including Belgium, the left bank of the Rhine, Piedmont, Genoa, Rome, Holland (annexed 1810), the Hanseatic ports and the Illyrian Provinces | Napoleon through prefects |
| **Satellite kingdoms** | Italy (Napoleon king; his stepson Eugène de Beauharnais viceroy), Naples (Murat), Spain (his brother Joseph from 1808), Westphalia (his brother Jérôme) | Napoleon's family and marshals |
| **Dependent states** | The Confederation of the Rhine (1806), the Grand Duchy of Warsaw (1807), the Swiss Confederation | Napoleon as protector |
| **Allies (under pressure)** | Austria (alliance after his marriage to the Austrian Marie-Louise in April 1810), Prussia (alliance 1812), Denmark | Independent rulers who feared him |
| **Enemies** | Britain, Portugal, and from June 1812 Russia | |

An empire of this size could be held only by an enormous army, and its weakness was that everything depended on victory.

### Declining popularity at home: conscription and the deteriorating economy

**Conscription.** Every year, young men were chosen by lot (the Jourdan law of 1798 still applied). The number called rose with each war; in 1813–14 the government took hundreds of thousands of young men, including boys who had not yet reached the age. A rich family could buy a substitute (*remplacement*), which made it a tax on the poor. Many conscripts avoided service. These "refractory" men hid, deserted or fled, and the government sent gendarmes and "mobile columns" into villages, quartered soldiers on their families and fined their parents. Estimates of French military deaths between 1803 and 1815 run to several hundred thousand, and some historians put the figure near a million. In areas such as the west, the south and the mountains conscription was deeply resented.

**The economy.** After the early years of order and revival, the economy deteriorated from 1810. The trade blockade damaged the ports (Bordeaux, Nantes, Marseille) and raised the price of colonial goods such as sugar and coffee. A commercial crisis in 1810–11 brought bankruptcies, credit shortages and unemployment, especially in textile towns. A poor harvest in 1811 pushed grain prices up in 1812. Taxes also rose, with the unpopular *droits réunis* and the salt tax.

**Other signs of trouble at home.** Napoleon's quarrel with the Pope, whom he had arrested in 1809 and kept prisoner until 1814, offended many Catholics. His divorce of Joséphine, who had no children, and his remarriage to Marie-Louise (April 1810) did not help; a son, the "King of Rome", was born in March 1811. In October 1812, when Napoleon was in Russia, General Malet, a prisoner, announced that the Emperor was dead and almost overthrew the government in a few hours, and few of the officials thought of the Emperor's baby son as his successor. The regime's loyalty rested on Napoleon alone.

### The failure to defeat Britain: Trafalgar and the Continental System

Britain was the enemy Napoleon could not reach. After he lost the chance of invading in 1805, when Nelson destroyed the French and Spanish fleets at **Trafalgar** (21 October 1805), Britain controlled the seas, paid for the coalitions against France and seized French colonies. Napoleon's answer was economic war.

:::def The Continental System
Napoleon's attempt to defeat Britain by closing the ports of Europe to British trade. It began with the Berlin Decree (21 November 1806), which declared the British Isles in a state of blockade and forbade all commerce with them, and the Milan Decree (17 December 1807), which extended the ban to neutral ships that obeyed British rules.
:::

Why it failed:
- **Smuggling.** The coastline of Europe was too long to police. British goods were smuggled through the Baltic, Heligoland and the Mediterranean, and the French customs, who were often bribed, could not stop them.
- **Britain suffered but survived.** British exports fell in 1808 and 1811–12, but it found new markets in Latin America and kept control of the sea, so it could continue to import and to trade.
- **France suffered too.** The ports declined, colonial goods became expensive, merchants went bankrupt and the poor paid more for sugar and coffee. Some industries (cotton, iron, beet sugar) gained from protection, but they could not make up for the losses.
- **It forced Napoleon into more wars.** To close every port he had to occupy Portugal and Spain, which led to the Peninsular War, and to quarrel with Russia when Tsar Alexander I, whose trade was hit, withdrew from the system at the end of 1810. The Russian invasion followed.

### The Peninsular War, 1808–14

In 1807 French troops crossed Spain to occupy Portugal, which was trading with Britain. In 1808 Napoleon forced the Spanish royal family to abdicate and placed his brother **Joseph** on the Spanish throne. On 2 May 1808 Madrid rose against the French; the next night French troops shot Spanish prisoners (the scene that Goya painted in *The Third of May 1808*, in 1814). A bitter war followed. The Spanish used guerrilla warfare (the word comes from this war), a French army surrendered to the Spanish at Bailén in July 1808, and a British army was landed in Portugal. Under Sir Arthur Wellesley (later Duke of Wellington) it defended Portugal behind the lines of Torres Vedras in 1810 and then drove the French back, winning at Salamanca (1812) and Vitoria (June 1813).

Within France the impact was:
- **Men and money**: some 200,000 or more French troops were tied down for six years, and tens of thousands died, taking men that were later needed in Germany and Russia.
- **Prestige**: the French were seen to be beaten. Austria went to war again in 1809, encouraged by Spain's resistance.
- **Morale**: the war was unpopular and seemed unjust, and conscripts dreaded being sent to Spain.
Napoleon himself is said to have called Spain "the ulcer" that destroyed him.

### Growth of nationalism in the Empire

Napoleon's armies carried the ideas of the Revolution, but his rule stirred up the opposite feeling in the people he conquered: nationalism, the belief that a people has a right to govern itself and should be loyal to its own nation. The causes were direct. The Empire demanded money and soldiers from its subject lands, closed their ports, imposed French officials and laws and put Napoleon's relatives on thrones.
- **Spain**: the guerrilla war was fought partly in the name of religion and king, but also of a Spanish nation.
- **Germany**: after Prussia's defeat at Jena (1806), reformers rebuilt the state, and writers such as Fichte (*Addresses to the German Nation*, 1807–08) called for a united German national spirit. In Austria's war of 1809 the Tyrolese under Andreas Hofer rose against Bavaria and France.
- **Russia**: in 1812 the war became the "Patriotic War", in which peasants burned their crops and resisted the invaders.
Nationalism turned the coalitions of 1813 into a war of peoples as well as of kings: in 1813 Prussia and then Austria joined the allies, and the other German states changed sides after Leipzig.

### The failure of the campaign against Russia, 1812

Alexander I's withdrawal from the Continental System and the Russian claim to influence in Poland led Napoleon to invade. In June 1812 the **Grande Armée** crossed the River Niemen. Estimates of its size range from about 450,000 to over 600,000 men, but fewer than half were French; the rest were Germans, Poles, Italians and others. The Russians retreated, burned what they left, and fought one great battle at **Borodino** (7 September), which Napoleon won but did not decide. The French entered **Moscow** on 14 September, but fires broke out in the city and the Tsar refused to negotiate. Napoleon waited five weeks, then began the retreat on 19 October. The army was destroyed by hunger, disease, Russian attacks and the winter; the crossing of the Berezina river (26–29 November) was a disaster. On 5 December Napoleon left the army to hurry to Paris. Historians' estimates of the survivors who returned fit to fight run from about 30,000 to about 100,000; the dead alone amounted to hundreds of thousands, and many more were captured.

In France the *29th Bulletin* of 3 December 1812 told the public of the disaster, but ended with the statement that "the health of His Majesty has never been better". The effect was deep:
- **Military**: Napoleon lost the best of his army and nearly all his horses, and he never recovered his advantage. He raised another large army in 1813, but of untrained boys.
- **Political**: the Empire's reputation for invincibility was gone. Prussia and then Austria turned against him.
- **Domestic**: new levies and taxes caused more resentment, and the families of the dead blamed the Emperor.

### Defeat at Leipzig, 1813

In 1813 Prussia, Russia, Austria, Britain and Sweden formed the Sixth Coalition. Napoleon won some victories in the spring and summer, but outnumbered, he was beaten at the three-day **Battle of Leipzig** (16–19 October 1813), the "Battle of the Nations", where about 200,000 French faced the allies' much larger forces. He retreated across the Rhine. The results were decisive: the Confederation of the Rhine collapsed, Holland rose, and the allies had a way into France. In December 1813 the allies declared that they were fighting Napoleon and not France, and offered peace on terms that left France larger than in 1789 (the Frankfurt proposals), but Napoleon delayed and refused.

### The allies capture Paris and the abdication, 1814

In January 1814 the allies invaded France. Napoleon, with a small army, fought a brilliant campaign in February, winning a series of battles, but he was vastly outnumbered. In March the allies agreed at Chaumont to stay together until victory. Resistance was local: some peasants in eastern France attacked allied stragglers, but there was no national rising, and French officials and marshals began to look to their own futures. **Paris** fell on **31 March 1814**, after a brief defence; the Tsar and the King of Prussia entered the city. Talleyrand, who had been working with the allies, formed a provisional government, and the Senate declared Napoleon deposed. His marshals refused to march on Paris. On **6 April 1814** Napoleon **abdicated** at Fontainebleau. By the Treaty of Fontainebleau (11 April) he kept the title of Emperor and received the island of Elba. Louis XVIII, brother of the executed king, was restored to the throne. (Napoleon returned from Elba in March 1815 for the Hundred Days and was defeated at Waterloo on 18 June 1815; these events are outside the syllabus dates.)

:::key Why did Napoleon fall? A ranking you can defend
1. **Over-extension** (the root): an Empire that depended on constant victory and on forcing other states to obey could not be held in peace.
2. **The Continental System and Spain**: the attempt to defeat Britain led to the economic crisis of 1810–11 and the Peninsular War, which tied down an army for six years.
3. **Russia (1812)**: the decisive military disaster; it destroyed the Grande Armée and made defeat possible.
4. **Nationalism and the Sixth Coalition**: it turned subject peoples and former allies against France.
5. **Decline at home**: conscription and taxes wore down his popularity, so few defended him in 1814.
6. **Leipzig and 1814**: the final defeats, in which the allies' numbers beat Napoleon's skill.
An answer at the top level weighs these: Russia was the turning point, but without the earlier weaknesses (Spain, the economy) France would have survived it, and without the allies' unity and nationalism the defeat at Leipzig would not have been final.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Napoleon signed the Concordat with the Pope in 1801.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Napoleon signed the Concordat because France was divided over religion. During the Revolution the Church had lost its lands and priests had been made to swear an oath. Many people in France were still Catholics and the Pope agreed to make peace. The Concordat was signed on 15 July 1801 and the Church was paid by the state."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but it describes the background and the terms. It never says why Napoleon wanted an agreement. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was to end religious division and bring peace to the west. The Civil Constitution of the Clergy and the persecution of refractory priests had split France, and the Vendée and the Chouan guerrillas had fought partly for the Church. If Napoleon could reconcile the Catholic majority to his regime, the civil war would lose its cause. A second reason was to weaken the royalists. The Pope and the exiled clergy had been the Bourbons' allies, so a treaty with the Pope took away their strongest support and gave Napoleon a base among peasants and priests."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support and tied to Napoleon's aims. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the control of the Church and the land question, and link the factors:

"A third reason was control. Under the Concordat the First Consul named the bishops, the state paid the clergy, and the Organic Articles of April 1802 allowed no papal order to be published in France without government permission. Napoleon therefore made the Church part of his administration, and used it to teach obedience. A fourth reason concerned the national lands. The Pope accepted the loss of the Church's property, which protected the thousands of families who had bought it, the people who feared a Bourbon return most and who were the regime's main supporters.

These reasons worked together. Religious peace was the aim, but the way to achieve it was to take the Church away from the royalists and bind it to the regime; and the settlement of the land question made sure that the propertied classes would accept it. The most important reason was political: Napoleon valued religion chiefly as a support for order, and he signed the Concordat because it served his government. By ending the religious quarrel on his own terms, he removed one of the main causes of unrest in France and one of the Bourbons' main hopes."

*Commentary.* Four factors, explained and **connected** (peace, weakening royalists, control and security of property), with a supported conclusion about the main reason. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

My true glory is not to have won forty battles. Waterloo will wipe out the memory of so many victories. But what nothing can wipe out, what will live for ever, is my Civil Code.

*Adapted from the Mémorial de Sainte-Hélène, by the Comte de Las Cases, who recorded Napoleon's conversations during his exile on St Helena; published in 1823.*

**Source B**

Husband and wife owe each other fidelity, help and assistance. The husband owes protection to his wife; the wife owes obedience to her husband. A husband may ask for divorce on the ground of his wife's adultery. The wife may ask for divorce on the ground of her husband's adultery only if he has kept his mistress in their common home.

*Adapted from Articles 212, 213, 229 and 230 of the Civil Code of the French, 21 March 1804.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the importance and the nature of the Civil Code.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A says that the Civil Code is Napoleon's greatest achievement and Source B is the Civil Code itself. They are different because A is about how important the Code is and B is about marriage. A is biased because Napoleon is writing about himself."

*Commentary.* One difference, asserted, nothing on similarity, and the judgement of bias is a label with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"Both sources treat the Civil Code as a major piece of law. Source A claims that it will 'live for ever', and Source B shows how detailed and wide-ranging it was, setting out rules for the most personal part of life, marriage. Both are therefore evidence that the Code reached into everyday life and was meant to last.

They differ in what they show about the Code's character. Source A speaks only of its fame and permanence, presenting it as a great act of statesmanship. Source B shows what it actually did to some people, and it is not flattering: the wife owes 'obedience' to her husband, and the rules on adultery treat the sexes unequally. So B shows the Code as a patriarchal law, a restoration of the father's authority after the Revolution, whereas A hides this by speaking of glory. Also, A concerns reputation, B concerns legal content.

The provenance explains the difference. Source A was recorded in exile on St Helena after Waterloo, by Las Cases, a devoted follower, and published in 1823. Napoleon was shaping his legacy, and wanted to be remembered not as the loser of Waterloo but as a law-giver; the comparison with 'forty battles' is part of that image. It is therefore a valuable source for how Napoleon wanted to be remembered, but it tells us little about the Code's contents. Source B is a legal text of 1804, made by lawyers working under Napoleon's own eye, so it gives accurate evidence of what the law said. It does not show how courts applied it, nor does it say anything about the aims of its authors. Taken together, the sources show the gap between the claim and the content: the Code was lasting and influential, as A says, but B shows that its equality was a legal equality of men."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences with support, differences explained through the different nature, origin and purpose of each source (an exile's self-image, a law of 1804) and the final comment on what each is good evidence for. A **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Napoleon's rule was based on force rather than consent.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For force: Fouché's police and informers; special courts (1801) and prisons without trial (1810); press cut from 73 to 13 (1800) and to four (1811); doctored plebiscite of 1800; conscription enforced by gendarmes; the execution of the Duc d'Enghien (1804).
- For consent: the end of instability and the peace of 1801–02; the Concordat and the amnesty; the Civil Code and the protection of the buyers of national lands; the stable franc and Bank of France; careers open to talent; victories; the support of the notables.
- Judgement: criterion = what held the regime together at different periods and when it was tested in 1814. Force was needed most when consent weakened (after 1810); the consent was to order and victory, not to Napoleon himself.

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Napoleon's rule was based on force. He set up a police state under Fouché, who used informers to report on what people said and opened letters. The press was censored, and in 1800 sixty of the seventy-three newspapers in Paris were closed. Plebiscites were organised by the government, and the 1800 result was changed by Lucien Bonaparte, who added a million votes. Conscription forced thousands of young men into the army. This shows that Napoleon's rule was based on force."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts. Other points are not mentioned. An answer made only of paragraphs like this stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet force alone cannot explain how a regime lasted fifteen years, because a large part of French society had good reason to support it. After the instability of the Directory, the Consulate brought peace (Lunéville in 1801 and Amiens in 1802), a stable franc backed by the Bank of France, and a prefectural administration that collected taxes and kept order. The Concordat and the amnesty ended the religious and political conflicts of the Revolution, and the Civil Code protected the property of those who had bought Church and noble lands, the people most afraid of a Bourbon return. The regime offered careers to able men of any origin, and the notables, the officials and the army had a stake in its survival. Even if the 1800 plebiscite was inflated, the real vote of about 1.5 million for the constitution against a handful against shows that opposition was small. These were not simply instruments of force: they were policies that gave people reasons to obey.

The balance changed over time, however. In the Consulate, the force used was aimed at a minority (royalists, Jacobins and brigands) and most people accepted the regime willingly. But from 1810 the regime depended more on coercion: conscription needed gendarmes and mobile columns, the press was reduced to four papers in 1811, and the state prisons held men without trial. The reason is that the consent had always rested on success. When war brought hunger, taxes and the deaths of hundreds of thousands of sons, the regime had to use more force to keep going, and when the army failed in 1812–14, almost nobody defended it. Even Talleyrand and Napoleon's marshals deserted him in 1814.

Overall, the statement is partly true, but it overstates the case. Napoleon's rule was based on force in the sense that it had no free elections and silenced opposition, but it was also based on consent among the groups who profited from order, from the Code and from his victories. The test is 1814: if the rule had rested on force alone, the police and the army would have held; that they did not, and that so few fought for the Emperor, shows that the consent had been conditional on success. The better view is that force kept down opponents and consent gave the regime its stability, until war removed the consent."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph analyses a perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (what held the regime together, and what happened in 1814) and shows change over time. With an opening paragraph on the case for force, this is Level 5 (18–20).
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (b) [25]: use all four sources and your own knowledge
**Source A**

Christians owe to the princes who govern them, and we owe in particular to Napoleon I, our Emperor, love, respect, obedience, fidelity, military service, and the taxes ordered for the defence of the Empire and his throne. To honour and serve our Emperor is therefore to honour and serve God himself. Those who fail in their duty would resist the order that God has established and risk eternal damnation.

*Adapted from the Imperial Catechism, taught to children in the Empire from 1806.*

**Source B**

Until 6 November the weather was perfect and the army's movement succeeded. From 7 November the cold began, and in a few days the roads were covered with ice. Our cavalry lost its horses, and the artillery and baggage could not follow. The health of His Majesty has never been better.

*Adapted from the 29th Bulletin of the Grande Armée, issued at Molodechno, 3 December 1812, on the retreat from Russia.*

**Source C**

A painting showing Napoleon, in a cloak that billows in the wind, on a rearing horse, pointing the way up a steep Alpine path. Carved on the rocks in the foreground are the names Bonaparte, Hannibal and Karolus Magnus (Charlemagne).

*Napoleon Crossing the Alps, painted by Jacques-Louis David, 1801. Napoleon crossed the Great St Bernard Pass in May 1800, in fact on a mule.*

**Source D**

Question put to the people: Does the people wish the imperial dignity to be hereditary in the descendants of Napoleon Bonaparte? Result: for, about 3,570,000; against, about 2,600.

*Adapted from the official result of the plebiscite on the Empire, announced in 1804.*

**Question.** How far do these sources agree that Napoleon's power depended on propaganda?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (religion used to teach obedience to the Emperor); C (the image of a heroic general linked with Hannibal and Charlemagne); D (an overwhelming plebiscite result which the regime published as proof of consent); B (the bulletin spins disaster: "never been better"). Challenge or qualify: B (it was frank about the cold, which suggests that even Napoleon's propaganda could not hide the truth); D (the result may show real support as well as management); and all four are the regime's own voice, so none shows what the people thought.

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: an official text, whose purpose was to teach every child obedience to Napoleon, with the Church's backing after the Concordat. It is strong evidence of the regime's aim to use religion for loyalty, but not of how far children or parents believed it; many clergy were unhappy, and the Pope was Napoleon's enemy by 1809.
- **B**: a government report, written by Napoleon himself, to announce a disaster. It is strong evidence of how the regime handled bad news (admitting defeat while shielding the Emperor), but it also shows the limits of propaganda: Parisians knew the army was destroyed by the end of 1812 whatever the bulletin said.
- **C**: a painting by the leading painter of the age, made for Napoleon's regime. It is a clear case of image-making, and its inaccuracy (the mule) is well known. It is good evidence of propaganda, but a work of art seen by a small public, so weak evidence of its effect.
- **D**: official figures produced by the regime, in a vote without secret ballot or alternatives. They are weak evidence of what people really thought, but strong evidence of how Napoleon claimed popular approval. Even if the numbers were inflated, as in 1800, the contrast with the vote against is consistent with real acceptance of the Empire among the propertied.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "The sources agree that Napoleon's regime worked hard to shape opinion. Source A uses religion to teach obedience, Source C turns a mule ride into a heroic image beside Hannibal and Charlemagne, Source D publishes a result of 3,570,000 to about 2,600 as proof of consent, and Source B shows even a disaster being presented with a calm sentence about the Emperor's health. All are the regime's own voice, which is itself evidence of how much it controlled what was said. But the sources do not show that Napoleon's power depended on propaganda. Source B shows that propaganda failed when the truth was too big, and my own knowledge supports this: in October 1812 Malet's false news that the Emperor was dead almost succeeded, and in 1814 propaganda could not stop the allies. Power also rested on things the sources do not show: the army and the victories that gave it prestige, the police, the administration of the prefects, the settlement of religion and the Civil Code that satisfied the propertied. On balance the sources agree that propaganda was an important tool, but only to a limited extent that it was the basis of Napoleon's power: it supported a regime that rested at least as much on success, administration and force."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and the context of 1800–14; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: To what extent was the failure of the Continental System the main reason for Napoleon's fall?
**Plan.** Criterion: which failure made defeat unavoidable, and which only made it faster.
- For: the Continental System ruined the ports and caused the crisis of 1810–11; it led to the occupation of Spain and Portugal (the Peninsular War, 1808–14) and to the break with Russia in 1810, so it led directly to the invasion of Russia; it lost Napoleon support at home.
- Against: Trafalgar had already closed the sea; the decisive blow was Russia in 1812, which destroyed the army; the growth of nationalism and the unity of the Sixth Coalition in 1813; conscription and the burden of war; Leipzig and the allied invasion of 1814.
- Judgement: the Continental System was the root cause of the wars that exhausted France, but the main reason for defeat was military, in Russia and at Leipzig.

**A Level 5 paragraph (the judgement).** "The Continental System was the most important underlying cause of Napoleon's fall, but it was not the main reason for it. The System drove him to occupy Portugal and Spain, where the Peninsular War cost him a large army for six years, and to the quarrel with Alexander I that ended in Moscow. It also brought a commercial crisis in 1810–11 and a shortage of colonial goods that lost him support in the ports. Yet the System itself failed because Britain survived by smuggling and new markets; what destroyed Napoleon's power was not economic war but military defeat. The Russian campaign destroyed the Grande Armée in 1812, and at Leipzig in 1813 the allies' united armies, strengthened by German nationalism, beat him with superior numbers, and the French people, tired of conscription, did little to resist the invasion of 1814. The Continental System therefore explains why Napoleon was at war so long, but not why he lost, which was defeat in the field by a coalition."

*Commentary.* A short Level 5 conclusion that distinguishes underlying from decisive causes, uses a criterion and ends with a clear comparative judgement. A full answer would add paragraphs on Russia and on nationalism with the evidence above.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far did the Civil Code change the lives of the French people?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Civil Code was a set of laws written by Napoleon's lawyers and promulgated on 21 March 1804. It had 2,281 articles. It said that everybody was equal before the law. It protected private property. It dealt with marriage, divorce, inheritance and contracts. It also said that a wife must obey her husband. Napoleon was proud of it and said that it would last for ever. It was a very important set of laws and it changed the lives of the French people a great deal."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is a **description** of the Code which never tests the question. It says that the Code "changed the lives of the French people a great deal" without saying whose lives, how, or how much, and it never compares the Code with what already existed or with other reforms. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): relevant description, few explicit links to the question.

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The Civil Code changed the lives of property owners more than anyone else. By confirming equality before the law and the security of the lands bought during the Revolution, it gave landowners and buyers of national property the legal certainty they needed, and the equal division of inheritance changed how families handled land. But for many it brought little change or even loss: workers were bound by the *livret* and banned from striking, and women lost independence, since a wife owed obedience to her husband and could not contract without his consent. The Code therefore changed most of what mattered to men with property, and least of what mattered to workers and women." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

Q: How did Napoleon build his military reputation before 1799?
A: Toulon (1793, brigadier general at 24), Vendémiaire (1795, the "whiff of grapeshot"), the Italian campaign (1796–97: Lodi, Arcole, Rivoli, and the Treaty of Campo Formio) and Egypt (1798–99), all publicised by his own newspapers and bulletins.
Q: Describe the coup of Brumaire in two or three sentences.
A: On 9 November 1799 the Ancients moved the councils to Saint-Cloud and gave Bonaparte command of the troops; on 10 November he failed at first before the Five Hundred, but Lucien Bonaparte called in the troops, who cleared the hall, and the remaining deputies named three provisional Consuls (Bonaparte, Sieyès, Ducos).
Q: What powers did the First Consul have under the Constitution of the Year VIII?
A: He proposed laws (through the Council of State), appointed ministers, officials, judges and diplomats, commanded the army and conducted foreign policy; the Tribunate and Legislative Body could debate or vote but not propose, and the Senate chose their members.
Q: Name four means Napoleon used to control France.
A: Prefects and ministries; Fouché's police and special courts; censorship and propaganda (press cut from 73 to 13 papers in 1800, four in 1811); controlled plebiscites and lists of notables.
Q: What did the Concordat do, and what were the Organic Articles?
A: The Concordat (15 July 1801) recognised Catholicism as the religion of most French people, had bishops named by the First Consul and instituted by the Pope, paid the clergy and confirmed the sale of Church lands. The Organic Articles (April 1802) added state controls that the Pope had not agreed to.
Q: Give three reforms of the Consulate other than the Civil Code and the Concordat.
A: The Bank of France (1800); prefects (1800); the lycées (1802); the Legion of Honour (1802); the franc germinal (1803); the amnesty for émigrés (1802).
Q: Why did the Peninsular War damage Napoleon?
A: It tied down 200,000 or more troops for six years; it was a guerrilla war the French could not win; British forces under Wellington drove them back; it encouraged Austria to fight in 1809 and damaged French prestige and morale.
Q: What happened at Leipzig (16–19 October 1813) and in March–April 1814?
A: The allies beat Napoleon at Leipzig, and the Confederation of the Rhine collapsed; in early 1814 they invaded France, entered Paris on 31 March, and Napoleon abdicated on 6 April and was sent to Elba.

:::try Exam practice ★ to ★★★
Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Napoleon was able to seize power in November 1799.
A: Indicative content: the Directory was unpopular and had lost the confidence of nearly every group (three coups, defeats in 1799, corruption, empty treasury); the French wanted order, not another coup; Bonaparte's fame from Italy and Egypt and the loyalty of the troops; Sieyès and the plotters (Lucien, Talleyrand, Fouché) needed a general and planned the legal form; luck and Lucien's action on 19 Brumaire. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects the weakness of the Directory (the opportunity) with the plotters and the army (the means), and judges which mattered most, e.g. the weakness of the Directory made the coup possible, but Bonaparte's army and prestige made it succeed.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why conscription caused growing opposition to Napoleon.
A: Indicative content: annual levies by lot, rising with each war; the cost in lives (several hundred thousand French deaths); the system favoured the rich, who could pay for a substitute; refractory men hid or deserted and families were fined or had troops billeted, which meant constant government pressure on villages; the Russian disaster and the calls for boys in 1813–14; resentment in the west, south and mountains. Level 4: connect the burden (deaths, unfairness) to the methods used (gendarmes, fines) and to the loss of support when the wars brought no victory, and conclude on the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why Napoleon reformed education.
A: Indicative content: to train loyal officers, officials and engineers for the state; to unify the nation under a single system (lycées in 1802, Imperial University in 1808); to teach loyalty to the Emperor and the Catholic religion, and to control the teachers; to create a body of educated men who owed their careers to him; the Revolution had planned but not built a system; primary education and girls were neglected because they were not needed by his purpose. Level 4: connect the aims (control and service) with the structure (discipline, a single University, scholarships), and judge the main purpose, which was political: loyal servants of the state.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'The Civil Code was the most important of Napoleon's domestic reforms.' How far do you agree?
A: For: it unified French law and survives in France and in many other countries; it confirmed the legal gains of the Revolution (equality before the law, an end to feudalism) and secured property, including the national lands; it gave stability to society; Napoleon himself valued it above his victories. Against: the Concordat settled the religious divisions that had led to civil war; the prefects and the centralised administration gave the regime its power; the Bank of France and the franc made the currency stable; the lycées trained the elite; the Code also made women dependent on their husbands and restricted workers. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "which reform did most to make the regime stable and last?", and judges, e.g. the prefectural system and the Concordat did more to secure the regime in the short term, but the Code was the most important lasting change.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ To what extent was the failure of the Russian campaign the main reason for Napoleon's fall in 1814?
H: List the reasons: Russia, Spain, the Continental System, nationalism, Leipzig, conscription and the economy at home.
H: Ask what Russia changed: what did Napoleon have before and after 1812?
H: Use a criterion such as "which failure made defeat unavoidable?"
A: For: the Grande Armée was destroyed (hundreds of thousands dead or captured); Napoleon lost his horses and veteran soldiers; his reputation for invincibility ended; Prussia and Austria changed sides in 1813; he had to rely on raw conscripts. Against: the Peninsular War had been draining men and prestige since 1808; the Continental System's failure and the crisis of 1810–11 weakened the economy and support; nationalism united the Sixth Coalition; Leipzig and the allied invasion of France decided the outcome; the lack of support at home in 1814. Level 5 judges, e.g., that Russia was the decisive blow because it turned a strong position into a weak one, but it was the product of the earlier decisions to try to defeat Britain by blockade and to occupy Spain, and the allies' unity and numbers finished the work.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the first box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about relations between Napoleon and the Church.
A: Similarities: both are official texts of the French government from 1801–02 about the Catholic Church; both confirm that the Church can operate openly in France. Differences: A (the Concordat) is an agreement with the Pope and speaks of free exercise of religion, with only police regulations; B (the Organic Articles) is a unilateral rule that puts the Pope's documents under government control. Explanation: Napoleon needed the Concordat to win Catholic support and peace, but wanted to keep the Church under state control; he added the Organic Articles after the Pope had signed, without his agreement. Provenance: A was negotiated with Pius VII, so it shows the compromise; B was published by the French government alone, so it shows Napoleon's real aim. Level 4 explains the difference through this context and judges which is better evidence of relations: B reveals Napoleon's control, A shows the settlement he had to accept.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the second box above). How far do these sources agree that Napoleon's fall was caused by his foreign policy and wars?
H: Decide which sources show the foreign policy (A, B, C) and which show Napoleon's own view (D).
H: Use your own knowledge to judge what the sources omit: conscription, the economy and opposition at home.
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose: A is an order, B a painting made after the war, C a declaration of enemies, D a ruler's act of abdication.
A: Support: A shows the blockade that began the Continental System, which led to the Peninsular War and to Russia; B shows the brutality of the war in Spain and the hatred it created (painted in 1814, after Napoleon's expulsion from Spain, so it also reflects the Spanish victors' view); C shows the allies fighting Napoleon's "dominance" beyond France's borders, which links the fall to his foreign conquests; D shows Napoleon accepting that he is the "obstacle to peace". Challenge: none of the sources mentions conscription, the economy or opinion in France; C is propaganda aimed at dividing France from Napoleon, so it exaggerates the idea that the allies were friendly to France; D is the formal act of a defeated ruler and says nothing of his reasons; B was painted after the event. Evaluation with knowledge: A is strong evidence of the policy but not of its results; B is an artist's view, true to the event but a symbol; C reflects the allies' aims, backed by the fact that many Frenchmen did not defend Napoleon. Level 5 judges how far: the sources agree that wars and foreign policy were central to the fall, but the sources and the context show that domestic exhaustion and the allies' unity also mattered, so the agreement is substantial but incomplete.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ How far were Napoleon's financial and economic reforms successful?
A: For: the Bank of France (1800) and the franc germinal (1803) gave a stable currency that lasted until 1914; an efficient tax-collection system under the prefects; taxes on drink and tobacco and war levies reduced the need to raise direct taxes; stable bread prices in Paris; encouragement of industry and the cotton and iron trades. Against: the Continental System ruined the ports and caused the crisis of 1810–11; indirect taxes were unpopular; the economy depended on war booty; bread prices in the provinces were less protected, and the 1811–12 harvest forced him to fix grain prices again; workers were restricted by the livret. Level 5 judges, e.g. that the reforms of 1799–1803 were a lasting success, but the economic policy of the Empire after 1806 was a failure because it was dominated by the war.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Some historians see Napoleon as the heir of the Revolution; others see him as its betrayer. How far does the evidence of 1799–1814 support one view more than the other?
H: List what he kept from the Revolution (the Civil Code, the sale of national lands, careers open to talent, the prefects) and what he ended (elections, free press, equality for women, the Republic).
H: Ask which mattered more to the people who had made the Revolution.
A: Heir: the Civil Code, equality before the law and the end of feudalism; the security of the buyers of national lands; careers open to talent in the army and administration; the system of prefects built on the Revolution's centralisation. Betrayer: no elected assemblies of any power, no free press, a police state, the reintroduction of hereditary monarchy and nobility, the Concordat's control of the Church, the loss of rights for women and workers, and endless wars. A* judgement: he kept the Revolution's social and legal gains (equality of rights for men, the property settlement) and ended its political ones (liberty and self-government), so he was heir of the Revolution's equality and betrayer of its liberty.
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Napoleon's bulletins are often used as evidence for the history of his campaigns. How reliable are they as evidence of the effect of war on France?
H: Who wrote the bulletins, for whom, and for what purpose?
H: What can a bulletin show about the regime even when it does not tell the truth?
A: Strengths: they show how the regime presented the war, what it wanted the public to believe and how it handled bad news (the 29th Bulletin admitted the cold and the losses); they were read across France and so shaped opinion; they are a precise record of the dates and places of campaigns. Weaknesses: written under Napoleon's supervision to glorify him, overstating victories and French successes and hiding or minimising defeats and casualties; they say nothing of conscription, hunger or protest in France itself; they are propaganda by a censored press. Judgement: they are unreliable on what happened in battle and on the cost to France, but they are very good evidence of Napoleon's methods of propaganda and of what the government feared the public would not accept.
