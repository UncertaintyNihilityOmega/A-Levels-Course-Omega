:::why
The historians of the Holocaust do not disagree about whether it happened, how many Jews were murdered or where. They disagree about **why**: whether it was Hitler's plan, the product of a chaotic state, a response to the pressures of war, or a mixture; about **who**: whether ordinary Germans and other Europeans killed because they hated, because they obeyed or because they conformed; and about **how it might have been stopped**: what Jews could do, and what the Allies could have done. Each answer also tells you about the person who gave it. A historian writing in West Germany in 1977 asked a different question from one writing in America in 1996 or in Israel in 2007. Paper 3 asks you to read an unseen extract from this debate and say what it shows about its author's interpretation and approach. This chapter gives you the debate, historian by historian, with the time and place that shaped each one.
:::

:::warn Respect the subject, and do not treat denial as a school
The historians in this chapter all start from the fact that the Nazi regime and its allies murdered about six million Jews. Write about their arguments as arguments, and about the victims as people. A "school" is a group of historians who reach different conclusions from the same body of evidence; **denial** rejects the evidence, and so it has no place among the schools (see the section "Evidence, and why denial is not an interpretation" in [the previous chapter](#hist.interp.holocaust.1)). The word "functionalist" does not mean that the Holocaust "just happened" or that no one is responsible: every historian here holds the perpetrators responsible.
:::

## How far was the Holocaust a consequence of racist ideas that existed before the Nazis?

The first question in the syllabus is about **continuity**: if you take the antisemitism of nineteenth-century Europe (religious hatred, racial theory, the pogroms, the stab-in-the-back legend, all in the previous chapter) and add them up, does the Holocaust follow? Historians give three answers.

:::key Three answers to "was it a consequence of earlier racist ideas?"
1. **Yes, a deep and specifically German tradition.** Antisemitism was so widespread and so murderous in German culture that, once the Nazis removed the limits, ordinary Germans were willing to kill. This is **Daniel Goldhagen**'s view.
2. **Partly: old ideas made it possible, but something new was needed.** Antisemitism was European, and was strong in countries where no Holocaust began. The Nazi regime added a new racial ideology, a dictatorship and a war. This is the view of **Saul Friedländer** (who calls the Nazi version "redemptive antisemitism") and of most historians who combine continuity with change.
3. **No, or only slightly: the Holocaust came from the Nazi state and the war.** Hatred of Jews was common but did not produce violence in Weimar Germany, and most Germans in the 1930s did not demand it. The decisive causes were the structure of the regime and the circumstances of 1939–42. This is the stress of the structuralists and functionalists below.
:::

### Goldhagen: "eliminationist antisemitism"

**Daniel Goldhagen**, an American political scientist, published *Hitler's Willing Executioners: Ordinary Germans and the Holocaust* in 1996. His argument has three claims. German society in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was marked by a particular kind of hatred, which he called **eliminationist antisemitism**: the belief that the Jews were a harmful presence that had to be removed, by conversion, expulsion or violence. This belief was widespread, deep and had existed long before 1933. When Hitler came to power the hatred was released, and ordinary Germans (not only the SS) took part willingly, in the police battalions, in the camps and on the death marches, and they did so with zeal and cruelty. Goldhagen concluded that the Holocaust was a German national project, and that no other explanation, such as obedience or fear, was needed.

The book sold in millions and was a public event, particularly in Germany, where Goldhagen toured in 1996 and where it prompted a national discussion. Historians were far more critical than the public. Their criticisms were:
- **Antisemitism was not unique to Germany.** It was strong in Russia, Poland, Romania, Hungary and France, and in Austria; so it cannot explain why the Holocaust was organised from Germany.
- **Weimar Germany's Jews were well integrated**, many married non-Jews and there was far less violence than in eastern Europe; before 1933 Jews were not usually murdered in Germany.
- **The Nazi vote of 1930–33** was driven by many things, among which antisemitism was not the main one (Kershaw).
- **The method.** Goldhagen used a small number of examples as if they stood for all Germans, and he did not give weight to evidence of reluctance, refusal or varied motives.
- **Ordinary Men.** Browning, whose study of Reserve Police Battalion 101 Goldhagen used, argued that the same evidence shows other motives (see "The perpetrators").
- **The question.** If the cause was an old hatred, why did it take until 1941? Critics said Goldhagen's thesis could not explain timing, which was a matter of war and decision. (Norman Finkelstein and Ruth Bettina Birn made some of the sharpest criticisms in *A Nation on Trial*, 1998.)

Goldhagen's supporters said that the critics underrated how far normal Germans knew and accepted the killing, and that he restored the victims and the perpetrators' hatred to the centre of the story. His book therefore belongs to the **continuity** side of the debate, and an extract that argues "antisemitism of the German people was the cause" is Goldhagen-like: a cultural explanation, a stress on motive and willingness, and a judgement on the German people.

### Friedländer: "redemptive antisemitism"

**Saul Friedländer**, a historian born in Prague in 1932 who survived the war as a hidden child in France and later worked in Israel and the USA, wrote *Nazi Germany and the Jews* in two volumes: *The Years of Persecution, 1933–1939* (1997) and *The Years of Extermination, 1939–1945* (2007). He argued that the Nazis had a special form of hatred, **redemptive antisemitism**, which was a mixture of racial fear and religious-style belief: Hitler and his circle saw the Jews as a mortal threat, and believed that the world would be "redeemed" only by their removal. This was not the same as the ordinary prejudice of the time. Friedländer also took an approach new at the time: he wove the voices of the victims, from diaries and letters, into the story with the decisions of the perpetrators, and he stressed that the Jews' experience was a part of history and not only its object. His account is a **synthesis** of ideology and circumstance (below).

### Hilberg and the long background

**Raul Hilberg**, an American historian born in Vienna in 1926, argued in *The Destruction of the European Jews* (1961) that the Nazis built on a history of anti-Jewish measures that went back to the early Church: conversion, expulsion and, finally, killing. His book is above all about the **process**: the German bureaucracy defined, expropriated, concentrated and then annihilated the Jews, and he showed how many offices (railways, banks, ministries) took part in routine ways. So Hilberg is both a historian of continuity (the long hostility) and of structure (the machinery), a combination that explains why an extract that stresses the bureaucracy may not be structuralist in the narrow sense.

:::key What to say about the continuity question
- Support: religious and racial antisemitism existed in Germany for centuries (Marr 1879, Chamberlain 1899); the Nazis built on it; Hitler's own ideas drew on it; popular indifference or hostility allowed persecution to continue; the Protocols and the stab-in-the-back legend.
- Challenge: antisemitism was European, yet the Holocaust began in Germany's war; no mass killing took place in Germany before 1938; in Denmark, Bulgaria and Italy hostility to Jews did not produce mass murder until Germans arrived; the Nazi vote was mostly about other things; and the **timing** (1941) needs an explanation about war.
- Judgement: older ideas made persecution possible and supplied the vocabulary; they did not make the Holocaust inevitable. The best answers say what was **new** (a racial state, a war of annihilation, the sequence of decisions of 1941).
:::

## Intentionalism and the role of Hitler

:::def Intentionalism
The interpretation that the Holocaust was the result of Hitler's intention: he had decided, early, that the Jews must be destroyed, and the Nazi state carried out his aim when circumstances allowed. The word was given to this view by its critics, in Timothy Mason's essay of 1981.
:::

**Lucy Dawidowicz**, an American historian, wrote *The War Against the Jews, 1933–1945* (1975). Her central claim was that Hitler had resolved to destroy the Jews from the beginning of his political career and that the war was the opportunity: the "war against the Jews" ran alongside the military war, and it had its own logic and priority. She traced the intention to Hitler's early speeches, his letter of 1919 and *Mein Kampf*, saw the persecution of the 1930s as steps in a direction already set, and argued that Hitler decided on killing before the invasion of the Soviet Union in 1941. Her book also gave a strong place to the Jewish side of the story: the communities, their leaders and their efforts.

**Eberhard Jäckel**, a German historian at Stuttgart, argued in *Hitler's Weltanschauung* (1969) that Hitler had a consistent worldview, with two aims, the conquest of living space in the East and the removal of the Jews, and that he held to it from the 1920s to the end. **Gerald Fleming**, a British historian, argued in *Hitler and the Final Solution* (published in German in 1982) that Hitler's personal will can be traced through the documents, that his orders were often given by word of mouth, and that the absence of a signed order is not evidence that there was no order.

Their evidence:
- Hitler's **early writing and speeches**: the letter to Gemlich (16 September 1919); *Mein Kampf* (1925–26); the 25 points (1920).
- His **public prophecy** of 30 January 1939 that a new world war would mean "the annihilation of the Jewish race in Europe", and his reference back to it in speeches, including to Nazi leaders on 12 December 1941, which Goebbels recorded.
- The **consistency of persecution** from 1933 to 1941, and the way the main measures were announced or approved by Hitler (the Nuremberg Laws).
- **Göring's letter to Heydrich of 31 July 1941**, and the Wannsee protocol's statement that the evacuation of the Jews to the East was subject to the Führer's authorisation.
- **Himmler's speeches** to SS leaders (Posen, 4 October 1943), which treat the killing as a task given to the SS.
- **Hitler's authority**: in a dictatorship that ran on the leader's will, nothing so large could happen without his approval. The T4 order of 1939 shows that Hitler was willing to sign authorisations for killing.

The criticisms:
- **There is no written Hitler order** for the murder of the Jews. Intentionalists answer that he gave verbal orders, as he often did; critics reply that this is an argument from silence.
- **Hitler's ideas did not produce a plan.** The policy until 1941 was emigration, and the Madagascar plan of 1940 and the Nisko scheme of 1939 were expulsions. If Hitler intended murder from 1919, why did he wait twenty-two years, and why try other means?
- **It stops at Hitler.** It cannot easily explain the part played by the Einsatzgruppen commanders, the Foreign Office, the railways, local rulers such as Greiser, and the many hands that made the murder possible; nor how a vague aim became a method.
- **The sources are thin.** Hitler said little in writing about the Final Solution, so intentionalists have to read his public rhetoric as a plan.

An extract that begins with the leader's mind, quotes *Mein Kampf* and the 1939 speech, treats the war as an opportunity, and describes the stages of persecution as steps towards an aim is intentionalist in approach. The question it asks is "what did Hitler want?"

:::note David Irving and the argument about Hitler's knowledge
In *Hitler's War* (1977), the British writer David Irving claimed that Hitler did not know of the extermination until late, and that Himmler and others had acted behind his back. This was the claim that Martin Broszat answered in his article of 1977 (below). Broszat did not argue that Hitler was innocent: he argued that the decision-making was more complex than Irving, and the intentionalists, assumed. Irving's claim was later shown to depend on distorting the evidence; in 2000 a High Court judge found in *Irving v Penguin Books and Lipstadt* that Irving had deliberately falsified the record. Do not confuse the historians' argument about **how** Hitler was involved with Irving's attempt to remove him from the story.
:::

## Structuralism: the nature of the Nazi state

:::def Structuralism
The interpretation that the nature of the Nazi state, with many competing offices and no clear lines of authority, drove the regime towards ever more radical policies. Hitler is seen as an authority who set a direction and settled disputes, not as a planner who directed each step.
:::

:::def Polycracy
"Rule of many centres": the view that the Nazi state was a collection of overlapping and competing agencies (the Party, the SS, the ministries, the Four-Year Plan, the army, regional leaders) and not a single organised machine.
:::

**Martin Broszat**, a German historian who became director of the Institute of Contemporary History in Munich, set out the structuralist picture in *Der Staat Hitlers* (1969; English *The Hitler State*, 1981): the regime was a polycracy, in which rival bosses competed for Hitler's favour and for power, and Hitler often avoided deciding. **Hans Mommsen**, a German historian at Bochum, took this further. He described Hitler as a "weak dictator" in the sense that he was reluctant to take decisions and that the system drove itself, and in an essay of 1983, "The Realisation of the Unthinkable" (in German, *Die Realisierung des Utopischen*), he argued that the Holocaust grew by **cumulative radicalisation**: each agency tried to outdo the others in showing zeal, the regime's own dynamism pushed policy further, and a policy that had been unthinkable became thinkable in a series of steps.

:::def Cumulative radicalisation
The process by which Nazi policy became more extreme step by step, as rival offices competed to carry out what they thought Hitler wanted, and each stage made the next possible. Mommsen's phrase.
:::

The structuralist evidence:
- **Competing agencies in the persecution.** Between 1933 and 1938 the Party, SA, the Interior Ministry, the Economics Ministry, Göring's Four-Year Plan and the SS all pursued their own measures, often against one another; Aryanisation, for example, was a scramble.
- **Pressure from below.** The boycott of April 1933, the violence of summer 1935 and Kristallnacht (set off by Goebbels, and criticised by Göring) show party activists forcing the pace.
- **Rivalry over the East.** Himmler, Frank, the Gauleiters and the army competed to rule the occupied territories; local Nazi leaders such as Arthur Greiser, in the Warthegau, sought to get rid of "their" Jews first.
- **Wannsee** shows many departments being brought in; the Reich Railways, the Foreign Office and the ministries took part in routine ways.
- **The Willikens speech.** In 1934 Werner Willikens, a state secretary in the Prussian agriculture ministry, told officials that it was their job to work "towards the Führer" by guessing what he wanted. Ian Kershaw used this in the 1990s as the key to the system (below).

The criticisms of structuralism:
- **It risks removing Hitler and ideology.** If the system drove itself, why were the Jews the target? Racial antisemitism was the Nazi belief that shaped what the competing agencies did.
- **The Führer's role was real.** Hitler appointed Himmler and Heydrich, approved the deportations in September 1941 and spoke to Nazi leaders on 12 December 1941; he was not a weak dictator on the Jews.
- **It explains the speed better than the target.** Critics said it described the "how fast" but not the "why".
- **Polycracy is a model.** The regime was often efficient: the Reich Railways ran thousands of trains, and the SS followed clear orders.

## Functionalism: the part of circumstances and war

:::def Functionalism
The interpretation that the Holocaust developed out of circumstances, above all the war and the failure of earlier plans, rather than out of a plan: the policy "functioned" as a response to problems, in a series of improvisations, until killing became the solution. Mason's label (1981); many historians use "functionalist" and "structuralist" for the same people.
:::

**Karl Schleunes**, in *The Twisted Road to Auschwitz: Nazi Policy toward German Jews, 1933–1939* (1970), argued that there was no straight road from Hitler's ideas to Auschwitz. Policy between 1933 and 1939 zigzagged: the Party wanted violence, the ministries wanted order, Hitler intervened at some moments and not at others, and the aim of emigration was real. Auschwitz was the end of a twisted road, not a straight line.

**Martin Broszat's** article of 1977 ("Hitler and the Genesis of the 'Final Solution'") is the best-known functionalist statement. He argued that there was no Hitler order for a programme of extermination in 1941 and no master plan before that; the policy grew from an impasse. The plans for expulsion (Nisko in October 1939, Madagascar in 1940) failed; the ghettos in the General Government filled with people who could not be fed or moved; and the Germans in the East, faced with a crisis, killed in one place and then another, so that killing became accepted as the only possible solution. Hitler's role was to approve and encourage these improvisations, not to originate them.

**Götz Aly** and Susanne Heim, in *Vordenker der Vernichtung* (1991; English *Architects of Annihilation*, 2002), and Aly in *"Final Solution"* (1995), added a new version: German planners in the East who were concerned with population movement and economic modernisation, such as the resettlement of ethnic Germans into the annexed territories, caused problems of space and food, and the murder of Jews emerged as a "solution" to those problems. They saw technocrats, not fanatics only, at work.

The functionalist evidence:
- The **failed expulsion plans** (Nisko, Madagascar) and the overcrowded ghettos with their official ration of 181 calories per day in Warsaw.
- The **local initiatives**: Chełmno was set up in the Warthegau, under Greiser, by a special detachment of the SS and police under Herbert Lange; the Einsatzgruppen in the East increased the killing from men to women and children in different places at different times.
- The **absence of a written order** and the way Göring's letter of 31 July 1941 and the protocol of Wannsee use euphemism.
- The **radicalising effects of war**: the invasion of the USSR, the hunger and the shooting of Soviet prisoners, and the failure to defeat the Red Army in 1941, which made the regime desperate.

The criticisms:
- **It underestimates ideology.** The Nazis' reasons for choosing Jews as the "solution" lay in their beliefs, and the Einsatzgruppen had instructions before the invasion.
- **It underestimates Hitler.** The deportations from the Reich needed his approval, the prophecy of 1939 and the speech of 12 December 1941 show his involvement, and Himmler acted with his authority.
- **It can be read as an excuse.** An account that stresses improvisation may seem to lessen responsibility. Its authors reply that it identifies how responsibility was spread across many people.
- **Evidence.** Later research on the centre's part, such as Christian Gerlach's work on December 1941, challenged the idea that the initiative lay mainly with the periphery.

:::key Structuralism and functionalism: what to say about the labels
- **Structuralist** is the right word when the extract explains the outcome by the **way the regime was organised**: competing offices, cumulative radicalisation, officials anticipating the leader's wishes.
- **Functionalist** is the right word when the extract explains it by **circumstances and problems**: war, failed plans, shortage, the local situation.
- Many extracts do both; many historians use the two words as one. The mark scheme asks for the label only when it is **supported by the whole extract**, with a because. If the extract mentions Hitler's will in one paragraph and improvisation in another, think of synthesis, or a main message with a sub-message.
:::

## Synthesis: putting intention and circumstance together

By the middle of the 1980s the two sides had moved closer. At conferences such as the one in Stuttgart in 1984 many historians accepted that Hitler's ideas mattered and that policy developed in stages. A **synthesis** interpretation takes the strongest points from both.

**Christopher Browning**, an American historian, had begun as a functionalist in *Fateful Months* (1985) but moved towards a middle position. In *The Origins of the Final Solution: The Evolution of Nazi Jewish Policy, September 1939–March 1942* (2004) he argued that the policy of murder emerged from a **sequence of decisions** in 1941. In the summer, in the "euphoria of victory" of the first weeks of the war against the Soviet Union, when the regime believed it would defeat the USSR within weeks, the killing of Soviet Jews was extended and the preparations to deal with the rest began; in the autumn the deportations from the Reich were begun and the killing sites at Bełżec and Chełmno were built. Hitler approved the steps, but they were taken in response to events and in competition among leaders, and they came together only in the winter of 1941–42.

**Ian Kershaw**, a British historian, wrote the biography *Hitler* (vol. 1, *Hubris*, 1998; vol. 2, *Nemesis*, 2000) and the essay "Working Towards the Führer" (1993). He argued that the answer lay in the relation between Hitler and the system: Hitler set the aims and the atmosphere, a will to destroy the Jews, but gave few detailed orders; officials and party leaders, anxious to win his favour, competed to carry out what they believed he wanted, and so the system radicalised itself. Hitler's role is to authorise and encourage, which is the middle ground between Dawidowicz and Mommsen.

**Saul Friedländer** (above) argued that the ideology of redemptive antisemitism formed the frame in which every decision was taken. **Peter Longerich**, a German historian in Britain, argued in *Holocaust: The Nazi Persecution and Murder of the Jews* (2010; German original 1998) that there was no single decision but a series of decisions in stages, in which Hitler played an active part, and that the intention to kill the Jews of Europe was built up in the course of 1941.

:::key Dating "the decision": why historians cannot agree
The table shows the main positions; the disagreement is partly about evidence (there is no Hitler order) and partly about the question: whether a "decision" is a single act or a process.
:::

| View | Date | Who |
|---|---|---|
| No decision; the policy grew by stages from improvisation | 1941–42, no single date | Broszat (1977), Mommsen |
| A decision in the summer of 1941, in the "euphoria of victory" | July–September 1941 | Browning (in his earlier work); the Göring letter of 31 July |
| A sequence of decisions in summer and autumn 1941 | July–October 1941 | Browning (2004); Longerich (2010); Philippe Burrin, *Hitler and the Jews* (1989) |
| A decision in principle in December 1941 | 12 December 1941 | Christian Gerlach (1998) |
| An intention from the first, carried out when war allowed | 1919–41 | Dawidowicz (1975) |

:::warn "Synthesis" is not a safe default
Examiners credit "synthesis" only if the extract really combines the two. Students lose marks by writing "this is a synthesis" for any extract that mentions both Hitler and the war. Ask: does the extract **explain the outcome** by a combination (the aims of the leader and the pressures of circumstances working together), or does it make one the cause and mention the other in passing? If it is the latter, name the main interpretation and treat the other as a sub-message.
:::

## The perpetrators

The syllabus asks three questions: who carried out the killing and why; was murderous behaviour the exception; and why did non-Germans take part?

### Ordinary Men

**Christopher Browning**'s *Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland* (1992) is a study of one unit. Battalion 101 was a Hamburg police battalion of about 500 men, mostly middle-aged, from working-class backgrounds, too old for the army and with little Nazi training. In June 1942 it was sent to the Lublin district. On **13 July 1942** at **Józefów** the battalion's commander, Major Wilhelm Trapp, told his men that they must shoot the Jews of the village, about 1,500 in all, and offered the older men a chance to step out. About a dozen did. The rest shot the Jews in the course of the day. By late 1943 the battalion had shot at least 38,000 Jews and had helped to deport about 45,000 to Treblinka.

Browning's source was the files of a West German judicial investigation of the 1960s, in which more than 100 of the men had been questioned, and what is striking is that they described what happened with some frankness. His explanation was that these were **ordinary men** who became killers through a combination of pressures: obedience to authority; **conformity** and the fear of seeming weak before comrades (the dozen who stepped out had to face the others); careerism; the brutalisation of war; the dehumanisation of the Jews by propaganda; and the way each step made the next one easier. He drew on social psychology (Stanley Milgram's experiments on obedience in the 1960s, Philip Zimbardo's prison experiment of 1971) but did not say that ideology played no part. His conclusion was disturbing in another way: if these men could do it, almost any group of men could. His book is a study of **situation** rather than of national character.

### Goldhagen's answer

Goldhagen used the same unit and the same files and reached the opposite conclusion: the men killed because they wanted to, since they hated Jews, and the evidence of their eagerness and their cruelty, as at the "hunts" and the death marches, showed it. The two books are the best pair to compare; in a 1998 afterword to *Ordinary Men* Browning replied to Goldhagen point by point, pointing out that most of the men never showed zeal, that the dozen who stepped out were not punished, and that Goldhagen read intentions into silence.

:::key Browning against Goldhagen: the same evidence
| | Browning (1992) | Goldhagen (1996) |
|---|---|---|
| Question | How did ordinary men come to kill? | Why did ordinary Germans kill? |
| Cause | Situation: peer pressure, obedience, careerism, war's brutality, propaganda | Culture: a widespread "eliminationist antisemitism" |
| The men | Ordinary, could have been anybody | Ordinary Germans, willing |
| Józefów | Most shot because refusing was hard in front of comrades | They killed because they believed it was right |
| Approach | Social psychology; the documents of a trial; a warning about human behaviour | Cultural history; the hatred of a national group; a judgement on a society |
| Weakness | Might understate ideology; one unit | Might overstate the unity of German attitudes; the timing |
:::

### Was murderous behaviour the exception?

The picture of the perpetrators has widened since the 1990s. At the centre were the SS, the Einsatzgruppen and the camp staff, for whom killing was a job. Around them were the Order Police, the army (the Wehrmacht took part in the murder of Jews and Soviet prisoners; a Hamburg exhibition of 1995–99 on the crimes of the Wehrmacht caused a national argument), the civil service, the railways, firms and doctors. The Eichmann trial turned attention to the **desk perpetrator**. Hannah Arendt, who attended the trial, described Eichmann in *Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil* (1963) as an unthinking functionary, not a monster. Later research, including Bettina Stangneth's *Eichmann Before Jerusalem* (2011), based on his own interviews of the 1950s, found him to have been a convinced antisemite, and this has led historians to treat Arendt's picture of Eichmann as partly wrong while keeping her wider point that bureaucracy can serve killing. Harald Welzer, in *Täter* (2005), argued from the records that people who did the killing felt they were serving a moral purpose in a world that Nazi ideology had made.

On the question of the **exception**: the evidence shows that most of the men in killing units did kill, but also that some refused or avoided it and that it was possible to do so without serious punishment, as at Józefów. Historians therefore see a range from fanatics, through men who killed with some willingness, to men who conformed, to a minority who refused.

### Why did non-Germans take part?

The Holocaust was not carried out by Germans alone. The Germans had too few men to do it, and in much of occupied Europe they relied on local help.
- **Lithuania and Latvia.** In June 1941, as the Germans arrived, local nationalists carried out pogroms in Kaunas and elsewhere; Lithuanian and Latvian auxiliary units, such as the Arajs Kommando in Latvia, shot Jews with the Einsatzgruppen. The Jäger report records 137,346 victims in Lithuania by 1 December 1941.
- **Poland.** On 10 July 1941 at **Jedwabne**, in the Soviet-occupied east, Polish inhabitants murdered a large number of their Jewish neighbours, while Germans were present. **Jan T. Gross**'s book *Neighbors: The Destruction of the Jewish Community in Jedwabne, Poland* (2001) argued that the killing was done mostly by Poles and put the number at 1,600; a Polish state inquiry found that Poles had carried it out, "inspired by the Germans", and that at least 340 had died. The book caused a national debate in Poland. Poles also hid and saved Jews, at the risk of death, and the Polish underground set up *Żegota*.
- **Ukraine.** Ukrainian auxiliary police took part in killings, including Babi Yar; the "Trawniki men", mostly Soviet prisoners of war trained as guards, staffed the Reinhard camps.
- **Romania and Croatia.** Allies of Germany carried out their own massacres: in Romania the regime of Ion Antonescu was responsible for the murder of between 280,000 and 380,000 Romanian and Ukrainian Jews (the figure of an international commission of 2004); in Croatia the Ustaša killed Jews, Serbs and Roma.
- **Western Europe.** The Vichy government passed anti-Jewish laws in 1940 and French police carried out the round-up of July 1942; Dutch, Belgian and Hungarian officials and police helped with registration and deportation.

Historians give several reasons: old local antisemitism; nationalism and the hope of German favour; the association, in the minds of many in eastern Poland and the Baltic, of Jews with the Soviet occupation of 1939–41 (a stereotype, since few Jews were communists, but powerful at the time); greed for Jewish property; German orders and pressure; and fear. They also stress that local conditions made a difference: in Denmark and Bulgaria, governments and public opinion protected the Jews. The question of local help matters for the continuity debate because it suggests that old antisemitism mattered, but it also shows how much depended on German power and presence.

## The victims

### Jewish councils and the argument about "passivity"

The first historians to ask how the Jews had acted were also the first to cause pain. **Raul Hilberg** (1961) argued that the Jewish councils, by carrying out German orders, had in effect helped the machinery of destruction, and he described a pattern in Jewish history of seeking to survive by compromise and compliance. **Hannah Arendt** (1963) wrote that without the help of Jewish leaders the chaos would have been great but the number of victims much lower; her remark caused a storm, since many survivors thought it blamed the victims. Both writers said that the Germans were responsible. The criticism was that they asked too little about what the choices were. **Isaiah Trunk**, a survivor and historian, in *Judenrat* (1972) used the records of hundreds of councils and showed that they differed greatly: some leaders tried to protect, some collaborated, some resisted, and nearly all faced a choice in which every outcome was terrible. Czerniaków's suicide on 23 July 1942 and Rumkowski's belief that work could save the ghetto (both in the previous chapter) are the standard cases.

### What counts as resistance?

**Yehuda Bauer**, an Israeli historian, argued in *They Chose Life: Jewish Resistance in the Holocaust* (1973) and *Rethinking the Holocaust* (2001) that resistance must be defined broadly. He used the Hebrew word **amidah**, "standing up against", for any act of defiance and survival: armed resistance (Warsaw 1943, Sobibór, the partisans), but also smuggling food, teaching children in secret, recording the story (*Oyneg Shabes*), praying, hiding. He argued that the narrow test (armed fighting) was wrong because the conditions (hunger, no weapons, no allies, collective punishment) made it almost impossible, and that holding on to humanity under such pressure was itself a form of resistance. Others, such as Hilberg, kept a narrower definition of resistance but agreed that the conditions were the cause. The syllabus question is therefore a question of **definition**.

:::key How far did Jews resist? Two definitions
- **Narrow (armed or organised physical resistance):** relatively rare: the Warsaw ghetto uprising (19 April–16 May 1943), revolts at Treblinka and Sobibór (14 October 1943), the Auschwitz revolt (7 October 1944), the partisans. Reasons for rarity: hunger, no weapons, collective reprisals, deception, isolation, hostile surroundings.
- **Broad (amidah):** very widespread: mutual aid, schools, secret archives, smuggling, hiding, religious life. On this definition most Jews who could, resisted.
- **Judgement:** the answer depends on the definition; the strongest answers state the definition they are using and show how the evidence changes under each.
:::

### Which victims? Should the definition include others?

The word "Holocaust" has been used in two ways. In the **narrow** sense it means the murder of the Jews. In a **broad** sense it can include all victims of Nazi racial policy: Roma and Sinti, disabled people, and others. Historians who defend the narrow sense, such as Bauer, stress that the Nazis aimed at a total, worldwide, unconditional murder of the Jews, wherever they lived, for no other reason than their descent, and that no other group was in the same position. Those who want a broader term stress the common roots in racial ideology, the link in people and methods between T4 and Reinhard (Friedlander, 1995), and the fact that Roma were also killed on racial grounds in great numbers. Donald Kenrick and Grattan Puxon wrote the first English-language account of the Roma genocide, *The Destiny of Europe's Gypsies* (1972), and for decades it was ignored; the West German government recognised it only in 1982. The disabled victims of T4 were murdered under a different justification. Many historians now use both: "the Holocaust" for the murder of the Jews, and "Nazi genocide" or "persecution" for the full range, with Roma and Sinti given their own place.

## The bystanders: Britain and the USA

The previous chapter set out what was known and done. Historians' arguments turn on how much more could have been done.

**Bernard Wasserstein**, in *Britain and the Jews of Europe, 1939–1945* (1979), argued that British policy was ruled by other priorities: the Palestine White Paper of 1939 and the wish not to inflame Arab opinion, fear of an influx of refugees, fear of fuelling antisemitism at home, and a refusal to treat the Jews as a separate case. The government did less than it could have done.

**David Wyman**, in *Paper Walls* (1968) and *The Abandonment of the Jews: America and the Holocaust, 1941–1945* (1984), argued that the USA had the means to save many more. His case: the State Department's obstruction under Breckinridge Long; quotas not filled; the delay in telling the public; the Bermuda Conference of 1943 as a cover for inaction; the late War Refugee Board (January 1944); and the refusal to bomb Auschwitz. He wrote an article in 1978, "Why Auschwitz Was Never Bombed", and argued that hundreds of thousands might have been saved. His book is strongly moral in tone, and the "abandonment" of the title is a judgement.

**William Rubinstein**, in *The Myth of Rescue: Why the Democracies Could Not Have Saved More Jews from the Nazis* (1997), argued the opposite: that the Allies could not have saved many more, because Europe was under German control, the neutral states were closed, the Nazis wanted to kill and not to expel, and the only rescue was victory. He thought the criticism of the Allies was hindsight. Critics replied that he understated what was possible, as in Hungary in 1944, where diplomatic pressure and the War Refugee Board did help to save tens of thousands in Budapest.

On **bombing Auschwitz** the argument goes on. The case for: it was within range of Allied bombers from Italy from the summer of 1944; the IG Farben works at Monowitz, a few miles away, was bombed on 20 August 1944; and the symbolic value would have been great. The case against: the effect on the gas chambers would have been uncertain; prisoners would have been killed; the Germans could rebuild; and the transports would have gone to other camps. Both sides agree that the debate shows what people thought they could and should do.

:::key Bystanders: how to use the three historians
- **Wasserstein** asks why British policy was so limited and answers by priorities and attitudes in London.
- **Wyman** asks why America did not act and answers by obstruction and a lack of will; his tone is a judgement.
- **Rubinstein** asks what was possible and answers: very little; his tone is a defence of the Allies and a criticism of the critics.
- **Test:** each can use the same facts (Riegner telegram, Bermuda, the War Refugee Board, the bombing requests) and reach different conclusions; ask in each extract what is assumed to have been possible.
:::

## How the time and place of writing shaped each interpretation

Historians write in a time and place, and their questions come from there. This is the "time and place" factor in the syllabus. A table of the main cases:

| Time and place | What shaped the writing | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1945–50s: after the war, the Cold War | Trials at Nuremberg gave the first documents; in West Germany many were silent or defensive; survivors and exiles wrote the first histories | Poliakov (1951), Reitlinger (1953) |
| 1961–65: the Eichmann trial, the Frankfurt Auschwitz trial | The trial made the bureaucratic killer and the survivors' testimony public; a new generation in Germany asked its parents' generation | Hilberg (1961), Arendt (1963) |
| 1970s: West Germany's Institute of Contemporary History; the USA | Historians in Munich studied the state and its administration; American Jewish historians stressed Hitler's will and the Jews' own story | Broszat (1969, 1977), Dawidowicz (1975) |
| 1978–79: television | The series *Holocaust* brought the subject to millions in the USA and in West Germany | A rise in public interest |
| 1986–87: the *Historikerstreit* | West German historians argued over whether the Holocaust was unique and how Germans should remember it; Ernst Nolte and Jürgen Habermas were the chief voices | The question of uniqueness |
| 1989–91: the end of the Cold War | Archives in Poland and the USSR opened; historians could study the East in detail | Browning (1992, 2004), Aly (1995), Gross (2001) |
| 1990s: public argument in united Germany; new genocides in Rwanda and Bosnia | A new interest in how ordinary people take part in killing, and in comparison | Goldhagen (1996), Browning's afterword (1998) |
| 2000s–2010s: memory and Europe | Debates on how each nation remembers; local collaboration; the "bloodlands" of eastern Europe | Gross (2001), Snyder (*Bloodlands*, 2010), Longerich (2010) |

Some examples of how time and place shaped the **approach**:
- **Dawidowicz** wrote as an American Jewish historian who had studied in Vilna just before the war. Her book puts Hitler and the Jews at the centre and gives the Jewish communities and their leaders a large place.
- **Broszat and Mommsen** wrote in West Germany in the 1960s and 70s, when a younger generation was asking how a modern state had done it; their focus on structures and systems treats the regime as something to be understood through the study of its institutions. They have been criticised for taking attention away from the victims.
- **Browning** drew on the records of West German trials of the 1960s, which had gathered testimony of ordinary policemen; without those files the book could not have been written.
- **Goldhagen**'s book appeared in the 1990s, a decade of public argument in united Germany about how the nation should remember the Nazi past, and it was read in that setting.
- **Friedländer** was a survivor, and his work brings Jewish voices, from diaries and letters published after the war, into the history alongside the perpetrators' documents.

:::warn Do not guess a historian's motives
The syllabus asks you to link an approach to its time and place, but only where the extract allows. "He was Jewish so he blamed Hitler" is an assertion and a verdict on the historian, not an inference from the extract. Say what the extract asks and assumes, and then say what in the context makes that natural: the available evidence, the questions of the day, the audience.
:::

## Reading an extract: signposts for each school

You cannot know which historian wrote a Paper 3 extract, but you can tell which tradition it belongs to from what it stresses and what it leaves out.

## Using the same evidence in different ways

The same fact can serve different arguments. A model of how examiners read the evidence:

| Evidence | Intentionalist reading | Structuralist / functionalist reading | Synthesis reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hitler's speech of 30 January 1939 | A statement of intent | A threat for foreign audiences; no order followed | A frame that Hitler returned to when war made killing possible |
| Nuremberg Laws, 15 September 1935 | A step in the plan | Drafted in days to satisfy pressure from below | Both: pressure forced the timing, ideology the content |
| Kristallnacht | The regime unleashes terror | Goebbels and the party out of control; Göring angry | The state turned a party outbreak into policy |
| Nisko and Madagascar | Tactics on the way to killing | Real attempts at expulsion that failed | Expulsion was a real aim, and failure widened the options |
| T4 | Hitler's willingness to kill | A local programme with its own aims | The training ground for the camps |
| Einsatzgruppen extend to women and children | Following the aim | A radicalisation in the field | Local steps that the centre accepted |
| Göring's letter, 31 July 1941 | Evidence of the order | Euphemism that does not mention killing | A step in a sequence of decisions |
| Chełmno, 8 December 1941 | The start of the plan | A local initiative in the Warthegau | Part of a sequence |
| Wannsee, 20 January 1942 | Where the plan was announced | Coordination, not a decision | A meeting that organised what was already under way |
| Himmler's Posen speech | Shows the aim and its source | Shows the SS spreading the guilt | Evidence of the regime's consciousness of what it did |

## Worked example: a full answer on a practice extract

:::eg Paper 3 [40]: What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer.
**Practice extract L** (about 190 words)

The picture of the Final Solution as the carrying out of a plan that Hitler had made years before cannot be sustained from the documents. No such programme existed, and no written order from Hitler has been found. The Nazi regime was a tangle of competing offices, each eager to show its zeal, and Hitler's part was to encourage radical solutions, not to draw up detailed policy. The plans for expelling the Jews, to Nisko in 1939 and to Madagascar in 1940, failed; the ghettos in occupied Poland filled with people who could not be fed or moved; and the officials on the spot, in the Warthegau and the occupied East, who had to cope with the crisis, began to kill. Each experiment made the next step easier, until killing became the policy. It was the pressure of events and the rivalry of the leaders, and not a master plan, that carried the regime from expulsion to murder. We must therefore turn from Hitler's speeches to the day-to-day practice of officials and the situation in which they found themselves.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Martin Broszat, "Hitler und die Genesis der 'Endlösung'" (1977). The words are not Broszat's.*

**Plan (read, find, test).**
- Contrast and judgement words: "cannot be sustained"; "no such programme existed"; "Hitler's part was to encourage"; "failed"; "each experiment made the next step easier"; "not a master plan"; "turn from … to".
- **Main interpretation (two linked aspects):** (1) the Final Solution was not the execution of a plan or an order of Hitler's, so the intentionalist picture is wrong; (2) it grew out of improvisation, as failed expulsion schemes and the pressures of ghettos and war led local officials in a competitive regime to kill, one step at a time.
- Sub-messages: Hitler encouraged radical solutions; officials were eager to show zeal; the Warthegau officials; the historian should look at officials' practice.
- Approach: against a Hitler-centred view; an interest in the workings of the state and in officials; documents as evidence of what officials did; a label of functionalist (explained by circumstance and improvisation) with structuralist elements (competing offices), each with a because.
- Knowledge to use: Nisko (October 1939), Madagascar (July 1940), ghetto rations (181 calories in Warsaw), the Warthegau and Chełmno (8 December 1941), the extension of the Einsatzgruppen's killing to women and children in the late summer of 1941, Göring's letter of 31 July 1941, Hitler's approval of deportations in September 1941, 12 December 1941, Wannsee.

**A Level 3 answer (about 20 of 40).**
"The extract says that Hitler did not give a written order for the Final Solution. So the historian thinks that Hitler did not know about it and that it was done by other officials like Himmler and Heydrich. The extract says officials were eager and that they began to kill. This is shown by the Einsatzgruppen who shot over a million Jews and by Auschwitz where about a million Jews were killed. Hitler hated the Jews and wrote about them in Mein Kampf and the Nazis passed the Nuremberg Laws in 1935 and had Kristallnacht in 1938. Then in 1941 they invaded the Soviet Union and in 1942 there was the Wannsee Conference."

*Commentary.* The answer picks up a sub-message (no written order) and builds a **different interpretation**, that Hitler did not know, which the extract does not say and which is the claim of Irving and not Broszat: the extract says Hitler "encouraged" radical solutions. The knowledge is a general account that is not tied to any claim. **Level 3** in Table A (10) and Table B (10), about 20 of 40.

**A Level 5 answer (about 32 of 40).**
"The main message is that the Final Solution was not the carrying out of a plan but developed out of events. The historian says that no 'master plan' existed and that 'no written order from Hitler has been found'. This fits the evidence that the policy was emigration until 1941, and the plans for expelling the Jews to Nisko in October 1939 and to Madagascar in July 1940 show that expulsion was a real aim. The historian then says the ghettos filled with people who could not be fed, and in Warsaw the official ration was 181 calories a day, so a crisis was created. The extract claims that officials in the Warthegau began to kill; Chełmno began killing on 8 December 1941 under Arthur Greiser's administration, which supports this.

The extract also says the regime was a 'tangle of competing offices'. This fits the competition between the SS, the Four-Year Plan and the Party over the occupied East. The approach is to look at officials and the situation and not at Hitler's speeches, and the historian says this directly in the last sentence."

*Commentary.* A detailed and accurate explanation of the first aspect and a good start on the second, with precise knowledge. But the answer does not explain how the two aspects form **one** argument, does not test the claim with evidence that cuts the other way (Hitler's approvals; the Göring letter), and gives the approach only a sentence. **Level 5** in Table A (16) and Table B (16): about 32 of 40.

**A Level 6 answer (about 37 of 40).**
"The extract argues one thing in two linked ways. Its main interpretation is that the Final Solution was not the execution of a plan or of an order of Hitler's, and that it grew out of the failure of earlier schemes and the pressures of events, as local officials in a competitive regime began to kill. The first aspect corrects a view; the second supplies a different explanation. Together they say that the road to murder was improvised, step by step.

The first aspect is a denial of a plan: 'No such programme existed, and no written order from Hitler has been found.' The evidence supports the part about policy up to 1941. Until October 1941 the declared aim was emigration, which was ended by the ban of 23 October; the Nisko scheme of October 1939 and the Madagascar plan of July 1940 were expulsion plans, and Madagascar was shelved because Britain was not defeated. The T4 killing of disabled people shows a different programme. But the claim needs testing. The absence of a signed order is not proof that there was no order, since Hitler gave many orders by word of mouth. Göring's letter to Heydrich of 31 July 1941 told him to prepare a 'complete solution' in Europe; the first deportations from the Reich began only after Hitler approved them in September; the Wannsee protocol of 20 January 1942 says that evacuation was subject to the Führer's authorisation; and Goebbels recorded Hitler's speech of 12 December 1941 on fulfilling the 'prophecy' of 30 January 1939. The extract says that Hitler 'encouraged radical solutions' but not that he approved them, so it gives him a smaller role than the evidence suggests, a limit that matters.

The second aspect is about cause: 'the pressure of events and the rivalry of the leaders'. The evidence is the overcrowding of the General Government (the Warsaw ghetto had over 400,000 people and a ration of 181 calories a day), the rivalry of Himmler, Frank and the Gauleiters, and the local initiative of the Warthegau, where Chełmno, which began gassing on 8 December 1941, was run by a special detachment under Herbert Lange for Gauleiter Arthur Greiser. A test: the Einsatzgruppen began mass shooting on 22 June 1941, and by the late summer were killing women and children, before Chełmno; and they went with instructions from Heydrich. The extract leaves out the ideology that made Jews the 'solution', and the instructions from the centre.

This is the approach. The historian asks what officials did and why, finds the answer in the structure of the regime and in a crisis, and corrects an older view. The phrase 'cannot be sustained' shows that the extract is written against the intentionalist picture, so it is fair to call it functionalist (it explains by circumstance and improvisation) with a structuralist element (the competing offices), because both labels are supported by the extract's own words. The two aspects fit together: if the regime was a competition of offices, and if expulsion failed, then a plan was not needed to produce murder, and so the question 'what did Hitler want?' is the wrong place to start. That is the logic of the approach, and its limit."

*Commentary.* The opening states the **whole** interpretation and its two aspects. Each aspect is explained with the extract's own words and tested with precise knowledge (Nisko, Madagascar, 23 October, 31 July, 12 December, 8 December, 181 calories). The approach is explained from the extract and the labels are used with their *because*. **Level 6** in Table A (19) and Table B (18): about 37 of 40. It is about 660 words, which is a little long for 55 minutes; a 520-word version that keeps the opening, one test and the approach would earn the same level.
:::

**A Level 3 paragraph (Table A: explains a sub-message or asserts).** "The historian was Jewish and so she blamed Hitler and was biased. The extract says Hitler hated the Jews from 1919. That shows she was not fair."

*Commentary.* "Biased" is an assertion and a verdict on the historian, which the question does not ask for, and nothing in the extract says the author's religion. The sub-message about 1919 is noticed but not explained. Level 3 at best.

*Commentary.* Accurate, close to the text and sound on the approach. But it does not say **why** the approach fits the interpretation, or what in the context shaped it. Level 5.

**A Level 6 paragraph (Table A: complete understanding of interpretation and approach).** "The approach is to begin with the leader's intention and to treat everything else as means. The extract uses Hitler's early writings (the letter of 1919, *Mein Kampf*) as proof that the aim was set early, and it treats the war as an 'opportunity' and not a cause. This fits the interpretation exactly: if the aim was fixed in 1919, then the stages of persecution between 1933 and 1941 are steps along a path already chosen, and the question 'what did Hitler want?' is the question that explains them. The approach is therefore intentionalist, and the label is supported because the whole extract, not only one sentence, makes Hitler's will the cause. The approach also leaves things out: it does not ask why the regime used emigration until 1941, or what the competing offices did, and those are the evidence that functionalists would use. In its time it also reflects a purpose: the extract keeps the Jews, as the object of the aim, at the centre of the story, which fits a historian who wanted the destruction of the Jews to be understood as the central event of the war."

*Commentary.* The approach is tied to the interpretation by a sentence of explanation ("If … then …"), supported by the extract's own words, with its limits, and given a label with its *because*. The last sentence is cautious: it says "fits" and does not claim to know the author's motives. That is complete understanding: Level 6.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What is the continuity argument, and who is its best-known supporter?
A: That the Holocaust grew out of antisemitic ideas that already existed in Germany and Europe. Daniel Goldhagen (*Hitler's Willing Executioners*, 1996) argued that a German "eliminationist antisemitism" made ordinary Germans willing killers; critics said that antisemitism was European and could not explain the timing.
Q: What is intentionalism? Name two historians and one piece of evidence they use.
A: The view that the Holocaust was Hitler's aim from early on, carried out when the war allowed. Dawidowicz (*The War Against the Jews*, 1975) and Jäckel (*Hitler's Weltanschauung*, 1969); evidence: Hitler's letter of 1919, *Mein Kampf*, the speech of 30 January 1939.
Q: What are two criticisms of intentionalism?
A: There is no written Hitler order and the policy until 1941 was emigration (Nisko, Madagascar), so a plan from 1919 is hard to show; it explains too little about the many agencies, officials and local initiatives that made the killing possible.
Q: What do "polycracy" and "cumulative radicalisation" mean, and who used them?
A: Polycracy: rule by many competing centres of power (Broszat, *The Hitler State*, 1969). Cumulative radicalisation: policy becoming more extreme step by step as rival offices competed (Mommsen, 1983).
Q: What did Karl Schleunes argue in *The Twisted Road to Auschwitz* (1970)?
A: That there was no straight line from Hitler's ideas to Auschwitz: between 1933 and 1939 policy zigzagged, driven by rivalries between the Party, the ministries and the SS, with emigration as the aim.
Q: Summarise Broszat's article of 1977 in three claims.
A: There was no master plan and no Hitler order for the extermination; the policy grew from the failure of expulsion plans and the crisis in the ghettos; local officials and agencies in the East radicalised it step by step, with Hitler approving.
Q: What did Götz Aly add to the functionalist argument?
A: That German planners concerned with resettlement and population policy in the East (*Vordenker der Vernichtung*, 1991, with Susanne Heim; "Final Solution", 1995) created problems of space and food for which the murder of Jews seemed a solution.
Q: How did Browning's *Origins of the Final Solution* (2004) combine intention and circumstance?
A: He argued for a sequence of decisions in summer and autumn 1941, in the "euphoria of victory" at the start of the war against the USSR, each approved by Hitler but taken in response to events.
Q: What is the Willikens speech and why did Kershaw use it?
A: In 1934 a Prussian state secretary told officials to "work towards the Führer"; Kershaw (1993) used it to argue that Hitler set the aims and officials competed to carry them out, so the system radicalised itself.
Q: What was Reserve Police Battalion 101 and what did Browning conclude?
A: A Hamburg police unit of about 500 middle-aged men which shot Jews in Poland from July 1942 (Józefów, 13 July 1942, about 1,500 killed; about a dozen stepped out when offered); Browning concluded that ordinary men can become killers through obedience, conformity, careerism and war.
Q: What is the main difference between Browning and Goldhagen?
A: Browning explains by the situation (peer pressure, obedience, war); Goldhagen by culture (a widespread German eliminationist antisemitism). They used the same unit and files.
Q: What did Jan Gross argue in *Neighbors* (2001)?
A: That the Jews of Jedwabne were murdered on 10 July 1941 mostly by their Polish neighbours; the Polish inquiry confirmed Polish participation, "inspired by the Germans", but found at least 340 victims, fewer than Gross's 1,600.
Q: What is amidah?
A: Yehuda Bauer's Hebrew term for "standing up against": a broad definition of resistance that includes any act of defiance and survival, not only armed fighting.
Q: Who argued that the Allies "abandoned" the Jews, and who argued that rescue was a "myth"?
A: David Wyman (*The Abandonment of the Jews*, 1984) argued that the USA could have done much more; William Rubinstein (*The Myth of Rescue*, 1997) argued that the democracies could not have saved many more.

**Practice extract M** (about 150 words)

Adolf Hitler resolved to destroy the Jews from the first days of his political career, and everything that followed was the realisation of that intention. His letter of 1919, the programme of 1920 and *Mein Kampf* show that he saw the Jews as the enemy of the German people and of the world. The persecution of the 1930s was a series of steps along a path that he had chosen, and its pace was set by what the world would tolerate. The war was the opportunity, not the cause: when it came, he could carry out what he had intended since the beginning. In 1939 he foretold the annihilation of the Jews in the event of a world war, and in 1941 he made it happen. The murder of the Jews was not a by-product of war but a war in its own right, fought alongside the other, with a logic of its own.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Lucy Dawidowicz, The War Against the Jews, 1933–1945 (1975). The words are not Dawidowicz's.*

**Practice extract N** (about 150 words)

It is a mistake to think of the Nazi state as a machine obeying a single will. It was a patchwork of competing offices, with no clear lines of authority, in which each leader sought to win favour by doing more than his rivals. Hitler seldom gave detailed instructions; he set a direction and left others to outbid one another in finding ways to follow it. In this atmosphere, measures against the Jews that had once seemed unthinkable became thinkable, and each radical step made the next one easier. The Holocaust was not planned in advance by the dictator; it was the result of a process of cumulative radicalisation in a regime that could not stop itself. To understand it we must study the working of the state and its rival agencies, and not only Hitler's beliefs.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Hans Mommsen, "Die Realisierung des Utopischen" (1983). The words are not Mommsen's.*

**Practice extract P** (about 160 words)

The men of Reserve Police Battalion 101 were not Nazi fanatics. They were middle-aged family men from Hamburg, drawn from the working class, too old for the army, who had grown up before the Nazis took power. Yet in July 1942 they shot Jews in a Polish village, and then went on to do so again and again. When their commander offered the older men the chance to step aside, only a dozen or so did so. Most found it easier to kill than to stand out from their comrades and to be thought weak. Obedience, the pressure of the group, the wish for advancement, the brutalising effect of war and the propaganda that had made the Jews seem less than human all played a part, and killing became a routine. If these men could do it, then almost any group of men could. This is a study of how people behave in a situation, and a warning.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Christopher Browning, Ordinary Men (1992). The words are not Browning's.*

**Practice extract Q** (about 150 words)

The Holocaust was a German project, and it was carried out by Germans who knew what they were doing and wanted to do it. For generations German culture had held that the Jews were a harmful presence that had to be removed. This belief, which we may call eliminationist antisemitism, was not the possession of a few fanatics; it was common sense in Germany. When the Nazis came to power, they removed the restraints, and ordinary Germans, in the police battalions and in the camps, took part willingly and with zeal, because they thought it right. They could have refused, and did not. To say that they were only following orders, or fearing their comrades, is to ignore what they did and said. It is the German people, and not only their leaders, whose beliefs we must understand.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Daniel Goldhagen, Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996). The words are not Goldhagen's.*

**Practice extract R** (about 150 words)

The United States could have saved many more Jews than it did. The means existed: the quotas for immigration were not filled, the State Department could have granted visas and did not, and in 1942 the facts of the killing were known in Washington. Instead the government hid behind rules, delay and the claim that the best rescue was victory. The Bermuda Conference was a cover for doing nothing, and the War Refugee Board was set up only in January 1944, when it was late. Bombing the gas chambers at Auschwitz or the railway lines to it was possible and was refused. The failure was not one of ability but of will: the leaders of the country did not think the lives of Jews were worth the effort. The word for what happened is abandonment.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of David Wyman, The Abandonment of the Jews (1984). The words are not Wyman's.*

**Practice extract S** (about 150 words)

We have been asking the wrong question about the Jews of Europe. "Why did they not resist?" assumes that resistance means taking up arms, and treats the people who were murdered as passive. But in the ghettos and camps, with hunger, no weapons, no allies, and the threat of the death of hundreds for the act of one, armed rebellion was possible only for a few and only at the end. If we define resistance as standing up against the Nazis' purpose, then it was everywhere: in the schools run in secret, the soup kitchens, the smuggling of food and of children, the archives buried in the ground, the prayers said and the hiding of the hunted. These acts took courage and often cost lives. The Jews were not the passive object of the Nazis' plan but the subject of their own history.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Yehuda Bauer, They Chose Life (1973) and Rethinking the Holocaust (2001). The words are not Bauer's.*

**Practice extract T** (about 170 words)

The decision to murder the Jews of Europe was neither the carrying out of an old plan nor an accident. It was taken in steps in the summer and autumn of 1941. When the German armies seemed to be winning the war in the East within weeks, the leadership felt free to deal with a problem that the war had made larger, and the killing of Soviet Jews was extended to women and children. In the following months, as the deportations from the Reich began and the killing sites were built, the policy was widened to the Jews of the whole continent. Hitler gave his approval to these steps, and nothing so large could have happened without it. But each step responded to events and to the initiatives of subordinates, and it was only when they came together that the "Final Solution" existed. The history of the Holocaust is the history of how ideology and circumstance combined.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Christopher Browning, The Origins of the Final Solution (2004). The words are not Browning's.*

:::recap
- **Shape of the debate**: 1945–50s first accounts (Poliakov 1951, Reitlinger 1953) → Hilberg (1961) and the Eichmann trial → Arendt (1963) → structuralism (Broszat 1969) and functionalism (Schleunes 1970, Broszat 1977, Mommsen 1983) against intentionalism (Jäckel 1969, Dawidowicz 1975, Fleming 1982) → Mason's labels (1981) and the convergence of the 1980s → synthesis (Browning 2004, Kershaw, Friedländer 1997 and 2007, Longerich 2010) → the perpetrators (Browning 1992, Goldhagen 1996, Gross 2001) → victims (Hilberg, Arendt, Trunk 1972, Bauer) and bystanders (Wasserstein 1979, Wyman 1984, Rubinstein 1997).
- **Intentionalism**: Hitler's aim from the first; evidence: the 1919 letter, *Mein Kampf*, the prophecy of 30 January 1939; weakness: no written order, emigration policy to 1941.
- **Structuralism and functionalism**: a competing, polycratic state; cumulative radicalisation; failed plans (Nisko, Madagascar), overcrowded ghettos, local initiative; weakness: they can lose Hitler and ideology.
- **Synthesis**: a sequence of decisions in 1941 with Hitler's approval; "working towards the Führer"; redemptive antisemitism. Not a safe default.
- **Continuity**: Goldhagen's eliminationist antisemitism against the evidence of European antisemitism and timing.
- **Perpetrators**: ordinary men (Browning) against willing Germans (Goldhagen); local help (Gross, Lithuania, Romania).
