:::try Warm-up
Q: What is the difference between a cause and a consequence? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))
A: A cause is something that helped to bring an event about; a consequence is something that followed from it. The same event can be a consequence of one thing and a cause of another: the bankruptcy of the French state was a consequence of war debts and a cause of the calling of the Estates-General.
Q: Why do historians prefer to say "a" cause rather than "the" cause? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))
A: Big events almost always have several causes at different levels (long-term conditions, short-term crises, triggers and individual choices), so the useful question is how the causes connect and which mattered most.
Q: What is a primary source, and why can a primary source still mislead? (from [History from zero](#hist.f.basics))
A: A primary source comes from the time being studied. It can still mislead because its author had a purpose, a position and limited knowledge, so you ask who made it, when, why and for whom.
:::

## Why the syllabus is split, and why historians still look wider

The Cambridge syllabus is built from **topics**. Each topic is one country or one set of events over a fixed period: France 1774–1814, Mussolini's Italy 1919–41, the USA 1945–61. Each exam question is on one topic, so you can prepare for it, and the board can set a fair paper. A student studies one option at AS (three topics), one topic at Paper 3 and at least two topics of one option at Paper 4. That is the reason for the shape of this course: one chapter per key question, grouped by topic.

The price of that shape is that it hides the connections. One history student who read an early version of this course said that the topics felt like separate boxes, with the story of the whole world missing. Historians do not work in boxes. They use three tools that cross borders.

- **Eras.** A stretch of years held together by a common process: an *age of revolutions*, an *age of empire*, an *age of total war*. The labels are conventions, chosen to make sense of a mass of events, and different historians draw the lines in different places. The great British historian E.J. Hobsbawm (1917–2012) wrote a famous series that used them: *The Age of Revolution 1789–1848* (1962), *The Age of Capital 1848–1875* (1975), *The Age of Empire 1875–1914* (1987) and *Age of Extremes: The Short Twentieth Century 1914–1991* (1994). He called 1789–1914 the "long nineteenth century" and 1914–91 the "short twentieth century". You do not need to agree with him. You need to see why the lines he drew are useful.
- **Phenomena.** Forces that appear in many countries: nationalism, liberalism, industrialisation, imperialism, socialism and communism, fascism, total war, decolonisation. The same idea showed up in Frankfurt in 1848 and Kuala Lumpur in 1957, and each case is easier to understand when you know the other.
- **Connections.** Direct links between events: a revolution in one country that frightened the rulers of another; a loan from one country that kept the economy of another running; a war whose result sent the loser's government into crisis. These are the links the exam rewards, because they are causes.

:::def Era
A period of history treated as a unit because one major process or set of events shaped it. The dates of an era are a convention chosen by historians, not a fact, so you should always be ready to say why the boundaries sit where they do.
:::

:::def Phenomenon (in history)
A force, idea or pattern that appears in more than one country or period, such as nationalism or industrialisation. Studying a phenomenon means comparing its different appearances and asking what they share and where they differ.
:::

:::note How to use this chapter
This chapter is a map, so it summarises and points. Each era section says what happened across the world, which chapters of the course belong to it, and a few concrete connections between them. Where a chapter teaches something in depth, the link says so and the detail stays there. Every chapter in the course now also ends with a short **Connections** box that points sideways in the same way. If you want only the story, read this chapter straight through; if you want to use it while revising, jump to the era that contains your topic.
:::

## Before 1789: the world the course starts in

:::note Not examined: the eighteenth century, in plain words
The course starts in 1774, with a French king and his money problems. Nothing in this section is examined. It is here because the people of 1789 were reacting to a world that already existed, and you cannot understand a revolution without knowing what it was a revolution against. Read it once; do not memorise dates.
:::

### Kings, empires and the idea of absolute rule

In 1750 almost every large state was a monarchy. In most of them the ruler held that his or her power came from God and answered to no assembly. This is **absolutism** (or absolute monarchy), and its model was France under Louis XIV, who reigned from 1643 to 1715 and built the palace of Versailles, where the nobility lived under his eye. The idea that kings ruled by **divine right** was already under strain, and you will meet the strain in [France (1)](#hist.eu.france.1).

Around France stood the other great powers.

- **The Habsburg monarchy** ruled Austria, Bohemia and Hungary, and its head was also, in name, the Holy Roman Emperor over hundreds of small German states. Under Maria Theresa (1740–80) and Joseph II (1780–90) it tried reform from above. Its map matters in [Germany (1)](#hist.eu.germany.1), because the German states of 1815 grew out of that patchwork.
- **The Ottoman Empire** covered the Balkans, the Middle East and North Africa. In 1683 its army had stood outside Vienna; by the eighteenth century it was slowly losing ground to Russia and Austria.
- **Russia**, made a European power by Peter the Great (ruled 1682–1725) and expanded by Catherine the Great (1762–96), swallowed much of Poland in the partitions of 1772, 1793 and 1795, shared with Prussia and Austria. Its rulers were autocrats and its peasants were serfs, bound to the land and to their lords. That is the world that [Russia (1)](#hist.eu.russia.1) comes out of.
- **Qing China**, under the emperor Qianlong (1735–96), was the largest and one of the richest states on earth. Its population is thought to have been about 300 million by 1800, though counts in this period are rough.
- **The Mughal Empire** in India broke up after the death of Aurangzeb in 1707. In 1757 at Plassey the British East India Company, a trading company with its own army, defeated the ruler of Bengal, and by 1765 it was collecting the revenue of Bengal. That is how a trading company became a power in Asia.
- **The Atlantic empires** of Spain, Portugal, Britain and France held vast colonies in the Americas, worked by the enslaved. France's richest colony, Saint-Domingue (later Haiti), was the world's largest producer of sugar.

### The Atlantic slave trade

The sugar, tobacco and cotton of the Americas depended on forced labour. The **Atlantic slave trade** carried about 12.5 million Africans onto ships between the sixteenth and the nineteenth centuries, and about 10.7 million of them arrived alive, according to the Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database (Voyages), the standard estimate, built from thousands of ships' records. The trade was at its height in the eighteenth century. It made fortunes for ports such as Bristol, Liverpool, Nantes and Lisbon, and it fed the early growth of European industry. It also lies behind two of the topics of the course: slavery is the central cause of the American Civil War in [Civil War and Reconstruction (1)](#hist.us.civilwar.1), and its legacy shapes every chapter on civil rights.

### Science and the Enlightenment

Before the 1700s most Europeans took their beliefs about nature and government from tradition and the Church. The **Scientific Revolution** (Copernicus in 1543, Galileo in the early 1600s, and Isaac Newton's *Principia* in 1687) showed that the heavens and the earth obey laws that human reason can discover. The **Enlightenment** of the eighteenth century applied the same confidence to society. One idea from each of its best-known writers is enough for now:

| Writer | One idea |
|---|---|
| John Locke (English, 1689) | People have natural rights, and a government rests on the consent of the governed; if it breaks the trust, they may replace it |
| Montesquieu (French, 1748) | Liberty is safest where the powers of making, enforcing and judging the law are held by different hands |
| Voltaire (French, many works) | Tolerance in religion; the Church and the state should not be able to punish people for what they think |
| Jean-Jacques Rousseau (Genevan, 1762) | Legitimate power comes from the people, and law should express the "general will" |
| Adam Smith (Scottish, 1776) | Free markets, not royal monopolies and privileges, create national wealth |

These writers did not cause revolutions by themselves, and many of their readers wanted reformed monarchies, not republics. But they gave educated people a **language**: rights, consent, the nation, the people, reason. Revolutionaries in Paris, Philadelphia and Port-au-Prince all used it.

### The Seven Years' War and the American Revolution

The **Seven Years' War** (1756–63) was fought between Britain and its allies on one side and France and its allies on the other, in Europe, North America, the Caribbean, West Africa, India and the Philippines. Some historians call it the first world war. France lost most of its empire in North America by the Treaty of Paris of 1763, and Britain gained Canada and the land east of the Mississippi. Both sides ended the war deep in debt.

Britain tried to make its American colonists pay part of the bill, with new taxes such as the Stamp Act of 1765. The colonists answered that they could not be taxed by a parliament in which they had no representatives. The quarrel became a war in 1775. On 4 July 1776 the Continental Congress adopted the **Declaration of Independence**, which says that all men are created equal and have rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness, and that governments get their power from the consent of the governed. It drew on Locke. France joined the colonists in 1778, partly for revenge, and the war ended with the Treaty of Paris of 1783, which recognised the United States.

For this course the American Revolution matters twice. It was the **model**: a written constitution (1787) and a declaration of rights (the Bill of Rights, 1791) built by a people who had thrown off a king. And it was a **debt**: France's help cost over a thousand million livres, almost all borrowed, and that debt led straight into the bankruptcy that starts [France (1)](#hist.eu.france.1). One revolution paid for another.

### Early industrialisation in Britain

In the later eighteenth century Britain began to change how it made things. Cotton spinning moved from cottages into mills driven first by water and then by steam. James Watt's improved steam engine (patented in 1769, widely built from the 1770s) burned coal to drive machines, and iron and coal fields became the centres of new towns. Britain had coal, capital, good rivers and ports, a growing population, a government that protected property and a large colonial market for cloth. This process is the **Industrial Revolution**, and it spread slowly: to Belgium and France, to the German states and the USA in the nineteenth century, to Russia and Japan late in it. The gap between industrial states and the rest drives much of what follows, from the scramble for Africa to the rise of Germany.

### Even earlier

The story is much longer than three centuries. The ancient world gave the Greek and Roman states, the Han and Qin empires of China, and great civilisations in India, Africa and the Americas. After the fall of the western Roman Empire (about 476), the medieval world included the caliphates of the Islamic world, the Byzantine Empire, the feudal kingdoms of Europe and the Mongol empire of the thirteenth century. The Black Death (1347–51) killed a large share of the population of Europe. The printing press (about 1450), the voyages of Columbus (1492), the Reformation (1517) and the Peace of Westphalia (1648) shaped the early modern world that the Enlightenment came out of. To follow any of it up, see the `:::links` block at the end of the chapter, where the best free sources are listed.

:::key What the pre-1789 world gives the course
1. **Absolutism under strain**: kings with power in theory and debts in practice (France, then everywhere).
2. **Empires and the slave trade**: the wealth and the injustice that later drove empire, abolition and civil war.
3. **The language of rights**: Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau and the Declaration of Independence.
4. **War and debt**: the Seven Years' War and the American War made states bankrupt, and bankruptcy forced reform.
5. **Britain's early industry**: the beginning of a gap in wealth and power that reshaped the world.

## Era 1: the age of revolutions, 1774–1849

**What happened across the world.** Historians often call these years the *age of revolutions* because a wave of upheavals reshaped three continents. Hobsbawm described them as a **dual revolution**: a political revolution in France and an economic one in Britain. Together they made the modern world.

- **The American Revolution** (1775–83) created a republic (above).
- **France, 1789–1815.** The Revolution abolished the Ancien Régime in 1789, became a republic in 1792, went through the Terror of 1793–94 and the Directory (1795–99), and was ended by General Napoleon Bonaparte, who ruled as First Consul and then as Emperor (crowned 2 December 1804). His armies conquered much of Europe before his defeat at Waterloo on 18 June 1815. You study this in [France (1)](#hist.eu.france.1), [France (2)](#hist.eu.france.2), [France (3)](#hist.eu.france.3) and [France (4)](#hist.eu.france.4).
- **The Haitian Revolution.** In 1791 the enslaved people of Saint-Domingue rose against their masters. After years of war against French, British and Spanish armies they proclaimed the independent state of Haiti on 1 January 1804, the first state founded by a successful slave revolt. The ideas of 1789 had reached the colonies, and the people there applied "liberty, equality" to themselves.
- **Latin America.** Napoleon's invasion of Spain in 1808 broke the link between Madrid and its colonies. Under leaders such as Simón Bolívar, most of Spanish South America won independence by 1824–26.
- **Europe after Napoleon.** At the **Congress of Vienna** (1814–15) the great powers tried to restore order and prevent another revolution. They created a German Confederation of 39 states under Austrian leadership. That is the Germany you meet in [Germany (1)](#hist.eu.germany.1). Further revolutions in 1830 (in France, which got a new king, and in Belgium, which broke away from the Netherlands) and the Greek war of independence against the Ottomans (1821–32) showed that the settlement did not hold.
- **The economic revolution.** Britain's industry spread. The first public steam railway opened in 1825, and railways, factories and cities changed Europe and North America. In the United States, cotton grown by enslaved workers fed the mills of Lancashire, which tied the slave South to the British industrial economy.
- **1848.** In February 1848 a revolution in Paris overthrew King Louis-Philippe. Within weeks there were revolutions in Vienna (March, forcing out the Austrian minister Metternich), Berlin (March), Milan and Venice, and in Hungary and Bohemia. Liberals demanded constitutions, nationalists demanded national states, and the poor demanded bread. By the end of 1849 most of the revolutions had been defeated by armies and divisions among the revolutionaries. Historians sometimes end the age of revolutions here, because after 1849 change came more by wars, diplomacy and industry than by barricades.

**Four connections to hold on to.**

1. **Ideas travel.** The Declaration of the Rights of Man of 1789 echoed the American Declaration of 1776 and was in turn the model for the liberal demands of 1848 across Europe. The *Rights of Man* were then claimed by the enslaved of Saint-Domingue, who were not mentioned in the Declaration.
2. **War exports revolution.** France's armies carried the new laws into the Rhineland, Italy and the Netherlands. In Germany that destroyed the Holy Roman Empire (it ended on 6 August 1806, when Emperor Francis II gave up the title) and reorganised hundreds of states into a few dozen. [Germany (1)](#hist.eu.germany.1) explains why that mattered: the map that German liberals and nationalists wanted to unify in 1848 was a map Napoleon had drawn.
3. **Trade can make a nation.** In 1834 the **Zollverein**, a customs union led by Prussia, removed tariff barriers between most German states. In the same decade, in 1832–33, South Carolina claimed the right to "nullify" a federal tariff in the USA. One country used a common market to bind its parts together; the other nearly broke over the same question. Both stories are about who controls tariffs, and both reappear in 1850–1914.
4. **A shock in Paris spread.** The revolutions of 1848 began in France and travelled east and south in weeks, carried by newspapers, letters, railways and the memory of 1789. Their shared causes were also international: the harvests of 1846–47 failed across Europe (the potato blight struck Ireland and the continent), prices rose, and an economic crisis in 1847 left many people unemployed. See the worked example at the end of this chapter.

Historians use 1789 as a dividing line, but the French Revolution grew out of the American war, the debts of the Seven Years' War and the Enlightenment. A good answer treats 1789 as a turning point within longer processes.
:::

## Era 2: nations, industry and empire, 1850–1914

**What happened across the world.** In these years the states that dominate the course were made, or remade, by war and by industry, and Europe and the USA spread their power over the rest of the world.

- **Nation-building by war.** Italy was unified in 1861 (the kingdom was proclaimed on 17 March 1861), and the capture of Rome in 1870 completed the process. In Germany, **Otto von Bismarck**, who became Prussia's Minister-President in September 1862, led Prussia to victory over Denmark (1864), Austria (1866) and France (1870–71), and the **German Empire** was proclaimed at Versailles on 18 January 1871. You study the years 1862–71 in [Germany (3)](#hist.eu.germany.3) and [Germany (4)](#hist.eu.germany.4). In the USA the Civil War (1861–65) decided that the Union would not be broken and that slavery would end. Three nation-making wars in ten years changed the balance of power.
- **Reform after defeat.** Russia's defeat in the Crimean War (1853–56) by Britain, France and the Ottomans convinced its government that serfdom was a weakness. Alexander II freed the serfs in 1861 (the manifesto was signed on 19 February Old Style, which is 3 March New Style). When his reforms ended with his assassination on 1 March 1881 (13 March New Style), the tsars turned back towards reaction, which is where [Russia (1)](#hist.eu.russia.1) begins.
- **Industry and the new powers.** Steel, chemicals, electricity and railways created a "second industrial revolution" in which Germany and the USA overtook Britain in output of steel by about 1900. The financial panic of 1873 began a long period of slow growth and falling prices in much of the world; governments responded with tariffs and with a search for colonies and markets. In the USA the **Gilded Age** (the 1870s to about 1900) saw huge fortunes, huge immigration and violent strikes, and the **Progressive Era** (about 1890–1920) tried to reform the problems. See [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (1)](#hist.us.gilded.1) to [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (4)](#hist.us.gilded.4).
- **Japan.** In 1853 an American fleet under Commodore Matthew Perry arrived in Edo Bay and demanded that Japan open to trade. The shock led to the **Meiji Restoration** of 1868, when young samurai leaders ended the rule of the shoguns and began a fast programme of industrialisation and a modern army and navy. Japan beat China in 1894–95 and then, to the world's surprise, Russia in 1904–05. See [Imperialism and world powers (3)](#hist.int.empire.3).
- **Empire.** By 1914 European states held almost all of Africa. The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 set rules for claiming it, and the "scramble" that followed left only Ethiopia and Liberia independent. Britain held India (ruled directly by the Crown after the rebellion of 1857), France held Indochina, the Dutch held Indonesia, and the British held the Malay states. The USA took the Philippines, Puerto Rico and Guam and made Cuba a protectorate after the Spanish–American War of 1898, and so entered the club. See [Imperialism and world powers (1)](#hist.int.empire.1), [(2)](#hist.int.empire.2) and [(4)](#hist.int.empire.4); the Malayan and Indonesian background is in [The route to independence (1)](#hist.d.malaysia.1) and [(3)](#hist.d.malaysia.3).
- **The road to 1914.** Germany's rise upset the balance of power. Alliances hardened into two blocs (the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary and Italy; the Entente of France, Russia and Britain), an arms race (including a naval race between Britain and Germany) and a series of Balkan crises led to the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand at Sarajevo on 28 June 1914. See [Origins of the First World War (1)](#hist.interp.ww1.1).

**Four connections.**

1. **Defeat brings reform, or revolution.** Russia lost the Crimean War and freed the serfs. It lost to Japan in 1904–05, and the defeats at Port Arthur, Mukden and Tsushima helped turn the unrest of 1905 into a revolution ([Russia (2)](#hist.eu.russia.2)). Germany's Prussia beat Austria in 1866 and France in 1870. Wars decided whether governments survived.
2. **Japan copied Prussia.** In the 1870s and 1880s Japan's leaders studied the strongest armies and constitutions in Europe. Itō Hirobumi, the main author of the Meiji Constitution of 1889, studied the constitutions of Prussia and Austria in Berlin and Vienna in 1882–83, and the Japanese army, which had begun with a French model, turned to the German (Prussian) one. The new Germany and the new Japan were each other's mirror images.
3. **Freedom without land.** In Russia in 1861 and in the American South after 1865, millions of people were freed from bondage, but without the land they needed. Russian peasants had to pay redemption payments for their allotments, and the freedpeople of the South, who had hoped for land, mostly became sharecroppers on the plantations of their former owners. Compare [Russia (1)](#hist.eu.russia.1) with [Civil War and Reconstruction (4)](#hist.us.civilwar.4): the same problem produced different outcomes.
4. **Tariffs and empire.** After 1873 Germany (1879), the USA and others put up tariffs to protect industry, and the scramble for colonies was partly a scramble for protected markets and raw materials. [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (1)](#hist.us.gilded.1) and [Imperialism and world powers (1)](#hist.int.empire.1) are two views of the same economic world.

:::note Hobsbawm and the "long nineteenth century"
E.J. Hobsbawm argued that the period from 1789 to 1914 formed one "long nineteenth century", held together by the rise of industrial capitalism and the nation-state and ended by the First World War. In *The Age of Empire 1875–1914* (1987) he stressed how the great powers' rivalry for colonies fed the tension of 1914. Critics point out that a European story hides what was happening in Asia and Africa, which is why C.A. Bayly's *The Birth of the Modern World 1780–1914* (2004) tells the same years as a story of global connections: new links between empires, trade and ideas, with Asia and Africa as active parts of the change and not only its victims.
:::

## Era 3: world war and its aftermath, 1914–29

**What happened across the world.** The First World War is the hinge of the course. Before it, the chapters are about how nations, industries and empires were built; after it, they are about how people tried to put a broken world back together, and what grew in the cracks.

- **The war.** After the assassination at Sarajevo (28 June 1914), Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia on 28 July, and within a week Germany, Russia, France and Britain were at war. It was fought on the Western Front in France and Belgium, in the east, in Italy, the Balkans, the Ottoman lands, Africa and at sea, and it drew in soldiers and workers from the empires of Britain and France, from India and Africa to Canada and Australia. Japan joined the Allies in 1914; Italy joined them in 1915; the USA declared war on Germany on 6 April 1917. The armistice came on 11 November 1918. Roughly 9 to 10 million soldiers were killed, and a flu epidemic in 1918–19 then killed tens of millions more, a toll that estimates put in a wide range.
- **Four empires fell.** The Russian empire (1917), the German and Austro-Hungarian empires (November 1918) and the Ottoman Empire (the sultanate was abolished in 1922 and Turkey became a republic in 1923) disappeared. New states, such as Poland, Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia, appeared in central Europe.
- **1917 in Russia.** In the February Revolution (March New Style) the Tsar abdicated after hunger and war weariness, and in the October Revolution (25 October Old Style, 7 November New Style) the Bolsheviks under Lenin seized power. They made peace with Germany at Brest-Litovsk in March 1918, won the Civil War by 1920–21, and in December 1922 formed the Soviet Union. You study this in [Russia (3)](#hist.eu.russia.3) and [Russia (4)](#hist.eu.russia.4).
- **The peace settlements.** The Treaty of Versailles with Germany (28 June 1919), and treaties with Austria, Hungary, Bulgaria and the Ottoman Empire, redrew the map. Germany lost territory, had to accept responsibility under Article 231 and pay reparations (fixed in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks), and had its army limited to 100,000 men. The **League of Nations** began work in January 1920, but the US Senate refused to ratify the treaty, so the USA never joined. See [International relations 1919–29 (1)](#hist.int.1919.1) to [(4)](#hist.int.1919.4).
- **The 1920s.** In Germany, French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr in January 1923 to enforce reparations, a hyperinflation wiped out savings, and Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch in Munich failed in November 1923. Stability then came from loans under the Dawes Plan of 1924 and from the diplomacy of Gustav Stresemann (Locarno 1925; Germany joined the League in 1926). In Italy, after the "Red Two Years" of 1919–20 and the disappointment of the "mutilated victory", Mussolini became prime minister in October 1922. In the Soviet Union, Lenin died in January 1924, and Stalin was rising. In the USA, a boom, consumer goods and cheap credit made the decade look prosperous, while immigration was limited in 1921 and 1924. In the colonies, nationalists such as Gandhi in India used the language of the peace settlements against the empires that had signed them.

**Four connections.**

1. **War brings revolution.** Defeat and hunger broke the Russian, German and Austro-Hungarian monarchies. Compare 1917 with 1789 ([France (1)](#hist.eu.france.1): war debts, hunger, a failing monarchy) and with 1905 ([Russia (2)](#hist.eu.russia.2): defeat by Japan). In each case a war exposed a weakness that peace had hidden.
2. **A revolution frightens its neighbours.** The Bolshevik victory scared the elites of Europe. In Italy, factory occupations in the north in September 1920 and strikes and land seizures helped persuade the middle class, landowners and some politicians to tolerate the Fascist squads that attacked the left ([Mussolini's Italy (1)](#hist.d.italy.1)). In Germany, the memory of the Spartacist rising and the Bavarian Soviet Republic of 1919 helped make the army and the middle class distrust the left. In the USA, the Red Scare of 1919–20 followed bombings and strikes.
3. **A peace settlement leaves losers and the disappointed.** Germany resented Article 231 and reparations. Italy resented the "mutilated victory": it had been promised more territory by the Allies in 1915 than it was given in 1919. China resented the decision at Paris in 1919 to give the former German rights in Shandong to Japan, which set off the May Fourth Movement of 4 May 1919. In three continents the same peace made three kinds of nationalist.
4. **The loans circle.** Germany paid reparations to Britain and France, who used them to pay their war debts to the USA. After 1924 American bankers lent money to Germany, which used it to pay reparations. The whole system depended on American money continuing to flow. When it stopped (see era 4), the circle broke, in Berlin as well as in New York.

## Era 4: depression, dictatorship and the road to war, 1929–41 (and the war to 1945)

**What happened across the world.** In 1929 the post-war world still looked as if it might be mended. By 1941 it was at war again. In between, the Great Depression broke the economies and politics of a dozen countries, dictators took power in three of the great powers, and the League of Nations failed to stop aggression.

- **The Crash and the Depression.** American share prices collapsed in October 1929 (the panic began on 24 October and the worst day was 29 October). Banks failed, American lending abroad dried up and the loans circle broke. Tariffs rose around the world (the USA's Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act of June 1930 was followed by retaliation), and in May 1931 the failure of Austria's largest bank, the Credit-Anstalt, spread a banking crisis across central Europe. Unemployment reached about 25 per cent in the USA in 1933, and about six million Germans were registered as unemployed when Hitler came to power. See [Crash, Depression and New Deal (1)](#hist.us.depression.1) and [(2)](#hist.us.depression.2).
- **Different countries, different answers.** The USA elected Franklin Roosevelt, who began the **New Deal** in March 1933 ([Crash, Depression and New Deal (3)](#hist.us.depression.3) and [(4)](#hist.us.depression.4)). In Britain a National Government cut spending and left the gold standard in September 1931. In Germany voters turned to the Nazis, whose share of the vote rose from 2.6 per cent in 1928 to 37.3 per cent in July 1932, and Hitler became Chancellor on 30 January 1933 ([Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1)). In the Soviet Union, Stalin's First Five Year Plan (from 1928), forced collectivisation and the Terror of 1937–38 formed a "revolution from above", officially unaffected by the Depression but at the cost of a famine in 1932–33 and millions of lives ([Stalin's Russia (3)](#hist.d.stalin.3)). In Italy, the state rescued the banks and industry through the IRI in 1933 ([Mussolini's Italy (3)](#hist.d.italy.3)). In Japan, hardship strengthened the army officers who argued for conquest.
- **The road to war.** Japan seized Manchuria in September 1931, and the League of Nations could only condemn it ([International history 1929–39 (2)](#hist.int.1929.2)). Italy invaded Abyssinia in October 1935. Germany reoccupied the Rhineland in March 1936. The Spanish Civil War (July 1936 to 1939) became a test of strength in which Germany and Italy backed General Franco and the Soviet Union supported the Republic. Germany annexed Austria in March 1938 and took the Sudetenland after the Munich Agreement of September 1938, and Britain and France followed a policy of appeasement ([International history 1929–39 (3)](#hist.int.1929.3)). Germany occupied the rest of Czechoslovakia in March 1939, signed the Nazi–Soviet Pact on 23 August 1939 and invaded Poland on 1 September, which brought Britain and France into the war ([International history 1929–39 (4)](#hist.int.1929.4)). In Asia, Japan began a full war with China in July 1937.
- **The war widens, 1939–45.** In June 1940 Germany conquered France. On 22 June 1941 it invaded the Soviet Union (Operation Barbarossa), and on 7 December Japan attacked the American base at Pearl Harbor and European and Asian wars became one world war. The Allies defeated Germany in May 1945 and Japan in August–September 1945 (Japan's formal surrender was signed on 2 September). Estimates of the dead of the Second World War run from about 70 million to about 85 million, including civilians killed by bombing, hunger and disease.
- **The Holocaust.** The Nazi regime persecuted Jews from 1933 (the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, the pogrom of 9–10 November 1938), ghettoised them in occupied Poland, and from 1941 moved to mass murder, first by shooting in the occupied Soviet Union and then in killing centres. About six million Jewish men, women and children were murdered. See [The Holocaust (1)](#hist.interp.holocaust.1) and, for the historians' arguments about who or what was to blame, [The Holocaust (2)](#hist.interp.holocaust.2); the regime's own policies are in [Hitler's Germany (4)](#hist.d.hitler.4).

A student who studies Hitler's rise without the Crash is missing its main cause. See the worked example below.
2. **Democracy and dictatorship answered the same problem.** The New Deal, the Nazi work-creation programmes, Mussolini's state rescue of industry and Stalin's Five Year Plans were all the same century's reply to the question the Depression asked: what should a government do when markets fail? Compare [Crash, Depression and New Deal (3)](#hist.us.depression.3), [Hitler's Germany (3)](#hist.d.hitler.3), [Mussolini's Italy (3)](#hist.d.italy.3) and [Stalin's Russia (3)](#hist.d.stalin.3).
3. **Each success encouraged the next.** Japan in Manchuria (1931), Italy in Abyssinia (1935), Germany in the Rhineland (1936), Austria (1938) and Czechoslovakia (1938–39) each went unpunished, and the League and the democracies were shown to be weak. The sanctions that Britain and France supported against Italy in 1935 pushed Mussolini towards Hitler, and the Rome–Berlin Axis followed in 1936.
4. **Wars join up.** The war in China (1937), the war in Europe (1939) and the German attack on the USSR (1941) were separate until the American entry in December 1941. Germany declared war on the USA on 11 December 1941, and the two conflicts became a single world war.

## Era 5: the Cold War and decolonisation, 1945–91

**What happened across the world.** After 1945 two superpowers, the USA and the USSR, faced each other in a long contest that never became a direct war between them, while the empires of Europe came apart.

- **The Cold War begins.** The wartime alliance broke down over Poland, Germany and the nature of the peace (the Yalta and Potsdam conferences of 1945). The USA responded to Soviet pressure in Greece and Turkey with the **Truman Doctrine** (12 March 1947) and offered the **Marshall Plan** to rebuild Western Europe (announced June 1947). The Soviet Union controlled Eastern Europe through communist governments. The Berlin Blockade (June 1948 to May 1949) was followed by the creation of two German states, and NATO was founded in April 1949. China became a communist state on 1 October 1949, and the Korean War (1950–53) made the Cold War global. See [Origins of the Cold War (1)](#hist.interp.coldwar.1) and [(2)](#hist.interp.coldwar.2); the American foreign policies are in [Post-war USA, 1945–61 (4)](#hist.d.usa45.4).
- **The USA at home and abroad.** Truman, Eisenhower, Kennedy, Johnson, Nixon, Carter, Reagan and Bush faced a growing economy, the fear of communism in the McCarthy years, the civil rights movement, the Vietnam War, Watergate and the end of the Cold War. See [Post-war USA, 1945–61 (1)](#hist.d.usa45.1) to [(4)](#hist.d.usa45.4); [A time of challenges, 1961–74 (1)](#hist.d.usa61.1) to [(4)](#hist.d.usa61.4); and [The USA, 1974–93 (1)](#hist.d.usa74.1) to [(4)](#hist.d.usa74.4).
- **Soviet control and collapse.** The USSR crushed the Hungarian revolution in 1956, built the Berlin Wall in 1961 and sent troops into Czechoslovakia in 1968. Economic stagnation followed in the Brezhnev years. After Mikhail Gorbachev came to power in March 1985 his reforms (*glasnost* and *perestroika*) loosened the hold, the revolutions of 1989 swept away the communist governments of Eastern Europe (the Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989), and the Soviet Union itself ended in December 1991. See [The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (1)](#hist.d.soviet.1) to [(4)](#hist.d.soviet.4).
- **The end of empire.** India and Pakistan became independent in August 1947. Indonesia proclaimed independence on 17 August 1945 and won Dutch recognition in December 1949. Malaya, after the Emergency (1948–60), became independent in August 1957, and Malaysia was formed in September 1963. Ghana (1957) led a wave of African independence, with 17 states independent in 1960 alone. Zimbabwe followed in 1980. See [The route to independence (1)](#hist.d.malaysia.1) to [(4)](#hist.d.malaysia.4).
- **Apartheid.** South Africa's National Party came to power in 1948 and built a system of racial separation that lasted until the first non-racial elections of April 1994, after Nelson Mandela's release in February 1990. See [End of minority rule (1)](#hist.d.safrica.1) to [(4)](#hist.d.safrica.4), which includes white minority rule in Rhodesia.

**Four connections.**

1. **The Cold War reached into decolonisation.** The superpowers courted new states and backed rival sides: in Vietnam (see [A time of challenges, 1961–74 (2)](#hist.d.usa61.2)), in Africa, and in South-East Asia. Western governments often treated white-minority and anti-communist governments such as South Africa's as useful, and nationalists in Africa and Asia often looked to Moscow or Beijing.
2. **The same fear, different places.** American anxiety about communism shaped the Red Scare after 1945 at home ([Post-war USA, 1945–61 (1)](#hist.d.usa45.1)), and the Western view of nationalist movements abroad, such as the Malayan Emergency of 1948–60, in which Britain fought communist guerrillas. Soviet anxiety about invasion from the West shaped its control of Eastern Europe, as it had shaped Stalin's Five Year Plans in the 1930s.
3. **Civil rights and decolonisation are connected.** Black Americans and African nationalists watched each other. Both movements used the language of self-determination from 1919, and Cold War rivals knew that racial injustice in the USA and in South Africa damaged their claim to lead the free world ([A time of challenges, 1961–74 (3)](#hist.d.usa61.3)).
4. **The end of one empire sets others free.** The weakening of the Soviet hold in Eastern Europe in the 1980s and its collapse in 1989–91 ended the Cold War, and also changed the calculations of governments in Southern Africa, where apartheid lost the argument that it was a bulwark against communism ([End of minority rule (3)](#hist.d.safrica.3)).

## Five hooks that join the topics

Each of these is a link that can turn a good answer into a better one. They come from the chapters already linked above; here they are in one list.

1. **Tariffs.** In the 1830s the Zollverein tied the German states together with a common customs area, while South Carolina's Nullification crisis of 1832–33 threatened to split the USA over a tariff. After 1873, tariffs returned worldwide and fed imperial rivalry; after 1929, the Smoot–Hawley tariff deepened the Depression. One policy, four decades, three continents. See [Germany (1)](#hist.eu.germany.1), [Civil War and Reconstruction (1)](#hist.us.civilwar.1), [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (1)](#hist.us.gilded.1) and [Crash, Depression and New Deal (1)](#hist.us.depression.1).
2. **Freedom without land.** Russia's emancipation of 1861 and the abolition of slavery in the USA (1865) both freed people without giving them the land to live on, and both left a bitter peasantry or a poor labour force. See [Russia (1)](#hist.eu.russia.1) and [Civil War and Reconstruction (4)](#hist.us.civilwar.4).
3. **Japan copying Prussia.** The new Japan studied the new Germany for its army and its constitution. See [Imperialism and world powers (3)](#hist.int.empire.3) and [Germany (4)](#hist.eu.germany.4).
4. **The Dawes loans and the Crash.** American loans kept Germany paying reparations and Europe calm after 1924, and their withdrawal turned an American crash into a European political crisis. See [International relations 1919–29 (4)](#hist.int.1919.4), [Crash, Depression and New Deal (1)](#hist.us.depression.1) and [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1).
5. **"Revisionist" means different things in different debates.** The word means a historian who revises the orthodox account, so it depends on what the orthodox account is. In the First World War debate, the orthodox view in the 1920s blamed Germany, and the first "revisionists" argued that blame was shared. In the Cold War debate the orthodox view blamed the Soviet Union, and the "revisionists" of the 1960s blamed American policy. Same word, opposite targets. See [Origins of the First World War (2)](#hist.interp.ww1.2) and [Origins of the Cold War (2)](#hist.interp.coldwar.2).

## Worked examples: using the big picture in your own answers

:::eg Put eight events in order, then explain two links across a border
**The task.** Put these eight events in the order they happened.

- A. The Bolsheviks seize power in Petrograd (7 November 1917, New Style)
- B. The emancipation of the Russian serfs (3 March 1861, New Style)
- C. The Wall Street Crash (October 1929)
- D. The Berlin Conference opens (November 1884)
- E. The storming of the Bastille (14 July 1789)
- F. The March on Rome (28 October 1922)
- G. The German Empire is proclaimed at Versailles (18 January 1871)
- H. The Battle of Tsushima (27–28 May 1905)

Then explain **two causal links between events in different countries**.

**The order.** E (1789), B (1861), G (1871), D (1884), H (1905), A (1917), F (1922), C (1929). A good way to remember it is by eras: E is era 1; B, G, D and H are era 2; A and F are era 3; C opens era 4.

**Link 1: the war with Japan and the Russian crisis of 1905 (H and Russia).** Japan, a state that had begun modernising only after 1868, defeated Russia on land and at sea: Port Arthur surrendered in January 1905, Mukden fell in March and the Baltic fleet was destroyed at Tsushima in May. Each defeat showed that the tsarist government was weak, and the revolution of 1905 spread through the army, the cities and the countryside during the year. The link crosses a border and is a cause: a result in Asia made a crisis in Europe worse. The careful point is that Tsushima did not start the revolution, because Bloody Sunday (January 1905) came first; the war made the crisis deeper and wider. See [Imperialism and world powers (3)](#hist.int.empire.3) and [Russia (2)](#hist.eu.russia.2).

**Link 2: the Bolshevik revolution and the rise of Fascism (A and F).** The victory of the Bolsheviks in Russia in 1917 and the revolutionary excitement that followed in Europe frightened the owners of land and industry in Italy. After the factory occupations of September 1920 and the strikes of the "Red Two Years", many landowners, industrialists and politicians decided that the Fascist squads, which attacked trade unions and socialist organisations, were useful allies. In October 1922 the king chose to invite Mussolini to form a government rather than use the army against the March on Rome. The link is not that Russia caused Fascism, since Italy had its own post-war crisis, but that fear of the Russian example changed what Italian elites would accept. See [Mussolini's Italy (1)](#hist.d.italy.1).

**A third link, for practice (C and Germany).** The American crash of 1929 led American banks to stop lending abroad. Germany, which had relied on those loans since 1924, fell into a deeper depression and mass unemployment, and the Nazi vote rose from 18.3 per cent in 1930 to 37.3 per cent in July 1932. This is the subject of the next example.
:::

:::eg Turn a one-country explanation into one with international context
**The question (in the style of an essay on [Hitler's Germany (1)](#hist.d.hitler.1)).** Why did the Nazi Party win so many votes in 1930–32?

**A one-country answer.** "The Weimar Republic had weak foundations. Many Germans hated the Treaty of Versailles and the 'stab in the back' story, and the Republic's governments were unstable. Hitler was a gifted speaker and the Nazis used posters, rallies and radio. They promised work and bread, and they blamed the Jews and the Communists. By 1932 the Republic had few defenders, and the Nazis won 37.3 per cent of the vote in July."

*Commentary.* This is accurate, and it names real factors, but it explains the **conditions** and the **methods**, not the **timing**. The Nazis had existed since 1920 and won only 2.6 per cent of the vote in May 1928. Nothing in the paragraph explains why the vote jumped to 18.3 per cent in September 1930 and 37.3 per cent in July 1932. The missing cause came from outside Germany.

**The same answer with international context.** "The sudden growth in support was a result of the Great Depression, which came to Germany from the USA. After 1924 Germany had paid reparations and rebuilt its industry with American loans under the Dawes Plan. When the New York stock market crashed in October 1929, American lending stopped and the loans were recalled, so German firms closed and registered unemployment rose from an average of about 1.9 million in 1929 to about 5.6 million in 1932, with more than six million out of work in the worst winter months. The government of Heinrich Brüning, who was Chancellor from March 1930, cut spending and wages to balance the budget, which deepened unemployment. The collapse of Austria's Credit-Anstalt in May 1931 spread the crisis to German banks, and in July 1931 the Danat Bank failed and the banks were closed for two days. Voters who lost jobs, savings or hope turned to the party that promised to break with the system: the Nazis rose from 2.6 per cent in 1928 to 18.3 per cent in September 1930, and to 37.3 per cent in July 1932. Resentment of Versailles and the Young Plan (1929–30) gave the Nazis their themes, but the Depression gave them their audience."

*Commentary.* The international context does three jobs. It explains **timing** (why 1930–32, not 1925), it explains **who** voted Nazi (those hit by unemployment and bank failure), and it lets a student make a **judgement** (the Depression was the decisive trigger; Versailles and propaganda were long-term conditions). In an exam answer on Paper 4, one clear sentence of this kind, with a date and a number, can lift an answer from a description of Nazi methods to an analysis of why those methods worked in 1930–32. You do not need to retell the American story; you need the one link and the evidence for it.
:::

:::eg Widen the lens: why did Japan change so fast after 1868?
**A one-country answer.** "After the Meiji Restoration of 1868 the new leaders abolished the old feudal domains (1871), created a conscript army (1873), built railways, telegraph lines and factories, and set up a national school system. They believed in the slogan 'rich country, strong army'. So Japan changed quickly because its government was determined and well organised."

*Commentary.* This describes the **how** but gives no clear reason for the **urgency**. Why was a government with a new constitution of its own making in such a hurry?

**With international context.** "The urgency came from outside. In 1853 an American fleet under Commodore Perry forced Japan to open its ports, and the treaties that followed in the 1850s were 'unequal': foreigners were tried under their own law, and Japan could not set its own import duties. Japanese leaders had also watched China be beaten by Britain in the Opium Wars (1839–42, 1856–60) and knew what could happen to an Asian state that did not modernise. The new government's answer was to send an embassy, the Iwakura Mission, round the USA and Europe in 1871–73, and then to copy what it saw: a Prussian-style army, a British-style navy, a constitution (1889) influenced by Prussia's, and Western law codes. The aim was to win back control of tariffs and the end of foreign legal privileges, which came in stages from 1894. So the speed of change was a reaction to the threat of imperialism, and the model came from the same powers that threatened Japan."

*Commentary.* Now the answer links Japan to the wider story of empire (the Opium Wars, the unequal treaties) and to Germany (the Prussian model), and explains the urgency. It is the kind of paragraph that earns the top level in [Imperialism and world powers (3)](#hist.int.empire.3), because it gives a **reason**, with dates, for the pattern of change.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
**Question (in the style of Paper 2 (a)):** Explain why revolutions broke out in many European states in 1848. [10]

A student wrote:

"In France the people overthrew King Louis-Philippe in February 1848 because he had refused to widen the vote, and a republic was declared. In Vienna there was a revolution in March and Metternich fled. In Berlin there was fighting in the streets and King Frederick William IV promised a constitution. In Milan the people drove out the Austrian army for five days. In Hungary the Magyars demanded their own government. So there were revolutions everywhere in 1848."

**The level.** Level 2 (3–5 marks) of the real Paper 2 (a) descriptors. Each sentence is accurate and relevant, but the paragraph is a **tour**, not an explanation: it takes the countries one by one and never says *why the revolutions came together*. The word "so" at the end claims a link that the paragraph has not shown.

**The fix.** Start with the shared causes, and use the countries as evidence. "The revolutions of 1848 broke out together because the states of Europe shared three problems. First, the harvests of 1845–47 failed (the potato blight struck much of the continent), food prices rose and an economic crisis in 1847 put many people out of work, so crowds in every capital had reason to riot. Second, liberals everywhere wanted constitutions and nationalists wanted national states, and rulers such as Louis-Philippe and Metternich had refused to give them. Third, the first success, in Paris on 24 February, showed that a government could fall, and news travelled quickly by newspapers, letters and railway. Within weeks Metternich fell in Vienna (13 March) and the Prussian king gave in in Berlin (March), as the same mixture of hunger and political demand met weak governments." Now the examples are evidence for three connected reasons, and it can go on to the decisive one. That moves the answer towards Level 3 or Level 4.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Which years do historians often call the age of revolutions in this course, and name three upheavals in it.
A: 1774–1849. For example the American Revolution (1775–83), the French Revolution (1789 onwards), the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), the independence of Spanish South America (about 1810–26), the revolutions of 1830 and the revolutions of 1848.
Q: What did Hobsbawm mean by the "long nineteenth century" and the "short twentieth century"?
A: The long nineteenth century is 1789–1914, from the French Revolution to the First World War, held together by the rise of industrial capitalism and the nation-state. The short twentieth century is 1914–91, from the outbreak of the First World War to the end of the Soviet Union, shaped by world wars, the Depression and the Cold War.
Q: How did the Seven Years' War and the American War of Independence help bring about the French Revolution?
A: Both were expensive wars. France borrowed over a thousand million livres for the American war, and by the late 1780s interest on its debts was the largest item in the royal budget, so the state could not pay its way without taxing the privileged orders, which led to the crisis of 1787–89.
Q: Name three empires that fell between 1917 and 1922.
A: The Russian Empire (1917), the German and Austro-Hungarian empires (November 1918), and the Ottoman Empire (the sultanate was abolished in 1922).
Q: What was the "loans circle" of 1924–29, and why did it matter in 1929–32?
A: Germany paid reparations to Britain and France, who used the money to pay war debts to the USA, while American bankers lent money to Germany so that it could pay. When American lending stopped in 1929, the circle broke and Germany fell into depression.
Q: Which came first, the Meiji Restoration or the proclamation of the German Empire, and by how many years?
A: The Meiji Restoration (1868) came first, by three years (the German Empire was proclaimed on 18 January 1871).
Q: Give two ways in which the Cold War affected decolonisation.
A: The superpowers courted new states and backed rival sides, as in Vietnam and in Africa; and Western governments saw anti-communist governments, such as South Africa's, as useful, while nationalists sometimes looked to Moscow or Beijing for support.
Q: About how many Jews were murdered in the Holocaust, and in what war did it happen?
A: About six million, during the Second World War, in the years when Germany's armies occupied much of Europe (mass murder began on a large scale in 1941).

:::try Join the dots
Q: What connects the Zollverein (1834) and the Nullification crisis (1832–33)?
A: Both were about tariffs. The Zollverein united most of the German states into one customs area under Prussian leadership, while South Carolina claimed the right to nullify a federal tariff, which showed that a tariff could divide a country as well as unite one.
Q: What connects Commodore Perry's arrival in 1853 and the Meiji Restoration of 1868?
A: Perry's fleet forced Japan to open to foreign trade, and the unequal treaties that followed undermined the shogun's authority and convinced a group of young leaders that Japan had to modernise to survive; they ended the shogunate in 1868.
Q: What connects the Treaty of Versailles (1919) and the May Fourth Movement in China?
A: The peace conference handed the former German rights in Shandong to Japan instead of returning them to China, and the news set off student demonstrations in Beijing on 4 May 1919 and a wave of Chinese nationalism.
Q: What connects the League's sanctions against Italy (1935) and the Rome–Berlin Axis (1936)?
A: Britain and France supported sanctions against Italy for invading Abyssinia, which pushed Mussolini away from them; Hitler did not join the sanctions and supported Italy, and in 1936 the two dictators agreed the Axis.
Q: What connects the emancipation of the Russian serfs (1861) and the end of slavery in the USA (1865)?
A: In both, millions of people were freed without enough land. Russian peasants had to pay redemption payments for their allotments and the freedpeople in the South mostly became sharecroppers, so freedom brought continuing poverty and discontent.
Q: Why does the word "revisionist" mean different things in the debate on the origins of the First World War and in the Cold War debate?
A: It means a historian who revises the accepted account, so the meaning depends on what the accepted account was. In the First World War debate the accepted view blamed Germany, so revisionists argued that blame was shared; in the Cold War debate the accepted view blamed the Soviet Union, so revisionists blamed American policy.
:::

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: Hobsbawm called 1914–91 the "short twentieth century". Using this chapter, give two arguments that support starting a new era in 1914 and one that challenges it.
H: Think about what ended in 1914–18 (four empires, the old balance of power) and what began (communism in power, the League, the American role).
H: Then look for something that did not change: for example the empires of Britain and France survived until after 1945, and industrialisation and nationalism went on.
A: For: the war destroyed four empires and created new states and a communist government; it changed the role of the state in the economy and of the USA in the world; it produced the peace settlements and resentments behind the dictators. Against: the British and French empires survived and even grew in the Middle East; industrial growth, nationalism and imperial rivalry before 1914 were what produced the war, so there are continuities; and in Asia and Africa some historians date change from other events (such as 1905 or 1945). A balanced answer judges that 1914 is a good dividing line for Europe and for the great powers, and less clear for the world as a whole.
Q: Choose one connection from this chapter that crosses a border and one that does not. Which one explains more, and why?
H: A good answer chooses a criterion, such as "which link is a cause of the event, rather than a similarity" and uses dates.
A: For example, the loans circle crosses a border and explains why the Depression reached Germany so fast; a comparison between 1861 and 1865 (freedom without land) is a similarity, not a cause. A strong answer says that causal links, where one event changes what happens in another country, explain more than parallels, but that parallels show patterns that help a historian ask better questions.

:::recap
- **Why a map**: the syllabus is split by topic so an exam question is definite; eras, phenomena and connections join the topics up. You need only your own options for the exams.
- **Before 1789 (not examined)**: absolute monarchy under strain; great empires (Habsburg, Ottoman, Russian, Qing, British, Spanish, French); the Atlantic slave trade (about 12.5 million embarked, 10.7 million arrived); the Scientific Revolution and Enlightenment; the Seven Years' War (1756–63); the American Revolution (Declaration 4 July 1776); early industrialisation in Britain.
- **Era 1, 1774–1849**: the age of revolutions (American, French, Haitian, Latin American, 1830, 1848); Napoleon redraws Germany; the Congress of Vienna; the Zollverein and the Nullification crisis.
- **Era 2, 1850–1914**: nation-building wars (Italy 1861, Germany 1871, the American Civil War); Russia's reforms after 1856; the Gilded Age; Japan after 1868; the scramble for Africa (Berlin Conference 1884–85); alliances and the road to Sarajevo (28 June 1914).
- **Era 3, 1914–29**: the First World War as the hinge; four empires fell; 1917 in Russia; the peace settlements and the League; the loans circle; Mussolini (1922) and Stalin's rise.
- **Era 4, 1929–41 (and the war to 1945)**: the Crash and the Depression; the New Deal, Nazism, the Five Year Plans; Manchuria, Abyssinia, the Rhineland, Munich, 1939; the world war from December 1941; the Holocaust (about six million Jews).
- **Era 5, 1945–91**: the Cold War; the USA at home and abroad; Soviet control and collapse; decolonisation and apartheid.
- **Hooks**: tariffs; freedom without land; Japan copying Prussia; the Dawes loans and the Crash; "revisionist" in two debates.
