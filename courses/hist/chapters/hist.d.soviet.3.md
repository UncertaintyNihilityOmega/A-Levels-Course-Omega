## Gorbachev's rise to power

### Who was Gorbachev?

Mikhail Gorbachev was born on 2 March 1931 in the village of Privolnoye in the Stavropol region of southern Russia. His grandfathers were peasants, and both were arrested in the 1930s, one of them accused of being a "saboteur" in the collectivisation drive. He studied law at Moscow State University (graduating in 1955) and then made his career in the party in Stavropol, becoming the regional First Secretary in 1970. Stavropol contained the spa towns of the Caucasus where senior party leaders took holidays, and he met there Yuri Andropov, Mikhail Suslov and Alexei Kosygin among others. That mattered: Andropov, as chairman of the KGB, took notice of a young, hard-working and relatively honest official.

In November 1978 Gorbachev moved to Moscow as the Central Committee Secretary for agriculture. He became a candidate member of the Politburo in 1979 and a full member in October 1980, at 49 the youngest man there by many years. The grain harvests of 1979 and 1981 were poor, but he was no longer seen only as an agricultural specialist. Andropov, when he became General Secretary in November 1982, gave him wider tasks, including the economy, and Gorbachev came to be seen as his heir. When Andropov was ill, Gorbachev chaired the Secretariat, and under Chernenko he often chaired the Politburo when Chernenko was too ill to attend. In December 1984 he gave a speech to an ideological conference in Moscow on the need to speed up change. Gorbachev and the Georgian Foreign Minister-to-be Eduard Shevardnadze later recalled a conversation of that time in which each said that "we can't go on living like this" (their memoirs, written years afterwards, so they are the accounts of men who liked to see themselves as early reformers).

### Why he became General Secretary

Konstantin Chernenko died on the evening of **10 March 1985**. The Politburo met the next day, and the Central Committee confirmed the choice of **Gorbachev as General Secretary on 11 March**. There were several reasons.

- **The mood of the party's leaders.** Brezhnev had died in 1982, Andropov in 1984 and Chernenko in 1985. Many senior officials felt that a fourth old and sick leader would make the leadership a laughing stock and the system look like a gerontocracy (see [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2)). Gorbachev was 54, vigorous and a good speaker, and the Politburo wanted energy.
- **Andropov's patronage.** Andropov had promoted him and Gorbachev had inherited his supporters, among them the KGB leadership. The reformers in the party looked to him as Andropov's heir.
- **His position in the machine.** Since 1983 he had been the senior Secretary in the Secretariat and, in Chernenko's illnesses, the person who chaired it. He knew the apparatus and had no clear enemies.
- **Support from senior figures.** Andrei Gromyko, the Foreign Minister for 28 years, proposed him, and Yegor Ligachev, a Secretary who had helped to run the party's personnel work, was one of his backers. Gromyko wanted a man who would not threaten the system, and he thought that Gorbachev would keep its foreign policy.
- **The weakness of his rivals.** The main alternative was Viktor Grishin, the 70-year-old party boss of Moscow, who was associated with corruption and the old Brezhnev way. Grigory Romanov of Leningrad, who had been thought of as a rival, was not trusted by his colleagues. Dmitry Ustinov, the Defence Minister who could have blocked Gorbachev, had died in December 1984.
- **Speed.** Gorbachev and his allies called the meeting quickly, within about a day of Chernenko's death, so that the opposition had no time to organise.

The choice shows that the leaders wanted change, but not how far. The men who elected Gorbachev expected him to reform within the system: more discipline, more efficiency and a more active foreign policy. They did not expect the changes of 1987–91.

### The changes he made

Gorbachev's first task was to build a team and to change the Brezhnev leadership. In 1985–86 many of the most senior men were retired and replaced:

- **Grigory Romanov** was removed from the Politburo in July 1985 and **Viktor Grishin** retired as Moscow party chief in December 1985. **Boris Yeltsin**, a builder and regional boss from Sverdlovsk who had come to Moscow, became the Moscow party chief on 24 December 1985.
- **Nikolai Tikhonov**, the 80-year-old head of the government, retired in September 1985 and was replaced by **Nikolai Ryzhkov**, an industrial manager.
- **Andrei Gromyko** was moved on 2 July 1985 to be head of state, a mainly ceremonial post, and **Eduard Shevardnadze**, a party official with no experience of foreign affairs, became Foreign Minister.
- **Yegor Ligachev** and **Alexander Yakovlev**, who stood for different wings of the party (Ligachev a cautious reformer and Yakovlev a radical one), became Secretaries who guided ideology and the press.
- Regional party secretaries and ministers were replaced in large numbers, and new men, many of them younger, were given posts. By the 27th Congress in February–March 1986 much of the top leadership had changed.

These changes served Gorbachev's purposes in two ways. They gave him a majority in the Politburo and the Central Committee, and they put in place people who would carry out the new policy. But the change of leaders was also a sign of the nature of his reforms. He tried to renew the system by changing its people and its methods, not its structure, and that was true until 1987.

:::key Why Gorbachev rose, and what he did first
1. **The leaders wanted a change from the gerontocracy**: three leaders had died in under three years, and Gorbachev was 54.
2. **Andropov had made him his heir**, and Gorbachev controlled the Secretariat during Chernenko's illness.
3. **His rivals were weak or old** (Grishin, Romanov), and Ustinov had died in December 1984.
4. **Gromyko and Ligachev backed him**; the vote was taken quickly.
5. **His first changes were of people and discipline**: Romanov, Grishin, Tikhonov and Gromyko moved; Ryzhkov, Shevardnadze, Yeltsin, Ligachev and Yakovlev promoted. The voters of the Politburo wanted energy, not a revolution.
:::

## The situation facing Gorbachev in 1985

When Gorbachev took over, the Soviet Union was a superpower with a stagnant economy, as [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2) explained. The syllabus lists six parts of the situation he faced.

### The state of the economy

Western estimates put the growth of Soviet GNP at about 1.8 per cent a year in 1980–85, against about 4.9 per cent in the 1960s, with productivity falling. The old method of growth (*extensive growth*, more workers, more raw materials and more capital) had run out: the labour force was no longer growing fast, the cheap raw materials of the European part were harder to reach in Siberia, and the economy used far more energy and steel for each unit of output than the West. The planning system, with output targets set by Gosplan and prices fixed by the state, gave managers no reason to innovate: they were judged on fulfilling the plan, and they hoarded materials and workers to make sure of it. The result was a country that made more steel, more tractors and more coal than the United States but could not make a reliable consumer product or a competitive computer.

:::def Uskoreniye (acceleration)
Gorbachev's first economic policy, announced in April 1985: faster growth through discipline, more investment in machine building and new technology, and a campaign against waste, absenteeism and drunkenness, all without changing the structure of the planned economy.
:::

The Soviet Union was also living from natural resources. Oil and gas gave it most of its hard currency, and the high oil prices after 1973 had paid for imports of grain, machinery and consumer goods. The country had become dependent on a price that it did not control. This was the first hidden weakness: a fall in the oil price would cut the state's earnings and show how little the economy had improved.

### Food shortages and rationing

Agriculture had been a failure since collectivisation. The USSR had to import large amounts of grain (tens of millions of tonnes in the mid-1980s), many of them from its Cold War rival the United States, even though it had more farmland than any other country. The reasons were old: collective and state farms with weak incentives, poor roads and storage that lost much of the crop before it reached the shops, and a distribution system that sent food where the plan said and not where people wanted it. Brezhnev's Food Programme of May 1982 had promised improvement and had not delivered it.

Shortages were part of everyday life. A joke of the time had it that the Soviet shopper always carried a string bag (an *avoska*, from the word for "perhaps") in case something turned up in a shop. Meat, butter, sugar and sausage were scarce, above all outside Moscow and Leningrad, and the capital's shops were supplied better than the towns around it, whose people came to Moscow by train to shop. Many towns used coupons to ration basic foods such as meat, butter and sugar in the early 1980s. The one part of agriculture that worked was the small private plot of the collective farmer: such plots covered about 3 per cent of the farmland yet produced about a quarter of the farm output. The existence of this contrast showed how poorly the system used its land.

### The budget deficit

On paper the Soviet state budget was in balance. In reality the deficit was hidden. The state fixed prices at low levels and paid subsidies, and wages rose faster than the supply of goods that people wanted to buy. The surplus of roubles piled up in savings accounts and in the hands of people who could not find anything to spend them on, a problem that economists later called the **monetary overhang** (money that exists, but cannot be spent on goods, so that its value is hidden by shortages). One standard account puts the deficit at about 2 or 3 per cent of GNP before Gorbachev, rising to about 6 per cent in 1986 and above 10 per cent by 1988 (estimates vary with the source and the definition, and some give a higher figure for 1988). Before 1985 the state covered the gap with its income from oil and vodka, which together gave a large share of its revenue. Gorbachev would hit both.

:::def Monetary overhang
The stock of money held by households that cannot be spent on goods, because prices are fixed below the level at which the shops would sell out and the shelves are empty. Inflation is "repressed": it shows as queues and shortages, not as higher prices, until prices are freed.
:::

### Absenteeism and discipline

The workforce reflected the system. A Soviet worker was guaranteed a job (there was no unemployment in the official sense), and the wage depended little on effort. Absenteeism, lateness and drunkenness at work were common, and the old saying "they pretend to pay us and we pretend to work" summed up the bargain of the Brezhnev years. Andropov had tried to change this in 1983 with raids in shops and cinemas to catch people who should have been at work, a campaign that was unpopular and short, and Gorbachev started with a similar belief that discipline would help. Alcoholism was a particular problem: official figures counted drinking per head as among the highest in the world, and Soviet doctors and demographers linked it to accidents, crime and the premature death of men.

### Living and working conditions

Life for most Soviet citizens in 1985 was more secure than it had been in 1953 and poorer than they expected. Housing was the best example. Millions of families had left the communal flats of the 1950s for their own flats in the large panel blocks of the Khrushchev and Brezhnev years, but others waited a decade or more for a flat, and many young couples lived with parents. The best food and goods went through special shops for the elite and the *nomenklatura*, and ordinary people could get them only through queueing, contacts or the black market (the "second economy" described in [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2)). Health care was free, but hospitals lacked basic supplies and the quality varied. The life expectancy of Soviet men, which had been rising in the 1950s, had stopped and then fallen, to about 62 years by the early 1980s, a sign of the effect of drink, poor medicine and pollution. In the factories, equipment was old, safety poor, and many workers did heavy manual work that machines did elsewhere.

### Pressure for change from within and outside the Soviet Union

By 1985 there were pressures for change, and Gorbachev acted because he felt them.

- **Reformers inside the system.** Economists and sociologists in the Academy of Sciences in Novosibirsk, among them Tatyana Zaslavskaya and Abel Aganbegyan, argued that the planning system could not work. Zaslavskaya's paper of 1983, known as the Novosibirsk Report, said that the production relations of the Soviet system held back growth and was leaked to the Western press in 1984. Andropov's short rule had shown that people in the party wanted action.
- **The technology gap.** The computer revolution was moving the West ahead, and the Soviet Union, where the copying of Western computers was already common, was not equipped to follow. Military leaders knew that the next generation of weapons depended on microelectronics.
- **The arms race.** Reagan's build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative (see [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2) and [the USA, 1974–93 (4)](#hist.d.usa74.4)) forced the question of what the USSR could afford. Defence took about 9 per cent of GNP by the official figure of 1989, and 15–17 per cent by the CIA's estimates.
- **Afghanistan.** The war was costing lives and money and was widely disliked.
- **Eastern Europe.** The satellites' debts, and the strikes of Solidarity in Poland in 1980–81 (see [part 4](#hist.d.soviet.4)), showed that the empire was costly to keep.
- **Other communist countries.** China under Deng Xiaoping had since 1978 allowed peasants and small firms to trade for profit, and its growth was fast.
- **The outside world and the Soviet people.** Western radio, video recorders and tourists spread knowledge of how people lived elsewhere, and many educated Soviet citizens knew by 1985 that their living standard was low.
- **Dissidents.** Sakharov and others (see [part 2](#hist.d.soviet.2)) had kept alive a demand for rights, though they were few.

:::key Why Gorbachev needed to act in 1985
1. **A slow economy**: growth of about 1.8 per cent a year in 1980–85 (Western estimates), a technology gap, and a reliance on oil and gas exports.
2. **Food shortages and rationing** in many towns, with grain imports and an unreformed farm system.
3. **A hidden budget deficit** and a monetary overhang, with oil and vodka revenue covering the gap.
4. **Absenteeism and drink**, and poor working and living conditions, with male life expectancy at about 62.
5. **Pressure from within** (Novosibirsk reformers, the technology gap, the arms race, Afghanistan) **and from outside** (Reagan, the satellites, China, Western contacts).
6. **But the crisis was not a collapse.** In 1985 the system was stable, with no serious opposition, and Gorbachev chose to reform it. Neither the economy nor the leaders forced his choice.
:::

## Gorbachev's "new thinking"

Gorbachev used the phrase **new thinking** (*novoye myshleniye*) for a break with the habits of the Brezhnev years, and he gave it the title of his book *Perestroika: New Thinking for Our Country and the World* (1987). It was not a plan. It was a set of ideas that developed as events forced him on, and its three central words came into use in this order: *perestroika* in 1985, *glasnost* in 1986 and *demokratizatsiya* from January 1987. The syllabus asks for the reasons for, the nature of and the consequences of each. The table gives the short answer, and the sections below explain it.

### Glasnost

:::def Glasnost
Gorbachev's policy of "openness": a relaxation of censorship and secrecy so that the problems of Soviet society, and the truth about its past, could be discussed in public. It began as a means of reform and became a political change in its own right.
:::

**Reasons.** Gorbachev believed that the party bureaucracy would not change unless it was exposed to public criticism, and that the economy could not improve without honest information, since officials who lied about output could not be corrected. He and Yakovlev also thought that the intelligentsia, who read and wrote, were his natural allies. The disaster at Chernobyl in April 1986 (see below) convinced him that secrecy cost lives and credibility, and after it glasnost went much further.

**Nature.** The change came gradually. In 1986 editors, such as Yegor Yakovlev (no relation to the Secretary Alexander Yakovlev) at *Moscow News* and Vitaly Korotich at *Ogonyok*, were given permission to print more, and writers and film makers followed. The film *Repentance*, made in Georgia in 1984, was released widely in 1987, and it was a parable of a tyrant like Stalin. Books banned for decades were published, among them Anatoly Rybakov's *Children of the Arbat* (1987) and, in 1988–89, Boris Pasternak's *Doctor Zhivago* and extracts of Solzhenitsyn's *The Gulag Archipelago*. In December 1986 Gorbachev telephoned Andrei Sakharov in Gorky to tell him that he could return to Moscow, and over the next three years many political prisoners were released. The "blank spots" of history were filled: Stalin's victims were rehabilitated (Bukharin in February 1988), and in 1989–90 the leadership admitted the secret protocols to the Nazi–Soviet Pact of 1939 and that the NKVD had killed the Polish officers at Katyn in 1940. Television changed, with live debates and the news programme *Vzglyad* ("Viewpoint") from 1987, and church leaders were allowed more freedom in 1988, the year that the Russian Orthodox Church celebrated a thousand years of Christianity in Russia. The Law on the Press of 12 June 1990 ended censorship in law.

**Consequences.** The effects went far beyond what Gorbachev meant.
- The party's story of itself fell apart. Once people read about the terror of the 1930s, the famine of 1932–33 and the secret deal with Hitler, the party's claim to have led the country to socialism lost its force.
- Public attention turned to present failings: shortages, pollution, the war in Afghanistan, corruption and the privileges of officials.
- Nationalist movements (in the Baltic States, Armenia, Georgia, Ukraine) could organise and publish, and used the discussion of the Pact to argue that their countries had been taken by force. See [part 4](#hist.d.soviet.4).
- Conservatives used the press to attack reform. In March 1988 the newspaper *Sovetskaya Rossiya* printed a letter by a Leningrad chemistry teacher, Nina Andreyeva, that defended Stalin and attacked the reformers. It was taken as a sign that senior leaders, among them Ligachev, were against Gorbachev, and it forced a reply in *Pravda* in April.
- Gorbachev lost control of the debate. He wanted glasnost to serve perestroika, and the press and public began to ask whether the whole Soviet system was a mistake.

### Perestroika

:::def Perestroika
"Restructuring": Gorbachev's programme for reforming the Soviet economy and, in time, its politics. After 1987 it meant reforms that gave enterprises independence and allowed private cooperatives, but it never created a market economy.
:::

**Reasons.** The stagnation described above. Gorbachev wanted to prove that socialism could compete with capitalism. He believed in the Soviet system and thought that it had lost its way after Lenin, not that it was wrong.

**Nature.** It came in stages.
1. **1985–86: acceleration (*uskoreniye*).** The aim was to speed up growth through discipline, more investment in machine building and campaigns against waste and drink. This was old-fashioned and top-down, and it failed to bring change.
2. **1987–88: radical economic reform** (see the next section).
3. **1989–91: a search for a way to a market.** From 1989 the Soviet government and its advisers debated one plan after another. Ryzhkov proposed a "regulated market" with slow price rises in 1990, and in September 1990 two economists, Stanislav Shatalin and Grigory Yavlinsky, produced the "500 Days" programme to move to a market in stages. Gorbachev did not choose any plan. He swung between them and the main price reform came only in April 1991.

**Consequences.** Most of the other sections of this chapter are its consequences: a weakened plan, fragmentation, falling trade, shortages and a growing deficit.

### Demokratizatsiya

:::def Demokratizatsiya
"Democratisation": Gorbachev's policy, from January 1987, of bringing elections, a choice of candidates and public debate into the Soviet political system, first inside the party and then in the state, in order to overcome the resistance of the bureaucracy and to make government answer to the people.
:::

**Reasons.** By the end of 1986 Gorbachev saw that his economic reforms were being blocked by ministers, managers and party officials, who feared for their positions. Acceleration had not worked. He concluded that he needed a political lever: if officials had to face elections and criticism from below, they would have to support change. He also held that the Soviet people had been treated as children and should be made responsible for the country.

**Nature.** At the Central Committee plenum of 27–28 January 1987 he proposed secret ballots and a choice of candidates in party and soviet elections. The 19th Party Conference (28 June–1 July 1988), broadcast on television, approved a law-governed state, the separation of party from state, and a new two-stage legislature. The constitution was amended in December 1988 to set up a **Congress of People's Deputies** of 2,250 members, a third of them elected by public bodies (among them the party itself) and the rest by voters in single-member districts, which would choose a standing Supreme Soviet. The election of 26 March 1989 was the first in the USSR since 1917 where voters often had a choice. Many party secretaries lost, among them Leningrad's party chief Yuri Solovyov, who stood unopposed and failed to win half the votes cast, and Boris Yeltsin, who had been removed from the Moscow party leadership in 1987 for criticising the slow pace of change, won Moscow with about 89 per cent of the vote. The Congress met from 25 May 1989 and was broadcast live; the country watched deputies such as Sakharov attack the government. In March 1990 the Congress repealed **Article 6** of the constitution, which had given the Communist Party the leading role, and created the post of executive President, to which it elected Gorbachev on 15 March 1990.

**Consequences.** The effect was to move power out of the party.
- **The party lost its monopoly** and its apparatus lost the authority to give orders; the local party secretaries were no longer the unchallenged rulers of their districts.
- **A real opposition appeared.** In the Congress a group of radicals, among them Sakharov and Yeltsin, formed the Interregional Group, and elections in the republics in 1990 brought nationalists and reformers to power in several of them.
- **Power passed to the republics.** The Russian republic's elections of March 1990 made Yeltsin chairman of its parliament in May, and Russia declared its sovereignty on 12 June 1990, so that Gorbachev now faced a rival with an elected mandate.
- **Gorbachev's own legitimacy was weak.** He was chosen President by the Congress, not by the people. In June 1991 Yeltsin was elected President of Russia by the voters, with about 57 per cent of the vote, and that gave him a claim that Gorbachev did not have.

:::key What the new thinking did, in one paragraph
Glasnost opened the way to criticism, perestroika weakened the planned economy without creating a market, and demokratizatsiya took power from the party and gave it to elected bodies, above all in the republics. Each was meant to strengthen the Soviet system. Together they removed the three supports of that system: its secrecy, its planned economy and the party's monopoly of power.
:::

## Economic change under Gorbachev

### Restructuring the economy

The reforms came in steps, and they overlapped.

- **Acceleration and discipline (1985–86).** The Central Committee plenum of 23 April 1985 announced *uskoreniye*. Ryzhkov's government raised investment in machine building. A new body to inspect the quality of goods (*Gospriyomka*) began work at the start of 1987, and many factories lost bonuses when their products were rejected. The campaign against "unearned income" in 1986 attacked the second economy and the black market, and it too was unpopular.
- **The campaign against alcohol (from 7 May 1985).** The decree raised the price of vodka, cut the number of shops that sold drink and the hours when they opened, and banned drink at official events. Its aims were health, discipline and output, and in the first years death rates fell and male life expectancy rose. But it cost the state a large part of its revenue (estimates of the loss run from about 10 to 15 billion roubles a year), it made people make moonshine and buy sugar to do so, and it was hated. The campaign was relaxed from 1987–88. The historian Chris Miller has argued that this lost revenue did as much harm to the budget as the fall in oil prices.
- **The foundations of reform (1986–87).** The Law on Individual Labour Activity (adopted 19 November 1986, in force from May 1987) allowed people to run small family businesses in some trades. In January 1987 a decree allowed **joint ventures** between Soviet organisations and Western firms (at first with at most 49 per cent foreign ownership). Between 1986 and 1987 a number of ministries and large enterprises were given the right to deal with foreign buyers and sellers directly.
- **The Law on State Enterprise (June 1987; in force from 1 January 1988).** This was the main reform. Enterprises were to be **self-financing** (*khozraschyot*: to cover costs from sales and not to be bailed out), to choose their own suppliers and some of their output, and to elect their directors. The central plan was replaced by "state orders" (*goszakaz*) for part of output and by contracts for the rest. But at first state orders covered most of an enterprise's output, prices stayed fixed by the state, and the ministries kept their powers. Enterprises received freedom without the market to guide it.
- **The Law on Cooperatives (26 May 1988; in force from 1 July).** It allowed private cooperatives to trade for profit in services, small manufacturing and catering, and within a few years several million people worked in them. Cooperatives gave the first legal private business since the 1920s, and they provided goods that the state did not. But they were hated by many: they raised prices, they were taxed heavily and resented as profiteers, and they bought goods from state factories at low prices and sold them at high ones.
- **Agriculture.** The state agricultural bodies were merged in 1985 and leasing of land to farm families was allowed from 1988–89, but collective farms were kept, and the farm sector did not change much.
- **1989–91.** The Soviet economy's problems made the government ask for a bolder plan, but Gorbachev never chose the "500 Days" and in 1991 prime minister Valentin Pavlov imposed unpopular measures: on 22 January 1991 the government withdrew 50 and 100 rouble notes from circulation to remove the excess money, and on 2 April it raised many retail prices sharply.

:::def Khozraschyot (self-financing)
The principle, introduced for Soviet enterprises by the Law on State Enterprise, that each firm should meet its costs from its own sales and not be rescued by the state if it failed. It was meant to make managers efficient, but it was applied to firms that still had fixed prices, state orders and no competition.
:::

### The 12th Five-Year Plan

The 12th Five-Year Plan (1986–90) was approved at the 27th Party Congress (25 February–6 March 1986). It was the plan of acceleration. It put the emphasis on machine building and technology: the output of the machine-building industry was to rise by 40–45 per cent, investment in the whole economy was to rise by about 23.6 per cent (against 15.4 per cent in the 11th plan), according to Western analyses of the plan as approved. The Congress also gave the party a new programme, and Gorbachev spoke of the need for radical change. The plan was out of date within a year. It had been drawn up before the oil price fell and before the Law on State Enterprise, and its targets could not be met. Growth was slow in the late 1980s, and output fell from 1990. The plan shows how Gorbachev began: he was still using the instruments of the old system, and investment alone could not solve a problem of incentives.

### Fragmentation

:::def Fragmentation (of the Soviet economy)
The break-up of the connections that held the Soviet economy together: the central plan lost its power to order supplies and deliveries, and enterprises, regions and republics began to protect themselves, to hold back goods and to make their own deals, before any market had grown up to replace the plan.
:::

Fragmentation is the central paradox of perestroika. The Soviet economy was a single machine: one factory in Ukraine made a part for a factory in Russia, which delivered to Kazakhstan, all by order of the plan. Gorbachev's reforms weakened the orders without putting a market in their place. In practice:
- **Enterprises** with some freedom raised wages and cut the output of cheap goods in favour of dearer ones, since prices were still fixed; they could not be made to deliver.
- **Money grew faster than goods.** Wages and the wage bill rose faster than output after 1988, so that the monetary overhang grew and the shops were emptier.
- **Regions held back food.** From 1990 many cities and regions stopped sending goods to others, and local authorities put up barriers against "exports" of their food.
- **The republics took power.** The Baltic States, Russia and Ukraine passed laws that put their laws above the Union's in 1990, in what was called a "war of laws" and "parade of sovereignties", and they began to control the trade and taxes on their territory. Moscow lost its power to collect the taxes that the Union needed, and the budget deficit grew.

The result was falling output and rising shortages. Official Soviet GNP fell by about 2 per cent in 1990 (a CIA estimate gives 4–5 per cent), and in the first quarter of 1991 output was about 8 per cent lower than a year before, according to a Brookings Institution study of 1991. Coupons, which had been used in some towns, were used across the country in 1990–91 for goods such as sugar, tea, soap and cigarettes.

### Declining foreign trade

Foreign trade was the economy's lifeline, and it weakened. The Soviet Union relied on oil and gas for most of its hard currency. When the oil price fell by more than half in 1986 (from about 30 dollars a barrel in late 1985 to about 10 in the middle of 1986, the Brent price), earnings fell sharply, and imports of machinery, grain and consumer goods had to be cut or paid for with loans. Gorbachev's reforms made it worse: the freeing of foreign trade to ministries and enterprises meant that the state no longer controlled how its hard currency was spent. Soviet hard-currency debt rose from about 26 billion dollars at the end of 1984 to between about 55 and 80 billion by 1990–91, according to the source and the definition. Trade with the satellite states, which had been the other main part of Soviet trade within Comecon, was damaged too. From 1 January 1991 the members of Comecon agreed to trade at world prices and in hard currency, and the system broke down; Comecon was dissolved on 28 June 1991. By then the USSR could not pay its bills, and in 1991 Gorbachev travelled to the G7 summit in London (July) to ask for help and received promises but no large loan.

## Chernobyl

At 01:23 on **26 April 1986**, reactor 4 of the Chernobyl nuclear power station, near the town of Pripyat in Ukraine, exploded during a test. The explosion and the fire that followed threw radioactive material into the atmosphere. Two workers were killed at once and about 28 more, among them firefighters, died within weeks of radiation sickness. On 27 April the town of Pripyat, with about 49,000 people, was evacuated, and later a zone of 30 kilometres was cleared, from which well over 100,000 people had been moved by the end of the year. Hundreds of thousands of soldiers, miners and workers, the "liquidators", took part in the clean-up over the next years.

The response was a test of glasnost, and it showed how deep secrecy ran. The Soviet authorities said nothing for two days. The world learned of the accident when Swedish monitors at the Forsmark nuclear plant found radiation on 28 April, and the Soviet news agency TASS issued a brief announcement that evening. The May Day parade in Kyiv went ahead on 1 May. Gorbachev spoke to the nation on television only on **14 May**, 18 days after the explosion, and attacked what he called Western exaggeration. The cost to the state was large (one estimate is 18 billion roubles), and the disaster damaged the reputation of Soviet technology. Estimates of the eventual deaths from cancers caused by the radiation vary widely, from about 4,000 to much higher numbers, and the figure is debated.

**Impact on Gorbachev's policies.**
- It **pushed glasnost forward**. Gorbachev later said that Chernobyl did more than anything to make glasnost real, since it showed that the system could not go on hiding its failings, and the press began to cover accidents, pollution and the safety of industry. In 1989 Ukraine's national movement *Rukh* gave the issue of Chernobyl a prominent place.
- It **strained the economy**: the costs of the clean-up came when the oil price had fallen.
- It **fed nationalism and the environmental movement** in Ukraine and Belarus, which was the worst hit by the fall-out.
- It **changed Gorbachev's view of nuclear weapons**: he said that the disaster, which came from one plant, showed what a nuclear war would mean, and it lent force to his arms control proposals (see below).

Gorbachev wrote in 2006 that Chernobyl "was perhaps the main cause of the Soviet Union's collapse five years later", even more than his launch of perestroika. Historians treat this as an exaggeration by a man looking back: the accident was one among many causes, and the system survived several years. But it was a moment when the claims of the system were shown to be false before the whole world.

## Social change under Gorbachev

The effects of the changes reached the ordinary Soviet citizen in five ways.
- **Wider freedoms.** People could read, say and see much more, form "informal" clubs and societies (thousands in 1987–88, from environmental groups to historical societies such as *Memorial*, which began collecting the names of Stalin's victims) and travel more easily. Religion was treated more tolerantly.
- **Declining living standards.** After a first improvement the shops became emptier. Queues for the staple foods and for consumer goods were longer in 1990–91 than in 1985, and rationing was general. For millions the new freedoms came with disappointment.
- **Inequality and resentment.** The cooperatives and the new businessmen made fortunes, while pensioners and state workers saw their incomes fall in real terms, and crime and corruption were more visible.
- **Strikes.** In July 1989 hundreds of thousands of miners in Siberia, Ukraine and Kazakhstan stopped work to demand better supplies of soap and food and better pay, and then, in a new development, to ask for political change. Another wave in 1991 called for Gorbachev's resignation.
- **Nationalism and ethnic conflict.** Disputes between Armenia and Azerbaijan over Nagorno-Karabakh began in February 1988; Soviet troops killed about twenty people in Tbilisi in April 1989 and more than a hundred in Baku in January 1990. See [part 4](#hist.d.soviet.4).

:::key Why the economy got worse under Gorbachev
1. **Oil and vodka**: the revenue from both fell in 1985–86, and the deficit widened from about 2–3 per cent of GNP to more than 10 per cent by 1988.
2. **Half-reform**: the Law on State Enterprise gave firms independence but kept fixed prices and state orders, so managers raised wages and cut cheap goods.
3. **Fragmentation**: the plan weakened without a market, regions and republics held back goods, and the "war of laws" cut the taxes of the centre.
4. **No price reform**: Gorbachev delayed freeing prices for political reasons (it would have meant visible price rises) until April 1991, and the monetary overhang kept growing.
5. **Falling foreign earnings and growing debt**: oil, the loss of control over trade and the breakdown of Comecon trade.
6. **Chernobyl**: an unplanned cost and a loss of trust.
:::

## Foreign relations: the new thinking abroad

### Why Gorbachev changed Soviet foreign policy

In foreign policy, Gorbachev's new thinking was more radical and more successful than at home. His reasons were these:
- **The cost of the arms race.** Defence took 9 per cent of GNP on the official figure announced in 1989 and 15–17 per cent on CIA estimates, and the technology gap meant that the USSR could not match Reagan's Strategic Defense Initiative. Gorbachev needed to cut this burden if perestroika was to work.
- **The failure of the old policy.** The deployment of SS-20 missiles in Europe from 1977 had led NATO to deploy Pershing II and cruise missiles in 1983, and the war in Afghanistan had isolated the USSR. Gorbachev and Shevardnadze concluded that the policies of the Brezhnev years had damaged Soviet security. In February 1986 he called Afghanistan a "bleeding wound".
- **The need for Western goods, credit and technology.** A calm world would make trade and loans easier, and he wanted to enter the world economy.
- **Ideas.** He argued that the world was interdependent, that security could not be won by a build-up of weapons (a state's security should be "common security", reached by agreement), and that the old Marxist idea of class struggle should not guide foreign policy. In May 1987 the Warsaw Pact accepted a defensive doctrine of "reasonable sufficiency" for its forces: forces large enough to defend the bloc but not large enough to attack.
- **Nuclear danger.** Gorbachev disliked nuclear weapons and said that Chernobyl had shown what even a single accident could do.
- **A new generation.** Shevardnadze and advisers such as Yakovlev and Anatoly Chernyaev were ready to drop the assumptions of the Brezhnev years.

### Changing relations with the United States

The relationship passed through four stages.
1. **Opening (1985–86).** Gorbachev announced a moratorium on Soviet nuclear tests in August 1985 and in January 1986 proposed the abolition of nuclear weapons by the year 2000. Reagan, who had called the USSR an "evil empire" in 1983, was ready to negotiate: his Secretary of State, George Shultz, urged talks, and Reagan himself disliked the idea of nuclear war.
2. **Agreement (1987–88).** The INF Treaty and then the Soviet withdrawal from Afghanistan removed the largest quarrels.
3. **Partnership (1989–90).** Reagan's successor George H. W. Bush was at first cautious, but at Malta on 2–3 December 1989 he and Gorbachev spoke of a new era, and in 1990 the two cooperated over the reunification of Germany and the reply to Iraq's invasion of Kuwait.
4. **Treaty and strain (1991).** START I, the first treaty to cut strategic nuclear weapons, was signed on 31 July 1991, but the United States offered no large aid to the Soviet Union and Bush, in Kyiv on 1 August, warned against "suicidal nationalism", which angered Ukrainian nationalists.

### The summit meetings

| Summit | Date | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| **Geneva** | 19–21 November 1985 | The first meeting of Reagan and Gorbachev. They said that a nuclear war "cannot be won and must never be fought" and agreed to hold more summits and exchanges. They disagreed about SDI |
| **Reykjavik** | 11–12 October 1986 | The leaders discussed halving strategic weapons and removing missiles from Europe, and spoke of abolishing nuclear weapons. The talks broke down when Gorbachev insisted that SDI stay in the laboratory and Reagan refused |
| **Washington** | 7–10 December 1987 | The **INF Treaty** was signed on 8 December, the first to abolish a whole class of nuclear missile (ground-launched missiles with a range of 500–5,500 kilometres) |
| **Moscow** | 29 May–2 June 1988 | Ratifications exchanged; Reagan, walking in Red Square, said that the "evil empire" was "another time, another era" |

The **INF Treaty** is the best evidence of the change. The Soviet Union accepted inspection on its territory, agreed to scrap more missiles than the United States did (1,846 Soviet and 846 American, 2,692 in all, by 1 June 1991), and dropped its demand that SDI be given up as the price of agreement. Reykjavik, which is often called a failure, taught both leaders that deep cuts were possible. It also showed the limits of Gorbachev's bargaining: the Soviet side offered a great deal and the American side would not give up SDI.

### Afghanistan and the United Nations speech

Gorbachev set himself to leave Afghanistan. In 1986 the Soviet leaders decided to withdraw within about two years, and the Geneva Accords were signed on 14 April 1988. The last Soviet soldier crossed the border on 15 February 1989.

On **7 December 1988**, in a speech to the General Assembly of the United Nations in New York, Gorbachev announced a cut of 500,000 men in the Soviet forces over two years, including the withdrawal of 50,000 men and 5,000 tanks from Eastern Europe, and the disbanding of six tank divisions there. He said that "freedom of choice" was a universal principle that applied to all countries. That sentence was the beginning of the end of the Brezhnev Doctrine, and its results are told in [part 4](#hist.d.soviet.4). He cut his visit short because of an earthquake in Armenia that day. In May 1989 he visited Beijing, ending the Sino-Soviet quarrel that had lasted since the 1960s.

### The consequences of the new foreign policy

- **Gains.** The arms race was eased, the threat of war fell, the Cold War ended in practice at Malta in 1989, and Gorbachev became the world's most popular statesman, winning the Nobel Peace Prize in October 1990.
- **Losses.** The Soviet Union abandoned allies and client states (the Eastern European regimes, Afghanistan's government, Cuba, Vietnam and others lost their support), and the army and the KGB felt that Soviet gains of 1945 had been given away. Marshal Sergei Akhromeyev, the Chief of the General Staff, resigned in December 1988. The hardliners who tried to seize power in August 1991 saw the foreign policy as a betrayal.
- **No economic reward.** The West gave credits and praise, but no large rescue. Foreign policy changed the world and did not mend the Soviet economy.

:::key Why Gorbachev's new thinking changed Soviet foreign policy
1. **The burden of defence spending** (9 per cent of GNP officially, 15–17 per cent on CIA estimates), and the SDI challenge.
2. **The failure of earlier policy**: the SS-20s and Afghanistan had brought NATO's missiles, sanctions and isolation.
3. **The need for trade, credit and technology** for perestroika.
4. **New ideas**: interdependence, common security, reasonable sufficiency and, from 1988, freedom of choice.
5. **Personal and political conditions**: Gorbachev's dislike of nuclear weapons after Chernobyl, a team of new advisers, and a US president (Reagan) who was ready to talk.
:::

## Did Gorbachev's response make the crisis worse?

The syllabus asks how Gorbachev "tried to respond", and an essay on this topic nearly always has to judge how far he succeeded. Choose criteria before you argue: the **economy**, the **political system**, the **empire** and **relations with the outside world**. Then set out the two cases.

| The case that his response made matters worse | The case that he did what was possible, or that the system was beyond repair |
|---|---|
| The deficit rose from about 2–3 per cent of GNP to more than 10 per cent by 1988; hard-currency debt more than doubled | The deficit and the debt were rooted in oil dependence, subsidised prices and the arms race, which came before him |
| Half-reform: fixed prices with free enterprises produced shortages and wage inflation; price reform came only in April 1991 | Price rises in the 1980s would have been dangerous, as the Polish strikes of 1970, 1976 and 1980 had shown |
| Fragmentation: the plan lost its power and republics took the taxes; output fell in 1990–91 | A command economy that could not be changed from within had to be reformed in some way, and acceleration had failed |
| Glasnost and demokratizatsiya destroyed the party's monopoly and unleashed nationalism, which broke up the Union | The party monopoly was the real barrier to reform, and the nationalism came from the old system's failures |
| The new foreign policy gave away the empire without gaining aid | Foreign policy was his greatest achievement: the arms race ended, the Cold War ended peacefully, and there was no civil war |
| He lost the support of both conservatives and radicals by hesitating | He kept faith with a principle that he would not use mass force to keep power |

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
- **Archie Brown**, *The Gorbachev Factor* (1996), argues that Gorbachev's leadership was decisive: a different General Secretary in 1985 would not have started the changes, and the system was not bound to collapse. Brown holds that Gorbachev's political reforms were his great achievement, a peaceful transformation of a one-party state, while the economy was his weakest area.
- **William Taubman**, *Gorbachev: His Life and Times* (2017), a biography that won the Pulitzer Prize, presents Gorbachev as a sincere reformer who wanted to improve socialism, whose character (a strength in his refusal to use violence, a weakness in his hesitation) shaped his decisions.
- **Chris Miller**, *The Struggle to Save the Soviet Economy* (2016), argues that the Soviet economy's collapse in 1990–91 came more from the policy choices of the late 1980s (the growing deficit and the loss of central control) than from the stagnation that Gorbachev inherited, and that Gorbachev's economic team did not understand how to move to a market.
Together they show the main disagreement: whether the crisis was a result of the system's decay, which no leader could have fixed, or of the leader's choices. Paper 4 rewards your own judgement, supported by evidence, more than a list of names.
:::

:::key A judgement you can defend
Gorbachev's reforms did not create the Soviet Union's problems, which were deep and which he inherited, but his economic reforms made the economic crisis worse in 1987–91 because they weakened the old system without building a new one, and his political reforms removed the party's monopoly, which let the nationalism of the republics break up the Union. In foreign policy he succeeded. The best verdict is that he **tried to reform a system that could not be reformed without being changed beyond recognition**, and that his gradual, hesitating course gave the worst of both worlds: the costs of change without the benefits.
:::

1. **Define your criteria first.** For "Gorbachev's reforms made the problems worse": the economy, the political system, the empire and the standing of the USSR abroad. For "reasons for the new thinking": domestic need, the failure of old policy, ideas and the international situation.

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, with a total out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'Gorbachev's reforms made the Soviet Union's problems worse.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement partly substantiated; a range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criteria: the economy (growth, budget, shortages), the political system and unity of the state, and the Soviet position in the world. Ask "worse than what?": the 1985 situation, or what would have happened without reform.
- For "worse": deficit from about 2–3 per cent of GNP to over 10 per cent by 1988; the anti-alcohol campaign (lost revenue); half-reform in the Law on State Enterprise (1 January 1988) with fixed prices; fragmentation and the "war of laws"; output down in 1990–91; coupons; debt up; glasnost and demokratizatsiya removed the party's authority; nationalism.
- For "no, the problems were already there": 1.8 per cent growth in 1980–85; oil dependence; hidden deficit and monetary overhang; technology gap; Afghanistan; the party's monopoly was the real obstacle; reforms exposed more than they caused; foreign policy gains; no bloodbath.
- Judgement: economy worse because of the pattern of reform, politics changed beyond control, foreign position better. The reforms did not create the problems but made the crisis worse in the short run.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Gorbachev's reforms made the Soviet Union's problems worse. His policy of perestroika was meant to improve the economy but there were shortages in the shops. Glasnost meant that people could criticise the government, and the Soviet Union broke up in 1991. There was also the disaster at Chernobyl in 1986, which was very costly. So Gorbachev made the problems worse, as the Soviet Union ended while he was in charge."

*Commentary.* The points are relevant, but the paragraph asserts the view and does not test it. It treats the end of the USSR as proof that the reforms made things worse, and it does not say what the problems of 1985 were, so there is nothing to compare. Chernobyl is listed as if the reforms caused it. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 3 (about 7), accurate but general. To move up: define "worse", state the 1985 starting point and give exact evidence on both sides.

**A Level 4 paragraph (the case for "worse", well supported, but not weighed).** "There is a strong case that Gorbachev's reforms deepened the economic crisis. In 1985 the budget deficit was hidden and small, at perhaps 2 or 3 per cent of GNP, but by 1988 it was above 10 per cent. The anti-alcohol campaign, begun on 7 May 1985, cost the state billions of roubles of revenue, and the oil price fell by more than half in 1986. The Law on State Enterprise, which took effect on 1 January 1988, gave managers independence but kept prices fixed, so firms raised wages and made fewer cheap goods, and the shops emptied. As the central plan weakened and the republics began to put their own laws first in 1990, trade within the Union broke down. Official GNP fell in 1990 and in the first quarter of 1991 was about 8 per cent below the previous year, and many goods were rationed by coupon. In this sense the reforms made the economic problems worse."

*Commentary.* Precise, explained and focused, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). It builds a clear case for the view with evidence on each point. But it offers no case against, and it does not ask whether the problems were caused by the reforms or only revealed by them. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs the other side, criteria beyond the economy and a judgement that weighs them.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The view can be tested against three criteria: the economy, the political system and unity of the state, and the Soviet position in the world. It also needs a standard of comparison: 'worse' than the Soviet Union of 1985, or worse than what it would have become without reform. On the economy the view is largely right, on the political system it is partly right, and on foreign relations it is wrong. The best judgement is that Gorbachev did not create the problems but made the crisis worse, and more visible, because his reforms weakened the old system before a new one could replace it.

On the economy the case for the view is strong. The Soviet Union of 1985 was stagnant, with growth of about 1.8 per cent a year in 1980–85 on Western estimates, but it was stable: the shops were poor but supplied, and the deficit was hidden. By 1988 the deficit was above 10 per cent of GNP on one standard account, against 2 or 3 per cent before. Gorbachev's own policies explain part of this. The campaign against drink, launched on 7 May 1985, cost the state perhaps 10 to 15 billion roubles a year in revenue, and Chris Miller has argued that this did as much harm to the budget as the oil price, which fell by more than half in 1986. The Law on State Enterprise of June 1987, in force from 1 January 1988, was a half-measure: it gave managers freedom, but prices stayed fixed and state orders still covered most output, so managers raised wages and cut the output of cheap goods, and money grew faster than goods. Then the plan broke up without a market to replace it. In 1990 the republics began to put their laws above the Union's, and trade between regions and republics fell. Official GNP fell about 2 per cent in 1990 (a CIA estimate says 4–5 per cent) and in the first quarter of 1991 it was about 8 per cent lower than a year before, while sugar, tea and soap were rationed across the country. Gorbachev delayed freeing prices until April 1991 because the political cost was high. On this criterion, the reforms made a stagnant economy into a collapsing one.

But the problems were not made by Gorbachev. The weaknesses that he inherited were deep: an economy that could no longer grow by adding labour and raw materials, a technology gap, dependence on oil, and a deficit that had been hidden by shortages. Any leader would have faced the fall in the oil price, and the leaders before him had known and avoided the problem, which is why Andropov and Chernenko had done little. The more cautious approach of acceleration in 1985–86 failed, and that was the reason that Gorbachev turned to deeper reform. There is also a question of whether another policy would have been better: the 500 Days plan was never tried, so that nobody can say whether a faster move to a market would have succeeded, and in Poland the price rises of 1970, 1976 and 1980 had brought strikes. The correct reading is that the reforms were too weak to work and too strong to leave the old system intact.

On the political system the view is only partly right. Glasnost and demokratizatsiya did destroy the authority of the party. After the election of 26 March 1989 and the repeal of Article 6 on 14 March 1990 the Communist Party had lost its monopoly, and Yeltsin, elected by the voters in June 1991, had a mandate that Gorbachev did not. Nationalists in the Baltic States, Georgia and Ukraine used the freedom to demand independence. Yet the party's monopoly had been the main obstacle to reform, and Gorbachev's decision to bring in elections was an attempt to overcome it, so that the political changes were also a response to problems and not only their cause. They exposed how weak the loyalty of the republics was, and the party's claim to legitimacy had already fallen away. In foreign relations the view fails altogether. The INF Treaty of 8 December 1987, the withdrawal from Afghanistan by 15 February 1989 and the end of the Cold War at Malta made the Soviet Union safer and ended an arms race that it could not afford.

Overall, the reforms made the Soviet Union's economic problems worse in the years 1988–91 and changed its political problems, but they did not create them and they improved its position abroad. A fair verdict is that Gorbachev tried to reform a system that could not be reformed in stages: each half-measure weakened the old order faster than it created a new one. The costs of change arrived first, the benefits never did, and what ended in 1991 was a system that had lost the means of holding itself together."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with **three criteria** and a standard of comparison, and it gives each side precise evidence (7 May 1985, 1 January 1988, the deficit, the 8 per cent fall, 26 March 1989, 14 March 1990, 8 December 1987). It separates the problems that Gorbachev inherited from the ones he added, and it ends with a judgement that depends on the argument. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13). Total about 27/30.

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the reasons for Gorbachev's 'new thinking' in foreign policy.
**What the levels want.** An "Assess the reasons" question needs a range of reasons, a criterion for ranking them, and a judgement about which mattered most. At Level 5 each reason is explained with precise evidence and linked to the others.

**Plan.**
- Criteria: how far did the reason produce the change, and how far did it make the change necessary?
- Economic: the burden of defence (9 per cent of GNP officially, 15–17 per cent on CIA estimates), the SDI challenge, the need for Western credit and technology.
- The failure of old policy: the SS-20s and Pershing II (1983), Afghanistan ("bleeding wound", February 1986), isolation.
- Ideas: interdependence, common security, reasonable sufficiency (May 1987), freedom of choice (7 December 1988).
- Personal and political: Gorbachev's dislike of nuclear weapons, Chernobyl, Shevardnadze and the new advisers, Reagan's readiness to talk, and a leadership with no one to resist.
- Judgement: the economic need was the main reason, because it made a change necessary, but ideas and people decided what the change would be.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The reasons for Gorbachev's new thinking in foreign policy can be ranked by one test: which of them made a change necessary, and which decided the form that it took. On this test the most important was the economic burden of the arms race, with the failure of the older policy next, and Gorbachev's own ideas and the personal circumstances decided how far and how fast the change went.

The economic reason was fundamental. Perestroika depended on resources, and the Soviet military took about 9 per cent of GNP on the official figure that Gorbachev announced in 1989 and between 15 and 17 per cent on the CIA's estimates, at a time when growth was under 2 per cent a year. Reagan's build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative of 23 March 1983 meant that the Soviet Union would have to spend still more on a technology that it did not have. A foreign policy of confrontation could not be paid for, and trade, credit and technology from the West were also needed. This explains why Gorbachev proposed the end of nuclear weapons by 2000 in January 1986, why he accepted inspection and an asymmetric treaty in the INF agreement of 8 December 1987, and why in his United Nations speech of 7 December 1988 he announced a cut of 500,000 men.

The second reason was the failure of the older policy. The deployment of SS-20 missiles from 1977 had led NATO to station Pershing II and cruise missiles in 1983, and the invasion of Afghanistan in December 1979 had ended détente, brought a grain embargo and an Olympic boycott and made the Soviet Union the target of a coalition of the West, China and the Muslim world. Gorbachev called the war a 'bleeding wound' in February 1986 and decided in the same year to leave. Shevardnadze and Gorbachev's advisers could say that the Brezhnev years had damaged Soviet security, and that is a stronger argument for change than economics alone, since it showed the generals that arms did not buy safety.

The third reason was the content of the ideas. Gorbachev believed that the world was interdependent, that the security of one state could not be built on the insecurity of another, and that the class struggle should not guide foreign policy. The Warsaw Pact's acceptance in May 1987 of 'reasonable sufficiency', and his declaration of freedom of choice in December 1988, were not forced by the economy; they were choices of principle, and they went beyond what the budget required. The disaster at Chernobyl of 26 April 1986, which he said made him see what a nuclear war would be, strengthened a dislike of nuclear weapons that he had held already.

Finally, the context allowed the change. The Politburo was full of his own appointees, Shevardnadze had replaced Gromyko in July 1985, and in Reagan, who had become ready to negotiate after 1983 and wanted to be rid of the nuclear threat, there was an American leader who could answer him. Without that response Reykjavik in October 1986 and Washington in 1987 would not have happened.

Overall, the economic burden was the most important reason, since without it there was no pressure for change; the failure of old policy gave the argument for it, and ideas and circumstances explain why Gorbachev went so far beyond what a more cautious leader would have done. The reasons also reinforced each other, for the economy made the new ideas attractive and the ideas gave a language for the retreat. What is clear is that the new thinking was not a plan but a development: the decision to leave Afghanistan, to accept the INF terms and to renounce force in Eastern Europe came one after another, each making the next easier."

*Commentary.* The essay states its test (necessary or formative), ranks four reasons with exact evidence (the 9 and 15–17 per cent figures, 23 March 1983, 8 December 1987, 7 December 1988, February 1986) and explains how they connect. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13). Total about 27/30.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Evaluate the view that glasnost was the most important of Gorbachev's reforms." [30]

A student wrote:

"Glasnost was Gorbachev's most important reform. Glasnost means openness and it let people say what they thought. Newspapers printed the truth about Stalin and the government. In 1986 there was an accident at Chernobyl and the government told people. Because people could criticise, they began to criticise the government and the communist party lost its power. Gorbachev also introduced perestroika and demokratizatsiya but glasnost was most important because it started everything."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The paragraph defines glasnost and makes some correct points, but it **asserts** its claim ("started everything") and does not compare the three reforms against any criterion. It also has a factual slip: the government did not tell people about Chernobyl at once, and the delay in 1986 is evidence of the limits of glasnost, not of its success. The other two reforms get one clause, and no date, figure or example is given.

**The fix.** Choose a criterion, weigh the reforms against each other and use evidence to prove the point. "If importance is measured by lasting effect on the Soviet system, glasnost mattered most, but it did not work alone. It began slowly (the Soviet public heard of Chernobyl on 28 April 1986, two days late, and Gorbachev spoke only on 14 May), and it widened until the Law on the Press of 12 June 1990 ended censorship. By exposing the terror of the 1930s and the secret protocols of the Nazi–Soviet Pact it destroyed the party's claim to a record of achievement and gave the Baltic nationalists a case. Yet perestroika decided whether people's lives improved, and its failure, in the shortages of 1990–91, gave glasnost its audience, while demokratizatsiya turned criticism into votes (26 March 1989) and ended the party's monopoly (14 March 1990). Glasnost was therefore the most important in destroying the old system, but it was the failure of perestroika that made the destruction decisive." Now each fact is evidence in a comparison, and the paragraph ends with a qualified judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Give three reasons why Gorbachev, aged 54, became General Secretary on 11 March 1985.
A: The leaders wanted a change after three old leaders died in under three years; Andropov had promoted him and he had run the Secretariat in Chernenko's illness; his rivals Grishin and Romanov were weak and Ustinov had died; Gromyko and Ligachev backed him; the meeting was called quickly.
Q: What is the monetary overhang, and why did it matter?
A: The money that people held but could not spend, because prices were fixed below the market level and goods were scarce. It hid inflation as queues and shortages, grew as wages rose faster than output after 1988, and made the effect of freeing prices dangerous.
Q: Give the dates of the Law on State Enterprise and the Law on Cooperatives, and say what each allowed.
A: The Law on State Enterprise was passed in June 1987 and took effect on 1 January 1988: enterprises were to cover their costs and run themselves, with state orders and fixed prices still in place. The Law on Cooperatives was passed on 26 May 1988 (in force from 1 July): private cooperatives could trade for profit in services, small manufacturing and catering.
Q: What was the Congress of People's Deputies, and why was the election of 26 March 1989 important?
A: A body of 2,250 deputies set up by the constitutional amendments of December 1988, a third chosen by public bodies and the rest in districts. The election was the first in the USSR since 1917 with a real choice of candidates: many party secretaries lost, Yeltsin won Moscow, and the televised Congress gave the opposition a platform.
Q: What did Chernobyl show about glasnost?
A: The Soviet authorities gave no public word for two days, until Sweden detected the radiation and TASS made a short announcement on 28 April 1986, and Gorbachev did not speak until 14 May. It showed the limits of glasnost in 1986 and the cost of secrecy, which led to more openness.
Q: What did the INF Treaty do?
A: It was signed in Washington on 8 December 1987 and abolished all American and Soviet ground-launched ballistic and cruise missiles with a range of 500–5,500 kilometres, with on-site inspection. By 1 June 1991 the two sides had destroyed 2,692 missiles (1,846 Soviet and 846 American).
Q: Why did Reykjavik fail, and why was it still important?
A: On 11–12 October 1986 the leaders discussed deep cuts and even the abolition of nuclear weapons, but Gorbachev insisted that SDI be confined to the laboratory and Reagan refused. It showed that large cuts were possible and led to the INF Treaty.
Q: What did Gorbachev announce at the United Nations on 7 December 1988?
A: A unilateral cut of 500,000 troops over two years, including 50,000 men and 5,000 tanks from Eastern Europe, and the principle that every state has freedom of choice.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ "Gorbachev's greatest mistake was to reform politics before the economy." Evaluate this view.
