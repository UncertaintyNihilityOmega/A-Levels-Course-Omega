@summary How to choose your options for Cambridge AS & A Level History (9489): what each AS option, Paper 3 topic and Paper 4 option contains, what the syllabus requires you to study, how to choose by interest and by overlap, how the topic rotation works by exam year, the three routes and the two exam series, and a worked plan for a school year.

:::why
Most students do not get to choose everything; many schools fix the options for them. But when you do have a choice, or when you are a private candidate who must decide, the choice matters for the next one to two years of your life. The syllabus lets you combine options in ways that overlap, so that what you learn in one paper helps in another. This chapter explains the rules, helps you choose and then turns the choice into a plan with real weeks and hours.
:::

:::exam What the syllabus requires
- **Papers 1 and 2 (AS):** you choose **one option** (European, American or International). Each option has **three topics**, and you study **all three**. In any exam year one topic is on Paper 1 (sources) and the other two on Paper 2 (essays).
- **Paper 3 (A Level):** you study **one** of three topics: the origins of the First World War, the Holocaust, or the origins and development of the Cold War.
- **Paper 4 (A Level):** you choose **one option** (European, American or International), each with three topics. Paper 4 has three questions per option, one on each topic, and you answer **two**, so you need **at least two topics**; studying all three gives you a real choice on the day.
The rotation of the Paper 1 topic is in the table below. Which combinations of options a school allows is the **school's** decision: ask your teacher or exam centre before you commit.
:::

## The options at a glance

At AS you choose among three options. Each is a different kind of history.

| Option | Topics | What it is like |
|---|---|---|
| **European**: Modern Europe, 1774–1924 | France, 1774–1814; Germany, 1815–71; Russia, 1881–1924 | Revolutions and the making of states: ideas (liberalism, nationalism), revolutions and strong leaders |
| **American**: the USA, 1820–1941 | Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77; Gilded Age and Progressive Era, 1870–1920; Crash, Depression and New Deal, 1920–41 | One country's story: slavery and war, industry and reform, boom and depression |
| **International**: international history, 1870–1939 | Imperialism and world powers, c.1870–1918; international relations 1919–29; international history 1929–39 | Empires, diplomacy and the causes of conflict: how the world slid into the Second World War |

Each topic has four key questions, and this course has a chapter for each. The AS chapters teach each topic twice over: for the source question (Paper 1) and the essay question (Paper 2). The whole AS option is twelve chapters.

At A Level you add two more papers.

| Paper | Choices | The three topics |
|---|---|---|
| **Paper 3** (interpretations) | one topic, two chapters | The origins of the First World War; the Holocaust; the origins and development of the Cold War |
| **Paper 4**, European option | at least two of three topics, four chapters each | Mussolini's Italy, 1919–41; Stalin's Russia, 1924–41; Hitler's Germany, 1929–41 |
| **Paper 4**, American option | at least two of three | Truman, Eisenhower and post-war USA, 1945–61; a time of challenges, 1961–74; the USA, 1974–93 |
| **Paper 4**, International option | at least two of three | The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953–91; end of minority rule in South Africa and Zimbabwe, 1948–94; the route to independence: Malaysia and Indonesia, c.1909–67 |

The words "European", "American" and "International" are used for both the AS and the Paper 4 options but they mean different periods. The AS European option covers 1774–1924; the Paper 4 European option covers 1919–41. This course uses the same labels as Cambridge.

## A closer look at each topic

Short descriptions help you picture what you would spend months on. They are summaries of the content in the syllabus, not a full list.

### AS European

- **France, 1774–1814.** The collapse of the Ancien Régime under a bankrupt monarchy, the revolution of 1789, the end of the monarchy in 1792, the Terror and the Directory, then Napoleon's rise to power and his fall in 1814. Ideas of rights and the nation meet money, hunger and war.
- **Germany, 1815–71.** From a loose German Confederation under Austrian leadership to the German Empire proclaimed in January 1871: the liberal and nationalist revolutions of 1848–49, Bismarck's appointment as Minister-President of Prussia in 1862 and the wars that unified the country.
- **Russia, 1881–1924.** The autocracy of the Tsars and the pressures on it, the revolution of 1905, the two revolutions of 1917, and how Lenin's Bolsheviks held on to power until his death in 1924.

### AS American

- **The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77.** Slavery and the growing quarrel between North and South, the war that began in April 1861, and the attempt to rebuild the South and to give rights to formerly enslaved people after 1865.
- **The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920.** Rapid industrial growth after 1870, its effects on workers, farmers, cities and immigrants, and the reformers who tried to deal with them.
- **The Great Crash, the Great Depression and the New Deal, 1920–41.** The boom of the 1920s, the Wall Street Crash of October 1929, President Hoover's response and the first and second New Deals of Franklin D. Roosevelt.

### AS International

- **Imperialism and the emergence of world powers, c.1870–1918.** The European partition of Africa, the rivalries that followed, and the rise of Japan and the United States.
- **International relations 1919–29.** The peace settlements after the First World War, the League of Nations, why tensions stayed high, and how relations improved in the later 1920s.
- **International history, 1929–39.** The rise of extremist governments, the failure of the League to keep the peace, appeasement and the outbreak of war in 1939.

### Paper 3 (A Level)

- **The origins of the First World War:** who or what was to blame for 1914, and how historians' answers have changed from 1919 to the centenary.
- **The Holocaust:** who or what was to blame, with the debates between historians about the roles of Hitler, the Nazi state, war and the perpetrators.
- **The origins and development of the Cold War:** who or what was to blame, with the traditional, revisionist and post-revisionist schools.

All three ask you to analyse **the way a historian argues**, using the context chapter's knowledge to test the interpretation.

### Paper 4 (A Level)

- **European:** Mussolini's Italy (1919–41); Stalin's Russia (1924–41); Hitler's Germany (1929–41). Each is studied on four key questions: how the leader gained control, how effectively he governed, how successful his economic policies were, and how far he transformed society.
- **American:** Truman, Eisenhower and post-war USA (1945–61); a time of challenges (1961–74: Kennedy, Johnson, Nixon, Cuba, Vietnam, civil rights and protest); the USA from 1974 to 1993 (the late 1970s, the New Conservatism, Carter and the end of the Cold War).
- **International:** the Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (1953–91: the satellite states, stagnation, Gorbachev and the collapse); the end of minority rule in South Africa and Zimbabwe (1948–94); the route to independence of Malaysia and Indonesia (c.1909–67).

## What must I study, and what is optional?

:::key The requirements, in one place
1. **AS:** all three topics of one option. You cannot drop a topic, because Paper 1 is set on one of them and Paper 2 on the other two (and the Paper 1 topic changes each year).
2. **Paper 3:** one topic, both its chapters (the context and the debate).
3. **Paper 4:** one option, **at least two** of its three topics. If you study all three you can answer the two you like best; if you study two you must be able to answer both of the questions on them.
4. **Whole-course extras are optional:** [History from zero](#hist.f.basics), [the big picture](#hist.f.eras), the skills chapters, the paper guides and mock papers help everyone, and nobody needs the topics outside their options.
:::

:::warn Do not guess which questions will be set
The Paper 1 topic for each year is known (see the table below), and its question is a source question. But **which question** comes up within a topic is not: the syllabus lists four key questions per topic, and any of them can be the focus. Study all four key questions of every topic you study. Do not skip one because you think it is "unlikely".
:::

## How to choose

If you have a choice, work through these questions in order.

1. **What do you find interesting?** You will read about the topic for months. Revolutions? Wars and diplomacy? A single country's politics? The topics you care about are the ones you will remember in the exam.
2. **What does your school teach?** A teacher who knows the topic is worth more than a better topic taught badly. If you study alone, check that you can find books, videos and past papers for it.
3. **What do you want to do next?** Many university courses in history, politics, law or international relations accept any combination, but check the entry requirements for the courses you want, and do not assume a particular option is required.
4. **How do the options overlap?** Topics in different papers share background. Overlap lets what you learn in one paper count again in another (see below). It does not reduce what you must learn, because each paper asks different questions.
5. **Be honest about reading.** The European and International options need more reading about unfamiliar places and names. If English is your second language, an option whose key words you already know can save time, but all options are manageable with the explanations in this course.

| If you … | Look at |
|---|---|
| like revolutions, ideas and strong leaders | European AS (France, Germany, Russia) |
| prefer one country with a clear story | American AS |
| like causes of wars and the world as a whole | International AS |
| are drawn to big debates about blame | Paper 3 (any of the three) |
| want to study dictators | Paper 4 European (Italy, Stalin, Hitler) |
| want to study the recent past | Paper 4 American (1945–93) or International (1953–94) |

### Overlaps: what helps what

These are some real links between options. None is a rule, and you do not need to choose by them; they only show how some choices share background and so save some effort.

| One choice | Shares background with |
|---|---|
| **International AS** (Imperialism, 1919–29, 1929–39) | Paper 3: [the First World War](#hist.interp.ww1.1) (the alliance system, Article 231 and Versailles); Paper 4 International: [South Africa and Zimbabwe](#hist.d.safrica.1) and [Malaya and Indonesia](#hist.d.malaysia.1) (empire and decolonisation) |
| **European AS** (France, Germany, Russia) | Paper 3: [the First World War](#hist.interp.ww1.1) (German unification and the Russian monarchy in 1914); Paper 4 European: [Stalin's Russia](#hist.d.stalin.1) (follows from [Russia (4)](#hist.eu.russia.4)) and [Hitler's Germany](#hist.d.hitler.1) |
| **American AS** (Civil War, Gilded Age, Great Crash) | Paper 4 American: [post-war USA](#hist.d.usa45.1) (it continues the story after the New Deal); Paper 3: [the Cold War](#hist.interp.coldwar.1) (the United States as a world power after 1945) |
| **Paper 3: the Holocaust** | Paper 4 European: [Hitler's Germany](#hist.d.hitler.2) (the Nazi state) |
| **Paper 3: the Cold War** | Paper 4 International: [the Soviet Empire](#hist.d.soviet.1); Paper 4 American: [post-war USA, 1945–61](#hist.d.usa45.4) (foreign policy) |

Each AS option can be matched with a Paper 3 topic and a Paper 4 option in several ways, depending on what your school offers. The list below shows three sample patterns, not the only ones.

- **The twentieth-century world:** International AS, the Cold War (Paper 3), International Paper 4 (the Soviet Empire plus South Africa and Zimbabwe or Malaya and Indonesia).
- **Revolutions and dictatorship:** European AS, the Holocaust (Paper 3), European Paper 4 (Stalin and Hitler, or all three dictators).
- **The United States:** American AS, the Cold War (Paper 3), American Paper 4 (USA 1945–93).

## Year of the exam: what is on Paper 1?

The Paper 1 topic follows a fixed rotation, which means you can see exactly which topic of your option will be set as a sources question in your exam year.

| Year | European: Paper 1 | American: Paper 1 | International: Paper 1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2027 | France | Gilded Age and Progressive Era | Imperialism |
| 2028 | Germany | Crash, Depression and New Deal | International relations 1919–29 |
| 2029 | Russia | Civil War and Reconstruction | International history 1929–39 |

The other two topics of your option are the Paper 2 essay topics that year.

| Year | European: Paper 2 | American: Paper 2 | International: Paper 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2027 | Germany, Russia | Civil War, Great Crash | 1919–29, 1929–39 |
| 2028 | France, Russia | Civil War, Gilded Age | Imperialism, 1929–39 |
| 2029 | France, Germany | Gilded Age, Great Crash | Imperialism, 1919–29 |

So a European student sitting in 2027 meets France as four **sources** and writes **essays** on Germany and Russia. All three topics must be learned in full: the source topic needs as much knowledge as an essay topic (the (b) question asks you to use your own knowledge as well as the sources).

:::tip Use the rotation to order your study
The topic that is on Paper 1 in your exam year benefits from early source practice, so if you can choose the order of study, do that topic's chapters first. Then use the other two to practise essays. Every AS chapter teaches both skills, so you lose nothing by starting with the Paper 1 topic.
:::

## Routes and timing

Choose one of three routes with your school or centre.

| Route | What it means | Typical time |
|---|---|---|
| **AS only** | Papers 1 and 2 in one series | One year |
| **Staged A Level** | Papers 1 and 2 (AS) in year 1, Papers 3 and 4 in year 2 | Two years |
| **A Level in one go** | All four papers in one series | Two years of study, one exam series |

There are two exam series each year, **June** and **November**. Which one is open to you depends on your centre; a private candidate should ask the exam centre which series it offers and when to enter. Cambridge gives about 180 guided learning hours for AS and about 360 for A Level. The AS grades are a–e, A Level grades A*–E.

:::note Which route suits you?
- **AS only** suits a student who wants a free-standing history qualification, or who is still deciding.
- **Staged** suits a student who wants to spread the load: you sit the AS marks after a year, then add the A Level papers, with the AS marks carried forward under the rules in the Cambridge Handbook.
- **All at once** suits students who study intensively and want one exam period; you have all four papers within one series, so the revision load is large.
Always confirm with your centre and read the Cambridge Handbook.
:::

## A plan for a school year

Here is a worked plan for the AS year of a student who studies about **6 hours a week for 36 weeks** (216 hours). It follows the study order in [History: the course map](#hist.overview).

| Weeks | What | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | [History from zero](#hist.f.basics) and [the big picture](#hist.f.eras) | 12 |
| 3–4 | [Sources skills](#hist.skills.sources) and [essay skills](#hist.skills.essays), with a first written answer | 12 |
| 5–11 | Topic 1: four key-question chapters | 42 |
| 12–18 | Topic 2: four key-question chapters | 42 |
| 19–25 | Topic 3: four key-question chapters | 42 |
| 26–30 | Revision: paper guides and mixed practice | 30 |
| 31–33 | Mock papers under time, with marking and review | 18 |
| 34–36 | Final revision and spare weeks | 18 |

For the **A Level year** of a staged student (**7 hours a week for 30 weeks**, 210 hours):

| Weeks | What | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | [How historians disagree](#hist.interp.skills) | 7 |
| 2–4 | Paper 3: the context chapter and the debate chapter of your topic | 21 |
| 5–21 | Paper 4: three topics of four chapters (17 weeks); with only two topics, weeks 5–15 (11 weeks) and the spare weeks go to revision | 119 |
| 22–27 | Revision: paper guides and mixed practice | 42 |
| 28–30 | Mock Papers 3 and 4 under time | 21 |

And a model **week** of 6 hours:

| Day | Session | Hours |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Read one section of the chapter, with the recall box at the start | 1 |
| Wednesday | Read the next section; make a timeline or factor grid | 1.5 |
| Friday | Test yourself: timeline, key people, one paragraph from memory | 1 |
| Saturday | One timed written answer, then mark it against the levels | 2.5 |

:::tip Rules for a plan that survives
1. Do not fall behind on **writing**. Reading feels productive, but marks come from written answers.
2. Mark every answer against the level descriptors in the paper guides, and note one thing to improve.
3. Leave spare weeks. Illness, trips and school events always arrive.
4. Revise early topics **while you learn new ones**: spend the first ten minutes of a session on an older chapter's recall questions.
:::

## Common questions

**Can I study a topic from the European option and one from the American option at AS?** No: at AS you study one **option** (all three topics of one section). Within Papers 1 and 2 you answer from one section only.

**Does my Paper 4 option have to match my AS option?** Your school decides which combinations it teaches. This course covers every option, so whichever combination you have, the chapters are here.

**Can I start with A Level topics before AS?** The staged route puts AS first. Papers 3 and 4 build on the skills of Papers 1 and 2, so it is rarely sensible to reverse them.

**What if I only have a few months?** Take the topics in order, do not drop the key questions, and write more and read less. [Study methods that actually work](#road.methods) shows how to learn quickly.

**I am a private candidate. Where do I start?** Ask a Cambridge exam centre which series and papers it offers and its entry dates, then see [Studying as a private candidate](#road.private).

:::recap
- **AS:** one option, all **three** topics. **Paper 3:** one topic. **Paper 4:** one option, at least **two** of its three topics.
- **Rotation:** one AS topic is on Paper 1 each year, and it is on Paper 2 in the other two years. 2027: France, Gilded Age, Imperialism; 2028: Germany, Great Crash and New Deal, 1919–29; 2029: Russia, Civil War and Reconstruction, 1929–39.
- **Choose** by interest, by what your school teaches, by what you want next, and by overlap; confirm combinations with your centre.
- **Routes:** AS only; staged (Papers 1 and 2, then 3 and 4); all four at once. Exams in June and November.
- **Plan:** about 100–130 hours for an AS option's twelve chapters, then revision and mocks; write a timed answer every week.
:::
