:::why
In 1964 the Soviet Union had launched the first satellite seven years before and the first man into space three years before, claimed to be catching up with the United States, and had just replaced a leader who promised communism within twenty years. By 1985 its leaders were so old that three of them had died in under two and a half years, its economy was growing more slowly than at any time since the 1920s, and it was fighting a war in Afghanistan that it could not win. Yet nothing had collapsed, and for most of those twenty years life for most Soviet citizens was calmer and slowly better than it had been under Stalin or Khrushchev. ## Political leadership, 1964–85

### Brezhnev and the "stability of cadres"

On **14 October 1964** the Central Committee removed Nikita Khrushchev. The charges against him were that he was erratic and too fond of reorganisations ("hare-brained schemes", as they were called), that his agricultural policy had failed (the USSR had to buy grain from abroad in 1963), that he had risked war over Cuba in 1962, and that he had made enemies among the officials whose jobs he kept changing. The men who plotted the removal wanted calm. Leonid Brezhnev became First Secretary of the party and Alexei Kosygin became Chairman of the Council of Ministers (head of the government). A third man, Nikolai Podgorny, became head of state in 1965. For several years they shared power.

:::def Cadres
In Soviet usage, the trained officials who ran the party, the state and the economy. "Stability of cadres" was Brezhnev's policy of not dismissing party officials without good reason, in contrast with Khrushchev's frequent reorganisations and purges.
:::

Brezhnev, born in 1906, was a party official from the Ukrainian steel town of Dniprodzerzhynsk who had risen through the machine and had been Khrushchev's protégé, and he was not a thinker or an orator. His aims, which can be found in what he did more than in what he said, were these:

- **Stability.** He wanted to end the fear and the constant change of the Stalin and Khrushchev years. Officials kept their posts, and dismissal for failure became rare. The party elite found this welcome.
- **Order in the system.** He kept the party's monopoly of power and he restored the central ministries that Khrushchev had abolished in 1957 (see below).
- **A better life, slowly.** Brezhnev promised more consumer goods, housing and pensions, an offer that has been called an unwritten "social contract": the party would provide security and a rising standard of living, and in return the people would not question its rule.
- **Military strength and security.** He built up the armed forces until the USSR reached parity with the USA in strategic nuclear weapons, and he wanted recognition of the post-war frontiers by the West. He called the Soviet system "developed socialism": a society that had achieved stability, so that rapid change was unnecessary.
- **Order in the empire.** He put down the Prague Spring and announced the doctrine that bears his name (see [part 1](#hist.d.soviet.1)).

Over time Brezhnev became the dominant figure. Kosygin's influence declined, and in 1977 Brezhnev took the post of head of state from Podgorny. In 1966 his title changed from First Secretary to General Secretary (the title Stalin had used), and in the later 1970s a modest cult of personality grew around him: he was made a Marshal of the Soviet Union in 1976, and in 1979 was awarded the Lenin Prize for Literature for memoirs that were largely written by others. A new constitution of 7 October 1977 repeated that the Communist Party was the "leading and guiding force" of Soviet society. His health failed from the middle of the 1970s, and for his last years the work of government was done by a small group around him. He died on **10 November 1982**, aged 75.

### Andropov and Chernenko

Brezhnev's successors were both old and ill, and their time in office was short.

- **Yuri Andropov** (born 1914) had been chairman of the KGB from 1967 to 1982, and had seen at first hand the dissidents and the corruption of the Brezhnev years. He became General Secretary on **12 November 1982**, two days after Brezhnev's death. He launched a campaign for discipline and against corruption: officials were dismissed, among them the Minister of the Interior, Nikolai Shchelokov, in December 1982, and the police rounded up people who were absent from work, in raids on shops, cinemas and bath-houses in 1983. He allowed small economic experiments, and he promoted younger men, among them **Mikhail Gorbachev**. But he was seriously ill with kidney disease from the summer of 1983, and he died on **9 February 1984**, aged 69, after fifteen months.
- **Konstantin Chernenko** (born 1911), a close ally of Brezhnev's, was chosen on **13 February 1984** as the candidate of the old guard. He was 72, and suffered from lung disease. His rule stopped the anti-corruption drive and restored the policy of stability, but he was incapable of leading, and he died on **10 March 1985**, aged 73.

The next day the Politburo chose Gorbachev, who was 54. The choice of such a man, after three leaders in two and a half years, shows how far the party's senior people had come to feel that a change was needed.

### Corruption and the nomenklatura system

The *nomenklatura* system described in [part 1](#hist.d.soviet.1) (the party's lists of the posts that it alone could fill, and the class of officials who held them) became the heart of the Brezhnev system. Because Brezhnev guaranteed the security of the cadres, the people in these posts came to see them as their own.

:::def Corruption (in the Soviet system)
The use of official position for private gain: bribes for jobs, housing or goods, the misuse of state property, falsified output figures and the sale of favours. In the Brezhnev years it was widespread at every level, and it was made easier by the party's monopoly, by shortages, and by the fact that no free press or independent court could expose it.
:::

How it worked:

- **Privileges.** Senior officials had access to special shops with goods that the public could not buy, better housing and dachas (country houses), cars, holidays and hospitals. These were often a bigger part of the reward than the salary.
- **Selling posts and favours.** Officials in republics and regions could build networks of loyal followers who paid for posts and protected each other. Because the centre wanted stability and reliable reports of success, it was slow to look closely.
- **Falsifying results.** Managers and officials had every reason to report that targets had been met, since promotion depended on it. This is the key to the **cotton affair**: in Uzbekistan, whose party boss **Sharaf Rashidov** led the republic from 1959 until his death in 1983, officials reported cotton harvests larger than the real ones for many years, and were paid by Moscow for cotton that did not exist, amounts that investigators put in billions of roubles. The investigation began under Andropov in 1983 and went on after Rashidov's death, and by the end of the decade it reached Moscow: in 1988 Yuri Churbanov, Brezhnev's son-in-law and a deputy minister of the interior, was sentenced to 12 years for taking bribes.

The effects were serious. Corruption took away the state's resources, made it hard to tell how the economy was really doing (the central planners were working with figures that were false), spread cynicism among the public, and damaged the party's claim to be the servant of the people. The scandals under Brezhnev were not exceptional events but a result of the system of stability that he had created.

### The development of the gerontocracy

:::def Gerontocracy
Rule by the old: a political system in which power is held by a small group of elderly men who keep their posts until they die or become incapacitated.
:::

Brezhnev's policy of stability meant that the men who held the top posts in 1964 were still there in 1982. The ages of the leaders at the end of the period show the effect: Brezhnev was 75 when he died, Kosygin 76, Mikhail Suslov, the party's chief ideologist, 79 when he died in January 1982, and the Defence Minister, Dmitry Ustinov, 76 when he died in December 1984. Nikolai Tikhonov became head of the government in October 1980 at the age of 75. Between 1980 and 1985 the Soviet Union lost Kosygin, Suslov, Brezhnev, Andropov, Ustinov and Chernenko, six of its most powerful men, to old age and illness.

Why did it happen? Because the system rewarded loyalty and length of service, not ability. In practice no one dared to propose retiring a leader, since each member of the group feared for his own position, and the Central Committee that had removed Khrushchev was now filled with men whom Brezhnev had appointed. Brezhnev's own illness was hidden, and decisions in the later 1970s were delayed or avoided. The effect on policy was to keep the status quo: the old men who had risen under Stalin had no interest in changing a system that had rewarded them. They also made the party seem out of touch with a population of whom a large and growing share were born after the war.

:::key Why the political system stagnated
1. **Stability became a goal in itself.** Brezhnev's "stability of cadres" ended the purges and the fear, but it also removed the pressure to perform and the chance to bring in new people.
2. **The nomenklatura gave the elite a stake in the system** and no reason to change it.
3. **Corruption and falsified figures** made the centre's picture of the economy and society unreliable.
4. **The gerontocracy** left the top posts to old men, who put off hard decisions.
5. **There was no way to correct the system from outside** (no free press, no independent courts, no opposition), so problems were hidden until they became crises.
:::

## The Soviet economy: reform, defence and stagnation

### The problems facing the economy

When Brezhnev took over, the Soviet economy was the second largest in the world and it had grown fast for thirty years. The growth had come from adding more of everything: more workers (many of them from the countryside), more factories, more coal, steel and oil. Economists call this **extensive growth**. By the 1960s that was running out. The supply of new workers from the villages was shrinking and, in the 1970s and 1980s, the number of young people entering the labour force fell. New resources, in Siberia and the Arctic, were far from the cities and cost much more to reach. For growth to continue, the economy had to get more out of each worker, machine and tonne of raw material: **intensive growth**, based on better technology and management.

:::def Stagnation (economic)
A long period of very slow growth or none in output and, above all, in productivity, in an economy that is not in a sudden crisis but cannot improve. In the Soviet case the word was popularised by Gorbachev and his supporters in the mid-1980s to describe the Brezhnev years, and it is a matter of debate whether it is a fair description (see the end of this chapter).
:::

The central planning system, described in the chapters on Stalin's Russia, was better at adding inputs than at raising productivity.

- **Rigid targets.** Gosplan set output targets and fixed prices. Firms that exceeded the plan found the target raised the next year, which taught managers to hold back and to understate what they could do.
- **No pressure to innovate.** A firm that changed its product or method risked missing its target, and a failing factory was never closed. In the Soviet system there were no bankruptcies, and no competition between firms.
- **Shortages and poor quality.** Prices did not move with demand, so goods in short supply stayed cheap and queues formed. Factories judged on the volume or weight of their output made goods that were heavy and badly finished.
- **Hoarding.** Managers kept spare workers, stocks and supplies as a protection against the plan's uncertainties. The economy as a whole was wasteful.
- **Agriculture.** Collective and state farms were badly run, short of machinery, fertiliser, storage and roads, and a large share of the harvest rotted or was lost on the way to the shops. The USSR, which had been a grain exporter before 1914, was by the 1970s a regular importer.

### Attempts to reform, 1964–85

**The 1965 reform.** The first and most serious attempt came from Kosygin. In September 1965 the Central Committee approved a package based on ideas that the economist Yevsei Liberman had put forward. The ministries abolished by Khrushchev in 1957 were restored in October 1965 (a recentralisation), but within them each enterprise was to have more freedom. Managers were to be judged by sales and profit, not simply by gross output, the number of plan targets was cut, and firms were allowed to keep part of their profits for bonuses and for investment.

It did not last. By the end of the decade it had been largely abandoned, for these reasons:

1. **The ministries and Gosplan resisted**, because an economy in which firms made decisions would reduce their own power.
2. **Managers were cautious.** Many did not want the risk of independence, and workers feared that a profit motive meant unemployment.
3. **The Prague Spring.** In Czechoslovakia in 1968 economic reform, led by Ota Šik, had come with political reform, and the Soviet leaders feared that the first led to the second. After 1968 the reformers were on the defensive.
4. **Brezhnev's own priority.** He valued stability and the support of the cadres more than economic efficiency, and he did not give Kosygin the backing he needed.
5. **Oil.** From 1973 the great rise in the price of oil on the world market brought the Soviet Union, a major producer, a large new income in hard currency (see below), which allowed it to put off reform.

**Agriculture.** At a plenum in March 1965 Brezhnev set out a policy for the countryside that raised the prices the state paid for farm produce, cut the compulsory quotas and increased investment in agriculture; collective farm workers were given a guaranteed wage (1966). Spending on farming rose to a large share of the state's investment but the harvests stayed uncertain, and a bad one in 1972 forced the Soviet government to buy more than 25 million tonnes of grain abroad, mainly in the United States. In 1982 a new Food Programme was approved, which shows that the problem had not been solved.

**The 10th Five-Year Plan (1976–80).** Brezhnev called it "the five-year plan of efficiency and quality". Its aim was to raise productivity and the quality of goods, to speed up the use of new technology and to develop Siberia (including the building of the Baikal–Amur Mainline railway, started in 1974). It missed most of its targets. Growth fell short of what was planned, the harvests of 1979, 1980 and 1981 were poor, and the transport system, the energy supply and the quality of consumer goods were all a problem. The 11th Plan (1981–85) began from a worse position.

### The impact of defence spending

The Brezhnev leadership built up the Soviet armed forces on a very large scale. It reached strategic parity with the United States by about 1970, and went on to expand its navy and to deploy new missiles (the SS-20 from 1977). How much this cost is disputed, because Soviet figures hid the real total and because converting roubles into dollars is hard.

| Source of the estimate | Share of GNP spent on defence |
|---|---|
| Official Soviet figure announced by Gorbachev in 1989 (77.3 billion roubles in 1989) | about 9 per cent |
| CIA estimates as revised in the 1980s (a roughly constant share from 1965 to 1984; reaffirmed by the CIA and the DIA in 1989) | about 15 per cent, given as 15–17 per cent in 1989 |
| Other estimates | higher, and contested: some analysts argue for 20 per cent or more |

So the range is wide: from about 9 to 17 per cent on the main figures. Whatever the true number, the burden was much heavier than America's (a CIA assessment of the late 1970s put the American share at about 7 per cent), and it fell on an economy perhaps half to 60 per cent the size of America's. The effects were these.

- **The best resources went to the military.** The defence industry had first call on skilled engineers, scientists, good materials and machine tools. The civilian economy, which received what was left, was starved of both.
- **Consumer goods and investment suffered.** The decision to match American arms limited what could be spent on housing, shops and agriculture, and on modernising old factories.
- **It was hard to cut.** The military and its industries were politically powerful, and their weapons programmes had their own momentum, so no leader tried seriously to reduce the budget before Gorbachev.
- **Foreign commitments added to it.** The USSR also subsidised Cuba, Vietnam and its other clients and the satellite states, and from 1979 the war in Afghanistan.

### The development of stagnation

By the end of the 1970s the signs were clear to Soviet economists, even if the public discussion was careful.

- **Slow growth.** The estimates in the chart show a fall to under 2 per cent a year in the early 1980s.
- **A technology gap.** The USSR could build rockets but not good computers, and it copied many Western designs. Western firms and governments restricted the sale of advanced technology.
- **Shortages and queues.** Meat, fruit and many consumer goods were hard to find; to get what you wanted you needed contacts.
- **The second economy.** A parallel, unofficial economy grew: people sold goods and services for private profit, worked on the side, stole from the state and bought through personal connections (*blat*, the Russian word for influence). Millions took part. It made life more bearable, and it showed that the official economy did not work.
- **Social problems.** Alcoholism, absenteeism and low morale at work; male life expectancy stopped rising and began to fall in the 1970s, an unusual development in an industrial country.
- **Oil as a cover.** The sharp increase in the price of oil after 1973 helped the USSR, a very large exporter, to earn hard currency, to pay for grain and Western goods, and to keep up its military spending without changing the system. Oil and gas gave the leaders a reason not to reform, and by the 1980s the economy's dependence on them was a weakness: when the price of oil fell in the mid-1980s, the gap was exposed.

:::key Why the economy stagnated
1. **The structure of central planning**: rigid targets, no competition, no bankruptcy and no signals from prices.
2. **The end of extensive growth**: labour reserves were used up, and new resources were in distant places.
3. **The failure of reform**: Kosygin's 1965 reform was blocked by the ministries, the managers and the leaders' fear of political change.
4. **Defence spending** of 9–17 per cent of GNP (depending on the estimate) took the best resources.
5. **Oil and grain imports** hid the problem in the 1970s and so removed the pressure to reform.
6. **A leadership that valued stability**: Brezhnev and his colleagues saw no reason to change a system that worked for them.
:::

## Foreign relations: détente, Afghanistan and the Second Cold War

### Détente with the West

:::def Détente
A relaxing of tension between states, in the Cold War in particular the period of the 1970s when the USSR and the USA signed arms limitation treaties, expanded trade and agreed on the status of Europe, while still competing for influence.
:::

The Soviet Union wanted détente for several reasons.

1. **Parity had been achieved.** By about 1970 the USSR had as many strategic nuclear weapons as the USA, so it could negotiate as an equal.
2. **Fear of China.** The Sino-Soviet split had led to armed clashes on the Ussuri River in March 1969, and Moscow was alarmed when President Nixon visited Beijing in February 1972.
3. **The economy.** The USSR wanted Western technology, credits and grain.
4. **Security in Europe.** It wanted the West to recognise the frontiers drawn in 1945, above all the new borders of Poland and the division of Germany.
5. **Arms control.** Arms racing was expensive for both sides.

The main steps were:

- **Ostpolitik.** West Germany's Chancellor Willy Brandt negotiated treaties with Moscow (August 1970) and Warsaw (December 1970) that accepted the post-war borders, and a Four-Power agreement on Berlin in 1971 improved access to the city. In 1972 East and West Germany signed the Basic Treaty, and in 1973 both joined the United Nations.
- **SALT I.** On 26 May 1972 Nixon and Brezhnev signed in Moscow the first Strategic Arms Limitation agreements: an interim agreement that put a cap on the number of strategic missile launchers, and the ABM Treaty, which limited each side to two sites for missile defence. It was the first visit to Moscow by a serving American president.
- **Trade and cooperation.** Large American grain sales (1972), other trade deals and the joint Apollo–Soyuz space flight (July 1975).
- **The Helsinki Final Act, 1 August 1975.** Thirty-five states, including the USSR, the USA, Canada and every European state except Albania, signed it. It had three "baskets". The first accepted that frontiers in Europe were inviolable and that states would respect each other's sovereignty, a long-standing Soviet aim. The second promised cooperation in economics, science and technology. The third promised respect for human rights and the freer movement of people, ideas and information.

:::key What the USSR gained and lost from détente
1. **Gains**: recognition of its post-war frontiers and of East Germany, nuclear parity confirmed by treaty, Western grain, credit and technology, and reduced fear of a two-front threat.
2. **Costs**: the Helsinki human rights promises, which gave dissidents in the USSR and the satellite states a document to quote (Charter 77 and the Moscow Helsinki Group of 1976 did so); Western trade led to debt in the satellite states; and the arms race was not stopped, only limited.
3. **Why it ended**: the USSR expanded its influence in Angola (1975–76) and Ethiopia (1977–78), and deployed SS-20 missiles in Europe from 1977, and American opinion turned against it. In June 1979 Carter and Brezhnev signed SALT II in Vienna, but the US Senate never ratified it. The invasion of Afghanistan finished it.
:::

### The invasion of Afghanistan, 1979

Afghanistan, a poor country on the Soviet Union's southern border, had a pro-Soviet communist coup in April 1978. The new government, led by Nur Muhammad Taraki, tried to impose land reform and the abolition of religious customs with great speed, and it provoked an armed revolt. In September 1979 Taraki was overthrown and killed by his colleague Hafizullah Amin, who was not liked in Moscow.

The decision to send troops was taken in Moscow on **12 December 1979** by a very small group (Brezhnev, Andropov, Ustinov and Gromyko), whom the rest of the Politburo then followed. Historians, including Vladislav Zubok in *A Failed Empire* (2007), stress these points:

1. **To save a socialist government.** Soviet leaders believed that if a pro-Soviet government fell, it would be a defeat and a signal. The Brezhnev Doctrine's logic, that a socialist state should not be lost, was applied outside the Warsaw Pact.
2. **Fear of Amin.** Moscow feared that Amin was unreliable and was making contact with the West.
3. **Fear of Islamic radicalism.** The revolution in Iran in 1979 raised the possibility that an Islamic movement would take power in Afghanistan and spread to the Muslim republics of Soviet Central Asia.
4. **A mistaken belief in a quick result.** The Soviet leaders expected a short operation, with troops that would stabilise the government and then leave.

On 24–27 December Soviet forces entered Kabul and crossed the border. On 27 December Soviet special forces stormed Amin's palace and killed him, and **Babrak Karmal** was installed as the new leader. The war that followed lasted nine years. Soviet forces, which grew to about 115,000, held the cities and main roads, but the **mujahideen**, the Afghan guerrilla fighters, held the mountains and the countryside, helped with money and weapons by the USA, Pakistan, Saudi Arabia and China (and, from 1986, American Stinger missiles). In April 1988 the Geneva Accords were signed, and the last Soviet troops left on **15 February 1989**.

**The impact on the Soviet Union** was large, though historians debate how large.

- **Casualties and cost.** About 14,500 Soviet soldiers died, according to the figure of the Soviet General Staff (a figure of about 15,000 is often given) and many more were wounded or fell ill. Hundreds of thousands of Afghans died, estimates run from several hundred thousand to over a million, and millions fled to Pakistan and Iran. The war also cost large sums of money in a time of economic strain.
- **Diplomatic damage.** The United Nations General Assembly condemned the invasion in January 1980 by a large majority. The USA responded with a grain embargo and a boycott of the Moscow Olympic Games (more than sixty countries did not attend), Carter asked the Senate to put SALT II aside, and he declared that the USA would defend the Persian Gulf. Détente was over, and the West became more united against the USSR. Muslim countries and China, which were hostile, drew closer to the USA.
- **Home front.** Soviet newspapers said little at first. Coffins came home to families, soldiers came back with experiences that they could not explain, and by the middle 1980s people were aware of a war that they did not understand. The dissident Andrei Sakharov, who protested, was exiled to the city of Gorky in January 1980. Gorbachev called the war a "bleeding wound" in 1986.
- **The army and the leadership.** The failure of the army to win showed the limits of Soviet power and weakened the old leadership's authority.
- **Link with other changes.** The war added to the strain from the Second Cold War, which meant more money for defence, and it helped to convince some Soviet leaders that the old policies had to change.

### The Second Cold War, 1979–85

With détente dead, relations between the USSR and the West went through a period of high tension, sometimes called the **Second Cold War**.

- **Europe's missiles.** The Soviet SS-20s deployed from 1977 led NATO to decide in December 1979 to deploy American Pershing II and cruise missiles in Europe unless the USSR removed them. The deployments began in November 1983, and the Soviet delegation walked out of the arms talks in Geneva.
- **Reagan.** President Ronald Reagan, elected in 1980, called the USSR an "evil empire" (8 March 1983) and began a rapid military build-up, including, from March 1983, the Strategic Defense Initiative, a plan for a missile defence system that the Soviet leaders believed they could not match.
- **Crises and scares.** On 1 September 1983 Soviet fighters shot down a South Korean airliner, KAL 007, killing all 269 people on board. In November 1983 a NATO exercise, Able Archer, was misread by some in Moscow as the cover for a real attack, and some historians believe the Soviet leadership was more nervous than at any time since the Cuban Missile Crisis.
- **A leadership that could not respond.** Brezhnev, Andropov and Chernenko were all ill, and the Soviet Union boycotted the 1984 Olympic Games in Los Angeles. Not until 1985 and Gorbachev was there a leader who could negotiate.

The Second Cold War deepened the stagnation. It raised defence costs at the moment when the economy was weakest, it cut off Western credit and technology, and it made Soviet leaders more suspicious. But it was also in part of the USSR's own making: the SS-20s, the Afghan invasion and the build-up of the 1970s had provoked it. How far American pressure caused the Soviet collapse is considered in [part 4](#hist.d.soviet.4).

:::key How foreign policy and domestic stagnation were linked
1. **Détente gave the system a breathing space** (grain, credit, technology) and so postponed reform.
2. **Afghanistan and the Second Cold War removed it**, adding costs and isolation just as growth fell below 2 per cent.
3. **The same old leaders** who failed to reform the economy made the decisions on Afghanistan, and no one outside the small group could question them.
:::

## Developments in the satellite states, 1968–85

After 1968 the satellite governments ruled under the Brezhnev Doctrine. The Soviet army stood behind them, and in Czechoslovakia it stayed on the ground. The leaders who were left (Husák, Gierek from 1970, Honecker from 1971, Kádár, Zhivkov and Ceaușescu) had learned from 1956 and 1968 that the Soviet Union would not tolerate a challenge to the party's monopoly. They tried instead to buy peace with a higher standard of living: the Soviet "social contract" in each satellite's own form. This worked for about a decade and then ran into the same economic limits as in the USSR, made worse by debt.

| State | Leader | The main developments |
|---|---|---|
| **Czechoslovakia** | Gustáv Husák, party leader from April 1969 (and President from 1975) | "Normalisation": hundreds of thousands of reformers were expelled from the party and lost their jobs, censorship returned, and the country settled into a grey stability. The regime offered consumer goods and weekend cottages in return for political silence. Dissent was small but persistent (Charter 77). |
| **Poland** | Władysław Gomułka to December 1970; Edward Gierek from 20 December 1970 | Gomułka's price rises of 12 December 1970 led to strikes on the Baltic coast, in which troops shot at least 40 workers, and to his fall. Gierek borrowed from the West to modernise industry and raise consumption. Debt grew and a new attempt to put up prices in June 1976 was withdrawn after riots. By 1980 the debt was over 20 billion US dollars (figures differ by source and between gross and net debt). |
| **East Germany** | Erich Honecker, who replaced Ulbricht on 3 May 1971 | Brezhnev wanted a leader who would accept détente with West Germany. Honecker promised "unity of economic and social policy": cheap food, rent and transport, and a large housing programme. He recentralised the economy, took Western credits (including a credit of about 1 billion Deutschmarks arranged in 1983) and expanded the Stasi. |
| **Hungary** | János Kádár | The most liberal state in the bloc in everyday life. The New Economic Mechanism of 1968 and later measures allowed private plots, small firms and travel to the West, a system often called "goulash communism". Hungary borrowed heavily from the West and joined the International Monetary Fund in 1982. |
| **Romania** | Nicolae Ceaușescu from 1965 | A harsh national-communist dictatorship that kept its independence in foreign policy (it refused to join the 1968 invasion and Ceaușescu condemned it), but that ran its people into poverty. In the 1980s it cut imports and living standards to pay off its foreign debt. |

### Economic reforms in the satellite states

Several satellites tried to reform their economies in the 1960s and 1970s. The Soviet Union and its own failed reform of 1965 set the limit: change was allowed only when it did not threaten the party's control.

- **East Germany**: a "New Economic System" from 1963 gave managers more freedom and used profit as a measure. Honecker ended it in the early 1970s, going back to central control, and made the economy a base for the "social policy".
- **Czechoslovakia**: Šik's reform (1965–68) was the boldest and was ended by the invasion.
- **Hungary**: the New Economic Mechanism (1 January 1968) abolished compulsory plan targets for firms and allowed more flexible prices. Soviet and party critics forced a partial retreat in 1972–74, but later governments revived many of its features, and in the 1980s small private enterprises were legalised. Hungary was the most successful reformer in raising the supply of consumer goods, though it ran up debts.
- **Poland**: Gierek's strategy was not a reform of the system but a plan to import Western machinery and licences (for example, for Fiat cars) and repay the loans with exports. When Western markets fell into recession after 1973, and when interest rates rose around 1979–81, the plan collapsed and the debt could not be repaid. Poland stopped paying in 1981.
- **Comecon.** In 1971 the council adopted a "Comprehensive Programme" for closer integration, but trade stayed mainly a matter of bilateral deals between governments, and the "transferable rouble" could not be converted into other currencies. After the 1973 oil price rise the USSR sold oil to its satellites at prices that lagged behind the world market (from 1975 they were based on a moving average of the previous five years). Some Western economists, among them Michael Marrese and Jan Vanous, argued in 1983 that this was a large Soviet subsidy to Eastern Europe; others disputed the size of the subsidy, and the question is part of the debate about whether the empire was a burden or a benefit to Moscow.

The pattern was that each reform was limited, partial and reversible. The reasons were the fear that economic freedom would lead to political demands (the lesson of Prague), Soviet disapproval, and the resistance of the officials who had most to lose. The satellites used Western credit as a substitute for reform: it paid for consumer goods in the 1970s and became a burden in the 1980s.

:::key Why satellite economic reforms had limited success
1. **Political limits**: reform could not touch the party's monopoly, and after 1968 this was the Brezhnev Doctrine's stated price.
2. **Opposition from officials** who ran the planning system.
3. **Western debt** replaced reform: Poland, Hungary, East Germany and Romania borrowed to buy consumer goods and technology, and had to repay in the 1980s, when interest rates were high and exports slow.
4. **Dependence on the USSR** for energy and raw materials, and on Comecon trade that gave little incentive to improve quality.
:::

### Social policies and developments

The regimes' social policies were a large part of their claim to legitimacy. Their elements were similar across the bloc:

- **Security.** Full employment, free health care and education, cheap food, housing and transport, and pensions. A person in the bloc knew that unemployment was not a danger and that basic needs would be met.
- **Women.** Women worked in large numbers, in a great variety of jobs, and the state provided nurseries and maternity leave in some countries (East Germany and Czechoslovakia especially). They also did most of the housework and queuing, which was a heavy "double burden". Romania, which banned abortion in 1966 to raise the birth rate, followed a harsher family policy.
- **Housing.** Governments built vast blocks of prefabricated flats, which were cheap and often badly built. East Germany made housing the centre of its social policy.
- **Consumption.** The late 1960s and the 1970s saw a growth in the numbers of televisions, refrigerators, washing machines and small cars (the East German Trabant and Czech Škoda). A buyer might wait years for a car. Czechoslovakia's cottages, Hungary's private plots and Poland's mainly private farms (most of Polish farmland remained in private hands) gave people some independent activity.
- **Young people.** Western music and fashions spread despite official disapproval. In Czechoslovakia the trial of the rock group Plastic People of the Universe in September 1976 became one of the events that led to Charter 77, and in East Germany the state allowed some Western-style clothes and music, while pressing young people into party youth movements.

By the end of the 1970s the contract was in trouble. Wages rose faster than the supply of goods, so shortages and queues got worse; in the early 1980s, the need to repay debt brought cuts in imports and living standards (in Romania, severe ones), and the regimes had little else to offer.

## Challenges to Soviet control

The word "challenge" must be used carefully. In the 1970s no state in the bloc was close to leaving it, and the forces described here were small. Their importance was in what they made possible later.

### The influence of the West

- **Radio and television.** Radio Free Europe, the Voice of America and the BBC reached millions each week. In East Germany nearly everyone who lived within range could watch West German television, which showed a wealthier society and the news the regime did not report (a part of the Dresden area that could not receive it was nicknamed the "Valley of the Clueless").
- **Ostpolitik and contact.** The treaties of 1970–72 made visits between the two Germanys easier, and when Willy Brandt visited Erfurt on 19 March 1970 crowds greeted him with cheers. Western visitors, relatives and tourists brought goods, news and comparisons.
- **Credit and trade.** Western banks lent money to the satellites; this tied their economies to the West but also gave the West some influence over them.
- **The Helsinki Final Act.** Its human rights pledges, which the Soviet bloc had signed in return for the recognition of the frontiers, gave the dissidents a legal language and a way of appealing to the West. Western governments began to discuss the human rights record of the bloc at follow-up meetings.

### Political activism

The regimes' response to the end of mass terror was a small but significant movement of open dissent, based on human rights and not on the overthrow of communism.

- **In the USSR**, the Moscow Helsinki Group (May 1976) monitored the observance of the Final Act, and Andrei Sakharov, a physicist and a winner of the Nobel Peace Prize in 1975, was a leading critic until he was exiled to Gorky in January 1980. Alexander Solzhenitsyn was expelled from the USSR in February 1974.
- **In Poland**, after the strikes and beatings of June 1976, a group of intellectuals formed the **Workers' Defence Committee (KOR)** on 23 September 1976, to help the families of workers who had been punished. It brought together intellectuals and workers, an alliance that the regime had feared since 1956, and it published bulletins and set up uncensored publications and a "flying university". Adam Michnik's essay "The New Evolutionism" (1976) argued that Poles should press for change from below, in peaceful ways, and should not expect or provoke a Soviet invasion.
- **In Czechoslovakia**, **Charter 77** was published on 6 January 1977 with 242 first signatories, among them the playwright Václav Havel, and had about 1,900 by 1989. It did not call for the end of communism. It said that the government was not respecting the rights that it had signed in the Final Act and in the 1966 UN covenants. The regime called it "anti-state" and responded by sacking signatories from their jobs, interrogating them, and imprisoning some: Havel was jailed from 1979 to 1983. In 1978 Havel's essay "The Power of the Powerless" argued that people could weaken a regime by "living in truth", by refusing to take part in its pretences.
- **In East Germany and Hungary**, dissent was smaller, shaped by the Church in East Germany and by small groups of intellectuals in Hungary, and was met by the Stasi and the police, and in Hungary by pressure and the offer of a comfortable life.

### The influence of the Church

- **Poland.** The Catholic Church was the strongest institution outside the party. Its power came from its history, its parish network and its Primate, Cardinal Wyszyński. On **16 October 1978** the Archbishop of Kraków, **Karol Wojtyła**, was elected pope as John Paul II, the first non-Italian pope since 1523, and the first ever from the communist world. During his first visit to Poland, from **2 to 10 June 1979**, enormous crowds, of hundreds of thousands and in Warsaw perhaps a million (estimates vary), gathered at every Mass. In his homily in Victory Square he said that "the exclusion of Christ from the history of man is an act directed against man", which a Polish audience heard as a judgement on a state that tried to exclude religion. The visit showed Poles, and the government, that a vast part of the nation could meet peacefully without the party, and it gave them a sense of their strength. A year later the strikes of August 1980 began (see [part 4](#hist.d.soviet.4)).
- **East Germany.** The Protestant Church, the only large independent organisation, reached an agreement with Honecker in March 1978 under which it was allowed to work as "the Church in socialism". It gave shelter to peace and human rights groups in the 1980s.
- **Elsewhere.** In Czechoslovakia and in Lithuania the Catholic Church was repressed but had a loyal following; the Hungarian Church was tightly controlled.

:::key How serious were the challenges to Soviet control, 1968–85?
1. **They were small and contained in the 1970s.** Charter 77 had about 1,900 signatories in a country of 15 million, KOR had dozens of members, and the regimes kept control by dismissals, arrests and surveillance.
2. **They changed the terms of the struggle.** They based opposition on rights and law (not violence), they used the regimes' own promises, and they built networks, publications and a vocabulary that were ready in 1980 and 1989.
3. **Poland was the exception.** There the Church, the intellectuals and the workers came together, and by August 1980 the challenge was not small any more.
4. **Soviet control did not depend on the satellites' popularity**: the Brezhnev Doctrine, the stationed troops and the secret police remained, and the Soviet leaders believed that they would be enough.
:::

## Was it stability or stagnation? Weighing the evidence

The syllabus asks why the period "became characterised by" stagnation. A strong answer first asks whether that description is right, and the word is itself an interpretation. Gorbachev and his allies used "stagnation" in the mid-1980s to justify change, and it has become the standard label. Some historians argue that it understates the real achievements and stability of the Brezhnev years.

| The case for stability (and some success) | The case for stagnation (and decline) |
|---|---|
| No terror: the mass arrests of the Stalin years did not return, and most people felt secure | Growth fell from about 4.9 per cent a year in the 1960s to about 1.8 per cent in the early 1980s (Western estimates), with productivity falling |
| Living standards rose for most people, with new flats, televisions and refrigerators, and security of jobs and pensions | Shortages, queues, a technology gap and a growing second economy by the late 1970s |
| Strategic parity with the USA, détente, and recognition of the post-war frontiers at Helsinki | Afghanistan and the Second Cold War, and an arms burden of 9–17 per cent of GNP |
| Order in the empire after 1968, with no serious revolt before 1980 | Dissidents, the Church in Poland, and satellite debt |
| The party elite had stable careers and the system survived two decades without a crisis | A gerontocracy, falsified figures and corruption on a large scale, and a leadership that avoided every decision |

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
- **Edwin Bacon and Mark Sandle** (eds), *Brezhnev Reconsidered* (2002), brought together historians who argued that the Brezhnev years should be studied for their own sake, not only as a prelude to collapse: that there were real improvements in living standards and in social provision, and that "stagnation" is too simple a label for a system that was stable and, in its own way, adapted to its needs. The contributors also record the slowdown in growth.
- **Stephen Kotkin**, *Armageddon Averted: The Soviet Collapse, 1970–2000* (2001), argues that oil and Western credit kept the Soviet system going through the 1970s and hid its weaknesses, and that the collapse that followed was not simply the result of stagnation but of the choices of the Soviet elite.
- **Archie Brown**, *The Rise and Fall of Communism* (2009), likewise stresses that the system could have lasted longer and that Gorbachev's choices ended it, so that the stagnation of the Brezhnev years does not by itself explain the collapse.
Use these to show that you know the label is debated. Paper 4 rewards your own judgement, supported by evidence, more than a list of names.
:::

:::key A judgement you can defend
"Stagnation" is a fair word for the economy (growth slowed to under 2 per cent a year, productivity fell, and reform failed), for the leadership (a gerontocracy that avoided decisions) and for the satellite states in the 1980s (debt and an exhausted social contract). It is less fair for the political system and for the lives of most citizens before the late 1970s, which were calmer and better than under Stalin or Khrushchev. The period was one of **stability that produced stagnation**: the policy of stable cadres, the party's monopoly and the avoidance of risk kept the system secure, and for the same reasons prevented it from reforming. The foreign adventure in Afghanistan and the Second Cold War put it under strain, but they did not cause the stagnation. They found it already there.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, with a total out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'The Brezhnev era was one of stability rather than stagnation.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement partly substantiated; a range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criteria: the economy (growth, productivity), the political system (renewal, responsiveness), living standards, and control of the empire and relations with the West.
- For stability: no purges ("stability of cadres"); rising living standards from the 1960s to the mid-1970s; strategic parity and détente (SALT I, 26 May 1972; Helsinki, 1 August 1975); no revolt in the satellites between 1968 and 1980.
- For stagnation: growth 4.9 per cent (1960s) to 2.5 (1970s) to 1.8 (1980–85), with falling productivity; failed reform (1965); defence at 9–17 per cent of GNP; corruption and falsified figures (cotton affair); gerontocracy; Afghanistan and the Second Cold War; the second economy.
- Judgement: stability in politics and daily life, stagnation in the economy and the leadership, and the first caused the second.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "The Brezhnev era was a time of stability. After Khrushchev, who changed things all the time, Brezhnev let officials keep their jobs, which was called stability of cadres. There were no purges like the ones under Stalin, so people felt safe. Most people had jobs and homes, and the USSR had as many nuclear missiles as the USA, and signed SALT I in 1972. This shows that the Brezhnev era was stable and was not stagnant."

*Commentary.* The points are accurate and relevant, but the paragraph argues only one side and treats "stability" and "stagnation" as opposites without testing either. The last sentence asserts the judgement. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 3 (about 7), accurate but general. To move up: give the case for stagnation as well and use exact evidence.

**A Level 4 paragraph (the case for stability, well supported, but not weighed).** "There is a strong case that the period was one of stability. Brezhnev's policy of stability of cadres ended Khrushchev's reorganisations and the fear of the Stalin years: officials could expect to keep their posts, and the Central Committee was filled with his supporters. The 'social contract' gave most people security and a slowly rising standard of living, with new flats, televisions and refrigerators and a guaranteed wage for collective farmers from 1966. Abroad, the USSR reached strategic parity with the USA by about 1970 and used it to sign SALT I on 26 May 1972 and the Helsinki Final Act on 1 August 1975, which accepted the frontiers of 1945. Within the empire, after the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 and the Brezhnev Doctrine there was no major revolt until 1980. In political terms the system was secure."

*Commentary.* Precise, explained and focused, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). It states a case for the view with evidence for each criterion. But it does not set it against the case for stagnation, and it never asks whether stability of this kind was a strength. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs the other side and a judgement that links the two.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The view can be tested against four criteria: the economy, the political system, living standards, and the Soviet position in the world and the empire. On the first and the political half of the second the Brezhnev era was stagnant, and on living standards and the empire it was stable. The best judgement is that stability and stagnation were not alternatives, since the first produced the second.

The case for stability is real. The policy of stability of cadres, which Brezhnev put in place after the fall of Khrushchev on 14 October 1964, ended both the terror and the constant reorganisation, and gave the party's officials a security they had never had. For ordinary people the 'social contract' meant jobs, cheap housing and food, and for many their first television, refrigerator or flat. Abroad, the achievement of strategic parity allowed the USSR to sign SALT I on 26 May 1972 and to see the frontiers of 1945 accepted at Helsinki on 1 August 1975. After 1968 the empire was quiet: between the invasion of Czechoslovakia and the strikes of August 1980 no satellite state saw a major revolt. In none of these respects does 'stagnation' describe the experience.

But in the economy, the evidence for stagnation is strong. Western estimates put the growth of Soviet GNP at about 4.9 per cent a year in the 1960s, 2.5 per cent in the 1970s and 1.8 per cent in 1980–85, and the same estimates show productivity falling in the 1970s and the early 1980s. The attempt to change this in September 1965 under Kosygin was blocked by the ministries and by the leaders themselves, who after the Prague Spring feared that economic reform would bring political change. The 10th Five-Year Plan of 1976–80, billed as the plan of efficiency and quality, missed most of its targets. Defence took between 9 per cent of GNP, on the official figure Gorbachev announced in 1989, and 15–17 per cent on the CIA's, and the economy depended on oil and on grain imports, which the oil price rise after 1973 paid for. The leadership shows the same pattern. Brezhnev was 75 when he died in 1982, Kosygin was 76 when he died in 1980 and Suslov was 79 when he died in January 1982; the cotton affair showed how falsified figures reached the top, and the two leaders who followed Brezhnev in November 1982 both died in office within two and a half years.

The two halves are connected, and that is the central point. The stability was the cause of the stagnation. The security that made cadres loyal also took away any pressure to perform or any means of correcting mistakes, and it kept in office the men least willing to change the system. The social contract rested on a rising standard of living and could not be kept when growth fell. Oil and détente allowed the system to postpone the choice, until the invasion of Afghanistan on 24–27 December 1979 and the Second Cold War removed that margin.

Overall, the view is right about the political and social surface and wrong about the economy and the direction of travel. A fairer description is that the Brezhnev era was stable and stagnant at the same time, and stable because it was stagnant: a system that avoided risk, change and challenge for twenty years, and could not afford to go on doing so. Historians such as Edwin Bacon and Mark Sandle, who have stressed the real achievements of the period, do not deny the slowdown; they dispute what the word means. On the evidence, the era was one of stability on the surface and stagnation underneath."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with **four criteria** and gives each side precise evidence (14 October 1964, 26 May 1972, 1 August 1975, 4.9, 2.5 and 1.8 per cent, the ages of the leaders). It does not leave the two halves apart: it argues that stability caused stagnation, and ends with a judgement that depends on that argument. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13). Total about 27/30.

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the impact of the war in Afghanistan on the Soviet Union.
**What the levels want.** An "Assess the impact" question needs a range of effects, a criterion for ranking them, and a judgement on which were greatest and how far the war, and not other causes, produced them. At Level 5 each effect is explained with precise evidence and set against other developments of the time.

**Plan.**
- Criteria: how large was the effect, how lasting, and how far was the war its cause?
- Foreign relations (large and immediate): the end of détente; the US grain embargo; the Olympic boycott; SALT II set aside; the Carter Doctrine; Western, Chinese and Muslim hostility; UN condemnation (January 1980).
- The economy (real but secondary): cost, in a time of slow growth (1.8 per cent a year, 1980–85); defence spending; the war was one of several burdens.
- Society and the army (slow but important): about 14,500 dead; the coffins; veterans; the failure to win; Sakharov's exile (January 1980); Gorbachev's "bleeding wound" (1986).
- The leadership (limited): a decision by four men; the war added to the sense that the old policies did not work.
- Judgement: greatest in foreign relations and in public confidence, small in causing the stagnation or the end of the USSR.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The impact of the war can be measured by its size, its length and how far the war was the cause. On these tests it had its greatest effect on the Soviet Union's relations with the outside world, a growing effect on its society and army, and a smaller one on its economy, where it added to problems that were already there.

The effect on foreign relations was immediate and large. The invasion of 24–27 December 1979 ended the détente that had given the USSR the Helsinki Final Act of 1975 and the grain and technology it wanted. In January 1980 the United Nations General Assembly condemned the invasion by a large majority; the United States imposed a grain embargo, Carter asked the Senate to put SALT II aside, and more than sixty countries stayed away from the Moscow Olympic Games. Carter declared in his Carter Doctrine that the USA would defend the Persian Gulf, and the Islamic world and China, which opposed the USSR, drew closer to Washington. The war thus helped to set off the Second Cold War, in which Reagan expanded American forces. The USSR had already deployed SS-20 missiles in Europe from 1977 and had been active in Angola and Ethiopia, so détente was already weakening, but Afghanistan made the break complete.

The effect on the economy is harder to isolate. The war took money, equipment and manpower, with forces that rose to about 115,000, at a time when growth was already down to under 2 per cent a year and defence took between 9 and 17 per cent of GNP, depending on the estimate. But most of the economic problems came from the system: the rigid planning, falling productivity and failed reform of 1965 were all in place before 1979. The war was a further burden, but it was not the cause of the stagnation, and when the price of oil fell in the mid-1980s that mattered more.

The effect on society and on the army was slower and perhaps greater. The war lasted nine years and two months and about 14,500 Soviet soldiers died, according to the Soviet General Staff's count. At first the newspapers said little, but the coffins came home to every republic and the veterans, called 'Afghantsy', returned with experiences that no one in authority would discuss. It was a war fought far from home, without the claim of defending the motherland that had justified earlier Soviet wars, and the army, the pride of the system, could not defeat Afghan guerrillas who were helped by the United States, Pakistan and China, and who from 1986 had Stinger missiles. Public protest was small, since Sakharov was exiled to Gorky in January 1980 for speaking out, but the war damaged confidence, and Gorbachev's description of it in 1986 as a 'bleeding wound' shows that by then the leadership saw it as a liability. The Geneva Accords of April 1988 and the withdrawal completed on 15 February 1989 were the result.

The effect on the leadership was limited. The decision had been taken on 12 December 1979 by Brezhnev, Andropov, Ustinov and Gromyko, and it showed how a small group of old men could take a major step without debate; but the leadership did not change because of it, and it was not until 1985 that new men came in.

Overall, the impact was greatest on the Soviet Union's international position, in which it ended détente and isolated the USSR, and substantial on the confidence of the army and the public. It was smaller on the economy and on the leadership, and it would be wrong to say that the war caused the collapse of the Soviet Union: the war was a symptom of a leadership that was out of touch, and an additional strain on a system that was already stagnant. It mattered most because it came when the Soviet Union had least room to absorb such a shock."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with criteria (size, length, causation) and then ranks four areas of impact, each with precise evidence (24–27 December 1979, the boycott, 115,000, 14,500, 15 February 1989), and it separates what the war caused from what it only added to. The judgement is comparative and tied to the evidence. Table A: Level 5 (about 14). Table B: Level 5 (about 13). Total about 27/30.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Evaluate the view that détente in the 1970s brought more benefit to the Soviet Union than to the United States." [30]

A student wrote:

"Détente was a time when the Soviet Union and the USA became friendlier. In 1972 Nixon and Brezhnev signed SALT I in Moscow, which limited the number of missiles and the ABM Treaty. In 1975 the Helsinki Final Act was signed by thirty-five countries. It had three baskets. The Soviet Union also bought grain from the USA. In 1979 Carter and Brezhnev signed SALT II, but it was not ratified. So the Soviet Union benefited a lot from détente because it got grain and agreements."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are broadly accurate, but the paragraph **lists events** and does not weigh benefits. It never says what the United States gained, so there is no balance, and "benefited a lot" is asserted from two facts. The mention of SALT II is not used. The "three baskets" are named but not explained, though their content is the key to the argument.

**The fix.** Choose a criterion, set the benefits against each other and use the evidence to prove a point. "Détente brought the USSR more tangible gains in the 1970s, but the gains were less secure than they looked. It gained recognition of the frontiers of 1945 at Helsinki on 1 August 1975, nuclear parity confirmed in SALT I on 26 May 1972, and Western grain and credit, which it needed after the harvest failure of 1972. The United States gained a limit on the missile race, a chance to ease the pressure of the Vietnam War, and the opening to China, which put more pressure on Moscow than on Washington. The balance of benefit also changed over time: the third 'basket' of Helsinki, on human rights, gave dissidents a document that they used against the Soviet bloc, from the Moscow Helsinki Group of 1976 to Charter 77. So the USSR gained more in the short term, and the West more in the long term." Now each fact is used as evidence in a comparison, and the paragraph ends with a qualified judgement.
:::

## Practice

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Who succeeded Brezhnev, and who succeeded him? Give the dates.
A: Yuri Andropov was General Secretary from 12 November 1982 to his death on 9 February 1984. Konstantin Chernenko followed on 13 February 1984 and died on 10 March 1985. Gorbachev was chosen the next day.
Q: What was the *nomenklatura* system, and why did it encourage corruption?
A: The party's lists of the posts that it alone could fill, and the privileged class that held them. Because the elite had security of tenure, privileges and no outside check (no free press or independent courts), officials could use their position for gain and had every reason to report success.
Q: What was the cotton affair?
A: A scandal in Uzbekistan, led by the party boss Sharaf Rashidov (1959–83), in which officials reported cotton harvests larger than the real ones and were paid by Moscow for cotton that did not exist; it was investigated from 1983 under Andropov, and in 1988 Brezhnev's son-in-law Yuri Churbanov was sentenced to 12 years for bribery.
Q: Give two reasons why Kosygin's reform of 1965 failed.
A: The ministries and Gosplan, whose power it threatened, resisted it; managers were cautious; after the Prague Spring in 1968 the leaders feared that economic reform led to political change; Brezhnev put stability first.
Q: Give the main estimates of Soviet defence spending as a share of GNP.
A: About 9 per cent on the official figure announced by Gorbachev in 1989, and about 15 per cent to 17 per cent on the CIA and DIA estimates; some analysts argue for more. The estimates differ because Soviet figures hid the total and converting roubles into dollars is hard.
Q: What did the Helsinki Final Act of 1 August 1975 contain, and why did it matter for the satellite states?
A: Thirty-five states accepted that European frontiers were inviolable (a Soviet aim), promised economic cooperation and promised respect for human rights and freer movement of people and information. Dissidents used the human rights promises, as in the Moscow Helsinki Group (1976) and Charter 77 (1977).
Q: Why did the USSR invade Afghanistan in December 1979?
A: To save a pro-Soviet government and avoid a defeat; fear of Amin's reliability; fear that Islamic radicalism after the Iranian revolution would spread to Soviet Central Asia; and a belief that the operation would be short. The decision was taken by Brezhnev, Andropov, Ustinov and Gromyko on 12 December 1979.
Q: Why was John Paul II's visit to Poland in June 1979 important?
A: It showed that enormous crowds could assemble peacefully, led by the Church and without the party, and it gave Poles a sense of unity and strength; it came fourteen months before the strikes of August 1980.

Q: [30 marks] ★ Assess the reasons why Kosygin's economic reform of 1965 failed.
A: Indicative content: the ministries and Gosplan resisted a loss of power; managers were wary of risk and the new profit measures; the leaders feared the political effects of economic reform after the Prague Spring of 1968; Brezhnev valued stability and gave Kosygin little support; oil revenue after 1973 removed the pressure to reform. A Level 5 answer sets a criterion (which reason, if absent, would have allowed the reform to work?) and judges, for example, that the lack of political backing was decisive because it let every other obstacle succeed.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which corruption damaged the Soviet system under Brezhnev.
A: Indicative content: for: privileges and the sale of posts; the cotton affair (Rashidov, billions of roubles for non-existent cotton, Churbanov); falsified figures that misled the planners; cynicism and loss of legitimacy. Challenge: corruption was a symptom of the system of stable cadres and the party monopoly, not a separate cause; the second economy made life more bearable; the effect on the economy was smaller than that of planning and defence spending. A Level 5 answer judges that corruption was serious mainly because it hid the truth and made reform harder.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that defence spending was the main cause of Soviet economic stagnation.
A: Indicative content: for: 9–17 per cent of GNP; the best engineers and materials; a hard burden on an economy about half to 60 per cent of America's; the arms race and the Second Cold War. Against: stagnation was rooted in central planning, the end of extensive growth and failed reform; the CIA's estimates were revised and Soviet figures hid the truth; oil and imports hid the problem. A Level 5 answer sets criteria (size, causation) and judges that defence was a major contributor and not the main cause.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons for the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan in 1979.
A: Indicative content: to save a pro-Soviet government (the logic of the Brezhnev Doctrine); fear of Amin; fear of Islamic radicalism spreading from Iran and Afghanistan to Soviet Central Asia; the end of détente and a wish to show strength; a belief in a short operation; a decision by four men (12 December). A Level 5 answer judges, for example, that the main reason was the wish to preserve a client state and the fear of the spread of Islamic militancy, with the decision-making process allowing a mistake to be made.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ "Détente in the 1970s brought more benefits to the Soviet Union than to the USA." Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content: for: recognition of the frontiers of 1945, parity, grain, credit and technology, and recognition of East Germany. Against: the US gained from limits on arms, the opening to China, and the Helsinki human rights pledges that were used against the bloc; détente collapsed because of Soviet conduct (Angola, Ethiopia, SS-20s, Afghanistan). A Level 5 answer separates short-term from long-term benefits and judges that the USSR gained more at the time and the West more in the end.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which economic reform in the satellite states was successful, 1968–85.
H: Choose criteria: raising living standards, growth, and the ability to last.
H: Compare Hungary, East Germany and Poland: what did each try and what happened?
H: Consider the limits set by Moscow and by the party monopoly.
A: Indicative content: Hungary's New Economic Mechanism (1968; partial retreat 1972–74; revival in the 1980s) raised consumer supply; East Germany recentralised under Honecker and bought social policy with credit; Poland's borrowing under Gierek raised living standards for a time and ended in debt (over 20 billion US dollars by 1980) and default (1981); Czechoslovakia's reform was ended by the invasion; Comecon integration stayed limited. A Level 5 answer judges that reforms were partial and reversible and that only Hungary's brought lasting gains, while the others used debt instead of reform.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ "Western influence was the main challenge to Soviet control of eastern Europe in the 1970s and early 1980s." Evaluate this view.
H: Set criteria: what do you mean by a "challenge to control"?
H: Compare Western influence with the Church and with political activism.
H: Think about Poland separately from the rest of the bloc.
A: Indicative content: for: Western radio and television; Ostpolitik; credit and debt; Helsinki's human rights pledges; contact with the West. Against: the Church in Poland (John Paul II in 1979) had a deeper effect on Polish society; the dissident movements (KOR, Charter 77) were home-grown and based on rights; the debt crisis was an economic challenge; the Brezhnev Doctrine and the secret police kept control. A Level 5 answer judges that the West's influence was important mainly as a means for the others, and that in Poland the Church was the main challenge.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that political activism did more to challenge communist rule in Czechoslovakia and Poland in the 1970s than economic problems did.
H: Define "challenge": a threat to the regime's authority, its survival or its policies.
H: Compare Charter 77 and KOR with the effects of the 1970 and 1976 price rises and the debt.
H: Judge the two countries separately before comparing.
A: Indicative content: for: Charter 77 (6 January 1977, 242 signatories) and KOR (23 September 1976) built networks and a rights language; Havel and Michnik; the Church. Against: in Poland the strikes against price rises (December 1970, June 1976) brought down Gomułka and forced the withdrawal of price rises in 1976, and the debt was the cause of the 1980 strikes; in Czechoslovakia the regime's bargain of consumption held until the 1980s. A Level 5 answer judges that in Poland economic problems were the immediate cause of challenge and activism provided its means, while in Czechoslovakia activism was the only visible challenge, and small.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ "The greatest failure of Soviet leaders between 1964 and 1985 was their inability to reform the economy." Evaluate this view.
H: Name other candidates: Afghanistan, the gerontocracy, the handling of the satellites, the Second Cold War.
H: Then ask what "failure" means: failure to achieve their own aims, or failure to prevent decline?
A: For: reform of 1965 abandoned; growth fell to 1.8 per cent; defence and oil dependence; no policy to solve it; every other problem was made worse by it. Against: the leaders' main aims were stability and security, and in the short term they achieved them; Afghanistan was a greater decision-making failure and did more damage to Soviet prestige; the gerontocracy was the root of the inability to act. A* judgement: the failure to reform was the most consequential because it was the one that left the Soviet Union unable to meet the challenges of the 1980s, but it came from the political choices (stability, no outside check) that were the real failure.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess how far the satellite states were an economic burden on the USSR in the 1970s.
H: Think of both sides: what the USSR gave and what it took.
H: Use the oil prices, Comecon, and the debt.
A: Burden: oil and raw materials sold below world prices after 1973 (the estimates of Marrese and Vanous, which are disputed); military cost of the troops and of the Pact; loans and support for troubled states, as in Poland. Benefit: markets and machinery from East Germany and Czechoslovakia; a buffer zone; the legitimacy of the system. A* judgement: the economic cost was real but not large compared with the USSR's other burdens, and the empire was held for strategic and political reasons, not for profit; the debate depends on how the subsidies are counted.
