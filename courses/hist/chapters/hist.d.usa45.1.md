:::why
In April 1945 a Missouri senator who had been Vice-President for only eighty-two days found himself President of the most powerful country on earth, with a war to finish, a bomb he had never heard of and a country that wanted to go home. Over the next eight years he had to turn a war economy into a peace economy, answer the demand of Black Americans for equal rights, face the fear of Communism that swept through the country, and win an election that every poll said he would lose. Whether he did these things well is the question this chapter asks, and it is a perfect Paper 4 question: the answer is neither "well" nor "badly", and a top-level essay has to say which parts of the record were success, which were failure, and why.

## America in 1945: the problems Truman inherited

### A rich country and a nervous one

The United States ended the war in 1945 richer than any other country. Its factories had produced much of the world's munitions, its farms fed its allies, and it had not been bombed. National output had grown enormously since 1939, and unemployment, which had been nearly 15 per cent in 1940, was under 2 per cent in 1945. But there were reasons to be nervous. Many Americans remembered that the last great war boom had been followed by the slump of 1920–21 and that the Depression of the 1930s had lasted until the war ended it. Economists and politicians feared that when more than 10 million men and women left the armed forces and war contracts were cancelled, unemployment would return. Nobody was certain that peace would be prosperous.

At the same time Americans were adjusting to a new place in the world. The Soviet Union had been an ally and now seemed a rival. There were shortages of housing, cars and consumer goods, which had been rationed or not built since 1941. Workers who had been held back by wartime wage controls wanted raises. Black Americans who had served in the forces, or moved north to work in war plants, expected change. Each of these pressures would become a problem for the new President.

### Truman and the conservative coalition

Harry S. Truman was born on 8 May 1884 and grew up in Missouri. He had been a farmer, a soldier in the First World War, an unsuccessful haberdasher (the owner of a men's clothing shop) and, with the help of the Pendergast political machine in Kansas City, a county judge and then a senator from 1935. He earned a reputation in the Senate for honest work as the chairman of a committee that exposed waste in war contracts. Roosevelt chose him as his running mate in 1944 to balance the ticket, and the two men met alone only twice before Roosevelt's death on 12 April 1945. On his first day Truman is reported to have told reporters that he felt as if the moon and stars had fallen on him.

He was a New Deal Democrat by conviction, but he was President of a party that was not united. The Democrats' majority in Congress depended on Southern Democrats, who were conservative on race, labour and spending. From 1937 these men had often voted with Republicans against Roosevelt's proposals, and the result is called the conservative coalition.

:::def Conservative coalition
The informal alliance in Congress, from 1937 until the 1960s, between most Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats, who together could defeat or water down liberal bills such as those on civil rights, labour and welfare. It was strongest on the committees, which Southerners chaired because of the seniority rule.
:::

This coalition is the most important single fact about Truman's domestic politics. Even in the years when the Democrats had a majority in Congress (1945–47 and 1949–53), Truman could not count on his own party. In the years when the Republicans controlled it (1947–49), he could count on almost nothing.

### What the war left behind: reconversion, strikes and prices

The first problem was **reconversion**: turning the wartime economy back to peacetime. Truman's answer on 6 September 1945 was a message of 21 points that asked for full employment legislation, higher unemployment benefits, a higher minimum wage, a permanent Fair Employment Practices Committee, housing, health insurance and more. It was the first statement of what would become the Fair Deal. Congress passed only the Employment Act (20 February 1946), a diluted version of the full employment bill: it created a Council of Economic Advisers to advise the President and declared it the government's responsibility to promote "maximum employment, production, and purchasing power". It did not guarantee anyone a job.

The second problem was prices and wages. During the war the Office of Price Administration (OPA) had held prices down and wages had been controlled too, and when the war ended workers wanted to catch up with the cost of living. Between January and May 1946 about 750,000 steel workers, about 340,000 coal miners and about 250,000 railroad workers went on strike, and by February 1946 nearly 2 million workers were involved in disputes. Truman, who had supported unions, now faced a public that blamed the strikers. On 17 May he seized the railroads by Executive Order 9727, and when the railroad brotherhoods struck on 22 May he went to Congress on 25 May and asked for power to draft strikers into the army. The strike was settled while he was speaking, and although the House passed the bill the same day, the Senate killed it. His own union friends never forgave him, and many Americans thought he looked both weak and bullying.

The OPA's controls were the other flashpoint. Farmers and meat packers withheld cattle from the market, so that by September 1946 meat was scarce, and on 14 October, three weeks before the midterm elections, Truman lifted the controls on meat and then on most other goods except rents. Prices jumped. Republicans used the slogan "Had enough?" and made a joke of the President's name: "To err is Truman". In the elections on 5 November 1946 they gained about 55 seats in the House and 12 in the Senate and controlled both houses for the first time since 1930.

:::key The state of the Truman presidency at the end of 1946
- **Economic problems**: strikes in steel, coal and rail; shortages of housing and meat; the end of price controls and a sharp rise in prices; fear of a slump.
- **Political problems**: a divided party; a conservative coalition in Congress; Republicans in control of both houses from January 1947 (the 80th Congress); a President whose opinion-poll rating fell below 40 per cent in 1946.
- **What it meant**: the Fair Deal was the programme of a President who had lost his congressional majority and whose own party did not follow him. This is the starting point for any judgement on how well he handled the issues.
:::

### Taft–Hartley, 1947

The new Congress reacted to the strikes by passing the **Taft–Hartley Act** (the Labor Management Relations Act), named after Senator Robert Taft of Ohio and Representative Fred Hartley of New Jersey. It outlawed the closed shop (the rule that only union members could be hired), banned secondary boycotts (pressure on a firm by refusing to deal with another firm) and some strikes, let the President obtain a court order for an 80-day "cooling-off" period before a strike that threatened national health or safety, required union officers to swear that they were not Communists, and in section 14(b) allowed states to pass "right-to-work" laws that banned any rule forcing workers to join a union. Truman vetoed it on 20 June 1947, calling it a "dangerous intrusion on free speech". The House overrode his veto the same day by 331 votes to 83 and the Senate overrode it on 23 June by 68 to 25, with 20 of 42 Senate Democrats voting against the President.

Taft–Hartley matters for three reasons. It showed the limits of Truman's power, since most of his own party voted against him on a labour issue. It gave him a campaign issue for 1948, because unions loathed the Act, and they worked hard to elect him. And it was never repealed, so that the unions' promise of repeal, made in 1948, became a broken Fair Deal pledge.

## Truman's Fair Deal, 1949–52

### The idea and the programme

In his State of the Union message on 5 January 1949, the first after his victory, Truman said that "every segment of our population, and every individual, has a right to expect from his government a fair deal". The phrase named his programme, the **Fair Deal**. It was in the New Deal's tradition and went beyond it: it proposed national health insurance, federal aid to schools, public housing, a higher minimum wage, wider Social Security, repeal of Taft–Hartley, farm income support, a permanent fair employment law, and public works for power and water in the West. It was the largest programme of liberal legislation since 1938.

:::def Fair Deal
President Truman's programme of domestic reform, named in his State of the Union message of 5 January 1949. It aimed to extend the New Deal with national health insurance, federal aid to education, public housing and slum clearance, a higher minimum wage, wider Social Security, farm income support and public power projects, and to give civil rights to Black Americans. Most of it was blocked by Congress.
:::

The Democrats had regained control of both houses in 1948 (the 81st Congress), so the programme had a chance. But the Southern Democrats chaired the key committees, and they backed the parts of it that were about money for the South (housing, farm supports, Social Security) and fought the parts about race or federal power over the states. The result was a mixed record, which the sections below take in the order of the syllabus.

### Welfare and social reforms: health, education and housing

**Healthcare.** Truman had first asked for compulsory national health insurance on 19 November 1945, supporting the Wagner–Murray–Dingell bill, which would have paid for hospital and doctors' bills out of a payroll tax. The American Medical Association (AMA) fought it with a nationwide campaign that called it "socialised medicine", at a time when the word "socialist" was becoming dangerous. The bill never reached a vote in either house. Truman had no majority for it even in the Democratic Congress, and the Cold War made the charge of socialism hard to answer. Instead, in 1946 Congress passed the Hill–Burton Act to pay for hospital building, a modest measure that Truman supported. The failure of national health insurance in the 1940s is one of the main reasons why American healthcare took a different shape from Britain's: the government later paid only for the old (Medicare, 1965) and the poor (Medicaid), and most working people kept private insurance.

**Education.** Truman asked for federal money for schools in 1949, to help states pay teachers and build classrooms after the "baby boom" of births. The proposal was killed by a quarrel over religion and race: Roman Catholics wanted money for their church schools, Protestants and many liberals did not, and Southerners feared that federal money might come with orders to desegregate. Smaller measures passed, such as the National School Lunch Act (1946), but the main bill failed, and general federal aid to schools did not come until 1965, after only the National Defense Education Act of 1958, passed in the panic after the Soviet Sputnik satellite, had given limited help.

**Housing.** The war had left a severe shortage. Veterans and their families were living with parents or in huts, and millions of families were "doubled up" with relatives. The **Housing Act of 1949** (signed on 15 July) was the Fair Deal's clearest success. It was backed by Senator Taft as well as the Democrats, an unusual alliance. The Act authorised 810,000 units of public housing over six years (135,000 a year), and gave federal loans and grants to cities to buy and clear slum land for rebuilding, called "urban redevelopment". It stated the goal of "a decent home and a suitable living environment for every American family". The results were less than the promise. Congress did not provide the money to build the full number of units, the slum clearance often destroyed more low-rent homes than it replaced (many of them Black families' homes), and the programme did not tackle segregation in housing.

### Economic policies and employment: wages, prices and farmers

**The minimum wage.** The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 had set 25 cents an hour, and by 1945 it was 40 cents. The amendment signed in October 1949 raised it to 75 cents an hour, from January 1950, a rise of 87.5 per cent, though inflation had eaten much of the gap. It was a clear success.

**Social Security.** The Social Security Amendments of 28 August 1950 brought about 10 million more workers into the old-age and survivors insurance scheme, among them many in agriculture and small business, and raised benefits by about 77 per cent. It was the largest extension since 1935 and another success, because it helped the elderly of every region and was hard for any member of Congress to oppose.

**Price controls.** The end of controls in 1946 was a mistake in the eyes of many voters, but Truman's attempts at control were weak. During the Korean War he created the Office of Price Stabilization in 1951, but by then the Fair Deal was already stalling.

**Farmers.** The **Brannan Plan**, proposed in April 1949 by Secretary of Agriculture Charles Brannan, would have replaced high price supports (minimum prices for crops guaranteed by the government) with direct payments to farmers, with a limit on payments to the largest farms, so that consumers would pay lower food prices while family farmers' incomes were protected. The American Farm Bureau Federation, which spoke for the larger farmers, and most Republicans and many Southern Democrats opposed it as a step towards state control of farming, and it was defeated. Congress passed instead an Agricultural Act in 1949 that kept high price supports for the main crops. This failure had political cost, but the 1948 election had already shown that farm prices were a powerful issue.

**Employment policy.** The Employment Act of 1946 made full employment an aim of government and created the Council of Economic Advisers, and in practice it let later governments claim a role in managing the economy. A recession struck in 1948–49 but was short, and unemployment stayed low (between about 3 and 6 per cent) during Truman's second term, helped by Korean War spending, so that the failure to pass a more radical programme was not felt as a crisis.

### Infrastructure: land reclamation, hydro-electric power and slum clearance

Truman believed in using public works to develop the West and to give cheap power. The Bureau of Reclamation continued the huge projects of the New Deal, the Columbia Basin Project (using water from the Grand Coulee Dam), the Central Valley Project in California and, from the 1944 Flood Control Act, the Missouri River Basin programme. These projects built dams and canals, irrigated farmland for new settlers and provided hydro-electric power. Truman wanted the federal government to supply the power to towns and farm co-operatives, rather than leave it to private companies. In 1949 he proposed a **Columbia Valley Administration** on the model of the Tennessee Valley Authority to plan the whole river. Private power companies and Western Republicans called it socialism, and Congress refused to create it.

So in the sense of "infrastructure" the Fair Deal had a quiet success, because reclamation and dam building went on and spread, and a failure over the organisation that was meant to run it. Slum clearance, as above, was part of the Housing Act of 1949. The syllabus asks about all three, and a strong answer says that the physical projects were the least controversial part of the programme because they brought money to Congress members' own districts.

:::key The Fair Deal: what passed, what failed, why
- **Passed or partly passed**: Housing Act 1949 (public housing and slum clearance); minimum wage from 40 to 75 cents (1949); Social Security extended to about 10 million more workers and benefits raised by about 77 per cent (1950); western reclamation and power projects continued; the Displaced Persons Acts of 1948 and 1950 admitted refugees from Europe.
- **Failed**: national health insurance; federal aid to education; repeal of Taft–Hartley; the Brannan Plan; the Columbia Valley Administration; a permanent fair employment law, a ban on the poll tax and an anti-lynching law.
- **Why it failed**: the conservative coalition; a powerful lobby against each part (the AMA, the Farm Bureau, private power companies, the churches over schools); the fear of "socialism" in the Cold War; racial politics in the South; and from June 1950 the Korean War, which turned Congress and money to defence.
- **Why the Act of 1949 passed**: where the Fair Deal brought benefits to the South or had Republican support (Taft on housing), it succeeded.
:::

### The Fair Deal in the end: a judgement

The Fair Deal was a bold programme that achieved perhaps a third of what it set out to do. The Housing Act, the minimum wage and Social Security were real gains that affected millions of lives, and what was not passed in 1949–50 was often passed in the 1960s (health insurance for the old, in Medicare in 1965; federal aid to schools in 1965; civil rights in 1964–65), so that the Fair Deal can be read as a draft of Johnson's Great Society (the reform programme of President Lyndon Johnson in the 1960s). But as a plan for the late 1940s it had failed by the 1950 midterm elections, in which the Democrats lost 28 seats in the House and five in the Senate, and in February 1952 Gallup (the polling firm) found only 22 per cent of Americans approving of Truman's performance.

A balanced assessment compares the programme with three tests: what was promised (a lot, little delivered), what was possible (Truman had no secure majority, so some failure was certain), and what endured (the minimum wage, Social Security and public housing, all of which were kept by Dwight Eisenhower, the wartime commander who became Republican President in January 1953; see [Post-war USA (2)](#hist.d.usa45.2)). It was also, as a political act, worth more than its laws. By proposing national health insurance and civil rights and fighting for them, Truman gave the Democrats a platform for the 1960s and held the New Deal coalition together in 1948.

## Fear of Communism and the Red Scare

### The reasons for fear

:::def Red Scare
A period of widespread fear in the United States that Communists were working to overthrow the government or spy for the Soviet Union. There was one after 1917–20; the second, after the Second World War, lasted from about 1947 to 1957 and is the one this chapter calls the Red Scare.
:::

Why did so many Americans believe, in 1947–54, that Communism was a danger at home? The reasons can be divided into events abroad, spy cases at home and political uses.

**External factors: Soviet expansion.** Between 1945 and 1948 the Soviet Union took control of Poland, Romania, Bulgaria, Hungary and, in the coup of February 1948, Czechoslovakia. In June 1948 it blockaded West Berlin. On 29 August 1949 it tested an atomic bomb, years before American experts had expected, and the United States' atomic monopoly was over. On 1 October 1949 Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China, which many Americans took for a defeat of their own policy ("Who lost China?"). On 25 June 1950 Communist North Korea invaded South Korea and American soldiers were soon fighting Chinese and Korean Communists. To many Americans the world seemed to be turning red, and it was natural to ask whether the cause lay at home.

**Concerns about infiltration and spying.** There was real spying. In September 1945 Igor Gouzenko, a clerk at the Soviet embassy in Ottawa, defected with documents that showed a Soviet spy ring in Canada with links to the United States and Britain. The Soviet Union had many agents in the United States during and after the war, a fact that the US Army's secret decryption project, later called Venona, revealed, though it was not made public until 1995 and so played no part in the opinion of the day. Several cases became famous:

- **Alger Hiss.** A former State Department official who had been at the Yalta Conference. In August 1948 Whittaker Chambers, a former Communist and a *Time* magazine editor, told HUAC that Hiss had been a Communist in the 1930s. Hiss denied it, sued, and was tried for perjury (lying under oath). The first trial in 1949 ended with a jury that could not agree, and on 21 January 1950 the second jury found him guilty and he was sentenced to five years. Hiss claimed innocence for the rest of his life; most, though not all, historians now think he was guilty. The Hiss case gave HUAC and a young Republican congressman, Richard Nixon, a national reputation.
- **Klaus Fuchs and the Rosenbergs.** Fuchs, a German-born British physicist who had worked on the atomic bomb at Los Alamos, confessed in January 1950 to passing secrets to the Soviets. His confession led to Harry Gold and David Greenglass and to Greenglass's sister and brother-in-law, Ethel and Julius Rosenberg. The Rosenbergs were convicted on 29 March 1951 of conspiracy to commit espionage and executed on 19 June 1953, the only American civilians executed for espionage in the Cold War. Julius is now generally thought to have spied; the case against Ethel was much weaker, and many people felt that the death sentence was excessive.

These cases made the idea of a Communist spy plausible. But most of the people who suffered in the Red Scare were not spies at all. The Communist Party of the USA had been at most about 75,000 to 80,000 strong in the mid-1940s, in a country of over 140 million. It was small, divided and falling after 1948, and the great majority of its members had joined in the 1930s out of idealism about poverty and racism, not to betray their country.

**Political uses.** Fear gave advantage to those who could use it, a point that matters for the party politics below. Businessmen who disliked the New Deal and unions could use it, Catholic organisations that were fiercely anti-Communist for religious reasons gave it a mass base, and the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover, which wanted more power, had its own reasons for stoking fear.

:::key Why Americans feared Communism, 1945–54
1. **The Soviet threat abroad**: Eastern Europe, Czechoslovakia and Berlin (1948); the Soviet atomic bomb (August 1949); Communist China (October 1949); Korea (June 1950).
2. **Real spying**: Gouzenko (1945); Hiss (1948–50); Fuchs (1950); the Rosenbergs (1951).
3. **Ideology and religion**: Communism seemed godless and a threat to private property.
4. **Political advantage**: Republicans wanted to end 20 years of Democratic rule; the FBI, HUAC and some business groups used it.
5. **The scale of the fear exceeded the scale of the danger**: the Party was small and falling, and most of those accused were not spies.
:::

### The Loyalty Program and HUAC

The fear shaped government action before McCarthy appeared. On 21 March 1947, the same month as the Truman Doctrine, Truman signed **Executive Order 9835**, the Loyalty Program. It ordered a loyalty investigation of every person entering civilian employment in the executive branch and of everyone already there. The FBI checked names, and boards in each department judged cases, with power to dismiss anyone if there were "reasonable grounds" for believing them disloyal (in 1951 Truman lowered the test to "reasonable doubt"). The Attorney General published a list of organisations judged to be "subversive", and membership of or association with one could be held against an employee. The accused often did not know who had accused them, and could not cross-examine. Over three million people were investigated; historians' counts differ, but about 300 to 400 were dismissed as security risks by the early 1950s and about 5,000 resigned. Few, if any, were proved to be spies.

:::def Loyalty Program
The scheme created by Truman's Executive Order 9835 (21 March 1947) to investigate the loyalty of every federal employee. It used FBI checks and departmental loyalty boards, and an Attorney General's list of "subversive" organisations. It was a Democratic President's measure, and it showed that the Red Scare began in the White House as well as in Congress.
:::

Why did Truman do it? Historians give two reasons that fit together. He wanted to protect himself against the charge that he was "soft on Communism", which Republicans were making after their victory in 1946 and which a Republican Congress was ready to investigate; and he wanted to calm the demand for stronger action, by providing a system with rules. He later regretted it, and in 1948 he dismissed the Hiss case as a "red herring", a remark that the Republicans used against him. The programme did a good deal of harm to civil liberties, and it gave the Red Scare the government's approval.

The **House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC)** had been created in 1938 to investigate fascist and Communist propaganda and was made a standing committee of the House in 1945. It was dominated by Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats. Its method was the public hearing, in which witnesses were questioned about their own and others' political history, and to refuse to answer, or to plead the Fifth Amendment (the right not to incriminate oneself), was taken as a confession. Its best-known work in these years was in two fields.

**Hollywood, 1947.** In October 1947, under its chairman J. Parnell Thomas, HUAC held hearings on Communist influence in the film industry. "Friendly" witnesses named colleagues. Ten writers and directors, among them the screenwriter Dalton Trumbo, refused to say whether they had been Communists, on the ground that the First Amendment protected their freedom of belief. The House voted on 24 November 1947 by 346 to 17 to cite them for contempt of Congress, and they were sentenced to a year in prison (two of them to six months) and served their sentences in 1950–51. The day after the vote, film company heads meeting at the Waldorf-Astoria hotel in New York announced that they would sack the Ten and would not knowingly employ Communists. This was the start of the **blacklist**, a secret list of people who could not work in the industry, which damaged hundreds of careers over the next decade.

**Hiss, 1948.** The committee's hearings on Chambers and Hiss, and Nixon's role in them, made it famous as an investigator of the government and gave it a reputation that it had not had before.

HUAC's effect was to establish the idea that one could be punished, without a trial, for beliefs, associations and a refusal to inform. Many people lost their jobs when a hearing named them, even though they were never charged with a crime.

### Laws against subversion

Alongside the loyalty checks, the Truman Justice Department used the Smith Act of 1940, which made it a crime to advocate the overthrow of the government by force. In October 1949 eleven leaders of the Communist Party were convicted under it after a nine-month trial, and the Supreme Court upheld their convictions in *Dennis v. United States* (1951). The **McCarran Internal Security Act** of September 1950 required Communist organisations to register with a Subversive Activities Control Board, barred their members from government jobs and passports, and allowed detention of suspected spies and saboteurs in an emergency. Truman vetoed it on 22 September 1950 as a threat to civil liberties and "the greatest danger to freedom of speech, press, and assembly since the Alien and Sedition Laws of 1798", but Congress passed it over his veto the next day. The veto shows that Truman was not a persecutor, but the fact that he had himself begun the Loyalty Program, and that his veto was overridden by a large majority including most of his party, shows how strong the tide was.

## Joseph McCarthy and McCarthyism

### The rise of McCarthy

Joseph R. McCarthy was a Republican senator from Wisconsin, elected in 1946 after a campaign in which he had called himself "Tail-Gunner Joe" (a claim about his war service that was exaggerated). He had achieved little in three years when, on 9 February 1950, he gave a speech to the Republican Women's Club of Wheeling, West Virginia. He said that there were Communists in the State Department. No recording of the speech survives and the number of names that he claimed is disputed: 205 is the figure usually given, but he also said 57 in a telegram days later and 81 cases in the Senate on 20 February. The numbers changed, but the charge worked, because it came at the moment of the Hiss conviction, the Soviet bomb, the fall of China and Fuchs's confession. McCarthy was a man of great energy and little scruple, who found that his accusations made headlines, and that it was hard for the press to disprove them in time.

A Senate committee under the Democrat Millard Tydings investigated and reported in July 1950 that McCarthy's charges were a "fraud and a hoax", but the Senate accepted the report only on party lines. Margaret Chase Smith, a Republican senator from Maine, with six colleagues, issued a "Declaration of Conscience" on 1 June 1950 criticising the use of fear in the Senate, but few followed. In the 1950 elections Tydings lost his seat in Maryland after a campaign in which McCarthy's supporters circulated a faked photograph of him with a Communist leader, and Nixon won a Senate seat in California by calling his Democratic opponent soft on Communism. After this nobody in the Senate cared to oppose McCarthy. In January 1953 he became chairman of the Senate Committee on Government Operations and, through it, of its Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations, with the power to subpoena witnesses (order them by law to appear and give evidence).

:::def McCarthyism
The practice of making accusations of disloyalty or subversion without proper evidence, and the climate of fear, blacklisting and loss of jobs that resulted. The word was coined by the cartoonist Herbert Block ("Herblock") in the *Washington Post* in March 1950. It is wider than Senator McCarthy himself, because the Loyalty Program, HUAC and the FBI also worked this way.
:::

### His methods and his targets

McCarthy's methods were consistent. He made a sweeping charge, gave a number or a name, shifted his ground when challenged, and attacked his critics as pro-Communist. In 1953 his subcommittee investigated the Voice of America, the overseas libraries of the State Department (some books were removed), and the Army Signal Corps at Fort Monmouth. His chief counsel, Roy Cohn, and an associate, G. David Schine, toured Europe examining US libraries. In 1951 he accused George Marshall, the wartime Army Chief of Staff and former Secretary of State, of being part of "a conspiracy so immense". He found few if any actual Communists. His effect was to make civil servants, teachers, writers and scientists afraid to say what they thought, and in the State Department, whose "China hands" (the experts on China) had been dismissed, to destroy the expertise that the country needed.

### The reasons for support: party politics

Why did McCarthy gain support? The syllabus names **party politics**. Four points explain it.

1. **Republican opportunity.** The Republicans had been out of the White House since 1933 and had lost the "unlosable" election of 1948. Attacking the Democrats as weak on Communism offered a way to win, and many Republicans, including Senator Taft, thought McCarthy useful whatever his methods. Even Republican leaders who disliked his methods did not want to stop him while he damaged the Democrats. McCarthy's own phrase for the whole period of Democratic rule was "twenty years of treason". In 1952 the Republican campaign repeated these charges, and Eisenhower, who despised McCarthy, deleted from a speech in Wisconsin a defence of his old chief Marshall, to avoid a fight with him.
2. **Democratic fear.** Democrats did not dare to oppose him, because they were afraid of being called soft on Communism, and some of them, such as Pat McCarran of Nevada, led the movement. Truman's own Loyalty Program had made the idea of disloyalty in government legitimate.
3. **A wide base.** McCarthy was supported by many Catholics, by some businessmen and by voters who resented the East Coast elite, which McCarthy attacked as "striped-pants" diplomats. About half of the people polled by Gallup in January 1954 had a favourable opinion of him and only 29 per cent an unfavourable one.
4. **HUAC and its Democrats.** It was not only a Republican scare. Some of HUAC's most active members were Southern Democrats, and HUAC and the loyalty boards had been at work before McCarthy.

:::key How far was McCarthyism a product of party politics?
- **For**: Republicans used it to attack Democratic administrations ("twenty years of treason"); they supported McCarthy while he hurt Democrats; Democrats were afraid to oppose him; elections of 1950 and 1952 were won partly on it.
- **Against**: the fear had real causes (Soviet expansion, the atomic bomb, spy cases); Truman's Democratic administration began the Loyalty Program; Democrats such as McCarran and Southern members of HUAC were leaders; the FBI and Catholic and business groups gave it independent strength; and most Americans, whichever party, were anxious.
- **Judgement**: party politics gave the movement its opportunity and its focus, but it was a fear that both parties shared and shaped.
:::

### The decline of McCarthy's influence

McCarthy fell fast, in about nine months, between the spring and winter of 1954. The reasons were these.

1. **Overreach.** In 1953 he began to attack the Army, which was the institution of the new President, Eisenhower (a general in the war). In 1954 his subcommittee investigated Fort Monmouth, and the Army accused McCarthy and Cohn of seeking special favours for Private Schine, who had been drafted. McCarthy, in turn, accused the Army of holding Schine hostage. The dispute led to the **Army–McCarthy hearings**.
2. **Television.** On 9 March 1954 Edward R. Murrow's programme *See It Now* showed McCarthy in his own words and in his own bullying style, and the programme was widely discussed. The hearings began on 22 April 1954 and were televised live, gavel to gavel, for 36 days until 17 June, and Americans saw McCarthy interrupting, shouting and making accusations without evidence. On 9 June the Army's lawyer, Joseph Welch, after McCarthy attacked a young member of Welch's firm, asked: "Have you left no sense of decency?" The audience in the hearing room applauded. Opinion changed. Gallup found 50 per cent favourable and 29 per cent unfavourable in January 1954 and 34 per cent favourable and 45 per cent unfavourable in June.
3. **Eisenhower's hidden hand.** Eisenhower refused to criticise McCarthy by name (he is reported to have said that he did not want to "get down in the gutter" with him), but behind the scenes he worked against him, and on 17 May 1954 he told executive officials not to give the subcommittee information about confidential discussions in the executive branch, which cut off McCarthy's supply of informers.
4. **The Senate.** A committee under the Republican senator Arthur Watkins recommended censure and on 2 December 1954 the full Senate voted 67 to 22 to censure him for refusing to cooperate with an earlier Senate committee and for abusing the Watkins committee itself. After the Democrats won control of the Senate in the 1954 elections he lost his chairmanship in January 1955.
5. **The changed climate.** The Korean War ended in July 1953 and Stalin died on 5 March 1953, so the sense of crisis relaxed, and a Republican President had made it less useful to say that the government was full of traitors. McCarthy had also lost his political purpose: with a Republican in the White House, an attack on the government was an attack on his own party.

McCarthy fell into drink and died on 2 May 1957 at the age of 48, but his influence ended in 1954. The Red Scare, however, did not end on one day: the Smith Act prosecutions continued until 1957, loyalty and security programmes ran on, and blacklists lasted into the 1960s.

:::note The historians' debate on McCarthyism (stretch: useful for A*)
Historians disagree about the role of McCarthy and the nature of the scare.
- **Ellen Schrecker**, *Many Are the Crimes: McCarthyism in America* (1998), argues that McCarthyism was wider than the senator who gave it his name. It was in her account a movement that began before McCarthy and was driven by the FBI, HUAC, the loyalty programmes of the Truman administration and a political climate in which both parties took part, and its effect was to damage the lives of many people and to narrow public debate. She does not deny that there was real Soviet espionage.
- **Alonzo Hamby**, *Man of the People: A Life of Harry S. Truman* (1995), and **David McCullough**, *Truman* (1992), are biographies that concentrate on the President. McCullough's admiring narrative, which won the Pulitzer Prize for Biography, stresses Truman's character, honesty and courage. Hamby's more analytical account judges Truman by the political constraints on him, including a conservative Congress and the Cold War. Both treat the Loyalty Program as a response to political pressure, not as a plan to suppress dissent.
- **The question for an essay** is how far Truman should be held responsible for the Red Scare that grew under him. An answer that blames only McCarthy ignores the Loyalty Program, and an answer that blames only Truman ignores the Republican Congress, HUAC and the force of events.
:::

## The 1948 election

### Truman's weakness in 1948

At the start of 1948 almost no one expected Truman to be elected. His approval rating in the polls was about a third. He had lost Congress in 1946. His party seemed to be splitting into three: the Southern Democrats were angry over civil rights, and the left wing of the party was drawn to Henry Wallace, Roosevelt's former Vice-President, whom Truman had dismissed as Secretary of Commerce in September 1946 after Wallace criticised his policy towards the Soviet Union. Some liberals tried to "dump Truman" and draft General Eisenhower, who declined. The Republicans, with the strongest chance since 1928, chose Governor Thomas E. Dewey of New York, who had lost to Roosevelt in 1944 but was an experienced and well-organised candidate, with the popular Governor Earl Warren of California as his running mate.

Truman's advisers had a plan. A memorandum written in November 1947 for Truman by his aides, associated with Clark Clifford, argued that he could not win Southern conservatives, or the left, but could rebuild the **New Deal coalition** of labour, farmers, Black voters, city voters and liberals, if he made the contest a fight between Democrats who stood for ordinary people and a Republican Congress that stood for the rich. Everything in the campaign followed from this.

### The split Democrats

The Democratic convention in Philadelphia, 12–14 July 1948, was a crisis. On civil rights, liberals led by Hubert Humphrey, mayor of Minneapolis, won a vote on the platform on 14 July (by 651½ votes to 582½) for a plank that called for federal action on civil rights, and delegates from Mississippi and part of Alabama walked out. Truman accepted the nomination in a speech after midnight on 15 July, an attack on the Republican 80th Congress, and said he would call Congress back into special session on 26 July (called "Turnip Day" because of an old Missouri saying about sowing turnips). He asked it to pass his programme, on housing, prices, the minimum wage and civil rights, and the Republican Congress passed almost nothing, which allowed him to call it the "do-nothing" Congress.

On 17 July the Southern walkout formed the **States' Rights Democratic Party**, called the **Dixiecrats**, in Birmingham, Alabama, and nominated Governor Strom Thurmond of South Carolina for President. Their aim was to carry Southern states and deny both main candidates a majority in the electoral college, so that the contest would go to the House of Representatives, where Southern power was strong. A different split followed on the left. In the same month the **Progressive Party** nominated Henry Wallace on a programme of friendship with the Soviet Union, and its attempt to challenge the Cold War drew the support of the Communist Party and of idealistic liberals.

At first the splits looked fatal. In fact they helped. By losing Wallace's followers Truman could answer the charge that he was soft on Communism, since the Progressives were visibly tied to the Communists. By losing the Dixiecrats he was freed to campaign for the support of Black voters in the northern cities. A President who had been pulled in all directions could now offer clear choices.

### The campaign: Truman and Dewey

**Truman's personality and campaigning.** He campaigned on a long series of railroad tours, the **whistle-stop campaign**, over many thousands of miles (counts vary with what is included, from over 20,000 to over 30,000 miles) and speaking at the rear of his train, often several times a day, in more than 300 speeches in all. He spoke simply, often without a text, and attacked the Republican Congress for the housing shortage, high prices and Taft–Hartley. Crowds shouted "Give 'em hell, Harry!". His plain manner, which looked unimpressive in newspapers, worked face to face. He drew very large crowds, which the reporters, who thought him doomed, did not pay much attention to.

**The key groups.** The Truman coalition was assembled from the following.
- **Labour.** The unions, angered by Taft–Hartley, which Truman had vetoed, worked hard for him and ran large voter registration drives.
- **Farmers.** In the autumn of 1948 grain prices fell, and farmers who had harvested a large crop found that Congress had let the Commodity Credit Corporation's authority to build storage bins lapse. At a ploughing contest at Dexter, Iowa, on 18 September, Truman blamed the "do-nothing" Congress. He carried Iowa, Wisconsin, Minnesota and Ohio, states with large farming populations that Dewey had been expected to win.
- **African Americans.** Truman's civil rights message of February 1948, the party's convention plank, and Executive Order 9981 on the armed forces (26 July) gave Black voters in northern cities a reason to vote for him, and historians generally think that their votes in cities such as Chicago, Los Angeles and Cleveland made the difference in the closest states (see the next section).
- **Others.** Truman recognised the new state of Israel on 14 May 1948, within minutes of its proclamation, which helped him with Jewish voters in New York and other cities.

**Conservative approval of Cold War policies.** The Truman Doctrine, the Marshall Plan (passed in April 1948) and the Berlin airlift (from June 1948) were popular, and Dewey, who supported them, could not use foreign policy against him. Truman's Loyalty Program had also answered Republican attacks on Communists in government, and conservatives who might have voted Republican on anti-Communist grounds had less reason to.

**Dewey's personality and the Republican campaign.** Dewey was efficient but cold, and some voters found him stiff and remote. He and his advisers were convinced that they were winning, because every poll said so, and they conducted a cautious campaign with general speeches that avoided controversy and did not answer Truman's attacks on the Republican Congress, which Dewey did not control but had to defend. He did not use the Cold War, and he did not defend the Congress or attack Truman's record. The Republicans also made the error of assuming that the voters who had been dissatisfied with Truman in 1946 would remain so. Those in the party who wanted to answer Truman could not persuade Dewey.

### The result

The polls had said Truman would lose. Gallup's last poll (15–25 October) gave Dewey 49.5 per cent and Truman 44.5 per cent; Roper (another polling firm) had stopped polling in September, and Newsweek's survey of 50 political journalists in October forecast a Dewey win. The *Chicago Daily Tribune* printed on the night of 2 November the headline "DEWEY DEFEATS TRUMAN" for its early edition, and Truman, photographed holding it up in St Louis, made it the most famous mistake in American journalism. The pollsters had stopped asking too early and missed the late swing, and used methods (quota sampling: interviewers were given set numbers of each type of voter to question) that overstated Republican voters.

| Candidate | Party | Electoral votes | Popular vote | Share | States |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Harry S. Truman | Democratic | 303 | about 24.2 million | about 49.5% | 28 |
| Thomas E. Dewey | Republican | 189 | about 22.0 million | 45.1% | 16 |
| Strom Thurmond | States' Rights Democratic | 39 | about 1.18 million | 2.4% | 4 (Alabama, Louisiana, Mississippi, South Carolina) |
| Henry Wallace | Progressive | 0 | about 1.16 million | 2.4% | 0 |

A candidate needed 266 of the 531 electoral votes. Thurmond's 39 are the 38 votes of his four states plus one Tennessee elector who broke his pledge. The race was closer than the electoral votes suggest. Truman won Ohio (25 electoral votes), California (25) and Illinois (28), a total of 78, each by less than one percentage point, and the three margins together were about 58,600 votes. Had Dewey won all three he would have had 267 votes and the Presidency, and if he had won any two of them Truman would have fallen short of 266 and the election would have gone to the House.

:::key Why Truman won in 1948
1. **Truman's personality and campaign**: his plain speech and energy, and the "do-nothing" Congress as a target, gave him the initiative.
2. **The New Deal coalition rebuilt**: labour angry at Taft–Hartley; farmers hurt by falling prices; Black voters won by civil rights measures; liberals and Jewish voters.
3. **Conservative approval of the Cold War**: the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan and Berlin airlift left Dewey no foreign-policy issue, and the Loyalty Program answered the charge of softness.
4. **Dewey's mistakes**: overconfidence, a vague campaign, no reply to attacks, a cold manner.
5. **The splits helped Truman**: the loss of Wallace and Thurmond cleared his image on both flanks.
6. **A turnout that favoured him**: the Republican vote fell short of expectations because many Republicans stayed at home.
:::

### Was it really a surprise?

The 2024 Paper 4 question on this topic asked whether Truman's victory was a "totally unexpected result". The answer is that it was unexpected to journalists and pollsters, but not to everyone: the result was close, Truman's campaign was strong, and the coalition he rebuilt had been the majority in every election since 1932. The polls were wrong in method, the Republicans were overconfident, and Truman had real strengths that were underestimated. An essay that says "it was a shock" has not answered the question; one that explains *why it was a shock and why it was not* has.

## Civil rights: Truman's promises and actions

### Problems facing African Americans after 1945

In 1945 Black Americans, who were about 10 per cent of the population, lived under a system of **segregation** and discrimination. In the South, where about three-quarters still lived in 1940, **Jim Crow** laws required separate schools, hospitals, buses, restaurants and parks. Black Southerners were kept from voting by the poll tax, literacy tests applied unfairly, white primaries (party elections in which only white voters could take part; until struck down in *Smith v. Allwright* in 1944) and violence: the Southern Regional Council estimated that about 3 per cent of voting-age Black Southerners were registered in 1940 and about 12 per cent in 1947. In the North, where more than a million had moved during the Great Migration of the war years, segregation was by custom more than by law: in housing, with restrictive covenants (clauses in house deeds that barred sales to Black buyers), in jobs (they were the last hired and first fired), and in unions. Racial violence was common. The armed forces were segregated, and more than a million Black men and women had served in them.

The war had raised expectations. Black soldiers who had fought fascism abroad came home to find they were still treated as second-class. The NAACP (National Association for the Advancement of Colored People) grew from about 50,000 members in 1940 to about 450,000 in 1946, and the Black press and Black churches campaigned under the "Double V" slogan (victory over fascism abroad and over racism at home). 1946 brought a series of outrages. On 12 February 1946 Isaac Woodard, a Black sergeant just discharged from the army, was taken off a bus in Batesburg, South Carolina, and beaten by the police chief, Lynwood Shull, who blinded him. On 25 July 1946 a mob lynched two Black couples, George and Mae Murray Dorsey and Roger and Dorothy Malcolm, at Monroe, Georgia. Both attacks reached the President.

:::def Segregation
The separation of Black and white people in schools, transport, housing and public places, enforced by law in the South (Jim Crow laws) and by custom and private rules in much of the North. The Supreme Court had allowed "separate but equal" facilities in *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896), though they were almost never equal.
:::

### Truman's background and motives

Truman's own attitudes were mixed. He was brought up in a Missouri family with Southern sympathies and used offensive words for Black people in private letters in his younger days. But he had a sense of fairness, he was outraged by the attacks on veterans, and he understood the politics. By the 1940s hundreds of thousands of Black voters in the northern cities were a swing group in states with many electoral votes, and Truman and his advisers saw that the Democratic Party could keep their votes only by acting. Truman's motives were a mix of conscience, a sense of justice that grew with experience, and electoral calculation. A good answer does not choose between them.

### Truman's actions, 1946–48

1. **The President's Committee on Civil Rights.** After a delegation of civil rights leaders, including the NAACP's secretary Walter White, told him of Woodard's case, Truman created the committee by Executive Order 9808 on 5 December 1946. It had 15 members, Black and white, chaired by Charles E. Wilson of General Electric. It reported on 29 October 1947 in a document entitled *To Secure These Rights*, which called for an end to segregation in all areas of life, including the armed forces, and proposed an anti-lynching law, a ban on the poll tax, a permanent Fair Employment Practices Commission, and a civil rights division in the Justice Department.
2. **A first.** On 29 June 1947 Truman became the first President to address the NAACP's annual conference, speaking at the Lincoln Memorial and saying that the federal government must act to protect the civil rights of all Americans.
3. **The ten-point programme.** On 2 February 1948 he sent Congress a special message based on the committee's report that asked it to pass anti-lynching, anti-poll-tax and fair employment laws and to protect voting rights. Congress passed none of them.
4. **The Executive Orders of 26 July 1948.** Because Congress would not act, Truman used his own powers. **Executive Order 9980** ordered fair employment practices in the federal civil service, and **Executive Order 9981** declared that there should be "equality of treatment and opportunity for all persons in the armed services without regard to race, color, religion or national origin", and set up a committee (the Fahy Committee) to see it done. The Order followed a meeting in March 1948 at which the Black labour leader A. Philip Randolph told Truman that Black men would refuse to be drafted into a segregated army. Integration was slow, because the Army resisted. But the casualties of the Korean War, which needed every soldier, made integration a practical necessity, and by the early 1950s most units were integrated and the Defence Department announced in 1954 that the last all-Black units were gone.
5. **Through the courts.** The Justice Department filed briefs on the side of the civil rights lawyers in important Supreme Court cases, including *Shelley v. Kraemer* (3 May 1948), in which the Court held that courts could not enforce restrictive covenants, and the cases of 1950 in which the Court weakened segregation in higher education and on railway dining cars (*Sweatt v. Painter*, *McLaurin v. Oklahoma*, *Henderson v. United States*).

### Opposition among Southern Democrats

The South was one-party and Democratic, and its senators and congressmen held the chairmanships of committees and had the Senate's filibuster rule to stop any bill. A **filibuster** is a very long speech, or a series of speeches, used to prevent a vote, and it could be stopped only by a vote of two-thirds of the Senate. Southern leaders, such as Senator Richard Russell of Georgia, used it against every civil rights bill. When Truman asked for the ten-point programme, Southern leaders attacked it as an attack on the Southern way of life, and Thurmond led a walkout and a third-party campaign. In 1950 a bill for a permanent Fair Employment Practices Commission was defeated in the Senate when a motion to end the debate failed. The Dixiecrats won only four states in 1948 and Thurmond returned to the Democratic Party, but the Southern bloc stayed powerful in Congress, and Truman never had a majority for his civil rights laws.

### Truman and the civil rights groups

His relationship with civil rights organisations was one of partnership and impatience. The NAACP, the National Urban League and the Black unions led by Randolph welcomed the committee and the Executive Orders and gave Truman much of his Black vote. Walter White was a regular visitor to the White House. But the leaders also pressed him for more, and they were frustrated at the lack of laws and at the slow pace of integration in the armed forces. The NAACP's legal team under Thurgood Marshall did more than the President to change the law, through the courts. By 1952 Truman was increasingly preoccupied with Korea, and the civil rights programme stalled.

### Assessing Truman on civil rights

Truman's **promises** were far greater than his **achievements** in law: no civil rights bill passed in his time. But his **actions** were bold. He was the first modern President to put civil rights at the centre of the national agenda, to set up a committee that told the nation what segregation was, to order the desegregation of the armed forces, and to risk the loss of the South for a moral cause. The measure to judge them by is what they changed: the armed forces were desegregated in his lifetime, the Black vote moved decisively to the Democrats, and the arguments of *To Secure These Rights* were used again in the 1950s and 1960s. A fair verdict is that Truman's civil rights record was the boldest part of his domestic record and also one of the least productive in legislation.

## How well did Truman deal with the internal issues? Weighing the record

Questions on this key question ask for a judgement on a whole record, or on one part of it. This table gives a way to compare the parts.

| Issue | The case that Truman dealt with it well | The case that he did not |
|---|---|---|
| Economy and strikes, 1945–47 | Avoided a postwar slump; unemployment low; Employment Act (1946) | Strikes, inflation and the meat crisis; lost Congress in 1946; veto overridden on Taft–Hartley |
| The Fair Deal | Housing Act, minimum wage and Social Security extended; kept New Deal alive | Health, schools, farm plan and Taft–Hartley repeal all failed |
| Red Scare | Vetoed the McCarran Act; refused to join the witch-hunt in speech | Started the Loyalty Program; "red herring" remark; the climate of fear grew under him |
| The 1948 election | Rebuilt the coalition; won against the odds | Victory owed much to Dewey's errors and the Republican Congress |
| Civil rights | Committee, Executive Orders 9980 and 9981, courts | No laws passed; integration slow; the risk was small once Black votes were in |

A strong answer separates **what Truman controlled** (his speeches, Executive Orders, vetoes and campaign) from **what he did not** (the Congress, the Cold War, the state of the economy). It then asks which standard is fair: against what he promised, against what was possible, or against what lasted. A defensible judgement is that he dealt with the domestic issues better than his low ratings in 1952 suggest, because his major successes (1948, the Executive Orders and Housing Act) came when he used the powers he did control, and his major failures came where Congress or the Cold War limited him; but that he must take real blame for the Loyalty Program and for being on the defensive against the Red Scare.

## Exam technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 4.1

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 4.1
1. **Define the criteria first.** For "Assess the success of the Fair Deal": criteria could be *what was promised against what passed*, *what lasted* and *what was possible with that Congress*. For a question about McCarthyism: *cause* (what made it possible), *support* and *fall*.
2. **One paragraph per reason or factor, each ending with a comparison.** Say how much that reason mattered and weigh it against another.
3. **Use precise evidence**: the date of the Housing Act (15 July 1949), 810,000 units, the 331–83 override vote, the 9 February 1950 Wheeling speech, the 67–22 censure, the 303–189–39 electoral vote, the 58,600-vote margin in three states.
4. **Keep within the question.** A question on the "internal issues" does not need the Korean War or the Berlin airlift except as causes of domestic events.
5. **Avoid narrative.** Do not tell the story of 1945 to 1954 in order; use events as evidence for an argument.
6. **Judge.** End with a view that says which factor was most important, why, and what would change it. Do not just repeat the question.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, and the total is out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'Truman's Fair Deal achieved little.' Assess this view.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement that is partly substantiated; a good range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, a balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criteria: (1) what passed, compared with what was promised; (2) whether it lasted; (3) whether it was achievable, given Congress.
- Achieved little: health insurance defeated (AMA, "socialised medicine"); federal aid to schools killed by the church-school quarrel; Taft–Hartley not repealed; the Brannan Plan and the Columbia Valley Administration rejected; no civil rights laws; the programme stalled after the 1950 midterms and the Korean War.
- Achieved something: Housing Act 15 July 1949 (810,000 units, slum clearance); minimum wage 40 to 75 cents (87.5 per cent rise); Social Security 1950 (about 10 million more, benefits about 77 per cent higher); reclamation and power projects; kept the New Deal alive under a hostile Congress; Eisenhower accepted it.
- Judgement: it achieved less than it promised, and less than the legislation of the 1960s, but it was not "little": three real gains and a base for Medicare and the Great Society.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Truman's Fair Deal did not achieve much because Congress was against it. The Republicans did not like it and so a lot of it did not pass. National health insurance did not pass and neither did help for schools. But some things did pass like the minimum wage and housing. So overall the Fair Deal achieved some things but not everything, and it was partly a success."

*Commentary.* Relevant, broadly accurate and it gives both sides, but it is general ("a lot of it", "some things") with no dates, numbers or names, and it blames "the Republicans" when the Democratic Southern bloc was just as responsible. The conclusion ("partly a success") is a restatement of the question. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 2 to 3 (about 6). To move up: use precise evidence, explain *why* each part failed, and judge against a criterion.

**A Level 4 paragraph (one side explained in depth, no comparison).** "The Fair Deal's welfare proposals largely failed. In his State of the Union message on 5 January 1949 Truman asked for national health insurance, federal aid to education and a higher minimum wage. National health insurance, which he had first proposed in November 1945, was defeated by a campaign by the American Medical Association which called it 'socialised medicine', and the bill never reached a vote. Federal aid to education died after a quarrel over whether money should go to Catholic schools, and Southerners feared that federal money would come with demands to desegregate. The Brannan Plan for farm incomes was defeated by the Farm Bureau, and the Columbia Valley Administration was condemned as socialism by the private power companies. *Commentary.* Precise, focused and well explained, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). But it covers only the failures. It does not set against them the Housing Act, the minimum wage or Social Security, or consider why the failures happened or whether any other result was possible. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). **A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The Fair Deal can be judged by asking three questions: how much of the programme passed, whether what passed lasted, and whether more was possible given the Congress that Truman had. On those tests the Fair Deal achieved less than it promised and less than its supporters hoped, but it did not achieve 'little': it achieved a small number of real and lasting gains, and its failures were mostly the result of constraints that Truman could not remove.

The case for the view is strong in the areas that mattered most to Truman. Compulsory national health insurance, first proposed on 19 November 1945, never came to a vote, since the American Medical Association's campaign branded it 'socialised medicine' at the moment when the word was becoming dangerous. Federal aid to education was killed in 1949–50 by a dispute over money for church schools, with Southern members adding a fear that federal money would bring desegregation. The Taft–Hartley Act, which Truman had promised the unions to repeal, remained law. The Brannan Plan of April 1949 was defeated by the Farm Bureau, and the Columbia Valley Administration was condemned by private power interests as socialism. The civil rights proposals, a permanent Fair Employment Practices Commission, an anti-lynching law and a ban on the poll tax, were stopped by Southern filibusters. By the time of the Democrats' loss of 28 House seats and five Senate seats in the November 1950 elections, the programme had stalled, and the Korean War, which began in June 1950, turned the country's attention and money to defence. A President whose approval rating fell as low as 22 per cent in 1952 could not recover it.

Yet 'little' is not fair. The Housing Act of 15 July 1949 authorised 810,000 public housing units over six years and, for the first time, federal money for slum clearance, and it passed with Senator Taft's support. The minimum wage was raised from 40 to 75 cents in 1949, a rise of 87.5 per cent. The Social Security Amendments of 28 August 1950 brought about 10 million more workers under old-age insurance and raised benefits by about 77 per cent, the biggest extension since 1935. Western reclamation and hydro-electric projects went on, and the Displaced Persons Acts allowed European refugees to enter. These measures affected millions of lives. They also lasted: Eisenhower, a Republican, kept all of them and expanded Social Security, and public housing and the minimum wage became permanent parts of national policy. In that sense the Fair Deal did what the New Deal had done, by establishing what governments could not afterwards abolish.

The reason for the pattern of success and failure was political. The measures that passed were those that offered something to Southern or Republican members: public housing and slum clearance to their cities, and a wage that was low even at 75 cents. The measures that failed were those that threatened a powerful interest (doctors, church schools, big farmers, power companies) or challenged the racial order of the South. A programme could succeed only where it avoided those fights, and the fights were central to what Truman wanted. Truman's own tactics were part of the problem, for he presented the whole programme at once and gave opponents the chance to mobilise against it, but it is hard to see how a President without a reliable majority could have done better.

Overall, the Fair Deal achieved less than its title promised but more than its critics allowed. The better verdict is that it was a bold programme that was achieved in about a third of its parts, and that its value was as much as a statement of the future as an act of the present: national health insurance for the old came in 1965 with Medicare, and federal aid to schools and civil rights laws in 1964–65. It achieved something real, and its failures mark the limits of what a Democratic President with a divided party could do."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with **criteria** (what passed, whether it lasted, what was possible) and uses them throughout. Each paragraph weighs one side with precise evidence (19 November 1945; 15 July 1949; 810,000 units; 87.5 per cent; 28 August 1950; 28 and five seats in 1950) and ends with a link to the question (Table B, Level 5: about 14). It distinguishes the measures that passed from the measures that failed and explains the pattern, and it reaches a judgement ("less than promised, more than 'little'") that is tested against the criteria (Table A, Level 5: about 14). Total about 28/30. The evidence is not a list, as each fact proves a point.

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'McCarthyism was mainly the product of party politics.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** This is an "Evaluate this view" question with one named cause. Level 5 asks for criteria and a sustained balance: not "was party politics important?" but *how much* and *compared with what*, with a judgement that tests the view.

**Plan.**
- Criterion: if the parties had not competed for advantage, would McCarthyism still have developed? This tests whether party politics was the main cause or a channel for others.
- For the view: Republicans out of power since 1933 and eager for an issue; "twenty years of treason"; Taft's use of McCarthy; the 1950 and 1952 elections (Tydings; the Republican platform); Democrats too frightened to resist; Truman's Loyalty Program (March 1947) as a defensive move against Republican charges; Tydings report accepted on party lines.
- Against: the Soviet atomic bomb, Czechoslovakia, Berlin, China, Korea; real spy cases (Gouzenko, Hiss, Fuchs, Rosenbergs); HUAC and Southern Democrats; the FBI; Catholic anti-communism and business groups; McCarthy's own ambition; the fall (Eisenhower, television, the Senate) was partly party-political too.
- Judgement: party politics supplied the opportunity and the choice of target, but not the fear, which came from events and spread through both parties.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The view can be tested by asking whether McCarthyism would have developed in the same way if there had been no rivalry between the parties. On that test party politics was a necessary condition of the form McCarthyism took and of its success in 1950–54, but it was not the main cause of the fear on which it fed, which had roots in events and in institutions that were not party-political.

The case for the view begins with the Republicans' position in 1950. They had been out of the White House since 1933 and had been beaten, against all expectations, in 1948. Charges that the Democrats were soft on Communism offered a way to win, and a party that had attacked 'twenty years of treason' welcomed a senator who made the charge with such force. Senator Taft was prepared to encourage McCarthy as long as he hurt the Democrats, and the 1950 midterms, in which the Democratic majority leader and Millard Tydings both lost, showed the weapon working. The Democrats, equally, were governed by party fear: the Tydings report calling McCarthy's charges 'a fraud and a hoax' was accepted by the Senate only on party lines, and few Democrats dared to defend the State Department. Truman's Loyalty Program, started in March 1947, was a response to Republican charges and to the fear of losing the 1948 election. The way in which McCarthy fell also shows politics at work: it was only when a Republican President, a Republican Senate committee under Watkins and the Army's lawyer combined against him in 1954 that he was beaten.

But party politics cannot explain the fear itself. By 1950 the Soviet Union had taken Eastern Europe and Czechoslovakia, blockaded Berlin and exploded an atomic bomb in August 1949; China had become Communist in October 1949; and on 25 June 1950 Korea was invaded. Real spy cases (Gouzenko, Hiss, Fuchs, the Rosenbergs) made treason seem likely. These events, not the Republicans, gave McCarthy his audience, and they would have produced a scare under any administration. Nor was the movement Republican alone. HUAC's most active members included Southern Democrats; Senator McCarran, a Democrat, wrote the Internal Security Act of 1950; and Truman's own Loyalty Program investigated over three million employees. The FBI under J. Edgar Hoover, Catholic anti-Communist organisations and parts of business had motives of their own, and Schrecker has shown that the main machinery of McCarthyism, the loyalty boards, the blacklists and the committees, was in place before McCarthy spoke at Wheeling on 9 February 1950. McCarthy himself was also a cause: it was his talent for publicity that took a movement of officials and committees to the front pages.

The strongest judgement is that the fear was bipartisan and the exploitation was partisan. Party politics determined who would lead the attack and when it would end: it ended in 1954 when, with a Republican in the White House, attacking the government became an attack on the Republican Party. But the origin of the fear lay in the Cold War and in a real if exaggerated espionage threat, so that party politics was an accelerator and not the engine. The view is thus half right: it explains the success of McCarthy, but not the existence of McCarthyism."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** (the counterfactual test), uses it throughout, and distinguishes the **cause of the fear** from the **use of the fear**. It gives the case for the view in precise detail and then tests it against events, institutions and Democrats' roles, and it reaches a judgement ("accelerator, not the engine") that depends on that distinction. Table A, Level 5: about 14. The evidence is precise and chosen to prove points (Table B, Level 5: about 13). Total about 27/30. To reach 30 it would need one more precise piece of evidence in the final paragraph, such as the 1954 midterm result or the Gallup figures.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess how far Truman dealt successfully with civil rights." [30]

A student wrote:

"Truman did a lot for civil rights. He was a President who cared about African Americans and he made a committee and wrote a report. He also made the armed forces desegregate. This was very good and showed he was committed. The South did not like it and some people left the Democratic Party. So Truman was very successful on civil rights and did more than any President before him."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are broadly right, but the paragraph **asserts** and does not argue. "A lot", "cared" and "very good" are evaluations with no evidence, there are no dates, names or document titles (*To Secure These Rights*, Executive Order 9981, 26 July 1948), and "desegregate" is overstated since the process was slow. It does not mention that Congress passed no civil rights law. "Successful" is never defined, so the claim "more than any President before him" is unsupported, and nothing is weighed against it.

**The fix.** Make a claim, use the detail as proof, and set a limit. "Truman's civil rights record was bold in action but unproductive in law. By setting up the President's Committee on Civil Rights in December 1946, whose report *To Secure These Rights* (October 1947) called for an end to segregation, and by Executive Order 9981 on 26 July 1948, he did more than any earlier President in this century, and the order began the desegregation of the armed forces. But none of his ten proposals of February 1948 became law, because Southern Democrats and Republicans blocked them, and the armed forces were not fully integrated until the early 1950s, under the pressure of the Korean War. The measure of success depends on the standard: against what Congress would allow he achieved little, but against what a President could do alone he achieved a good deal." Now each fact is evidence for a point, the criterion is stated, and there is a limit and a judgement.

Q: What was the conservative coalition, and why did it matter for Truman?
A: A working alliance in Congress between most Republicans and conservative Southern Democrats, formed from 1937, which could defeat liberal bills. Southern Democrats chaired many committees by seniority and could filibuster, so Truman could not count on his own party for the Fair Deal or civil rights even when the Democrats had a majority.
Q: What did the Taft–Hartley Act do, and what were the vote counts in the veto override?
A: It banned the closed shop and secondary boycotts, allowed an 80-day cooling-off period before strikes that threatened national health or safety, made union officers swear non-Communist affidavits and allowed states to pass right-to-work laws. Truman vetoed it on 20 June 1947; the House overrode him on the same day by 331 to 83 and the Senate on 23 June by 68 to 25.
Q: Which parts of the Fair Deal passed, and which failed?
A: Passed: the Housing Act (15 July 1949), the minimum wage rise from 40 to 75 cents (1949) and Social Security extended in 1950. Failed: national health insurance, federal aid to education, the Brannan Plan, the Columbia Valley Administration, repeal of Taft–Hartley and civil rights laws.
Q: What was Executive Order 9835, and what did it do?
A: Signed on 21 March 1947, it started the Loyalty Program: a check of every federal employee by the FBI and departmental boards, with dismissal if there were "reasonable grounds" for belief in disloyalty (changed to "reasonable doubt" in 1951), and an Attorney General's list of subversive organisations. About 300 to 400 were dismissed as security risks and about 5,000 resigned.
Q: What happened at the Wheeling speech and to the Tydings committee?
A: On 9 February 1950 McCarthy told a Republican women's club in West Virginia that Communists were working in the State Department, with a number that varied between 205, 57 and 81. The Tydings committee reported in July 1950 that his charges were a "fraud and a hoax", but the Senate accepted its report only on party lines.
Q: Give three reasons for the decline of McCarthy's influence in 1954.
A: Overreach (the attack on the Army); television exposure in the Army–McCarthy hearings (22 April–17 June 1954) and Welch's rebuke on 9 June; Eisenhower's quiet action and refusal to let officials testify (17 May); the Senate's censure by 67 votes to 22 on 2 December 1954; and a changed climate after Stalin's death and the end of the Korean War.
Q: How many electoral votes did each candidate win in 1948?
A: Truman 303, Dewey 189, Thurmond 39, Wallace none (531 in all; 266 needed). Truman won 28 states, Dewey 16 and Thurmond four.
Q: Why did Dewey lose in 1948, despite leading the polls?
A: He ran an overconfident, vague campaign and did not answer Truman's attacks; Truman rebuilt the New Deal coalition (labour angry about Taft–Hartley, farmers hurt by falling prices, Black voters, liberals) and made the "do-nothing" Congress the issue; the polls stopped too early and used methods that overstated Republican support.
Q: What did Executive Order 9981 do, and when?
A: On 26 July 1948 it declared equality of treatment and opportunity in the armed services without regard to race and set up the Fahy Committee to see it carried out. Integration was slow until the Korean War made it necessary.
Q: Why was it so difficult for Truman to pass civil rights laws?
A: Southern Democrats and Republicans formed a majority against them, Southern senators controlled committees and could filibuster (a cloture vote needed two-thirds), and the South was a one-party region whose votes Truman needed in other matters. The Dixiecrat revolt of 1948 showed the political cost.

:::recap
- **The starting point**: Truman became President on 12 April 1945; strikes in 1946, the end of price controls on 14 October 1946, and a Republican win in the 1946 midterms; a conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats in Congress.
- **Taft–Hartley**: passed over his veto (House 331–83, 20 June; Senate 68–25, 23 June 1947); never repealed.
- **The Fair Deal** (5 January 1949): passed the Housing Act (15 July 1949; 810,000 units), a minimum wage of 75 cents (from 40) and Social Security extended in 1950 (about 10 million more); failed on health insurance, aid to schools, the Brannan Plan and the Columbia Valley Administration.
- **The Red Scare**: fear from Soviet expansion, the atomic bomb (August 1949), China (October 1949), Korea (June 1950) and the spy cases (Hiss, Fuchs, the Rosenbergs); the Loyalty Program (21 March 1947); HUAC and the Hollywood Ten (1947); McCarran Act (1950).
- **McCarthy**: Wheeling, 9 February 1950; chairman of the Government Operations Committee from January 1953; Army–McCarthy hearings (22 April–17 June 1954); Welch (9 June); censure on 2 December 1954 by 67 to 22; died 2 May 1957.
- **1948**: Truman 303, Dewey 189, Thurmond 39; the New Deal coalition rebuilt; won Ohio, California and Illinois by about 58,600 votes in all.
- **Civil rights**: committee (5 December 1946) and *To Secure These Rights* (29 October 1947); ten-point programme (2 February 1948); Executive Orders 9980 and 9981 (26 July 1948); Dixiecrats; no civil rights laws passed.
- **Judgement**: a record of real but limited success, strongest where he acted alone (vetoes, Executive Orders, the 1948 campaign) and weakest where Congress and the Cold War limited him; the Loyalty Program is the main mark against him.
:::
