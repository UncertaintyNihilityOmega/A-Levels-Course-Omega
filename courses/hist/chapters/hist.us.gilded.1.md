## What "rapid industrialisation" means

:::def Industrialisation
The change from an economy based mainly on farming and handwork to one based on factories, machines, mines and railroads, in which most output and most jobs come from industry and transport rather than agriculture.
:::

:::def The Gilded Age
The period of the 1870s to the 1890s in the United States, named after the novel *The Gilded Age: A Tale of Today* (1873) by Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner. "Gilded" means covered with a thin layer of gold: a time of dazzling new wealth that hid poverty, corruption and speculation underneath. The syllabus topic runs from 1870 to 1920 and covers both the Gilded Age and the Progressive Era that followed.
:::

The scale of the change is the first thing to learn, because essay questions assume you know it. The numbers below are rounded and come from the Historical Statistics of the United States and the censuses; the compilers of early railroad and steel figures differ by a few per cent, which is why the text says "about".

- **People.** The population rose from about 38.6 million in 1870 to about 76.2 million in 1900 and about 106 million in 1920. In 1870 about 26 per cent of Americans lived in towns and cities; by 1900 about 40 per cent did, and by 1920 more than half (51 per cent) did, for the first time.
- **Railroads.** Track in use rose from about 53,000 miles in 1870 to about 193,000 miles in 1900, more than three and a half times as much.
- **Steel.** Output of steel rose from under 100,000 tons in 1870 to about 1.25 million tons in 1880, about 4.3 million in 1890 and about 10 million in 1900.
- **Coal.** Output of coal rose from roughly 35 million short tons in 1870 to roughly 270 million in 1900, more than a sevenfold increase.
- **Trade.** The value of merchandise exports rose from about $390 million in 1870 to about $1.4 billion in 1900.
- **World position.** On the estimates of the economic historian Paul Bairoch, the United States made about 15 per cent of the world's manufactured goods in 1880, about 24 per cent in 1900 and about 32 per cent in 1913, overtaking Britain between 1880 and 1900.

The charts show that growth was fastest in the 1880s and 1890s and that railroads came first: track rose about 3.6 times between 1870 and 1900, while steel output rose more than a hundredfold, from a very small base. Look at the units before you quote a figure in an exam. Steel is given in *long* tons here, so "10 million" and "11 million" refer to the same output.

:::key What made the growth "rapid"
1. **Speed**: in one generation the United States moved from a farming country to the largest industrial producer in the world.
2. **Scale**: firms grew from workshops of dozens to works of thousands; a single steelworks could employ more people than a whole town had held in 1850.
3. **Breadth**: railroads, steel, coal, oil, machinery, electricity and food processing all grew together, each feeding the others.
4. **The home market**: nearly all of the output was sold inside the United States, to a population that was doubling.
:::

Historians do not agree on exactly how fast the whole economy grew, because statistics from the 1870s are estimates. But they agree on the direction and the order of magnitude, and on the question this chapter answers: *what combination of causes made it possible?*

## The impact of the Civil War on industrialisation

The war ended in April 1865, and everything this chapter describes happened after it. That raises a question you must be able to discuss: did the war cause the industrial boom, or did it only get in the way?

### What Congress did in wartime

When the Southern states left the Union in 1860–61, their senators and representatives left Congress. The new Republican Party, which stood for free labour, business and expansion, held a majority for the first time and passed laws that the South had blocked for years.

- **The Homestead Act (20 May 1862)** offered any adult citizen, or person intending to become one, who headed a family (including single women, and Black Americans after emancipation) 160 acres of public land free apart from a small filing fee. The settler had to live on it and farm it for five years, or could buy it after six months at $1.25 an acre.
- **The Pacific Railway Act (1 July 1862)** chartered the Union Pacific Railroad to build west from the Missouri River and the Central Pacific to build east from California. The government lent each company United States bonds for every mile of track ($16,000 a mile on flat ground, $32,000 in the foothills and $48,000 in the mountains) and gave it land along the line, doubled by an amendment in 1864.
- **The Morrill Land-Grant Act (2 July 1862)** gave each state public land to sell to found colleges teaching agriculture and engineering, so the new industries had trained managers and engineers.
- **Tariffs.** The Morrill Tariff of 1861 and later wartime acts raised import duties to pay for the war and to protect Northern manufacturers. Duties stayed high after the war.
- **Banks and money.** The Legal Tender Acts (1862–63) issued about $450 million of paper money, the "greenbacks", not backed by gold. The **National Banking Act of 25 February 1863** (revised in 1864) allowed banks with federal charters to issue a single national currency, secured by government bonds. A tax of 10 per cent on the notes of state banks, which took effect after the war, drove the old state notes out of use.
- **War contracts.** The Union army of over a million men needed uniforms, boots, rifles, blankets, canned food, railroads, steamships and telegraph lines. Contracts to supply them built larger factories and standard sizes of clothing and shoes, and pushed meatpacking towards Chicago, where the Union Stock Yards opened on 25 December 1865.

:::def Tariff
A tax on imported goods. A protective tariff is set high enough to make imports dearer than home-made goods, so that home industry is protected from foreign competition.
:::

### Opening of the West

The Homestead Act and the railroads were meant to settle the Great Plains and the Rocky Mountain states, which the Union government had neither the people nor the roads to hold. Over the years the Act was in force (it was repealed in 1976) about 1.6 million households received a claim and about 270 million acres of land passed into private hands, roughly one tenth of the area of the United States.

The reality was harder than the promise. Quarter-section farms of 160 acres were too small to be profitable on the dry Plains, a large share of claims failed, and much of the best land went instead to railroads, states and speculators. But the settlers who stayed turned the West into the biggest wheat and corn farming region in the world, supplying Eastern cities and Europe, and bought tools, cloth, furniture and railroad tickets from Eastern factories. Gold and silver strikes (the Comstock Lode in Nevada in 1859; the Black Hills in Dakota in 1874), cattle ranching along the trails to the railheads, and copper and lead mines created more demand for rails, machines and steam power.

The West was not empty. The army and the settlers took it from Native nations, in the wars on the Plains, in the near destruction of the buffalo herds (from tens of millions to fewer than a thousand animals by 1889) and in the Dawes Act of 1887, which broke up tribal lands. The "opening of the West" for industry meant the closing of it for the people who lived there.

In 1893 a young historian, Frederick Jackson Turner, told the American Historical Association at the Chicago World's Fair that the existence of free land at the edge of settlement had shaped American democracy and energy. His paper took its starting point from the 1890 census, which reported that the unsettled area had been so broken up by settlements that there could hardly be said to be a frontier line any longer. Later historians have criticised Turner for ignoring the West's Native peoples and for overstating the role of free land, but few doubt that cheap land helped to draw people, crops and capital to the West after 1865.

### Railroad expansion

Railroads were the first big business, the largest employer, and the largest customer for iron, steel and coal. They deserve the most space in this chapter.

**The first transcontinental line.** The Union Pacific built west from Omaha, Nebraska, using mainly Irish immigrants and Civil War veterans; the Central Pacific built east from Sacramento, California, using as many as about 12,000 Chinese workers, a majority of its workforce at the peak, who cut through the Sierra Nevada in winter. The lines met at Promontory Summit, Utah, on **10 May 1869**. Four more transcontinental routes followed by 1893: the Northern Pacific (1883), the Atchison, Topeka and Santa Fe (1880s), the Southern Pacific (1883) and the Great Northern (1893), which was built without a federal land grant.

**Public help.** Government at every level paid for the boom. The federal government gave the railroads well over 100 million acres, together with loans, and states and towns added more land, money and tax exemptions. The railroads sold the land to settlers to raise cash and to create customers.

**Scandal and overbuilding.** The same help produced fraud. The Union Pacific's insiders paid themselves enormous construction profits through a company called the Crédit Mobilier, and the scandal broke in 1872, naming congressmen who had accepted shares. The historian Richard White, in *Railroaded* (2011), argues that the transcontinentals were built far ahead of demand, that their owners made fortunes from construction and speculation rather than from running trains, and that their debts helped cause the depressions of the 1870s and 1890s. Both things can be true: the lines were overbuilt and badly financed, and the railroads still changed the economy.

**Why railroads mattered to industrialisation** (the question behind many exam answers):
- **A market for industry.** Rails, locomotives, wagons, bridges, telegraph wire and station buildings made the railroads the largest buyer of iron and steel until the 1880s, and the burning of coal in locomotives raised demand for coal. The Bessemer steel industry started with rails.
- **A national market.** Freight rates fell sharply, so a Chicago meatpacker or a Pennsylvania steelmaker could sell nationwide. Mail-order firms such as Montgomery Ward (founded 1872) and Sears (1893) used the railroads to reach farm families.
- **Finance and management.** Railroads were so big and complicated that no owner could run them alone. They raised money on Wall Street by selling stocks and bonds, and invented salaried managers, divisions, timetables and cost accounts. The business historian Alfred Chandler, in *The Visible Hand* (1977), argued that these methods spread to steel, oil, meatpacking and retailing, creating the modern corporation.
- **Standardisation.** On 18 November 1883 the railroads adopted four standard time zones, and in 1886 the southern lines changed to the standard track gauge of 4 feet 8½ inches, so that wagons could run from coast to coast.
- **Settlement.** The lines carried settlers west and farm produce east, and they created towns such as Omaha, Kansas City and Denver.

### Industrial expansion

The war had also shown what factories could do. Interchangeable parts, mass-produced rifles, ready-made uniforms and food canning all expanded between 1861 and 1865, and many wartime profits were invested afterwards. Andrew Carnegie, who worked for the Pennsylvania Railroad's Thomas Scott in the war, John D. Rockefeller, who built his fortune on refining oil in Cleveland, and J.P. Morgan, who became a banker, all laid the foundations of their fortunes in the 1860s. The Northern victory also ended the argument that had held up Northern policy for decades. With slavery abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment (1865) and the Republicans governing, tariffs, banks and railroads no longer had a Southern veto.

:::key Four ways the Civil War helped industrialisation
1. **Policy**: Republican laws of 1861–65 (Homestead, Pacific Railway, land-grant colleges, tariffs, national banks) shaped the economy for a generation.
2. **Demand and skills**: war contracts created bigger factories, standard sizes and new methods, and trained a generation of managers.
3. **Politics**: Southern defeat removed the opposition to high tariffs and federal help for railroads; the Republicans then held the presidency for most of 1869–1913.
4. **Capital**: the war raised taxes, created a national bond market and made fortunes that could be invested.
:::

:::note The historians' debate: did the Civil War cause the industrial boom?
- **Charles and Mary Beard**, in *The Rise of American Civilization* (1927), called the war a "second American Revolution" that handed power from Southern planters to Northern industrialists, who then built an industrial nation.
- **Thomas Cochran**, in an article of 1961 ("Did the Civil War Retard Industrialization?"), pointed out that the growth of manufacturing slowed in the 1860s compared with the 1850s and the 1870s, and argued that the war held back industry; the boom owed more to peacetime forces.
- Many historians now take a middle view: the war damaged the South and distorted some industries, but the laws of 1862–64 and the end of the Southern veto set the conditions in which the boom took place. So the war was a cause of the *kind* of industrialisation (high tariffs, federal bank charters, railroads on public land) more than of its existence.
Use this at AS to show you can weigh a long-term cause that works through policy and politics.
:::

:::warn Dates to keep straight
The Homestead Act and Pacific Railway Act were passed in **1862**, during the war; the railroad was finished in **1869**, four years after it. The boom this chapter explains is mainly **1870–1900**. Do not write that the Civil War "started" in 1870 or that the railroad "was built during the Civil War".
:::

## Population growth

Industry needs workers, and customers. Between 1870 and 1900 the population doubled, and the cities grew far faster than the country.

### Natural increase and mass immigration

Most of the growth came from natural increase, but about 26 million immigrants arrived between 1871 and 1920, and most of them settled in the industrial North-East and Midwest, where the jobs were.

- **Who came.** Until the early 1880s the "old immigrants" came mainly from Britain, Ireland, Germany and Scandinavia. From the 1880s, and in the majority from the mid-1890s, most came from southern and eastern Europe: Italians, Poles, Slovaks, Hungarians, Greeks and Jews fleeing persecution in the Russian empire. They were called the "new immigrants". In several years between 1905 and 1914 more than a million people arrived in a single year; the record was about 1.3 million in 1907.
- **Why they came (push and pull).** Push: land shortage and falling grain prices in European farming, military service, and persecution of Jews in Russia, especially after 1881. Pull: wages several times higher than in Europe, cheap steamship passage (the Atlantic crossing was down to about a week), letters from relatives and recruiting by railroads and employers.
- **Where they worked.** They filled the mills, mines, packinghouses, clothing workshops and building sites. In steel, coal, meatpacking and textiles, immigrants and their children often made up most of the workforce. Employers valued them as cheap labour, and they could be set against each other: companies sometimes mixed nationalities in a workplace, and used newly arrived workers as strikebreakers (see the next chapter).
- **Where they lived.** They settled in neighbourhoods near their work and in the cities where friends and relatives already lived: in 1900 more than a third of the people of New York City had been born abroad. Cities such as Chicago, Milwaukee and Cleveland had foreign-born majorities in some districts.
- **Customers.** Immigrant families bought clothes, furniture, food and tickets, adding to demand.

**Controls on immigration.** The federal government began to restrict entry. The **Chinese Exclusion Act of 6 May 1882**, the first major federal law to bar a group of immigrants by nationality, suspended the entry of Chinese labourers for ten years; the Geary Act of 1892 renewed it and an Act of 1902 made it permanent. The **Contract Labor Law of 1885** banned employers from paying to bring in workers under contract, in response to trade unions' fears that firms were importing cheap workers to lower wages. The Immigration Act of 1891 made immigration a federal matter, and **Ellis Island** (opened 1 January 1892) processed arrivals in New York harbour, turning back those who failed health checks or were likely to become dependent. The great majority of Europeans who arrived were admitted. The restrictions show that Americans welcomed workers but feared competition, a theme you will meet in the Paper 1 practice below.

:::def Mass immigration
The arrival of very large numbers of immigrants in a short period: about 26 million people arrived in the United States between 1871 and 1920, a flow that supplied labour for the new industries and changed the country's cities and politics.
:::

### The migration of emancipated slaves

Four million enslaved people were freed in 1865. For most of the Gilded Age they stayed in the South: in 1900 about nine in ten African Americans still lived in the former slave states, most of them as farm labourers or sharecroppers. The Great Migration of millions to Northern industrial cities belongs to the First World War years and after, but its beginnings were earlier.

- **The Exodusters.** After the end of Reconstruction in 1877 (the withdrawal of federal troops and the return of white Democratic control to the South), terror, debt and falling cotton prices drove many Black Southerners to leave. In 1879 and 1880 tens of thousands (estimates run from about 20,000 to 40,000) left Mississippi, Louisiana and Texas for Kansas, with the encouragement of organisers such as Benjamin "Pap" Singleton. They were called Exodusters, after the Book of Exodus. Others moved to Oklahoma, where all-Black towns such as Langston were founded after the land rush of 1889, or to the cities of the North and West.
- **Black workers in industry.** A smaller number took jobs in the new industries, in the coal and iron works of Birmingham, Alabama, in the mines, as railroad workers and Pullman porters, and in the stockyards and steel mills of the North, where they were often hired as strikebreakers and excluded from skilled jobs and many unions. In the South the convict-lease system rented prisoners, most of them Black, to coal mines and railroads at very low cost.

The effect of this migration on industrialisation was real but smaller than that of immigration. What mattered more was that a cheap, poorly protected Southern workforce kept the South agricultural, and that the North looked to Europe for its labour.

:::key Population growth and industrialisation: the links
1. **Labour**: millions of workers for mills, mines and railroads; labour stayed cheap, which helped profits.
2. **Markets**: a larger and richer population bought more goods, especially food, clothing and housing.
3. **Cities**: crowding together made big factories, stores and services possible, and created the problems of the next chapter.
4. **Mobility**: workers moved to where industry needed them, from farms to towns and from Europe to America.
:::

## The availability of natural resources

Few countries have had so many resources in one place, close to water transport. This is a necessary cause, but not a sufficient one: Russia and China also had huge resources.

### Raw materials and food

- **Iron ore.** The Lake Superior ranges supplied most American iron ore. The Mesabi Range in Minnesota, found in 1890 by the Merritt family, was so rich and so near the surface that it could be dug out with steam shovels from open pits; the first ore left in 1892. Ore-carrying steamers took it down the Great Lakes to the ports of Ohio and Pennsylvania.
- **Coal and coke.** The Pittsburgh seam of Pennsylvania and the Connellsville coal fields, which Henry Clay Frick turned into coke (coal baked so that it burns hotter) for blast furnaces, made Pittsburgh the capital of steel: ore from Minnesota, coal from the hills, and rivers and railroads between.
- **Copper and other metals.** Copper from Michigan and Montana (Butte) was needed for electrical wire; lead, zinc and silver came from the Rockies.
- **Oil.** The first American oil well was drilled at Titusville, Pennsylvania, in 1859. Kerosene for lamps was the first big product, and by the 1880s American oil was a major export.
- **Timber.** White pine from Michigan, Wisconsin and Minnesota, then the forests of the Pacific North-West, supplied houses, railroad ties and bridges.
- **Food.** The Midwestern prairies and Great Plains produced enormous grain and meat surpluses. Better reapers, steel ploughs and barbed wire (from 1874) raised output; refrigerated railroad cars, used by meatpackers such as Gustavus Swift from the late 1870s, took dressed beef to Eastern cities. A cheap, plentiful food supply meant that factory workers could be fed and farm labour could be spared.

### Energy resources

Industry runs on energy. In 1870 water power and wood were still important, but by 1900 steam engines burning coal supplied most power. US coal output rose from roughly 35 million short tons in 1870 to roughly 270 million in 1900. Coal was cheap and plentiful, and could be moved cheaply by the railroads and the Great Lakes. Oil began to matter for lighting and, after 1900, for engines. Hydroelectric power at Niagara Falls (1895) showed what electricity could do. Because energy was cheap, American firms could use more machines per worker than their European rivals, which raised output per worker.

### Access to export markets

Most of what the factories made was bought at home, but exports mattered. Cotton remained the largest single export, wheat and flour, meat and lard and refined oil grew rapidly, and by 1900 manufactured goods (machinery, sewing machines, reapers, locomotives, steel) were a rising share. The value of exports rose from about $390 million in 1870 to about $1.4 billion in 1900. Europe's demand for American grain and meat paid for imports of machinery and capital. From the 1890s businessmen and politicians argued that the country needed foreign markets to absorb its surplus output, a motive for the acquisition of Hawaii and the Philippines in 1898 and for the Open Door notes on China of 1899.

:::key Resources: why they were an advantage
1. **Variety**: iron, coal, oil, timber, farmland, copper and silver all in one country.
2. **Access**: lakes, rivers and rails to move them cheaply.
3. **Food surplus**: it fed the cities and paid for imports.
4. **Cheap energy**: it encouraged mechanisation.
But resources are only useful when someone has the money, workers and transport to use them, which is why the other causes matter.
:::

## Lack of regulation, and support, from state and Federal governments

The syllabus names two things together: a *lack of regulation* and *support*. They look opposite, but both are true, and a good answer explains how.

:::def Laissez-faire
A French phrase meaning "let it be": the belief that government should interfere as little as possible in the economy and leave business to the market. In the Gilded Age most Americans believed in it in principle, while governments helped business in practice.
:::

The Federal government was small. Its only large source of money was tariffs (about half of federal revenue) and excise taxes. There was no income tax after 1872 (a new one in 1894 was struck down by the Supreme Court in 1895), no central bank, no minimum wage, no limit on working hours, no safety law for factories and no controls on prices or on the way companies could join together. State governments were more active, but generally weak against large corporations. Courts tended to defend the freedom to make contracts and the rights of property.

**Government support.** At the same time governments gave business gifts: land and loans to railroads, tariffs against foreign competition, the patent system, favourable laws on incorporation, and troops and courts to put down strikes.

### Tariffs

The high protective tariff was the core of Republican economic policy from the Civil War to 1913. It kept foreign steel, textiles and tools out, which let American makers charge higher prices and build their plants safely. The McKinley Tariff of October 1890 (named after William McKinley, then chairman of the House Ways and Means Committee) raised the average duty to about half of the value of the imported goods it covered; the Dingley Tariff of 1897 raised it even higher on many goods. Supporters said tariffs protected "infant industries" and the high wages of American workers. Critics, mainly Democrats and farmers, said tariffs raised the prices of everything the farmer bought, while he sold his crops on a world market, and that they let trusts (combinations of firms that no longer compete) overcharge. Grover Cleveland devoted his whole annual message of 1887 to demanding lower duties. Historians still argue whether tariffs created industrial growth or whether industry would have grown anyway behind such a large home market. The balance of opinion is that they helped some industries (tinplate, textiles, steel in its early years) and were less necessary once American steel became the cheapest in the world.

### Money supply and the availability of capital

Industry needed vast amounts of money (capital) to build railroads and mills. Where did it come from?
- **Reinvested profits.** Carnegie put most of his profits back into new plant, which was the main source of capital for steel.
- **Savings and Wall Street.** Americans' savings, and the sale of shares and bonds on the New York Stock Exchange, financed railroads.
- **Foreign investment.** British and European investors lent huge sums for American railroads; this capital paid for a large part of the lines built before 1900.
- **Investment bankers.** Firms such as J.P. Morgan and Company raised money, reorganised bankrupt railroads and arranged mergers. Morgan's presence in a company reassured investors.

The *money supply* was a political quarrel. After the war the government withdrew greenbacks (paper money issued during the war that was not backed by gold) and returned to gold, resuming payment of paper money in gold on 1 January 1879. Under the **gold standard** the dollar is worth a fixed weight of gold, and money may be issued only as the gold supply allows. The **Coinage Act of 1873** stopped the free coinage of silver (before it, anyone could bring silver to the mint to be made into dollars, so ending it kept the money supply from growing; its opponents called it "the Crime of '73"); the **Bland–Allison Act of 1878** and the **Sherman Silver Purchase Act of 1890** made the Treasury buy silver; the **Gold Standard Act of 14 March 1900** defined the dollar in gold alone. Because the number of dollars did not grow as fast as the economy, fewer dollars were chasing more goods, and prices fell for most of the time from 1873 to 1896. Falling prices were good for creditors, savers and bankers, who were repaid in dearer dollars, and made it easier to attract foreign investors who trusted the gold dollar. They were hard on farmers and other debtors, whose debts stayed fixed while the price of their crops fell: the cause of the farmers' anger in the next chapter.

### Banking systems

The National Banking Act created a uniform national currency, and by the 1890s there were thousands of national and state banks. But the system was weak. There was no central bank to lend to banks in difficulty or to adjust the supply of money. Country banks kept their reserves in city banks, and city banks in New York, so that when money was short, the pressure built up in New York and spread outwards. The money supply did not rise and fall to meet the seasons, so every autumn the harvest drained money from the cities. The result was a series of banking panics (1873, 1884, 1890, 1893, 1907) that closed banks and factories. In 1895, and again in 1907, J.P. Morgan himself organised the rescue of the Treasury and of Wall Street; this was the clearest proof of the lack of regulation and led to the Federal Reserve Act of 1913 (see [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (3)](#hist.us.gilded.3)).

:::key Government: helped, but did not regulate
1. **Help**: land and loans to railroads, protective tariffs, patents, land-grant colleges, troops against strikers.
2. **No regulation**: no income tax, no central bank, no controls on wages, hours, prices, safety or mergers until the Interstate Commerce Act (1887) and Sherman Act (1890), and these were weakly enforced (see [the next chapter](#hist.us.gilded.2)).
3. **Effect**: firms could expand fast and cut costs, but at the price of panics, monopoly and conflict.
4. **Do not say**: "the government did nothing". It did a great deal for business, and nothing for workers.
:::

## Technological innovation

The late nineteenth century was one of the great ages of invention. Robert Gordon, in *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016), calls the period from 1870 the start of a "special century", in which electricity, the internal combustion engine, running water, telephones and chemicals changed life more than any later period. For this chapter, the important point is that new techniques made industry cheaper and let it grow.

| Date | Innovation | Who | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1850s–70s | Bessemer process; open-hearth furnace | Henry Bessemer; William Kelly; Siemens and Martin | Cheap, strong steel for rails, bridges, machines and buildings |
| 1873–74 | Typewriter | Sholes and Glidden; made by Remington | Speed in offices; new clerical jobs |
| 1876 | Telephone | Alexander Graham Bell | Instant business communication |
| 1879–82 | Incandescent lamp; central power station | Thomas Edison | Electric light and power |
| 1880s | Alternating current | George Westinghouse | Electricity sent long distances |
| 1880s–90s | Gasoline engine, motor car | Otto, Benz and Daimler (Germany); Duryea, Ford | A new transport industry, from 1900 |
| 1878–90s | Refrigerated railroad cars | Swift, Armour | Dressed meat sold across the country |

### Steel

Steel is iron with a little carbon, much stronger than iron and much more durable, but until the 1860s it was too expensive to make in bulk. The **Bessemer process** blasted air through molten iron to burn out impurities and make steel in minutes rather than days. The first American Bessemer works started in the 1860s. **Carnegie's Edgar Thomson Works** at Braddock, near Pittsburgh, opened in 1875, designed by Alexander Holley and named after the president of the Pennsylvania Railroad, Carnegie's best customer. Carnegie built for volume, kept detailed cost accounts for every process, employed chemists and bought his own coal, coke and ore. In the **open-hearth** process, which slowly overtook the Bessemer by 1908, the steel is made in a bath of metal heated by a furnace, which gives a more reliable steel in larger batches. The **basic** processes of the late 1870s allowed American makers to use ores with phosphorus.

The effects were huge. The price of Bessemer steel rails fell roughly tenfold between the late 1860s and 1898 (about $170 a ton in 1868's paper dollars to about $17 in 1898), so railroads could be built cheaply. Steel then made possible skyscrapers (the steel-frame Home Insurance Building in Chicago, 1885), the Brooklyn Bridge (1883), machine tools, ships and, later, cars. In 1900 the United States made more steel than any other country.

### Electrical power and the internal combustion engine

**Electricity.** Thomas Edison, in his research laboratory at Menlo Park, New Jersey, made a practical long-lasting incandescent lamp in 1879 and opened the **Pearl Street station** in lower Manhattan on **4 September 1882**, the first central power station selling light in the United States, to about 60 customers at first. George Westinghouse backed alternating current, which could be sent over long distances, and won the "battle of the currents": the 1893 Chicago World's Fair was lit with it, and the power station at Niagara Falls began to supply Buffalo in 1896. Electric streetcars (the first successful system opened in Richmond, Virginia, in 1888) let cities spread out, and electric motors began to replace the line shafts and belts driven by a steam engine in factories. The effect on factory output was mostly after 1900: electric motors supplied about five per cent of factory mechanical power in 1899 and more than half by 1919, so electricity helped the *later* growth more than the boom of the 1870s and 1880s.

**The internal combustion engine.** Developed in Germany in the 1870s and 1880s (Nikolaus Otto's four-stroke engine in 1876, Karl Benz and Gottlieb Daimler's engines in the 1880s), it was taken up in America by the Duryea brothers (1893) and Henry Ford (his first car in 1896). Ford Motor Company was founded in 1903, the Model T appeared in 1908 and the moving assembly line began at Highland Park in 1913. In 1900 there were about 8,000 registered motor vehicles in the United States; in 1910 about 458,000 and by 1920 more than 8 million. The engine also powered tractors and aeroplanes (the Wright brothers' first flight was on 17 December 1903). This created demand for steel, rubber, glass and oil and, for the first time since the railroad, a new huge industry. But, again, its main effect on the American economy was after 1900.

### The typewriter and the telephone

The **typewriter**, perfected by Christopher Latham Sholes and made by the gunmakers E. Remington and Sons from 1873, and the **telephone**, patented by Alexander Graham Bell on 7 March 1876, did not make steel or coal. But they transformed offices. Large companies needed to send orders, accounts and letters quickly between head offices, plants and sales teams. Together with the telegraph, the typewriter and the telephone made it possible to manage firms that stretched across a continent. They also created new jobs, mainly for women: the number of stenographers and typists, a few thousand in 1880, was over 100,000 by 1900, and most were women.

### The US system of patents

A patent gives an inventor the sole right to make and sell an invention for a number of years. The American system, based on the Constitution, examined each application to see that it was new, charged a modest fee, and protected individual inventors, not just companies. The numbers show how many people used it: the first patent numbers of 1861 were about 31,000 and those of 1900 about 640,000, so more than 600,000 patents were issued in under forty years. Many of them were small improvements, but the system rewarded the work, encouraged firms to hire inventors, and led to industrial research laboratories such as Edison's at Menlo Park and the General Electric laboratory (1900). Edison alone was awarded over 1,000 patents. Inventors became national heroes, and a business that owned the patent for a vital machine, such as Bell's telephone or Westinghouse's air brake, could become a monopoly.

:::key Technology: how much did it matter?
1. **Steel** was the key. It made railroads, bridges, buildings and machines cheaper and stronger, and was the base of the biggest industry of the age.
2. **Electricity and the engine** mainly shaped growth after 1900, but their invention before 1900 created industries (electrical equipment, motor cars) and pointed the way.
3. **Typewriter and telephone** made large-scale management possible, but did not drive growth by themselves.
4. **Patents** gave a reward for invention and made research a business.
Technology needed capital, markets and workers to be used: it was a cause that depended on the others.
:::

## Contemporary social attitudes towards commercial growth and success

Many Americans admired business success, and few asked for controls. That attitude was itself a cause of growth, because it shaped what government would allow and what talented young people chose to do.

- **The self-made man.** Americans believed in a land of opportunity. The novels of **Horatio Alger Jr** (more than a hundred, starting with *Ragged Dick* in 1868) told how poor boys rose by honesty, hard work and a lucky break. In practice his heroes usually reached modest comfort, not great wealth, but his books made success seem open to all.
- **The gospel of wealth.** In June 1889 the steelmaker **Andrew Carnegie** published an article called "Wealth", soon known as "The Gospel of Wealth". He argued that competition, though hard on individuals, was best for the race because it ensured "the survival of the fittest in every department", and that great fortunes were the reward of talent. But the rich had a duty to use their wealth for the public good during their lives: "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced." He gave away more than $350 million, much of it to libraries, universities and peace causes. Rockefeller made gifts of a similar kind, including to the University of Chicago, founded in 1890.
- **Social Darwinism.** The English philosopher **Herbert Spencer** coined the phrase "survival of the fittest" in 1864 after Charles Darwin's *Origin of Species* (1859), and applied it to human society. When he toured the United States in 1882, leading Americans honoured him with a banquet in New York. At Yale, **William Graham Sumner** argued in *What Social Classes Owe to Each Other* (1883) that government should not take from the thrifty to help the poor, and in an essay of the same decade, "The Challenge of Facts" (published in 1914), that society could choose "liberty, inequality, survival of the fittest" or "not-liberty, equality, survival of the unfittest". Historians such as Richard Hofstadter (*Social Darwinism in American Thought*, 1944) saw this as the ruling ideology of the age; Robert Bannister (1979) argued that very few American thinkers used the term or followed Spencer so closely, and that Hofstadter exaggerated. The sensible view: Social Darwinism was a respectable argument used to defend inequality, but business leaders also drew on older religious ideas, including the Protestant work ethic.
- **The critics.** Not everyone agreed. **Henry George**'s *Progress and Poverty* (1879), which sold hundreds of thousands of copies within a few years, began its first chapter: "This association of poverty with progress is the great enigma of our times." He proposed a tax on land to share the gains. He ran for mayor of New York in 1886 and finished second, ahead of Theodore Roosevelt. Edward Bellamy's novel *Looking Backward* (1888) imagined a socialist future. Twain and Warner satirised greed. Churches' "Social Gospel" and the labour movement and Populists (next chapter) expressed the anger.

:::key Attitudes: why they matter for industrialisation
1. They **justified** letting business grow without controls: wealth was seen as the reward of ability, and government interference as harmful.
2. They **encouraged** investment, risk-taking and invention: success was admired.
3. They **delayed** reform: the first regulation (1887, 1890) was weak and the Progressive reforms did not come until the early twentieth century.
4. They were **contested**: George, Bellamy, the labour unions and the Populists show that many Americans rejected them.
:::

## Why was there rapid industrialisation? Weighing the causes

:::key A judgement you can defend
Natural resources and population were the **necessary conditions**: without them no boom was possible. But Russia and China also had them. What turned potential into growth was a set of **enabling choices**: the Republican laws of the Civil War, a large protected home market, capital from home and abroad, patents, and an attitude that left business free. And the **driver** that connected all the others was the railroad, which created the market, absorbed the steel and coal, and invented the modern corporation. Technology (steel above all) made the railroads and then everything else cheaper. So the best answers do not say "X was the cause" but "X was the necessary background, Y the trigger, Z the multiplier".
:::

:::warn Common mistakes
- **"Laissez-faire" does not mean "no government help".** Write "little regulation but a great deal of support".
- **Do not list inventions without saying what each did for output.** The telephone changed offices; steel changed industry.
- **Do not say electricity transformed factories in the 1880s.** Its main industrial effect was after 1900.
- **Do not mix up old and new immigrants** or say that all Black Southerners left the South in this period; about nine in ten stayed.
- **Do not claim the Homestead Act peopled the Plains by itself.** Railroads, land companies and the failure of many claims are part of the picture.
- **Keep the three government policies apart:** tariffs (protection), money (gold and silver) and banking (no central bank).
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (from the 9489 mark schemes). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why the expansion of the railroads helped industrial growth in the United States after 1870.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "The railroads grew from about 53,000 miles in 1870 to about 193,000 miles in 1900. The first transcontinental railroad was finished in 1869, and others followed. The government gave the companies land and loans. A lot of people moved west on the railroads. Railroads used a lot of steel and coal. So railroads helped industry to grow."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes something about the railroads. Only the last two touch on industry, and neither explains how. Causation is claimed by "so", not shown. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "The railroads helped industrial growth first by creating demand. Building about 140,000 additional miles of track in thirty years needed rails, locomotives, wagons, bridges and fuel, and until the 1880s rails were the main product of the new steel industry, so the railroads' orders paid for steelworks such as Carnegie's Edgar Thomson Works, opened in 1875, which could then sell to others as well. A second reason was that they made a national market. Freight rates fell, so a packer in Chicago or a steelmaker in Pittsburgh could sell across the continent, which allowed larger factories and cheaper products. Mail-order companies such as Montgomery Ward, founded in 1872, used the railroads to reach farm families."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was that the railroads taught business how to be big. A line of thousands of miles could not be run by its owner, so the companies appointed salaried managers, divided the work into regions and kept accounts of every cost. They also raised the money on Wall Street by selling shares and bonds, to British as well as American investors. Steel, oil and meatpacking firms later copied these methods, and the investors who financed railroads moved to finance them.

These factors were linked. The railroads' demand for rails built the steel industry; the steel industry's falling prices (steel rails fell roughly tenfold in cost between the late 1860s and 1898) made more railroads affordable; and the larger market the lines created justified bigger factories, which in turn needed the management methods that the railroads had invented. The most important reason was the creation of the national market, because demand for steel and coal depended on it and the new methods of management were only needed once firms were large enough to supply it. It was not the railroads alone that produced growth, since land grants, loans and foreign capital paid for them, but without the railroads those other causes would not have had a single national market in which to work."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (demand, market and management reinforce each other), and a supported conclusion about the most important factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 2 (b) [20]: 'Technological innovation was the main reason for rapid industrialisation after 1870.' How far do you agree?
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (9–12): explains one side. Level 4 (13–16): explains both sides with detailed evidence, but the conclusion may not compare them. Level 5 (17–20): both sides analysed and a **clear comparative judgement** about which is stronger, and why.

**Plan (2–3 minutes).**
- For: steel (the Bessemer and open-hearth processes; rails fell roughly tenfold in price; output about 77,000 tons in 1870 to about 10 million in 1900); electricity and the engine (Pearl Street 1882; Ford 1908; cars from about 8,000 in 1900 to over 8 million in 1920); typewriter and telephone (offices, managers, jobs); patents (over 600,000 in forty years).
- Against 1, railroads and the market: track tripled; the national market and demand for steel; White and Chandler.
- Against 2, capital and government: tariffs, land grants, banks, Wall Street, foreign money.
- Against 3, people and resources: immigration (about 26 million, 1871–1920), cheap coal and ore, food surplus.
- Against 4, timing: electricity and the engine mattered mainly after 1900 (electric motors about 5 per cent of factory power in 1899, over half by 1919).
- Judgement: steel was a main reason for the growth of the *iron and steel and railroad* economy, but technology only worked because the market, capital and labour were there. Criterion: what made technology profitable?

**A Level 3 paragraph (explains one side).** "Technological innovation was very important. The Bessemer process and the open-hearth furnace made steel cheap, so steel rails fell in price roughly tenfold between 1868 and 1898. Edison opened the Pearl Street power station in 1882 and Bell patented the telephone in 1876. The typewriter helped offices and Ford made the car. There were over 600,000 patents in forty years. This shows that technological innovation was the main reason for rapid industrialisation."

*Commentary.* Well supported, but one-sided, and the last sentence asserts rather than explains: a list of inventions is not a judgement about the *main* reason. An answer made only of paragraphs like this, with other factors merely mentioned, stays in Level 3.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet technology alone does not explain why the United States, and not another country with the same inventions, grew so fast. The decisive condition was the market. The Bessemer process was invented in Britain, and the telephone and the electric lamp were quickly copied in Europe. What the United States had was a single continental market of 76 million people by 1900 with no internal tariffs, linked by about 193,000 miles of railroad. The railroads came first: they were built from the 1860s with land grants, government loans and British capital, before steel was cheap, and it was their orders that built the steel industry. Richard White argues that the lines were built before the traffic existed, but even he accepts that they pulled the economy together. Technology responded to this demand rather than creating it.

A second alternative is capital and government policy. Carnegie's low costs depended on high tariffs that kept British rails out until his works were competitive, on the Wall Street financing of his customers and on his own reinvested profits. The ability of firms to grow to enormous size depended on the freedom they had from regulation. Immigration supplied the workers: about 26 million arrived between 1871 and 1920, and they staffed the mills and mines. Finally, much of the technology listed above had little effect on output before 1900: electric motors supplied about five per cent of factory power in 1899, and over half by 1919; the car industry was tiny in 1900. Those inventions belong to the story of the Progressive Era's growth, not to the rapid industrialisation of the 1870s and 1880s.

Overall, technological innovation was a main reason, because steel was central to the whole industrial economy, but it was not *the* main reason. It was a multiplier of other conditions. Resources and people made growth possible; the railroads and the national market made it profitable; capital and the policy of low regulation and high tariffs made it safe; and technology, above all steel, made it cheap. If one cause has to be chosen, the railroad, a technology in itself but one that created the demand for the others, matters more than the later inventions."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (what turned technology into growth) and distinguishes necessary conditions from the main driver. With an opening paragraph on the case for technology, this is Level 5 (19–20). Note that the answer also separates the *early* technology (steel) from the *later* technology (electricity and the engine): spotting the dates is itself a mark of a strong answer.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

The existence of an area of free land, its continuous recession, and the advance of American settlement westward, explain American development. ... And now, four centuries from the discovery of America, at the end of a hundred years of life under the Constitution, the frontier has gone, and with its going has closed the first period of American history.

*Adapted from "The Significance of the Frontier in American History", a paper read by the historian Frederick Jackson Turner to the American Historical Association at Chicago, July 1893. The two sentences come from the beginning and the end of the paper.*

**Source B**

Up to and including 1880 the country had a frontier of settlement, but at present the unsettled area has been so broken into by isolated bodies of settlement that there can hardly be said to be a frontier line.

*From a bulletin of the Superintendent of the Census for 1890, as quoted by Frederick Jackson Turner in 1893.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about the end of the frontier in the United States.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Both sources say the frontier has gone. Source A says 'the frontier has gone' and Source B says there can hardly be said to be a frontier line. Source A is by a historian and Source B is from the census, so they are different types of source."

*Commentary.* One similarity, supported. No difference of substance, and the comment on the type of source is a general label with no context. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"The sources agree on the central fact: by the early 1890s the frontier, the moving edge of settlement, was over. Source B states that 'up to and including 1880' there was a frontier of settlement but that now there can 'hardly be said to be a frontier line', and Source A, whose author quotes this same bulletin in his paper (as the provenance of Source B says), concludes that 'the frontier has gone'. Both also treat 1890 as a turning point.

They differ on what the end of the frontier means. Source B is a statement of fact about the pattern of settlement and it says nothing about its significance; the census reports the loss of the frontier line as a technical change in how it will collect figures. Source A claims much more: that free land and the westward advance 'explain American development', and that its end closes 'the first period of American history'. It is a large interpretation that goes far beyond what the census data can show.

The context and provenance explain why. Source B was written by the Superintendent of the Census to describe the 1890 returns, in which the Bureau stopped measuring a frontier line because settlers had spread across the West, a fact that fitted the railroads, the homesteads and the end of the Plains wars at Wounded Knee in December 1890. Its purpose was administrative, and its authority is the government's statistics. Source A was an academic paper by a young historian reading it at Chicago in 1893, where the World's Columbian Exposition celebrated four centuries since Columbus. Turner wanted to make a new argument, against historians who explained America by European origins, so he turned the census statement into a theory. Source A is therefore strong evidence of how educated Americans interpreted the end of the frontier, and good evidence for Turner's argument, but weak as evidence of what actually happened in the West, which Source B, though narrow, records more reliably. Neither source mentions the Native peoples or the railroads, which limits both."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the census and the 1893 Exposition) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the sources' value for the specific issue in the question. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.
:::

**Source A**

The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth, so that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship. And while the law [of competition] may be sometimes hard for the individual, it is best for the race, because it insures the survival of the fittest in every department. The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced.

*Adapted from "Wealth", an article by the steelmaker Andrew Carnegie in the North American Review, June 1889 (later known as "The Gospel of Wealth"). Three sentences from different parts of the article, in Carnegie's words; the words in square brackets are added.*

**Source B**

Let it be understood that we cannot go outside of this alternative: liberty, inequality, survival of the fittest; not-liberty, equality, survival of the unfittest. The former carries society forward and favors all its best members; the latter carries society downwards and favors all its worst members.

*From "The Challenge of Facts", an essay by William Graham Sumner, a professor at Yale University, written in the 1880s and published after his death in The Challenge of Facts and Other Essays (1914). He made the same case in public in his book What Social Classes Owe to Each Other (1883).*

**Source C**

This association of poverty with progress is the great enigma of our times. It is the central fact from which spring industrial, social, and political difficulties that perplex the world.

*From the first chapter of Progress and Poverty, a book by the writer and reformer Henry George, 1879.*

**Source D**

We meet in the midst of a nation brought to the verge of moral, political, and material ruin. The fruits of the toil of millions are boldly stolen to build up colossal fortunes for a few.

*From the preamble to the Omaha Platform of the People's Party, written by Ignatius Donnelly and adopted at the party's convention, 4 July 1892.*

**Question.** 'Americans in the Gilded Age generally admired great wealth.' How far do these sources support this view?

**What the levels want.** Level 3 (11–15): sources used on both sides, but evaluation is generic ("biased"). Level 4 (16–20): detailed use of all four, accurate, explicit links, context used to interpret them. Level 5 (21–25): the sources **evaluated** in context as stronger or weaker evidence, and a judgement on "how far".

**Step 1: sort the sources.** Support: A (wealth is the reward of the fittest and a sign of success, with only a duty to use it well); B (inequality is the price of liberty and progress). Challenge: C (poverty beside progress is an "enigma", a problem); D (fortunes are "stolen"; the nation is near "ruin"). Qualify: A (the rich "die disgraced" if they keep their wealth, so even the admirer of wealth set limits).

**Step 2: evaluate each source in context (the step that reaches Level 5).**
- **A**: written by the man who owned Carnegie Steel, for readers of an elite magazine; it justifies the wealth of its author, so it is self-interested. But Carnegie did give away more than $350 million, so it was not just words, and the article also argued for heavy taxes on inheritances. Strong evidence of how successful businessmen wished to be seen, weak evidence of what ordinary Americans thought.
- **B**: an academic polemic against social reform from a Yale professor; this essay was printed only after his death, but he argued the same in his book of 1883 and in his teaching. Historians disagree about how widely such ideas were held (Hofstadter thought they dominated; Bannister thought their influence exaggerated), so it is evidence of one educated opinion, not of public opinion.
- **C**: a bestselling book (hundreds of thousands of copies within a few years), and George finished second in the New York mayoral election of 1886, ahead of Theodore Roosevelt. That makes C good evidence that criticism had a mass audience. But it comes from a reformer with a remedy to sell.
- **D**: a party platform, deliberately dramatic, written to win votes; "stolen" and "ruin" are exaggerations. But the People's Party's presidential candidate, James B. Weaver, won about one million votes (about 8.5 per cent) and 22 electoral votes in 1892, so it reflected a real movement, mainly of farmers.

**A Level 5 paragraph and judgement.** "Sources A and B support the view that great wealth was admired, but they come from the winners and defenders of the system: a steel-maker and a professor writing to justify inequality as the natural result of competition. Carnegie's own limit, that a man who dies rich 'dies disgraced', shows that even admirers of success disliked hoarded wealth. Sources C and D challenge the view, and they speak for large groups: George's book sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and the People's Party won about a million votes in 1892. On balance the sources support the view only partly. Americans did admire success and the self-made man, as the novels of Horatio Alger and the continued election of pro-business Republicans show, and the reform laws of the 1880s were weak. But admiration was for the individual who earned a fortune, not for concentrated wealth as such, and by 1892 resentment of 'colossal fortunes for a few' was strong enough to found a national party. The word 'generally' overstates it: the Gilded Age admired wealth in the middle and upper classes, and distrusted it among farmers and workers."

*Commentary.* Every source is used accurately and evaluated using its nature, origin, purpose and context; the answer explains where sources support and where they qualify; the judgement answers "how far" directly. Level 5.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "'Government policy was the main reason for rapid industrialisation after 1870.' How far do you agree?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The government helped industry in many ways. In 1862 Congress passed the Homestead Act, which gave 160 acres to settlers, and the Pacific Railway Act, which gave land and money to the Union Pacific and the Central Pacific. The National Banking Act was passed in 1863. The McKinley Tariff of 1890 put the average tax on imports at about half. The government also gave out patents and sent troops against strikers. So government policy was the main reason for rapid industrialisation."

**The level.** Accurate detail, but it is a **list**: it names laws without saying what each did for industry, ignores other causes, and ends with an assertion. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "Government policy mattered most through the railroads. The Pacific Railway Act of 1862 gave the Union Pacific and Central Pacific loans and land, and the transcontinental line was completed in 1869; the railroads then bought the steel, coal and timber that built the industrial economy. Tariffs protected steel until Carnegie's costs fell below Britain's. However, policy only made profitable what resources, immigrants and technology made possible: the government paid for the line but could not supply the iron ore of the Mesabi or the 26 million immigrants. And much of what the government did was to leave business alone. So government policy was a necessary enabler but not the main driver." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: Name four wartime laws or policies of 1861–65 that shaped the economy after the war.
A: The Morrill Tariff and later wartime tariffs; the Homestead Act (1862); the Pacific Railway Act (1862); the Morrill Land-Grant Act (1862); the Legal Tender Acts (greenbacks); the National Banking Act (1863, revised 1864). Any four.
Q: Why did the Homestead Act not settle the Plains as quickly as hoped?
A: 160 acres was too small to make a living on the dry Plains, a large share of claims failed, and much of the best land went to railroads, states and speculators rather than to settlers.
Q: Give three reasons why railroads were important for industrial growth.
A: They bought huge amounts of steel, coal and timber; they created a national market; they created the modern corporation (salaried managers, Wall Street finance, cost accounting); they linked and settled the West.
Q: Who were the "old" and "new" immigrants?
A: The old immigrants came before about 1880 from Britain, Ireland, Germany and Scandinavia; the new immigrants came mainly from southern and eastern Europe (Italians, Poles, Slovaks, Hungarians, Jews from the Russian empire) from the 1880s, in the majority after about 1896.
Q: What were the Exodusters?
A: Black Southerners, tens of thousands, who migrated to Kansas in 1879–80 to escape violence, debt and poverty after the end of Reconstruction.
Q: What was the difference between the tariff, the gold standard and the banking system as government policies?
A: The tariff taxed imports to protect American industry; the gold standard fixed the value of the dollar in gold (Gold Standard Act 1900) and limited the money supply; the banking system (the National Banking Acts) created national banks and currency but had no central bank to rescue banks in a panic.
Q: Which technology mattered more to industrialisation before 1900: steel or electricity?
A: Steel. Cheap Bessemer and open-hearth steel transformed railroads, building and machinery by 1900; electricity's main effect on factories came after 1900 (about 5 per cent of factory power in 1899, over half by 1919).
Q: What was the "Gospel of Wealth"?
A: Andrew Carnegie's article "Wealth" (June 1889), which argued that great fortunes were the reward of competition but that the rich must use their wealth for the public good during their lives.

Q: [10 marks] ★ Explain why Congress passed so many laws to help settlement and business in 1861–65.
A: Indicative content: the Southern states and their senators had left Congress, so the Republicans, who stood for free labour, business and expansion, could pass laws the South had blocked (Homestead Act, Pacific Railway Act, land-grant colleges, higher tariffs); the war needed money (greenbacks, taxes, tariffs, the National Banking Act to sell bonds) and a transcontinental railroad also bound California to the Union; Republican leaders believed that cheap land and railroads would strengthen free labour against the Slave Power. A Level 4 answer (9–10) connects these: war needs and Southern absence gave Republicans the chance, and ideology explained what they did with it. It ends with a judgement on the main reason.
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why immigration was important to industrial growth in the United States after 1870.
A: Indicative content: labour for mills, mines, packinghouses and building sites at low wages, which kept costs down (about 26 million arrived between 1871 and 1920); settlement of the Midwest and Plains with farmers who fed the cities; consumers for goods and housing; skills and entrepreneurs (Carnegie himself was an immigrant from Scotland); employers used ethnic divisions to weaken unions; immigrants lived in growing cities, which created markets and construction. Level 4: connect labour supply to the ability to profit (low costs) and to the markets that their presence created, and judge which mattered most (labour supply).
Q: [10 marks] ★★ Explain why many Americans accepted great inequalities of wealth in the 1880s.
A: Indicative content: the belief in the self-made man (Horatio Alger); the work ethic and the religious belief that success showed virtue; Social Darwinism (Spencer, Sumner) said competition was natural and progress needed it; the Gospel of Wealth said the rich were trustees for society and gave to libraries and colleges; prosperity gave many ordinary people higher living standards, and cheap goods; there was little alternative: governments were small and unions weak. Level 4: connect ideas (justification) with experience (rising living standards and real opportunities for some), and conclude which mattered most.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ 'Natural resources were the main reason for rapid industrialisation after 1870.' How far do you agree?
A: For: coal, iron ore (the Mesabi), oil, timber, copper and fertile farmland, all within reach of rail and water; cheap energy encouraged machinery; the food surplus fed cities and paid for imports; exports of cotton, grain and meat. Against: resources existed before 1870 but were not used; they needed railroads, capital (including from Britain), technology (the Bessemer process; open pits on the Mesabi) and labour (immigrants); other countries had resources but did not industrialise; government policy (tariffs, land grants) made development profitable. Level 5 (17–20) uses a criterion, e.g. "necessary or sufficient?", and judges, e.g., that resources were the necessary condition but not the main reason, since the same resources lay unused for decades before the railroads, capital and technology arrived.
Q: [20 marks] ★★ To what extent did the Civil War cause rapid industrialisation after 1870?
A: For: Republican laws (Homestead, Pacific Railway, land-grant colleges, tariffs, banks); war contracts; removal of the Southern veto; capital and fortunes made; the national bond market. Against: Cochran's argument that manufacturing growth slowed in the 1860s; the war's destruction in the South; the key developments (steel, oil, electricity, mass immigration) came after 1870 and depended on other causes (technology, resources, immigration); other countries (Germany, Japan) industrialised without such a war. Level 5: judges that the war shaped the policy framework and the political conditions more than it created the boom itself, so it was an important enabling cause but not the main driver.
Q: [15 marks] ★★ Read Sources A and B (in the box above). Compare these two sources as evidence about American attitudes to immigration in the 1880s.
A: Similarities: both treat immigration as something about which the nation decides; both come from the early 1880s and show that Americans were thinking about who should enter. Differences: A welcomes the poor and the homeless (an ideal of asylum and opportunity); B excludes a group by law (an act of restriction). A is a poem, an appeal to feeling, and written to raise money for the Statue of Liberty; B is a statute, which reflects political pressure from the Pacific coast, where white workers feared Chinese competition. Explanation: they are not contradictory; the poem expresses an ideal about European immigrants, the statute a racial exclusion of Asians. Level 4 explains the differences through nature and purpose and context and judges which is better evidence of actual policy (B) and of the ideals Americans cherished (A).
Q: [25 marks] ★★★ Read all of the sources (in the box above). How far do these sources agree that Americans welcomed immigrants in the late nineteenth century?
H: Decide which sources welcome immigrants (A) and which restrict or criticise them (B, C, D).
H: Use your own knowledge of the numbers of immigrants admitted: how many were turned away?
H: Evaluate each source by its nature and purpose: a poem, a law, a magazine article, a labour law.
A: Support: A (welcome and shelter for the poor); your own knowledge (about 26 million arrived between 1871 and 1920 and the great majority of Europeans who reached Ellis Island were admitted; employers actively recruited workers). Challenge: B (the Chinese excluded by law), C (a leading economist and former census chief describing southern and eastern Europeans with contempt, expressing the prejudice behind the later restriction movement), D (a law to stop employers from bringing in contract labour, which reflected union fears about wages). Evaluation with knowledge: A is a fundraising poem, with its famous lines added to the statue only in 1903, so it expresses an ideal and little about 1883 policy; B is a law and so the strongest evidence of official policy, but it concerns one group; C is one author's opinion, albeit influential (he also headed the Massachusetts Institute of Technology), and published during the depression that followed the Panic of 1893, when immigrants were a target for blame; D protected workers already in the country and did not stop free immigrants. Level 5 judges how far: e.g. Americans welcomed immigrants as workers and as an ideal, but not all immigrants, and not always as neighbours or competitors, so the sources agree only to a limited extent.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'Government support, not lack of regulation, was the key to rapid industrialisation.' How far do you agree?
H: Separate "support" (land, loans, tariffs, patents, troops) from "lack of regulation" (no income tax, no central bank, no controls on mergers).
H: Ask which one the railroads, the steelmakers and the oil companies depended on most.
A: For: land grants and loans built the transcontinentals; protective tariffs allowed American steel and textiles to develop; patents rewarded invention; the national banks supplied currency; troops and courts protected property against strikes. Against: businesses grew largest where they were least regulated (Standard Oil, Carnegie Steel, Wall Street); freedom to combine allowed economies of scale and falling prices; weak regulation let firms raise capital from foreign investors; resources, population and technology would have driven growth in any case. Level 5 distinguishes the two, uses a criterion (which one the leading industries could not have grown without) and judges, e.g., that support was essential to railroads, regulation's absence to the trusts, so both matter, with support the earlier and more necessary.

:::try Stretch for A*
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ Alfred Chandler, in *The Visible Hand* (1977), argued that modern business enterprise, run by salaried managers, replaced the "invisible hand" of the market, and that the railroads began it. How far does the evidence of 1865–1900 support this view as an explanation of rapid industrialisation?
H: List what the railroads pioneered (management hierarchies, cost accounting, Wall Street finance) and which other industries copied them.
H: Then ask whether managers caused growth or were a response to growth, and whether firms also grew by combination and financial deals.
A: Support: the railroads were the first firms too big for an owner to run; they invented divisions, timetables and costs per ton-mile; Carnegie's cost accounting and Standard Oil's integrated structure copy them; the new firms reached national markets with lower costs. Challenge: Chandler explains the structure of firms more than the causes of growth (resources, capital, immigrants); White shows the railroads' finance was dominated by speculation and political favours; the trusts grew by buying competitors and by rebates (secret discounts the railroads gave to the biggest customers), as well as by efficiency; managerial firms were a result of the demand created by the market. A* judgement: Chandler gives a powerful explanation of how big business worked and why it was efficient, but the railroads' main effect on industrialisation was to create the national market and demand, with management as the way firms coped with it.
Q: [20 marks] ★★★ 'The transcontinental railroads were built too early and at too great a cost to the public to be called a cause of industrial growth.' Evaluate this view with reference to Richard White's *Railroaded* (2011).
H: What does White say about the finances of the transcontinentals and the gap between building and traffic?
H: Distinguish the effect of the *construction* of the lines (orders for steel, jobs) from the effect of the *operation* of the lines (a national market).
A: Support: the lines were built far in advance of traffic, with land grants and loans, enriching insiders through construction companies such as the Crédit Mobilier; many went bankrupt in the depressions of 1873 and 1893, costing investors and the public; the government gave more than it received. Challenge: even overbuilt lines pulled the Plains and Mountain West into the national economy, ordered vast amounts of steel and carried grain and cattle; the fact that investors lost money does not mean the economy lost, since the lines remained; later railroads in the 1880s followed real traffic. A* judgement: White is right that the transcontinentals were financial adventures for their owners, but the ordinary railroad network of the East and Midwest, which was built to carry traffic, was the real engine of industrialisation; the transcontinentals mattered for the West's settlement more than for industry.
