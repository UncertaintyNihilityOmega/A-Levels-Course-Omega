:::why
In 1853 Japan had no steamships, no railways and a government that had refused to deal with the outside world for over two hundred years. In 1905 it destroyed a Russian fleet, and in 1919 it sat among the five great powers at the Paris Peace Conference. No other country outside Europe and North America did anything like it in this period. Explaining how is a lesson in the central skill of this course: telling apart the causes that mattered most (fear of the West, a strong state, a ready-made base of skills) from the events that simply show the result (victories, treaties, recognition). It also explains why the map of world power looked so different by 1918, and why other Asian peoples noticed.
:::

## Japan before 1853: a country closed to the world

To see how large the change was, you need to know what Japan was in 1853.

For over two hundred years Japan had been ruled by the **Tokugawa shoguns** from Edo (today's Tokyo). The emperor lived in Kyoto, honoured but without real power. The shogun ruled directly over about a quarter of the land and indirectly over some 260 **domains** (*han*), each held by a lord (*daimyo*) with his own samurai warriors and his own taxes.

:::def Shogun
The hereditary military ruler of Japan from 1603 to 1868, who governed in the name of the emperor. The shogun's government is called the **shogunate** or *bakufu* (tent government).
:::

Society was ordered by status: samurai at the top, then peasants, artisans and merchants. Samurai were about 5–6 per cent of the people. They lived on fixed stipends paid in rice, and they were the officials and soldiers of the system. Merchants were officially at the bottom, but they were the richest group in towns such as Osaka, and they lent money to samurai who were falling into debt. This is one reason the system was under strain well before foreign ships arrived: peasant uprisings over taxes were frequent, and the great famine of the 1830s showed how weak the government's response could be.

The Tokugawa had also shut the country. From the 1630s the policy called ***sakoku*** ("closed country") forbade Japanese to leave, banned Christianity and limited contact with outsiders. Only the Dutch (confined to the small island of Dejima at Nagasaki) and the Chinese could trade, and a little contact came through Korea and the Ryukyu Islands.

:::def Sakoku
The Tokugawa policy of strictly limiting foreign contact, from the 1630s to 1853–54. Only Dutch and Chinese merchants could trade, at Nagasaki. It did not mean Japan knew nothing of the world: "Dutch learning" (*rangaku*) kept educated Japanese informed of Western science and weapons.
:::

Two things made the arrival of Western warships in 1853 a crisis rather than an annoyance. First, the Japanese knew what had happened to China. Britain's victory in the First Opium War (1839–42) and the Treaty of Nanjing, which opened Chinese ports and gave Britain Hong Kong, had been reported by the Dutch and Chinese traders. Second, the West's technology had moved on: the new steam-powered warships of the 1840s and 1850s could sail against wind and tide, and no Japanese gun on shore could stop them.

## Relations with the western powers

### Commodore Perry's mission, 1853–54

The United States wanted Japan for three reasons. American whaling ships worked the north Pacific and wanted ports for water and supplies, and Japan treated shipwrecked sailors harshly. After the Mexican War (1846–48) the USA reached the Pacific coast, with the gold of California to export, and it wanted a steamship route to China. Steamships burned coal and needed coaling stations, and Japan had coal. These ideas fitted the American belief in its "manifest destiny" to spread across the continent and beyond.

President Millard Fillmore chose **Commodore Matthew C. Perry** to lead a squadron to Japan. Perry's plan was to impress the Japanese by force and display. On **8 July 1853** his four ships, two of them steam-powered and painted black (the Japanese called them the "black ships"), anchored off Uraga in Edo Bay, close to the capital. He refused to deal with local officials, insisted on meeting a senior one, and threatened to land and deliver the President's letter in person. A week later, on 14 July, the letter was received at nearby Kurihama. Perry said he would return for the answer.

**Source A** is the heart of the letter he carried.

:::note Source A: the letter that Perry carried
I send you this public letter by Commodore Matthew C. Perry, an officer of the highest rank in the navy of the United States, and commander of the squadron now visiting your imperial majesty's dominions. … I have no other object in sending him to Japan but to propose to your imperial majesty that the United States and Japan should live in friendship and have commercial intercourse with each other. … Our steamships can go from California to Japan in eighteen days. … We know that the ancient laws of your imperial majesty's government do not allow of foreign trade, except with the Chinese and the Dutch; but as the state of the world changes and new governments are formed, it seems to be wise, from time to time, to make new laws.

*Shortened from the letter of President Millard Fillmore to the Emperor of Japan, 13 November 1852, carried by Commodore Perry and received at Kurihama on 14 July 1853.*
:::

Read it carefully. The letter is polite and talks of friendship and trade. It says nothing about force. But the ships outside told the Japanese what lay behind the courtesy, and the reference to "eighteen days" shows why the USA wanted Japan: it was the next stop on the way to China.

The shogunate was in a bind. It had no navy that could fight, but it also knew the Emperor in Kyoto, and many lords, hated the idea of giving in. The senior minister, Abe Masahiro, did something new: he asked all the daimyo for their opinions. This was the first time in over two hundred years that the shogunate had publicly sought advice, and it weakened its claim to be the unquestioned ruler. When Perry came back in February 1854 with a larger squadron, the shogunate gave way. The **Treaty of Kanagawa** (31 March 1854) had these main terms:
- The ports of **Shimoda** and **Hakodate** were opened to American ships for coal, wood, water and provisions.
- Shipwrecked American sailors were to be treated kindly.
- The USA could appoint a consul at Shimoda.
- A "most-favoured-nation" clause gave the USA any privileges that Japan later gave to other countries.

:::warn Kanagawa was not a trade treaty
Students often write that Perry "opened Japan to trade" in 1854. The Treaty of Kanagawa opened two ports for supplies and promised help to shipwrecked sailors. Trade, treaty ports and the extraterritorial rights came four years later with the Harris Treaty. Britain, Russia and the Netherlands made similar agreements with Japan in 1854–55, but the real breakthrough was 1858.
:::

### The unequal treaties

The American consul, **Townsend Harris**, arrived at Shimoda in 1856 and spent two years persuading the shogunate to sign a full commercial treaty. He used the example of China. Britain and France, he warned, had just forced new terms on the Chinese in the Second Opium War, and would come to Japan next; better to sign a fairer treaty with the USA now. On **29 July 1858** the Treaty of Amity and Commerce (the Harris Treaty) was signed. Britain, France, Russia and the Netherlands signed similar treaties within weeks (together they are called the **Ansei treaties**).

The main terms:
- Foreign trade at **treaty ports**: Kanagawa (soon moved to Yokohama), Nagasaki and Hakodate at once; Niigata and Hyogo (Kobe) later; Edo and Osaka were opened to foreign residence and trade.
- **Extraterritoriality**: foreigners accused of crimes were tried by their own consul under their own law, not by Japanese judges.
- **Fixed tariffs**: duties on imports were set by treaty at low rates (about 5 per cent on most goods by 1866) and Japan could not change them without the foreign powers' agreement.
- A **most-favoured-nation** clause for each power.

:::def Unequal treaties
Treaties forced on Asian states by Western powers, in which the Western side gained privileges (such as extraterritoriality, low fixed tariffs and treaty ports) that the other side did not receive in return. For Japan these were the treaties of 1858 and the agreements that followed.
:::

:::def Extraterritoriality
The right of foreigners in a country to be tried under their own law by their own officials instead of by the local courts. Japanese leaders regarded it as an insult to national independence as well as an obstacle to controlling foreigners.
:::

The treaties were "unequal" in three ways. Only the Western side had the legal protection of extraterritoriality. Only the Western side was told what tariffs it paid. And Japan had no right to leave: the most-favoured-nation clauses meant that every concession to one power was extended to all. Japan's leaders felt the humiliation, and they also felt the practical damage. Foreign cheap cotton goods undercut Japanese hand-spinners, and because gold was worth far less in Japan relative to silver (about five to one, against about fifteen to one abroad), foreign traders bought gold and shipped it out. The shogunate cut the gold content of its coins to stop the drain, and prices rose sharply in the 1860s, hurting poor townspeople and samurai on fixed stipends.

Worse for the shogunate, the senior minister Ii Naosuke signed the 1858 treaty without the Emperor's consent, which was refused. Anti-foreign samurai under the slogan ***sonnō jōi*** ("revere the emperor, expel the barbarians") now attacked both foreigners and the shogunate. Ii was assassinated outside Edo Castle in March 1860. The lesson learned by the domains that fought the foreigners was more important still:
- In **1863** a British naval force bombarded Kagoshima, the capital of Satsuma, after Satsuma retainers had killed a British merchant, Charles Richardson, at Namamugi in September 1862.
- In **1863–64** the Chōshū domain fired on foreign ships in the Shimonoseki Strait, and in September 1864 a fleet of British, French, Dutch and American ships destroyed its forts.

Both domains drew the same conclusion: the foreigners could not be expelled by swords. The only way to equality was to learn their methods. Satsuma and Chōshū began to buy modern rifles and ships, and in 1866 they made a secret alliance against the shogunate.

:::key Japan's relations with the West, 1853–68
1. **Pressure by force and display** (Perry, 1853–54) from a USA that wanted coal, supplies and a route to China.
2. **Unequal treaties** (1858) that took away control of tariffs and of law on Japanese soil.
3. **Humiliation and division**: the shogunate looked weak, the Emperor became a rallying point, and anti-foreign anger turned against the government.
4. **A change of tactics**: the strongest domains learned from defeat that Japan must modernise to win equality.
:::

## The Meiji Restoration, 1868

The last shogun, **Tokugawa Yoshinobu**, gave up his powers to the Emperor in November 1867, hoping to keep a leading place in a new council of lords. His opponents, the samurai leaders of Satsuma and Chōshū such as Saigō Takamori and Ōkubo Toshimichi, would not accept that. On **3 January 1868** they seized the Imperial Palace in Kyoto and proclaimed the **Restoration of imperial rule**. A short civil war, the **Boshin War**, followed: the shogun's forces were beaten at Toba–Fushimi (27–30 January 1868), Edo surrendered without a fight in April, and the last resistance ended at Hakodate in June 1869.

The new government governed in the name of the Emperor, the fifteen-year-old Mutsuhito, who was given the reign-name **Meiji** ("enlightened rule"). The capital moved from Kyoto to Edo, renamed Tokyo ("eastern capital"). Historians still call the 1868 change the "Meiji Restoration" although it was not a return to the past: the men who made it were low-ranking samurai who knew they had to change.

:::def Meiji Restoration
The overthrow of the Tokugawa shogunate in January 1868 and the return of governing power, in name, to the Emperor Meiji (reigned 1867–1912). It began a period of rapid political, social and economic change, often described as a "revolution from above" because the new rulers were themselves samurai leaders.
:::

The **Meiji oligarchs** were a small group of former samurai from Satsuma and Chōshū (and a few court nobles such as Iwakura Tomomi) who ran Japan in the emperor's name until the 1890s: Ōkubo Toshimichi, Kido Takayoshi, Itō Hirobumi, Yamagata Aritomo, Iwakura Tomomi and others. They were practical men who had seen foreign power at first hand and had no time for the idea of expelling the foreigners.

## Why Japan modernised so quickly

**Source B** is one of the first public statements of the new government.

:::note Source B: the Charter Oath
Deliberative assemblies shall be widely established and all matters decided by open discussion. All classes, high and low, shall be united in vigorously carrying out the administration of affairs of state. The common people, no less than the civil and military officials, shall all be allowed to pursue their own calling so that there may be no discontent. Evil customs of the past shall be broken off and everything based upon the just laws of Nature. Knowledge shall be sought throughout the world so as to strengthen the foundation of imperial rule.

*The Charter Oath, proclaimed in the name of the Emperor Meiji at Kyoto, 6 April 1868; English translation after W. W. McLaren, as quoted in De Bary and others, Sources of Japanese Tradition.*
:::

The Charter Oath is vague, but it is clear about direction: assemblies, an end to old customs, and above all knowledge from "throughout the world". Its last line also says why: to "strengthen the foundation of imperial rule". The new rulers wanted what they called ***fukoku kyōhei*** ("rich country, strong army"), because only a rich and strong Japan could free itself from the unequal treaties.

:::def Fukoku kyōhei
"Rich country, strong army": the slogan of the Meiji government. It said that national wealth (factories, railways, trade) and military strength went together, and that both were needed to keep Japan independent.
:::

### Political and social change

The government first had to make a single state out of hundreds of domains.
- In 1869 the lords of the four strongest domains gave their lands and registers to the Emperor, and the others followed. In **August 1871** the domains were abolished altogether and replaced by prefectures run by governors sent from Tokyo. The old lords received pensions.
- The four-class status system was ended. All classes could take a surname, choose their work and live where they wished.
- The samurai were the problem. Their stipends took roughly a third of the government's spending, and they were no longer the only soldiers. The state turned their pay into government bonds in 1876 and in the same year banned the wearing of swords in public. Many samurai were ruined.
- The **conscription law** of 10 January 1873 made every man liable for army service at the age of twenty, with many exemptions at first. This made a national army of ordinary men, led by officers, and it ended the samurai's monopoly of arms.
- The last great samurai revolt was the **Satsuma Rebellion** (January–September 1877) led by Saigō Takamori, one of the founders of the new government, who had broken with it over what he saw as its betrayal of the warriors. The conscript army won. Saigō died on 24 September 1877. The government's army had proved itself, and no major samurai revolt followed.

The government also moved, slowly, towards representative government because of popular pressure, the "Freedom and People's Rights Movement" of the 1870s and 1880s, and because the oligarchs believed that a constitution would impress the Western powers. In 1881 the Emperor promised a constitution by 1890. **Itō Hirobumi** studied European constitutions (above all Prussia's) in 1882–83 and drafted a constitution that the Emperor "granted" on **11 February 1889**. It set up a **Diet** of two houses, a House of Peers and an elected House of Representatives, but only about 450,000 men (roughly 1 per cent of the population) paid enough tax to vote at first. The Emperor was sacred and sovereign ("a line of Emperors unbroken for ages eternal"), the cabinet answered to him and not to the Diet, and the army and navy ministers had to be serving officers, which later gave the services power over any government. The first Diet met in November 1890.

:::def Meiji Constitution
Japan's constitution of 11 February 1889, in force from 1890. It made the Emperor sovereign and commander of the army and navy, created a two-house Diet with limited powers, and guaranteed some rights "within the limits of law". It was the first written constitution in Asia and gave Japan the look of a modern state.
:::

### The economy and industrialisation

The new government needed money and industry quickly. Its main steps were:
- **Land tax reform (1873).** The old rice tax paid to lords was replaced by a cash tax fixed at 3 per cent of the assessed value of land, paid by the owner (cut to 2.5 per cent in 1877 after peasant riots). The state now had a steady money income, which provided most of its revenue in the 1870s. The cost fell on the peasants.
- **Model industries.** The state built and ran factories, mines and shipyards to teach new methods. The Tomioka silk-reeling mill (1872), with French engineers, is the best known.
- **Infrastructure.** The telegraph (Tokyo–Yokohama, 1869) and the first railway, from Shimbashi in Tokyo to Yokohama (about 29 km, opened on 14 October 1872) were built with British help. A national postal service, a modern currency (the yen, 1871) and banks followed; the Bank of Japan was set up in 1882.
- **Selling to private business.** In the 1880s the state, short of money, sold most of its factories at low prices to well-connected businessmen. From these grew the great business groups called **zaibatsu**: Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo and Yasuda. They gave Japan large, well-financed firms in shipping, mining, banking and manufacturing.
- **Textiles first, heavy industry later.** Silk was the main export, and cotton spinning grew rapidly from the 1880s with machines and Indian raw cotton. Iron and steel came later: the state's Yawata Steel Works began production in 1901, paid for in part with the Chinese indemnity of 1895.

:::def Zaibatsu
The large family-controlled business groups (Mitsui, Mitsubishi, Sumitomo, Yasuda) that came to dominate Japanese banking, shipping, mining and industry. Many began by buying state enterprises cheaply in the 1880s, and they stayed close to the government.
:::

### Western technology and advisers

Japan did not copy blindly. The government hired hundreds of foreign experts (called *oyatoi gaikokujin*, "hired foreigners") to build railways, lighthouses, mines and schools and to train Japanese who would take over. It also sent its own men abroad. The most important journey was the **Iwakura Mission** (23 December 1871 – 13 September 1873), about a hundred officials, students and attendants led by the senior statesman Iwakura Tomomi, with Ōkubo, Kido and Itō among them. They visited the USA and many European states. They hoped to revise the unequal treaties, and failed, because the Western powers would not discuss revision until Japan reformed its laws. But the mission returned with a different lesson: Japan must catch up first. Ōkubo and Itō came back convinced that national strength had to come before political reform, and that Germany, a young state that had built its power quickly, was a better model than Britain or France.

Japan chose its models carefully:
- **Navy:** Britain, the greatest naval power. British advisers trained Japanese officers from 1873 and British yards built the best ships; the battleship *Mikasa*, Tōgō's flagship in 1905, was built at Barrow-in-Furness.
- **Army:** at first France, then (after Prussia's victory over France in 1870–71) Germany. A German officer, Major Jakob Meckel, taught at the Army Staff College from 1885.
- **Law:** French and then German codes.
- **Industry and trade:** Britain and the USA.

### Education

The Meiji leaders saw education as the root of national strength. The **Fundamental Code of Education** (1872) planned a national system of elementary, middle and university schools, open to all children whatever their sex or family background. Attendance was at first low (about 28 per cent in 1873, roughly 40 per cent of boys and 15 per cent of girls), because families resented the fees and the loss of children's work. But compulsory attendance was extended (four years in 1886, six in 1907), and by about 1905 around 95 per cent of children were enrolled. Tokyo University was founded in 1877. Japan thus built up a literate workforce, able to read instructions, serve in a modern army and run machines. It started from a good position: under the Tokugawa, temple schools and domain schools had already made perhaps four in ten boys literate.

Education was also a means of instilling loyalty. **Source C** shows what the state wanted schools to teach.

:::note Source C: the Imperial Rescript on Education
Ye, Our subjects, be filial to your parents, affectionate to your brothers and sisters; as husbands and wives be harmonious, as friends true; bear yourselves in modesty and moderation; extend your benevolence to all; pursue learning and cultivate arts, and thereby develop intellectual faculties and perfect moral powers; furthermore advance public good and promote common interests; always respect the Constitution and observe the laws; should emergency arise, offer yourselves courageously to the State; and thus guard and maintain the prosperity of Our Imperial Throne coeval with heaven and earth.

*From the Imperial Rescript on Education, issued in the name of the Emperor Meiji, 30 October 1890 (23rd year of Meiji), in the standard English translation; shortened. It was read aloud at school ceremonies.*
:::

The Rescript puts together two goals: learning ("pursue learning and cultivate arts") and loyalty ("offer yourselves courageously to the State"). Schools taught reading, arithmetic, science and ethics, and children bowed to the Emperor's portrait. The combination produced soldiers and workers who were both skilled and obedient.

### Military development

The Meiji leaders saw military strength as the guarantee that Japan would never become another China. Their steps were conscription (1873), a General Staff Office on the Prussian model (1878) that reported directly to the Emperor and not to the cabinet, military schools, arsenals and shipyards, and a steady rise in spending on the navy. The Rescript to Soldiers and Sailors (1882) told the forces that their duty was to the Emperor. By 1894 Japan had a well-trained army of conscripts and a modern fleet built in Britain, and the Sino-Japanese War would show it.

### Weighing the reasons for rapid modernisation

Put the steps together and ask why they worked so quickly in Japan, when other Asian states that met the West (China, Korea, the Ottoman Empire) struggled.

| Reason | How it helped | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| **Fear of the West** | The unequal treaties, the example of China, and the bombardments of 1863–64 created urgency and a shared aim: equality | Fear alone did not produce results in other states |
| **A strong, motivated leadership** | The oligarchs were young, practical, willing to destroy their own class privileges (samurai stipends), and ruled with little opposition at first | They also used force and suppressed protest |
| **A ready base** | High literacy, a commercial economy with rich merchants, skilled craftsmen, a tradition of learning from outside (Dutch learning), and an experienced bureaucracy of samurai | Japan was poor in resources: little coal and iron, and pressure on the land |
| **Selective borrowing** | Japan chose the best model in each field and adapted it, with its own officials in control, rather than letting foreigners run its institutions | The hired foreigners were expensive and were replaced as soon as possible |
| **A unifying idea** | Loyalty to the Emperor gave a nation of domains a single identity (Sources B and C) | It also fed an uncritical nationalism |
| **Peace, then limited war** | No major war until 1894 allowed a quarter of a century of building | The government's first priority was always the military |

:::key Why Japan modernised so quickly
1. **Fear of the West** gave urgency and a target: end the unequal treaties and avoid China's fate.
2. **A strong state** under the Meiji oligarchs took the lead, with centralised taxes, conscription and schools.
3. **A ready base** of education, commerce and craft skill meant that new technology could be used at once.
4. **Selective borrowing** copied Western methods without Western control.
5. **Nationalism**, centred on the Emperor, made sacrifice acceptable.
The best answers rank these. A good judgement is that fear of the West was the *trigger* and the strong state was the *engine*, while the ready base explains why it worked in Japan and not elsewhere.
:::

:::exam Use the question word
"Why did Japan modernise?" and "How far was modernisation driven by fear of the West?" ask for different answers. The first needs several causes connected. The second needs you to test fear against the others (the strong state, the ready base) and judge. Write the judgement first in your plan.
:::

## International recognition: revising the treaties and Korea

By the 1880s Japan had a navy, an army and a constitution. Recognition as an equal by the Western powers was harder to win, and the Japanese pursued it in two ways: by persuasion (a campaign to revise the unequal treaties) and by showing it could behave as a great power does, with an empire.

### Revising the unequal treaties

The Iwakura Mission's failure in 1872 showed that the powers wanted proof that Japan had Western-style law and courts. The government spent twenty years trying. In the 1880s the foreign minister, Inoue Kaoru, built a ballroom, the Rokumeikan, where Japanese officials and foreigners danced in European dress, and offered to employ foreign judges. The idea of foreign judges caused an outcry and he resigned in 1887. In 1886 the British steamer *Normanton* sank off the coast of Japan; the British captain and crew took the lifeboats and 25 Japanese passengers drowned. The British consular court acquitted the captain, and public anger showed how hated extraterritoriality had become.

The breakthrough came when Britain, the most important trading partner, changed its mind. With a new constitution, new law codes (French and German style) and, by 1894, an army and navy clearly capable of fighting, Japan seemed a reliable partner. Britain was also worried about Russia and wanted a friend in East Asia. On **16 July 1894**, just before the war with China, Japan and Britain signed a treaty by which British extraterritorial rights would end in five years, in 1899, and the other powers followed. Control of tariffs was regained only in **1911**, when the new treaties took effect (17 July), fifty-three years after the Harris Treaty. Treaty revision took the whole Meiji era and was the single most important objective of Japanese foreign policy.

### Korea and the war with China, 1876–95

Japan's attention turned early to Korea, the peninsula only about 200 km across the sea from Japan. The Japanese saw it as a dagger pointed at their own country: a weak state that could fall to a stronger power, above all Russia, and then threaten their security. Korea was also traditionally a tributary of China, whose rulers insisted that they were its overlord.

In 1875 a Japanese gunboat provoked an incident at Ganghwa island. In February 1876, Japan used it to impose the **Treaty of Ganghwa**, copying what Perry had done to Japan twenty-two years before: it opened three Korean ports (Busan, then Wonsan and Incheon) to Japanese trade, gave Japanese extraterritorial rights, and described Korea as an independent state, meaning independent of China. Japan was now using the methods of the West on a neighbour.

Competition with China followed. In 1882 Korean soldiers rose against Japanese influence in the Imo Incident and China sent troops. In 1884 a pro-Japanese group in Seoul took power in a coup, and Chinese troops put it down. Japan and China then agreed at Tientsin (1885) to withdraw their troops and to tell each other before sending them again. In 1894 a peasant uprising, the Donghak Rebellion, led the Korean king to ask for Chinese help. China sent troops, Japan sent a larger force, and in July 1894 the Japanese took the royal palace in Seoul and sank a Chinese transport ship. War was declared on **1 August 1894**.

The **Sino-Japanese War** (1894–95) astonished the world. China was far larger, but its forces were divided and badly led. Japan won at Pyongyang (15–16 September 1894), destroyed much of the Chinese fleet at the Battle of the Yalu (17 September), took Port Arthur in November and the naval base of Weihaiwei in February 1895. The **Treaty of Shimonoseki** (17 April 1895) gave Japan:
- China's recognition of the "full and complete independence and autonomy" of Korea (Article 1);
- the island of **Taiwan** (Formosa), the Pescadores and the **Liaodong peninsula**, with Port Arthur;
- an indemnity of 200 million silver taels (a very large sum, several times Japan's yearly revenue); and
- new trading rights in China.

Six days later, on **23 April 1895**, Russia, Germany and France (the **Triple Intervention**) told Japan to give Liaodong back, on the grounds that its possession would threaten Beijing and the peace of the Far East. Russia, which had its own plans for Manchuria, led the move. Japan had no way to resist three great powers, and gave it up in return for an extra 30 million taels. In 1898 Russia took a lease on Port Arthur for itself. The humiliation was never forgotten, and it taught the Japanese two lessons: that they needed a still stronger navy, and that sooner or later they would face Russia.

:::key The results of the war with China
1. **Korea** was declared independent of China, leaving Japan and Russia as rivals for influence.
2. **Taiwan** became Japan's first colony.
3. **An indemnity** paid for the navy, Yawata Steel and Japan's adoption of the gold standard.
4. **Prestige**: the Western powers took Japan seriously for the first time, and that helped it gain the treaty revision of 1894.
5. **A Russian enemy**: the Triple Intervention and Russia's lease of Port Arthur (1898) made Russia the main threat.
:::

By 1900 Japan had also joined the powers in a joint action. During the **Boxer Rising** in China, Japan sent the largest contingent (about 20,000 men) of the international army that relieved the foreign legations in Beijing in August 1900. Russia occupied Manchuria in the same crisis and did not leave.

## The Anglo-Japanese Alliance, 1902

**Source D** is the central article of the treaty signed in London on 30 January 1902 by Lord Lansdowne, the British Foreign Secretary, and Hayashi Tadasu, the Japanese minister in London.

:::note Source D: the Anglo-Japanese Alliance
The High Contracting Parties, having mutually recognised the independence of China and Korea, declare themselves to be entirely uninfluenced by aggressive tendencies in either country, having in view, however, their special interests, of which those of Great Britain relate principally to China, whilst Japan, in addition to the interests which she possesses in China, is interested in a peculiar degree, politically as well as commercially and industrially in Korea, the High Contracting Parties recognise that it will be admissible for either of them to take such measures as may be indispensable in order to safeguard those interests if threatened either by the aggressive action of any other Power, or by disturbances arising in China or Korea.

*Adapted from Article 1 of the Anglo-Japanese Agreement, signed in London, 30 January 1902; the closing words, about protecting the lives and property of subjects, are left out.*
:::

### Why Britain signed

By 1900 Britain's leaders feared that they could no longer protect the empire alone.
- **Overstretch.** The Boer War (1899–1902) had shown how thin British forces were, and the humiliation of having to send nearly half a million men to fight farmers had damaged confidence. Britain's navy was supposed to match the next two largest fleets combined (the Two-Power Standard), but those fleets were now French and Russian, with Germany's building up as well.
- **Russia in the Far East.** After the Boxer Rising, Russia kept its troops in Manchuria and pushed into Korea, threatening British trade in China and, in the long term, India. Britain could not stop it alone.
- **Germany had said no.** In 1898–1901 Britain had made several attempts at an agreement with Germany, which wanted Britain to join the Triple Alliance. These failed, partly because Germany would not help against Russia in Asia.
- **Naval economy.** An alliance with Japan, whose navy was the strongest in East Asia after Britain's, meant that Britain could pull battleships back to home waters, a vital step as the German fleet grew.

### Why Japan signed

- **Security against Russia.** The Triple Intervention and the occupation of Manchuria showed that Russia would not accept a Japan on the mainland. Some Japanese leaders, led by Itō Hirobumi, wanted a deal with Russia first (*Man-Kan kōkan*, "Manchuria for Korea") and went to St Petersburg in late 1901, but Russia would not give up its position. The prime minister Katsura Tarō and the foreign minister Komura Jutarō chose Britain.
- **Prestige.** An alliance with the world's strongest empire, as an equal, was a statement about Japan's status that no other Asian state had gained.
- **Insurance.** The alliance's terms (Articles 2 and 3) said that if one party went to war with a single enemy over its interests in China or Korea, the other would remain neutral. If a second power joined that enemy, the ally would come to its help. So if Japan fought Russia, France (Russia's ally) would be deterred from joining in, since that would bring Britain in on Japan's side. That is exactly what happened in 1904–05.

### The consequences

- **For Japan:** it could now take on Russia without risking a repeat of 1895. It also gained British loans and naval intelligence in the war. The alliance was renewed in 1905, when it was extended to cover India and to make each side defend the other against attack from one power, and again in 1911. It ended after the Washington Conference, in 1923.
- **For Britain:** it freed its navy for the North Sea, and gave it a friendly power in East Asia, which later made it easier to settle disputes with France and Russia (the Entente Cordiale of 1904 and the Convention of 1907: see [Imperialism and world powers (2)](#hist.int.empire.2)).
- **For Russia and Europe:** Russia was warned that it could not use force in Asia without a war in which Japan had British support, but it did not heed the warning. France and Russia made a declaration of their own in March 1902 about the alliance and the Far East.
- **For the world:** the treaty was the first alliance between a European great power and an Asian state on terms of equality, and it was widely seen as the end of the idea that only white nations were great powers.

:::def Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902)
A treaty between Britain and Japan, signed in London on 30 January 1902. Each side recognised the other's special interests in China (and in Korea for Japan), promised neutrality if the other fought one power over them, and promised help if two or more powers attacked. It ended Britain's "splendid isolation" and prepared Japan's war against Russia.
:::

:::warn The alliance did not make Britain fight in 1904
Students often write that Britain "helped Japan win the Russo-Japanese War". Britain did not fight. The alliance bound it to neutrality unless a second power joined Russia, and none did. Britain's help was indirect: British yards had built Japan's best warships, Britain would not let the Russian Baltic Fleet coal at its ports (so the fleet depended on German coal ships), it passed on intelligence and its bankers helped Japan to borrow in London.
:::

## The Russo-Japanese War, 1904–05

### Causes

Russia and Japan both wanted Manchuria and Korea. Russia, after leasing Port Arthur from China in 1898, was building the **Trans-Siberian Railway** and its branch across Manchuria to Port Arthur, and it kept its troops in Manchuria after the Boxer Rising. It held a timber concession on the Yalu River on the Korean border (1903). Japan saw this as a threat to Korea and therefore to itself. Talks between August 1903 and January 1904 failed: Japan offered to recognise Russian interests in Manchuria in exchange for Russia recognising Japan's in Korea; Russia refused to limit its freedom in Manchuria and wanted a neutral zone in northern Korea. On 6 February 1904 Japan broke off relations.

### The course of the war

- **8–9 February 1904.** Without a declaration, Japanese destroyers made a night torpedo attack on the Russian Pacific Fleet at **Port Arthur**. War was declared on 10 February. The Russian fleet was crippled and blockaded; Japan controlled the sea around Korea and could land armies.
- **Spring 1904.** Japanese armies crossed the Yalu River (1 May) and cut off Port Arthur from the rest of Manchuria. The siege that followed was bloody, and General Nogi's troops took 203 Metre Hill in December and the fortress surrendered on **2 January 1905**.
- **February–March 1905.** The **Battle of Mukden** (20 February – 10 March) was the largest land battle fought to that date; the Russian army retreated north. Japan won, but at a very heavy cost, and its reserves of men and money were almost spent.
- **27–28 May 1905.** The Russian Baltic Fleet, sent from the Baltic in October 1904 on a voyage of about 18,000 nautical miles, was destroyed by Admiral **Tōgō Heihachirō** at the **Battle of Tsushima** in the strait between Korea and Japan. Of the Russian battleships and coastal battleships, all eleven were lost (seven sunk, four captured), more than 5,000 Russians were killed and about 6,000 captured; the Japanese lost three torpedo boats and 117 men.

### Why Japan won

:::key Reasons for Japan's victory
1. **Naval strength and tactics.** Japan's modern fleet, many ships British-built, was well trained, and Tōgō concentrated it. The surprise attack on Port Arthur, and the control of the sea, decided where the war would be fought.
2. **A modern, well-trained army** with a German-style General Staff, and well-planned campaigns. The soldiers were disciplined and fought bravely, though at heavy cost (sieges and Mukden).
3. **The Anglo-Japanese Alliance.** It kept France out, gave Japan British support in finance and intelligence, and made it hard for the Russian fleet to coal in neutral ports.
4. **Finance.** Japan raised war loans in London and New York, helped by bankers such as Jacob Schiff of Kuhn, Loeb, who disliked Russia's treatment of Jews. Without them Japan's small gold reserves could not have paid for the war.
5. **Russian weaknesses.** The fighting was 8,000 km from Russia's heartland, supplied by one railway line, single-track and, until 1904, with a gap round Lake Baikal. Russia's command was divided (the viceroy, Alekseyev, and General Kuropatkin quarrelled), its generals underrated Japan, and the Baltic Fleet had to travel round the world.
6. **Revolution at home.** After Bloody Sunday (22 January 1905, New Style) strikes and mutinies (including the battleship *Potemkin* in June) forced the Tsar to look for peace.
:::

The strongest answers add an assessment. Many historians stress Russian weakness: Russia could have gone on, as it had the larger resources, but its government lost the will. Yet Japan's victory was no accident either. Its leadership had spent forty years on the army and navy, and without the Meiji modernisation and the alliance, it would have been impossible.

### The peace of Portsmouth

Japan was exhausted, and asked President **Theodore Roosevelt** to mediate. The talks were held at Portsmouth, New Hampshire, and the treaty was signed on **5 September 1905**. Its terms:
- Russia recognised that Japan had "paramount political, military and economical interests" in Korea (Article 2).
- Russia transferred its lease of Port Arthur and the Liaodong peninsula and the southern branch of the Manchurian railway to Japan, and withdrew from southern Manchuria.
- Russia gave Japan the southern half of Sakhalin (the part south of the 50th parallel), with fishing rights.
- There was **no indemnity**.

The last point angered the Japanese public. They had been told of victory after victory, and had borne high taxes and the loss of about 80,000–86,000 men (estimates of the dead, including disease, vary; the Russian figure was about 43,000–71,000). Riots broke out in Tokyo on 5 September, at Hibiya Park, and martial law was declared. Roosevelt received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1906. The Japanese public's reaction is a reminder that government and people might see the same event differently.

### The impact on Korea and on international relations

- **Korea.** Japan acted at once. A treaty of 17 November 1905 made Korea a Japanese **protectorate**, with a Japanese Resident-General (Itō Hirobumi was the first) in charge of its foreign relations. In 1907 the Korean emperor Gojong was forced to abdicate after sending secret envoys to the Hague peace conference. Itō was assassinated by the Korean nationalist An Jung-geun at Harbin on 26 October 1909, and Japan **annexed** Korea outright with a treaty signed on **22 August 1910** (announced on 29 August). Korea remained a Japanese colony until 1945.
- **Russia.** Defeat in the Far East turned Russian attention back to Europe and the Balkans. This helped to make the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 possible, and Russia made agreements with Japan in 1907 and 1910 dividing Manchuria into spheres of influence.
- **Britain and the USA.** Britain valued Japan as an ally. The USA, which had welcomed Japanese victory at first, became wary of a Pacific rival. In July 1905 the American Secretary of War, William Taft, and the Japanese prime minister Katsura Tarō exchanged a memorandum (the Taft–Katsura memorandum) in which, according to the record, the USA accepted Japanese control of Korea and Japan said it had no designs on the Philippines. Relations soured over Japanese immigration to California (the schoolchildren segregation crisis of 1906 and the "Gentlemen's Agreement" of 1907–08). President Roosevelt sent the Great White Fleet round the world in 1907–09, with a call at Yokohama in October 1908; see [Imperialism and world powers (4)](#hist.int.empire.4).
- **Europe and "the Yellow Peril".** Kaiser Wilhelm II had long used the phrase "Yellow Peril" for a feared Asian threat. After 1905 many European and American leaders feared that a modern Asian power could challenge white rule.
- **Asia and the colonial world.** The news was seen as proof that a non-European country could beat a great power by modernising. Nationalists in China, India, Vietnam, Egypt and Turkey, among others, said it gave them hope. In the next years many Chinese students went to study in Japan, including Sun Yat-sen's circle. The effect on actual colonial rule was less: Japan's own conduct in Korea quickly showed that it was an imperial power too.

:::key The impact of 1904–05 on international relations
1. **Japan** became a recognised great power in East Asia, with a protectorate over Korea and a foothold in Manchuria.
2. **Russia** was weakened in Asia and turned towards Europe, with consequences for the Balkans and the alliance system.
3. **Britain and Japan** strengthened their alliance (renewed 1905), while the **USA** began to see Japan as a possible rival.
4. **The idea of white superiority** was shaken, which inspired Asian nationalists, and alarmed Western colonial powers.
:::

## Japan in the First World War and its position by 1918

### Why Japan entered the war

Japan was bound by no treaty to fight in Europe, and the Anglo-Japanese Alliance covered only East Asia and India. Britain at first asked for only limited help, against German armed merchant ships. The Japanese government under **Ōkuma Shigenobu**, with **Katō Takaaki** as foreign minister, saw an opportunity: Germany held the leased territory of **Kiaochow** (Qingdao, or Tsingtao) in China and islands in the Pacific, and Europe was at war. On 15 August 1914 Japan sent Germany an ultimatum to withdraw its warships and hand over Kiaochow. When it was not answered, Japan declared war on **23 August 1914**.

### What Japan did

- **Tsingtao.** A Japanese force of some 23,000 men, with a small British contingent, besieged the German base (27 August to 7 November 1914), which fell after 72 days.
- **The Pacific.** The Japanese navy seized the German-held Marshall, Caroline and Mariana Islands (except American Guam) in October 1914, without a fight.
- **Escort duties.** From 1914 the navy patrolled the Pacific and Indian Oceans against German raiders. In April 1917, after Britain asked for help against the U-boats, a Japanese squadron of eight destroyers reached Malta (13 April); the force grew to 17 ships and escorted troop ships and merchant ships in the Mediterranean.
- **Industry and trade.** Cut off from European suppliers, Asia bought from Japan. The war boom diversified Japanese industry (steel, shipbuilding, chemicals), exports roughly quadrupled between 1913 and 1918, and Japan became a creditor for the first time. Inflation brought rice riots in August 1918.
- **Siberia.** In 1918 Britain, France and the USA sent troops to north Russia and Siberia, partly to help the Czech Legion and to oppose the Bolsheviks. The USA suggested that each should send only about 7,000 men. Japan sent about 70,000, and stayed until October 1922, long after the others, which made the USA and Britain suspect it of wanting Siberian territory.

### Japan and China: the Twenty-One Demands

Japan's main gain was at the expense of China, then weak and divided under President Yuan Shikai. On **18 January 1915**, Katō presented Yuan with the **Twenty-One Demands**, in five groups:
1. Group 1 (four demands): China was to accept whatever Japan agreed with Germany over Shandong, and to give Japan further rights there.
2. Group 2 (seven demands): extended Japan's leases in south Manchuria and Inner Mongolia to ninety-nine years, with rights to buy land and mine.
3. Group 3 (two demands): gave Japan control of the Hanyeping iron and coal company.
4. Group 4 (one demand): barred China from giving any more coastal or island territory to a third power.
5. Group 5 (seven demands): the most far-reaching. China was to employ Japanese advisers in finance, police and the army and to buy at least half its arms from Japan; this would have made China a Japanese satellite.

Japan tried to keep Group 5 secret, but China leaked the demands to the other powers. After protests from Britain and the USA, Japan dropped Group 5, and on **7 May 1915** gave an ultimatum on the rest. Yuan accepted on 9 May and the treaties were signed on 25 May 1915. Chinese opinion was outraged: 9 May became a "Day of National Humiliation". The episode damaged Japan's reputation in the West, but did not stop it from gaining more at the peace. In 1917 Japan secured secret promises from Britain, France, Russia and Italy that they would support its claims to Shandong and to the German islands north of the equator, in return for naval help.

### Paris, 1919

Japan was one of the "Big Five" at the **Paris Peace Conference** (with the USA, Britain, France and Italy), and held a permanent seat on the Council of the League of Nations. Its aims were to keep what it had taken and to gain an explicit recognition of equality.
- **Racial equality.** The Japanese delegation, led by Makino Nobuaki, proposed to add to the Covenant of the League a clause on the equal treatment of the nationals of member states, "making no distinction, either in law or in fact, on account of their race or nationality" (the first draft, presented on 13 February 1919). A version of the proposal came to a vote in the League commission on 11 April 1919. Eleven of the seventeen members present voted in favour and none against, but the chairman, President Wilson, ruled that so important a change needed a unanimous vote. The strongest opposition came from the British Empire delegation, where the Australian prime minister, Billy Hughes, was the most outspoken. Japan felt insulted, and the rejection fed its distrust of the West.
- **Shandong.** Wilson and the British gave way to Japan's claim, in view of the secret agreements of 1917. The Treaty of Versailles transferred German rights in Shandong to Japan (Article 156). Chinese students protested on 4 May 1919 (the May Fourth Movement), and China refused to sign the treaty. Japan promised to return the territory to China, and did so in 1922, after the Washington Conference.
- **The islands.** The German Pacific islands north of the equator became a Japanese **mandate** under the League (the South Seas Mandate).

### Japan's global position by 1918

By the end of the war Japan was a great power in its own right, with a mixed record:
- **Strengths:** the third-ranking navy in the world (after Britain and the USA, by most counts); a modern industry that grew fast in the war boom; an empire of Taiwan, Korea, southern Sakhalin, the Kwantung leased territory, the South Seas Mandate and, in practice, a sphere in Manchuria; a seat among the Big Five and on the League Council.
- **Weaknesses:** it still lacked iron, coal and oil, and relied on imports; its leadership was divided between the elected politicians and the military, who answered to the Emperor; it was resented by China, distrusted by Britain and the USA, and had been refused racial equality.

:::key Japan's rise: the main stages
1. **1853–68: forced opening**, the unequal treaties, and a change of regime.
2. **1868–94: modernisation**, a "revolution from above" that created a modern state, economy and army.
3. **1894–1905: recognition by victory**, over China and then Russia, and by the Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902).
4. **1905–10: empire**, with Korea annexed and a foothold in Manchuria.
5. **1914–19: world power**, with gains from the First World War, a seat at the Paris Peace Conference and a position among the Big Five.
:::

## Why did Japan emerge as a world power? Weighing the causes

This is the question behind most essay titles on this key question. Sort the causes by type and then by how they connect.

| Type | Causes | How it worked |
|---|---|---|
| Long-term | The Tokugawa legacy: literacy, commerce, a trained bureaucracy; the humiliation of 1853–58 | Gave Japan the means and the motive to change |
| Medium-term | Meiji modernisation: strong state, army and navy, industry, education | Created the power |
| Short-term | War with China (1894–95); the Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902); war with Russia (1904–05) | Showed the power to the world and won recognition |
| Opportunity | Rivalries among the Western powers (Britain against Russia; the First World War) | Allowed Japan to expand without a united opposition |

:::key A judgement you can defend
Japan's rise rested on **modernisation** (the state's decisions after 1868), but the world *recognised* it as a power because of **success in war**, and the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902 helped make that possible. Victory over Russia in 1905 was the moment of recognition, not the cause of the power. Japan's own efforts were the necessary cause, and the rivalries of the Western powers provided the opportunity. The First World War then confirmed the position and gave Japan new territory.
:::

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: not needed for AS, useful for A*)
- **W. G. Beasley**, *The Meiji Restoration* (1972), argued that the Restoration was a political change made by samurai leaders who reacted to the foreign threat and to the shogunate's failure to meet it, and that the more radical social change followed from decisions made after 1868.
- **Marius Jansen**, *The Making of Modern Japan* (2000), traced modern Japan's success to the long Tokugawa background: a commercial economy, a literate society and a developed state, which made rapid change possible.
These views are not rivals. One stresses the political break, the other the long preparation. You can use this to show that the best explanation combines the trigger (the foreign threat) with the base (the Tokugawa inheritance). Paper 1 and Paper 2 reward your own argument from evidence, not a list of historians.
:::

## Worked examples: exam answers built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027 (FACTS.md). Read the commentary as an examiner would: it names the level, the mark range and what would move the answer up.

:::eg Paper 2 (a) [10]: Explain why Britain signed an alliance with Japan in 1902.
**What the levels want.** Level 2 (3–5) describes factors; Level 3 (6–8) explains factors with relevant detail; Level 4 (9–10) connects the factors and reaches a reasoned conclusion.

**A Level 2 answer (about 4 marks).** "Britain signed an alliance with Japan in 1902 because it was worried about Russia. Britain was also fighting the Boer War. The alliance was signed in London by Lord Lansdowne and Hayashi. Britain gave up its splendid isolation. Japan had a good navy and was growing in power."

*Commentary.* Accurate and relevant, but each sentence describes. "Worried about Russia" is a reason, but it is not explained: what was Britain afraid of, and how would Japan help? The signing details are not a reason. Level 2.

**Moving to Level 3 (6–8): explain each factor.** "One reason was the threat from Russia in the Far East. After the Boxer Rising of 1900 Russia kept its troops in Manchuria and was pushing into Korea, which threatened British trade in China and, over time, India. Britain could not stop Russia alone, but Japan, whose own interests in Korea were threatened, had a modern navy and an army that had beaten China in 1894–95. A second reason was that Britain was overstretched. The Boer War (1899–1902) had drawn nearly half a million men to South Africa and showed that Britain had no friends in Europe. The navy was meant to match the next two largest fleets combined, but those were now French and Russian, and German building was growing, so Britain could not keep a large battle fleet in the Far East as well as at home."

*Commentary.* Two factors, each explained with precise support. Level 3.

**A Level 4 answer (9–10): connect the factors and conclude.** Add the third factor and show how they work together:

"A third reason was that Britain had tried other partners first. In 1898–1901 Britain had approached Germany several times, but Germany would not agree to help against Russia in Asia and wanted Britain to join the Triple Alliance on its terms. Britain had refused to join, as that would end its freedom and lead into European quarrels, but it still needed a partner. Japan wanted no part in European affairs.

These factors were linked. Russia's pressure in the Far East created the danger; the overstretch of the Boer War meant that Britain could not meet it with its own forces; and the failure of the German talks left Japan as the only suitable partner. The alliance solved all three problems cheaply: Britain promised only neutrality if Japan fought one power and help if two attacked, so it had no obligation to fight Russia, but would deter France from supporting Russia. It also allowed the Royal Navy to concentrate in home waters. The decisive reason was the strain on British resources, since without that Britain could probably have lived with Russia in Manchuria, and the other factors determined who the partner would be and where the alliance would apply."

*Commentary.* Three factors, explained and **connected** (the danger, the lack of means and the lack of alternatives), and a supported conclusion about the decisive factor. Level 4, 10 marks. About 350–450 words in 12–15 minutes is the target.
:::

:::eg Paper 1 (a) [15]: compare two sources as evidence
**Source A**

*Shortened from the letter of President Millard Fillmore to the Emperor of Japan, 13 November 1852, carried by Commodore Perry.*

**Source B**

The High Contracting Parties, having mutually recognised the independence of China and Korea, declare themselves to be entirely uninfluenced by aggressive tendencies in either country, having in view, however, their special interests, of which those of Great Britain relate principally to China, whilst Japan, in addition to the interests which she possesses in China, is interested in a peculiar degree, politically as well as commercially and industrially in Korea, the High Contracting Parties recognise that it will be admissible for either of them to take such measures as may be indispensable in order to safeguard those interests if threatened either by the aggressive action of any other Power, or by disturbances arising in China or Korea.

*Adapted from Article 1 of the Anglo-Japanese Agreement, signed in London, 30 January 1902; the closing words, about protecting the lives and property of subjects, are left out.*

**Question.** Compare these two sources as evidence about Japan's relations with the western powers.

**What the levels want.** Level 2 (4–7): similarities **or** differences, or both asserted without support. Level 3 (8–11): similarities **and** differences, each supported from the sources. Level 4 (12–15): a developed comparison that **explains why** they agree and differ, using context and the provenance of each source.

**A Level 2 answer (about 6 marks).** "Source A is a letter from the American President to the Emperor of Japan asking for trade. Source B is a treaty between Britain and Japan about China and Korea. They are about different things. Source A is from 1852 and Source B from 1902. Source B is more reliable because it is a treaty and treaties are official."

*Commentary.* Some accurate description, but it only notes that the sources differ in subject. The comment on reliability is a general assertion. Level 2.

**A Level 4 answer (14–15 marks).**

"Both sources use the language of friendship and mutual benefit while advancing the interests of a power. Source A speaks of 'friendship' and trade 'for the benefit both of Japan and the United States'; Source B speaks of mutual recognition of independence and of being 'uninfluenced by aggressive tendencies'. In each, the polite wording hides self-interest: the USA wanted a route and coaling stations for its China trade (the 'eighteen days' from California), and Britain and Japan reserved 'special interests' in China and Korea which they would defend by 'such measures as may be indispensable'.

The key difference is the relationship the sources show. In Source A one power asks another to open its doors: the President addresses 'your imperial majesty' as someone to be persuaded, but the letter came with a squadron of warships, and Japan had no real choice. In Source B Britain and Japan sign as equals, each recognising the other's interests, and it is Japan, not Britain, that claims a 'peculiar' interest in Korea. The shift from being asked to being treated as a partner is the change in Japan's status over fifty years. Another difference is the object: Source A seeks access to Japan; Source B concerns what two powers will do in somebody else's territory (China and Korea), which shows Japan now acting as an imperial power itself.

The context and provenance explain these differences. Source A is a public letter from a head of state to a ruler in a country closed since the 1630s, written to persuade, and it avoids any mention of force. It was one side of an unequal relationship, as shown by the 1858 treaties that followed. Source B is a treaty text, drafted by diplomats and signed by both governments, intended to be read by other powers, above all Russia. It says nothing of Russia, but its purpose is to deter it. For the question, Source A is good evidence of how the West approached Japan in 1853, but the purpose of persuasion means it understates the pressure; Source B is better evidence of Japan's new standing, though what it leaves out (Japan's military and naval strength, which made Britain want the alliance) is what really explains it."

*Commentary.* Similarities and differences, each supported with short quotations; the differences explained through context (the unequal relationship of 1853, the equal one of 1902) and through each source's nature and purpose; a final comparison of the value of each source for the specific issue. This is a **developed comparison**: Level 4.

**Level 5 paragraphs (alternative perspective, then the judgement).**

"Yet victory in 1905 was not the first time that the other powers recognised Japan. The war with China had already persuaded Britain to sign the 1894 treaty ending extraterritoriality from 1899, and in 1902 Britain, the strongest power, made an alliance with Japan as an equal. Both came before Tsushima. The alliance matters because Japan could not have fought Russia with confidence without it: it kept France out of the war. Without the alliance, Japan's victory might not have happened, and so the recognition that followed was in part the result of earlier recognition, not only of the victory.

More important still, victory was possible only because of forty years of modernisation: the conscript army, the British-built fleet, the General Staff, the schools that produced literate soldiers, and the loans raised in London and New York on the credit of a modernised state. The other powers noticed the result in 1905, but the cause was the Meiji state. And the war did not end the matter. Japan was exhausted, the treaty gave it no indemnity, and it was only in 1911 that it won full tariff control, in 1914–19 that it joined the war as an ally, took Tsingtao and the German islands and won a seat among the Big Five at Paris. The Americans, on the other hand, began to see Japan as a rival rather than a partner after 1905, a different kind of recognition.

Overall, victory over Russia was the main reason for the *public and general* recognition of Japan as a world power, because it was a dramatic test that every government and newspaper could understand, and it was followed at once by a renewed alliance and an American-brokered peace. But it was not the main reason for Japan's power, which came from modernisation, nor the first recognition, which came in 1894–1902. The statement is therefore true of the moment, but not of the cause."

*Commentary.* Each paragraph explains an alternative perspective with precise evidence and links it explicitly to the question. The conclusion makes a **comparative judgement** using a clear criterion (recognition against power) and separates the moment from the cause. With an opening paragraph on the case for victory, this is Level 5 (19–20).

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "How far was the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902 the main reason for Japan's victory in the Russo-Japanese War?" [20]

A student wrote:

"The Anglo-Japanese Alliance was signed in London on 30 January 1902 by Lord Lansdowne and Hayashi Tadasu. It had six articles. Article 1 recognised the independence of China and Korea. Article 2 said that if one of them was at war with another power, the other would be neutral. Article 3 said that if a second power joined in, the other would help. It was to last five years. Britain was worried about Russia and about the Boer War. Japan went to war with Russia on 8 February 1904 and won the battle of Tsushima in 1905."

**The level.** The facts are accurate, but the paragraph is a **description** of the treaty and a list of dates. It never says how the treaty helped Japan win, and it does not compare the treaty with other causes. It reaches Level 2 (5–8): "relevant description with few explicit links to the question".

**The fix.** Start with an argument, use only the detail that proves it, then compare. "The alliance was an important condition of the victory because of Article 3. It meant that if France, Russia's ally, joined the war against Japan, Britain would have to fight with Japan, so France stayed out, and Japan did not face the combination of powers that had humiliated it in 1895. British naval yards had also built much of Japan's fleet, and London helped Japan to borrow money. However, the alliance did not win the war. Britain did not fight, and the battles were won by a Japanese army and navy that had been built over forty years, by tactics such as the night attack on Port Arthur and by Tōgō's concentration of the fleet at Tsushima, and Russian failures also counted. So the alliance was a *necessary condition* but not the *main cause*." Now each fact is evidence for a judgement.

Q: What were the main terms of the Treaty of Kanagawa (1854), and what did the Harris Treaty (1858) add?
A: Kanagawa: two ports (Shimoda and Hakodate) opened to American ships for supplies, kind treatment of shipwrecked sailors, a consul at Shimoda and a most-favoured-nation clause. The Harris Treaty of 29 July 1858 added trade at treaty ports, extraterritoriality and tariffs fixed by treaty (about 5 per cent by 1866).
Q: Why were the treaties of 1858 called "unequal"?
A: The Western side had extraterritorial rights, a fixed low tariff that Japan could not change, and most-favoured-nation status; Japan received no matching rights. They limited Japan's control of its own law and trade.
Q: Name three steps in the Meiji modernisation and say why each mattered.
A: For example: abolition of the domains (August 1871), which created a central state; conscription (10 January 1873), which created a national army; the land tax reform (1873), which gave the state a steady cash income; the Fundamental Code of Education (1872), which began a school system; the Constitution (11 February 1889), which gave Japan the form of a modern state.
Q: What was the Iwakura Mission, and what did it show?
A: A journey of about a hundred officials and students to the USA and Europe from 23 December 1871 to 13 September 1873. It failed to revise the unequal treaties, but its members returned convinced that Japan must first strengthen itself, and that Germany was a better model than France or Britain.
Q: What did Japan gain from the Treaty of Shimonoseki (1895), and what was the Triple Intervention?
A: China recognised Korea's independence and ceded Taiwan, the Pescadores and the Liaodong peninsula, and paid an indemnity of 200 million taels. On 23 April 1895 Russia, Germany and France forced Japan to give back Liaodong, for an extra 30 million taels.
Q: Give three reasons why Japan won the Russo-Japanese War.
A: Any three of: the modern navy and Tōgō's tactics (Tsushima); the well-trained army and General Staff; the Anglo-Japanese Alliance, which kept France out and brought British support; loans from London and New York; Russian weaknesses (distance, divided command, the single railway) and the 1905 revolution.
Q: What did the Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905) give Japan, and why were many Japanese angry with it?
A: Russia recognised Japan's paramount interests in Korea and handed over the Port Arthur lease, the Liaodong peninsula and the southern railway, and gave southern Sakhalin. There was no indemnity, which led to the Hibiya riots in Tokyo.
Q: Describe what Japan did in the First World War and what it gained at Paris in 1919.
A: It declared war on Germany on 23 August 1914, took Tsingtao (7 November 1914) and the German Pacific islands, pressed China with the Twenty-One Demands (18 January 1915), sent destroyers to the Mediterranean in 1917 and troops to Siberia in 1918. At Paris it was one of the Big Five and gained Shandong and the South Seas Mandate, but its proposal on racial equality was rejected.

> The Satsuma Rebellion (1877) was the samurai's reaction, and the conscript army defeated it.
? What was the result of the Triple Intervention of 23 April 1895?
- Japan kept the Liaodong peninsula
+ Russia, Germany and France forced Japan to return Liaodong to China
- Japan was forced to give up Taiwan
- Britain joined Japan against Russia
> Japan returned Liaodong for an extra indemnity of 30 million taels, and Russia leased Port Arthur in 1898.
? Why did the Anglo-Japanese Alliance (30 January 1902) help Japan in 1904?
- Britain sent its fleet to fight Russia
- It guaranteed that Japan would receive an indemnity
+ It deterred France from helping Russia, since a second power joining the war would bring Britain in on Japan's side
- It gave Britain the right to control Korea
> Britain did not fight. Article 3 promised help only if two or more powers joined the war.

- The Yalu River
+ Tsushima
- Port Arthur
> Mukden (February–March 1905) was a land battle; Port Arthur surrendered on 2 January 1905.
? What did the Treaty of Portsmouth (5 September 1905) not give Japan?
- Paramount interests in Korea
- Southern Sakhalin
+ A money indemnity
- The Port Arthur lease
> The lack of an indemnity caused the Hibiya riots in Tokyo.
? When did Japan annex Korea?

- 1919

+ To extend Japanese control over China, with the most far-reaching demands (Group 5) later dropped
- To return Shandong to China
- To gain a seat on the League Council

:::recap
- **Opening:** Perry's squadron (8 July 1853); Treaty of Kanagawa (31 March 1854: Shimoda and Hakodate); Harris Treaty (29 July 1858): treaty ports, extraterritoriality, fixed tariffs. Revised 1894 (effective 1899) and 1911 (tariffs).
- **Restoration (1868):** shogunate overthrown (3 January 1868); Charter Oath (6 April); a "revolution from above" by samurai leaders.
- **Modernisation:** domains abolished (August 1871), conscription (10 January 1873), land tax (1873), railway (1872), zaibatsu, schools (1872; Rescript 30 October 1890), Constitution (11 February 1889); Iwakura Mission (23 December 1871 – 13 September 1873). Motto: *fukoku kyōhei*.
- **Korea and China:** Treaty of Ganghwa (1876); war declared 1 August 1894; Shimonoseki (17 April 1895): Korea independent, Taiwan, Liaodong, 200 million taels; Triple Intervention (23 April).
- **Alliance:** Anglo-Japanese Alliance (30 January 1902): neutrality if one power, help if two; Britain's overstretch, Russia in Manchuria; Japan's security and status.
- **Russo-Japanese War:** Port Arthur attack (8–9 February 1904); Port Arthur falls (2 January 1905); Mukden; Tsushima (27–28 May 1905); Portsmouth (5 September 1905); Korea a protectorate (17 November 1905), annexed (22 August 1910).
- **First World War:** war on Germany (23 August 1914); Tsingtao (7 November), the Pacific islands; Twenty-One Demands (18 January 1915); Mediterranean destroyers (13 April 1917); Siberia (about 70,000 men, 1918–22); Paris 1919: Big Five, racial equality rejected, Shandong and the South Seas Mandate.
- **Judgement:** modernisation was the necessary cause; victory in 1905 was the moment of recognition; the alliance and the rivalries of the powers gave the opportunity.
:::
