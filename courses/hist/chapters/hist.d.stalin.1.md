:::why
When Lenin died on 21 January 1924, the Communist Party ruled an empire of about 140 million people, and not one of its leaders was sure who would rule the party. Five years later the question was settled: Stalin had beaten every rival, and the rivals he beat were more famous, better educated and better speakers than he was. How a man whom colleagues thought a useful, rather dull administrator became a dictator is one of the great puzzles of the twentieth century. ## The power vacuum after Lenin

### The party Lenin left

By 1922 the Soviet Union was a one-party state. The Communist Party had banned every other party, and since the Tenth Congress of 1921 it had also banned factions inside itself. It ran the government, the army, the police, the press and the trade unions. This was the context for the succession: whoever controlled the party would control the country.

The party had three bodies at the top. The **Politburo** (political bureau) of the Central Committee took the main decisions; in 1924 it had seven full members. The **Orgburo** (organisation bureau) ran party organisation and appointments. The **Secretariat** carried out decisions and kept the records, and its head, from April 1922, was the **General Secretary**. The Central Committee of about fifty members was elected by the **Party Congress**, which was supposed to meet each year and was the party's highest authority on paper. Below them were regional and local party committees, each with a secretary. The party therefore had a pyramid shape: the congress delegates were chosen by the regional organisations, the regional organisations were led by secretaries, and the secretaries were appointed, in practice, by the centre.

:::def Politburo
The inner committee of the Communist Party's Central Committee, in which the main political decisions of the Soviet state were taken. After Lenin's death it had seven full members: Zinoviev, Kamenev, Stalin, Trotsky, Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky.
:::

:::def General Secretary
The head of the party's Secretariat, a post created in April 1922 and held by Stalin. It was thought of as an administrative job, but its holder directed party appointments, so that over time he could place his own supporters in key posts across the country.
:::

### Lenin's illness

Lenin had been shot and wounded by Fanny Kaplan in August 1918, and the strain of leading the Revolution and the Civil War wore him down. He suffered his first stroke on 25–26 May 1922. He came back to work in October, but suffered a second stroke on 16 December 1922 and a third on 9 March 1923, which took away his power of speech. He lived on, mostly in a house at Gorki outside Moscow, until 21 January 1924, when he died at the age of 53. He never named a successor. His illness gave Stalin and his colleagues nearly two years in which Lenin's authority was present in name but not in practice, and in which they could prepare for the succession.

Lenin's last months were not peaceful. On 18 December 1922 the Central Committee made Stalin responsible for Lenin's medical regime, which meant that Stalin controlled who could see the sick man. A few days later Stalin shouted at Lenin's wife, Nadezhda Krupskaya, on the telephone because she had passed on a message from Lenin, and Krupskaya complained to Lenin. At the same time Lenin was angry about the **Georgian affair** of 1922: Stalin and his ally Sergo Ordzhonikidze had pushed the Georgian Communists, some of whom wanted to keep a measure of independence, into a union with Russia, and Ordzhonikidze had struck a Georgian Bolshevik during an argument. Lenin accused Stalin of "Great Russian chauvinism" and on 5 March 1923 dictated a letter breaking off personal relations with him. A few days later the third stroke ended his working life.

### Lenin's Testament

Between 23 December 1922 and 4 January 1923 Lenin dictated a series of notes to his secretaries, known as the **Letter to the Congress** or **Testament**. He meant them to be read at the next Party Congress. They were a short assessment of the men who might lead the party, and they warned that a quarrel between Stalin and Trotsky could split it.

:::def Lenin's Testament
The notes Lenin dictated from 23 December 1922 to 4 January 1923, in which he weighed the strengths and weaknesses of the party's leaders, warned that the conflict between Stalin and Trotsky threatened the party's unity, and in a postscript of 4 January 1923 asked the party to find a way to remove Stalin from the post of General Secretary because he was too rude.
:::

Lenin's comments, in summary (this is a paraphrase, not a quotation):
- **Stalin** had gained great power as General Secretary and might not use it with enough caution. In the postscript he said Stalin was too rude for the post, and that the party should find a way to replace him with someone more tolerant, loyal, polite and considerate.
- **Trotsky** was perhaps the most capable man in the Central Committee, but was very self-confident and too attracted by the administrative side of affairs.
- **Zinoviev and Kamenev**'s opposition to the October rising in 1917 "was not an accident" but should not be held against them personally.
- **Bukharin** was the party's most valuable theorist and its favourite, but his views were only doubtfully Marxist, because there was something "scholastic" about him.
- **Pyatakov** was able, but too fond of administration to be trusted in political matters.

Two things about it matter for Paper 4. First, Lenin criticised everyone. He did not choose a successor, and his remarks about Trotsky could be used against Trotsky as easily as the remarks about Stalin could be used against Stalin. Second, the key passage about Stalin was a *postscript*, which his enemies could say was written when Lenin was ill, in a bad temper and under the influence of his wife. The Testament was therefore dangerous to Stalin but not fatal.

:::note Reading the Testament: provenance and use
*Adapted from Lenin, "Letter to the Congress", dictated between 23 December 1922 and 4 January 1923, Collected Works, volume 36, in the English translation published on the Marxists Internet Archive.*

"Comrade Stalin, having become Secretary-General, has unlimited authority concentrated in his hands …"

"Stalin is too rude and this defect, although quite tolerable in our midst …" (postscript of 4 January 1923)

The translation says "Secretary-General"; the post is usually called General Secretary. **How to use it as evidence:** it is a private document from the founder of the state, written when he knew he was dying, so it shows how a man who knew Stalin well saw the danger. It does not show that Stalin was *unpopular*, and it was kept from the public until 1956, when it was first published in the USSR. Its importance for the power struggle lies in what happened to it in May 1924, below.
:::

### The suppression of the Testament

The Testament was addressed to the Party Congress, and on 18 May 1924, just before the Thirteenth Congress, Krupskaya gave it to the Central Committee. The Central Committee was in an awkward position, because the document called for the removal of the General Secretary and also criticised Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin. The leaders of the triumvirate decided to keep it from becoming a public issue. It was read to the heads of the delegations, not to the whole Congress, in a closed meeting. Kamenev read it out, and Zinoviev, who feared Trotsky more than he feared Stalin, argued that Lenin's fear about Stalin had proved unfounded, since the party had seen Stalin working loyally with his colleagues. Stalin offered to resign. The Central Committee refused, by a majority, and Trotsky, who might have been expected to use the document, sat silent. The Testament was never published in the Soviet Union during Stalin's life. It was published in the West, in the *New York Times* in 1926, and did not appear in the USSR until 1956, three years after Stalin's death.

### Lenin's funeral

Lenin's death on 21 January 1924 was the greatest public moment of the 1920s. His body lay in state in the Hall of Columns in Moscow for several days, and hundreds of thousands of people queued in severe frost to file past. The funeral took place on 27 January. Petrograd was renamed Leningrad on 26 January. Within weeks the body was embalmed and put on show in a mausoleum on Red Square, against the wishes of Krupskaya, who said that Lenin would have hated the idea of a shrine.

The funeral mattered for the succession in two ways. First, **Trotsky was absent**. He had left Moscow on 18 January for the Caucasus to recover from a fever, and was in Sukhumi when Lenin died. He wrote later that Stalin had told him by telegram that the funeral would be on Saturday, when it was on Sunday, so that he could not get back in time. Stalin's defenders point out that Trotsky could have returned at once, and that he did not. Historians cannot be certain how far deception was involved, but the result is clear: Stalin and Zinoviev appeared in the public ceremonies as Lenin's heirs, and the best-known figure in the party did not appear at all, which looked like indifference. Second, Stalin used the occasion to cast himself as Lenin's faithful follower. On 26 January 1924, at the Second Congress of Soviets, he gave a speech in the form of a series of solemn vows to the dead leader, in a style that echoed the liturgy of the church in which he had been trained at the Tiflis seminary. Zinoviev and Kamenev did not match it.

### Collective leadership

With Lenin gone, the party said that it would be led collectively. **Collective leadership** meant that no single man would take Lenin's place: the Politburo would decide together, under the party's principle that decisions were taken by committee. In practice the Politburo was not a team. It was a group of seven ambitious men, each of whom thought that he, or his friends, ought to lead. The rhetoric of collective leadership served everyone's interest except Trotsky's, because it prevented the one man who seemed to have the stature to take over from doing so.

:::def Collective leadership
The system of rule by the Politburo as a group which the Communist Party claimed to follow after Lenin's death, in which no single leader was to replace Lenin. In practice it was a struggle between rivals, and it ended when Stalin dominated the Politburo by 1929.
:::

:::key The power vacuum, in short
1. **No successor.** Lenin died without naming one, and his Testament criticised all the leading candidates.
2. **No rules.** The party had banned factions and had no way of settling a dispute between its top leaders other than a vote in bodies that the leaders themselves controlled.
3. **A prize no one could share.** The man who led the party would lead the state, so each rival's defeat was permanent.
4. **A weak opening for Stalin.** The Testament threatened him, but its suppression, Trotsky's silence and Lenin's own criticism of all the others meant it did little harm.
:::

### Leon Trotsky

Trotsky (born Lev Bronstein, in 1879, in Ukraine, in a Jewish family) was the obvious candidate to anyone who looked only at the history of the Revolution. He had chaired the Petrograd Soviet in 1917 and led the Military Revolutionary Committee that took the capital in October. He had been Commissar for War from 1918, built the Red Army and won the Civil War from the armoured train in which he travelled from front to front. He was a writer and speaker of rare power and was widely read abroad.

But in 1924 he was weaker than his fame suggested. Lenin had once been a bitter opponent of his, and he had been a Menshevik (or close to them) until he joined the Bolsheviks in the summer of 1917, which meant that older Bolsheviks regarded him as a latecomer. He had no base in the party apparatus. His arrogance and his contempt for slower men wounded colleagues, and he made little effort to build friendships or to win supporters. Many leaders feared that as head of the Red Army he might become a "Bonaparte", a military dictator who would end the Revolution in the way Napoleon had ended the French one, a fear that his rivals encouraged. He was also a target of anti-Semitism in the party, which was usually unspoken but real. His record was not helped by mistakes in 1922–24 (below): he refused Lenin's request to become a deputy chairman of Sovnarkom, he stayed silent in May 1924 and he missed the funeral.

### Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev

Zinoviev and Kamenev were close political partners who had been in Lenin's circle since before 1917. Zinoviev ran the party in Petrograd (now Leningrad), the second city, and chaired the Communist International (Comintern), the body that directed foreign Communist parties. He was a strong orator, and he was the man who most feared Trotsky. Kamenev led the Moscow party and was deputy chairman of Sovnarkom, and as the Politburo's cool chairman he was respected for his intelligence. The two men could have been formidable, but in October 1917 they had published their opposition to the planned rising in a newspaper, which Lenin had called a betrayal, and they were thought to lack courage. Zinoviev's reputation was damaged again by the failure of the German "October" of 1923, which he had urged as head of the Comintern. Kamenev's wife was Trotsky's sister, but this made no difference to their rivalry.

### Nikolai Bukharin, Alexei Rykov and Mikhail Tomsky

Bukharin, the youngest at 35, edited *Pravda* and was the party's best-known theorist. Lenin called him, in the Testament, the favourite of the whole party, and his friendliness made him widely popular. He had been on the far left of the party in 1918, when he opposed the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, but by 1924 he was the strongest defender of the NEP, a change of mind that made some call him unprincipled and others naive. As a politician he was not ruthless enough for the contest. Rykov succeeded Lenin as chairman of Sovnarkom, which made him the head of the Soviet government on paper, and he was a practical administrator who shared Bukharin's belief in the NEP. Tomsky led the trade unions, and was one of the few leaders who had himself been a worker. The three men formed what became known as the **Right**, because of their defence of the NEP. Between them they had the government (Rykov), the trade unions (Tomsky) and the party's main newspaper (Bukharin), but no mass party base.

### Joseph Stalin

Stalin was born Ioseb Jughashvili in Gori, in Georgia, in 1878. (The Soviet state gave his birthday as 21 December 1879, a year and three days after the date in the Gori church records, and it was on that basis that it celebrated his fiftieth birthday in 1929.) He trained for the priesthood at the Tiflis seminary and was expelled in 1899, then became a professional revolutionary, working in the Caucasus, helping to organise a bank robbery in Tiflis in 1907 to raise party funds and spending years in Siberian exile. He joined the Bolshevik Central Committee in 1912, edited *Pravda* in 1917 and was Commissar for Nationalities from the first Soviet government until 1923. He had played a part in the Civil War, notably at Tsaritsyn in 1918, where he quarrelled with Trotsky. He was a member of the Politburo from its creation in 1919.

His strengths were practical. He worked extremely hard, had a retentive memory for names and details, and he was patient. He took trouble to know people, he listened and he remembered who had helped him. He spoke simply, which made him seem approachable to party officials, and he adopted the dress and manner of an ordinary party worker. He also understood the machine of the party better than anyone else, because he had been running it since 1919. His weaknesses seemed obvious. He was not a brilliant speaker or writer, and his Russian was spoken with a Georgian accent. He had no great reputation in 1917 (the memoirist Nikolai Sukhanov, writing in the early 1920s, had called him a "grey blur"). Trotsky thought him a mediocrity. Lenin's Testament called him too rude.

:::key Why Stalin was underestimated
In 1924 most of the leaders thought Stalin was the least dangerous of them. He was no orator, no intellectual and no hero of the Revolution; he did a dull job, and had no army behind him. Each of them thought that his own talents would count for more in the end and that Stalin was a useful ally against someone else. That error lay behind the changing alliances below, in which Zinoviev and Kamenev helped to defeat Trotsky, and then Bukharin helped to defeat Zinoviev and Kamenev, and each of them was destroyed in turn. When you write about this you must avoid hindsight: do not write as if everyone could see in 1924 that Stalin would be a dictator.
:::

## Stalin's position in the party

Stalin's greatest strength was not his personality but his posts. The Soviet system ran through the party, and Stalin held, at once, a series of jobs that gave him control of the party's machinery.

- **General Secretary** (from 3 April 1922). The Secretariat ran the party's paperwork, and its head decided what went on the agenda, who was told what, and above all who was appointed where.
- **Orgburo.** Stalin sat on it from its creation in 1919, and it handled the party's organisation and personnel.
- **Politburo.** Stalin was a full member from 1919, so he took part in every major political decision.
- **Rabkrin** (the Workers' and Peasants' Inspectorate), which he headed from 1919 to 1922 and which checked officials across the state, gave him knowledge of them and a reason to talk to them.
- **Commissar for Nationalities** (1917 to 1923).

No other leader held an equivalent combination. Trotsky had the Red Army and a great name, Zinoviev and Kamenev had the two main cities, Bukharin had the newspaper, but only Stalin had the machinery of appointments.

:::def Nomenklatura
The lists of important posts in the party and state, and the lists of people approved as suitable to fill them, which the party's Secretariat controlled. Anyone who wished to be a regional party secretary, a factory director or a newspaper editor needed the party's approval, which gave the General Secretary great patronage.
:::

### How the machine worked

The party's structure turned this control into votes. The delegates who attended the Party Congress, and elected the Central Committee that elected the Politburo, were chosen by regional organisations, and the regional secretaries who led them were themselves chosen from the centre. The General Secretary could therefore promote people who supported him, move opponents to distant posts and rely on the votes of the delegates who owed him their careers. He did not need to be popular in the party at large. He needed only the people who counted at congresses. A regional secretary who owed his job to Stalin would make sure that the delegates from his region voted for Stalin's policies, and the numbers soon added up. Stalin's supporters filled the Secretariat and Orgburo: Vyacheslav Molotov (a Central Committee secretary from 1921), Lazar Kaganovich (head of the party's personnel department), Kliment Voroshilov, Sergo Ordzhonikidze (who headed the Central Control Commission from 1926) and others who stayed loyal to him for decades. Sergei Kirov took over the Leningrad party after Zinoviev's defeat in 1926.

### The Lenin Enrolment

In 1924, as a tribute to the dead leader, the party opened its doors to workers on a large scale. This **Lenin Enrolment** brought in about 240,000 industrial workers between January and May 1924; membership, about 446,000 on 1 January 1924, was over a million by January 1926. It made the party more working-class, and in the process it also made it younger, less educated and less used to the debates of the Civil War generation. Many of the new members were glad to have a place in the party and tended to be respectful of party officials and to follow the lead of the secretaries who signed them up. They had no loyalty to Trotsky's brilliant but demanding style. It was one of the ways in which the apparatus changed the party's make-up.

:::key Why the General Secretary's post mattered so much
1. **Appointments.** He controlled which officials went to which jobs, so that regional secretaries owed their careers to him.
2. **Congresses.** He influenced who became delegates, and so who would vote in the party's top bodies.
3. **Information.** The Secretariat's files told him what everyone had said and done.
4. **The cloak of impartiality.** The work looked administrative, so rivals did not see it as a political weapon until it was too late.
:::

## Ideological differences

The struggle was not only about people. The party's leaders disagreed about how to build socialism in a poor, mostly peasant country that had had no revolution in the advanced West, and the arguments gave each of them a cause, a platform and a way to attack the others. Stalin used the disagreements better than anyone, and was very careful to appear a moderate and to follow whichever policy suited him at the time.

### The NEP versus industrialisation

In the 1920s about four in five Russians were peasants. The NEP had brought the farms and the factories back to pre-war levels, but it also produced problems that frightened Communists. Prosperous peasants (called **kulaks**, a term the Communists used loosely and rather unfairly for richer peasants) were doing well, private traders (**NEPmen**) were visible in the towns, and there were about a million unemployed workers. In 1923 the **scissors crisis**, in which industrial prices were high and farm prices low, so that peasants refused to buy factory goods, had shown how fragile the arrangement was. Industry needed to grow faster, but it needed capital, and the money could come from only a few places: the peasants, foreign loans or the state. The party was divided over which.

**The Left** (Trotsky and the economist Yevgeny Preobrazhensky, who stated the case in *The New Economics*, 1926) argued that the NEP gave too much to the peasants and that the party must industrialise rapidly under a central plan. Preobrazhensky coined the idea of "primitive socialist accumulation", meaning that the state would extract a surplus from the countryside, by higher prices for industrial goods and lower ones for farm produce, to pay for the factories. The Left also attacked the growth of bureaucracy in the party and demanded more internal debate.

**The Right** (Bukharin, Rykov, Tomsky) argued the opposite. Socialism would be built through a strong alliance (the **smychka**) between workers and peasants. If the peasants prospered they would buy more factory goods, and industry would grow with the market; a policy of squeezing them would end in rebellion, as it had in 1921. Bukharin said in 1925 that the party should tell the peasants "enrich yourselves", a phrase for which he was attacked for the rest of his life and which he later withdrew, and he spoke of "riding into socialism on a peasant nag" at a slow pace.

**Stalin** began on the side of the Right. In 1925–27 he defended the NEP against the Left, and he accused Trotsky of wanting to rob the peasants. That was shrewd, because the Right's policy was popular in the party, where memories of War Communism were fresh, and because it let him pose as a moderate. But the policy had a problem: by 1927 the state could not buy enough grain at its set prices, and in the winter of 1927–28 collections were millions of tons short. Stalin's response, in January 1928, was to go to Siberia, to order the use of emergency powers against peasants who were "hoarding" grain and to take back much of the Left's programme. By 1929 it was clear that his policy was industrialisation at the expense of the peasants, the policy he had attacked when it came from Trotsky. That change of front let him accuse the Right of defending the kulaks and so of betraying socialism.

:::def New Economic Policy (NEP)
The policy of 1921 to 1928, in which the Bolsheviks replaced grain requisitioning with a tax in kind, allowed peasants to sell surplus produce and permitted small private trade, while the state kept control of heavy industry, banking and foreign trade. In the 1920s it was the central issue between the Left, who wanted to end it, and the Right, who wanted to keep it.
:::

### Socialism in One Country versus Permanent Revolution

The second argument was about whether the Soviet Union could build socialism without help from the rest of the world. Marx had expected socialist revolution in the advanced industrial countries, not in backward Russia, and the Bolsheviks in 1917 had assumed that their rising would spark one in Germany. German revolution failed in 1918–19 and again in October 1923, and the Bolsheviks were left isolated.

Trotsky answered with **Permanent Revolution**, a theory he had first worked out in 1906. Revolution in a backward country, he said, could not stop at the stage of a middle-class republic and had to go on to socialism, but it could not survive on its own in a hostile capitalist world; the Soviet Union's survival therefore depended on revolutions in more developed countries, which the party must work to bring about.

Stalin's reply came in the closing months of 1924, in an article that grew out of a speech attacking Trotsky. **Socialism in One Country** said that the USSR, with its great size, its resources and its working class, could build a socialist society on its own while waiting for world revolution. In his *Foundations of Leninism* of April 1924 he had said the opposite, that the efforts of one country were not enough, and he changed his line within months, so it was not an inheritance from Lenin, as he suggested. Bukharin gave the new line its theoretical defence, and the Fourteenth Party Conference adopted it in April 1925.

The new doctrine had a strong appeal, for three reasons. It was patriotic and positive: it told the party that its sacrifices had a purpose and that Russia could build a new society itself. It fitted the party's mood after the failure of the German revolution. And it made Trotsky's position look defeatist and pessimistic, as if he believed that the Soviet Union could not survive without revolutions abroad that were not happening. Trotsky's reply, that the party was abandoning internationalism, could be answered by calling him a faction-maker who despised the Russian people and the party's work.

:::def Permanent Revolution
Trotsky's theory that a revolution in a backward country such as Russia had to go beyond a middle-class stage to socialism, but could not be completed in one country alone and depended on revolutions in more advanced countries. His opponents used it to say that he did not believe in the Soviet Union's ability to build socialism.
:::

:::def Socialism in One Country
Stalin's doctrine, stated in 1924 and adopted by the party in 1925, that the USSR could build a complete socialist society by its own efforts, without waiting for revolutions in other countries. It gave the party a patriotic goal and became the basis for later policies of rapid industrialisation.
:::

:::key The ideological argument, in short
| Issue | Left (Trotsky, later Zinoviev and Kamenev) | Right (Bukharin, Rykov, Tomsky) | Stalin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Economy | End the NEP; fast industry paid for by the peasants | Keep the NEP; peasants' prosperity drives growth | 1925–27: with the Right. 1928: takes over the Left's policy |
| Revolution | Permanent Revolution: depend on world revolution | Socialism in One Country, slow pace | Socialism in One Country |
| Party | More internal democracy; attack the bureaucracy | Keep discipline | Keep discipline; "unity" |
:::

## The power struggle in stages

The struggle went in four stages. At each one Stalin was allied to the men who were the biggest threat to his current target, and at each one he finished by turning on his allies.

### Stage 1, 1923–25: the triumvirate against Trotsky

**The triumvirate.** Zinoviev, Kamenev and Stalin had worked together while Lenin was ill, and they decided jointly what the Politburo would discuss. The three were united by one thing: fear of Trotsky. Zinoviev and Kamenev were especially afraid that a man with Trotsky's reputation and army command would take over. Stalin was happy to be the junior partner, and to take the work that the other two thought dull, such as running the party's organisation. In effect he used them as a shield.

**The attack on Trotsky.** In October 1923, in the midst of the economic crisis, 46 prominent party members sent a private letter, the **Declaration of the 46**, criticising the party leaders and the lack of debate. In December Trotsky published a letter, the **New Course**, calling for more democracy in the party and attacking the "bureaucratisation" of the apparatus. His rivals said this was an attack on the party by a man who despised it, and the Thirteenth Party Conference of January 1924, held while Trotsky was ill, condemned the Opposition for factionalism, using the ban of 1921.

**The Lenin cult and the funeral** (above) then moved the debate into the field of loyalty to Lenin, in which Trotsky was at a disadvantage. At the Thirteenth Congress in May 1924, when the Testament was read, Trotsky said nothing. In autumn 1924 Trotsky published *The Lessons of October*, a study of 1917 that said, among other things, that Zinoviev and Kamenev had opposed the rising. It was a damaging reminder of their past, but it handed them and Stalin a gift. They launched a "literary discussion", in which a large number of articles said that Trotsky was a Menshevik who had always been at odds with Lenin, was trying to revise Leninism and had a theory of Permanent Revolution that was not Bolshevik at all. Stalin wrote his own attack, "Trotskyism or Leninism?", in November 1924.

**The result.** On late January 1925 the Central Committee relieved Trotsky as Commissar for War. He was not expelled and he stayed in the Politburo, in a minor post (the head of the concessions committee, then the electrical board). His defeat was political, not physical: the Red Army never moved, and Trotsky refused to ask it to. By taking him out of the Red Army, his enemies had removed the means by which he might have fought back.

### Stage 2, 1925–26: Stalin and the Right against Zinoviev and Kamenev

Once Trotsky was beaten, the alliance of the triumvirate had no purpose, and Zinoviev and Kamenev drifted away from Stalin. They disliked **Socialism in One Country**, which seemed a departure from Leninism, they were alarmed by Bukharin's "enrich yourselves" line, which suggested that the party was favouring the kulaks, and they noticed that Stalin's power was growing faster than theirs. Zinoviev and Kamenev moved to the Left. Krupskaya also came to support them. Stalin, who needed new allies, turned to Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky, and the five were soon voting together in the Politburo.

The break came at the **Fourteenth Congress** in December 1925. Zinoviev and Kamenev attacked the policy towards the peasants, and Kamenev, in a famous moment, said that the party should not allow a single leader to set himself above it. The Leningrad delegation, controlled by Zinoviev, supported them, but nearly all the other delegations voted for Stalin and Bukharin. The Leningrad party was then taken over, in 1926, by Stalin's ally Kirov, and Zinoviev lost his power base. The Central Committee elected in 1925 was dominated by Stalin's supporters, and Zinoviev was dropped from the Politburo in July 1926. This was a more important defeat than Trotsky's, because Zinoviev and Kamenev were members of the triumvirate, men who had run the party jointly with Stalin.

### Stage 3, 1926–27: the United Opposition

Beaten separately, the three old rivals did what they might have done earlier and joined forces in 1926, as the **United Opposition**. Trotsky, Zinoviev and Kamenev put their case that the NEP had enriched the kulaks and NEPmen, that industry needed to grow faster and that the party was strangled by bureaucracy and by Stalin's control of it. They had real arguments and a large following in Moscow and Leningrad, and for a few months they were dangerous. But by then Stalin controlled the machinery. Their meetings were broken up and their speakers shouted down, by party members brought in for the purpose. The OGPU arrested some of their supporters, and the Politburo accused them of factionalism and of preparing a split. They were also hurt by their own past, because their new alliance looked like an alliance of old enemies who had no common principle other than opposition to Stalin. When, in 1927, they warned that the party's policy in China (the Comintern had told the Chinese Communists to work with Chiang Kai-shek's Nationalists, who massacred them in Shanghai in April 1927) was leading to disaster, events seemed to prove them right, but Stalin deflected the criticism onto them.

The end came in autumn 1927. Zinoviev and Trotsky were expelled from the Central Committee in October, and on **12–14 November 1927**, just before the tenth anniversary of the Revolution, they were expelled from the party. The Fifteenth Congress of December 1927 then condemned the Opposition and expelled a number of its leading members, including Kamenev. Trotsky was exiled to Alma-Ata, in Kazakhstan, in January 1928 and deported from the USSR in February 1929; he lived in Turkey, France, Norway and Mexico, writing against Stalin, and was murdered by a Stalinist agent, Ramón Mercader, in Mexico in August 1940. Zinoviev and Kamenev admitted their errors and were allowed back into the party in 1928, a humiliation that would be remembered when they were put on trial in 1936.

### Stage 4, 1928–29: the defeat of the Right

By late 1927 Stalin no longer needed the Right. In winter 1927–28 the grain crisis showed that the NEP was in trouble, and Stalin changed policy, adopting, in effect, the programme of the Left that he had just destroyed. The party had to squeeze grain from the countryside, and then to plan rapid industrialisation. Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky opposed forced requisitioning because they remembered what happened in 1921. They did not form a faction (they had voted for the ban on factions), and they did not appeal to the party outside. Bukharin did meet the disgraced Kamenev in July 1928, and spoke of Stalin as an unprincipled "Genghis Khan", and Stalin learned of it, and used the meeting to accuse Bukharin of plotting with the Left. Bukharin's "Notes of an Economist" in September 1928 was a restrained criticism of Stalin's policy.

Stalin's method was patient. He did not remove them at once. In 1928 he said there were no differences, and then he used the Central Committee majority, which he had packed, to criticise them in stages. In April 1929 the Central Committee condemned the Right's views as a "Right deviation". Bukharin lost the editorship of *Pravda* in April 1929 and his Politburo seat in November. Tomsky lost his post at the head of the trade unions in 1929, and Rykov was replaced by Molotov as head of Sovnarkom in December 1930. All three admitted their errors and were left in minor jobs. In 1929 Stalin was the master of the Politburo.

:::warn Hindsight
Do not write that the struggle was bound to end as it did. In 1925 Trotsky and Zinoviev had more public support than Stalin, in 1927 the Opposition forced the leadership to disrupt party life to stop them, and in 1928 many in the party were uneasy about the break with the NEP. Stalin did not know in 1924 that he would be a dictator, and he made use of opportunities as they came. Your answer should show that Stalin's rivals made choices that could have been different and that his methods, while ruthless, were still those of a party politician and not yet those of a terror state.
:::

## Repression and propaganda to 1929

### The machinery of repression

Stalin's victory between 1924 and 1929 was won chiefly by votes, appointments and expulsions rather than by terror. That is an important point of comparison with the Great Terror of the 1930s. Nobody who lost the power struggle was shot before 1929. Trotsky was exiled and deported, Zinoviev and Kamenev were allowed back, Bukharin was kept in office for a time. But the machinery of repression was in place and its use grew.

1. **The ban on factions (1921).** The Tenth Congress resolution "On Party Unity" had a clause, not published at the time, that let the Central Committee expel a member from the Central Committee itself by a two-thirds vote if he broke party unity. It was applied to the Opposition in 1926–27. A critic of Stalin could be called a "factionalist" and expelled, whereas Stalin's own supporters, who organised openly, were just following the party line.
2. **Control commissions and expulsions.** The Central Control Commission, headed from 1926 by Ordzhonikidze, could discipline members. Expulsion from the party in the 1920s meant losing one's job, ration entitlements and housing, which was punishment enough for most.
3. **The OGPU.** The political police was the **GPU** from February 1922 and the **OGPU** from November 1923. It was headed by Felix Dzerzhinsky until his death in July 1926 and then by Vyacheslav Menzhinsky, and in the 1920s it dealt with non-party opponents (priests, former members of other parties, "bourgeois specialists", peasant rebels) and, from 1927, began to arrest members of the Opposition. In September 1927 it claimed to have found an Opposition printing press, and the OGPU took part in the exile of Trotsky and his followers to remote parts of the country.
4. **Show trials.** The **Shakhty trial** (18 May–6 July 1928) was the first. Fifty-three engineers and technicians, including three Germans, were accused of sabotage ("wrecking") in the coal mines of the Donbas town of Shakhty, on the orders of former mine-owners and foreign enemies. Eleven were sentenced to death, and five were shot. The charges were false, but the trial served several purposes: it gave the leaders a scapegoat for economic problems, it frightened the old "bourgeois specialists" and it began the pattern of the show trial, in which accused people confess to crimes in public. It was the model for the trials of the 1930s.

:::def Show trial
A public trial staged for propaganda, with a result decided in advance, in which defendants confess to crimes, usually invented, so as to blame them for the state's failures and to warn the public. The first Soviet example under Stalin was the Shakhty trial of 1928.
:::

### Propaganda: Lenin's disciple

The party also won its battles by controlling who spoke and what was said. It controlled the press (*Pravda* and the provincial papers), publishing and the schools, and a censorship office, Glavlit, had existed since 1922. This control was incomplete in the mid-1920s, because Bukharin edited *Pravda* and the oppositions could circulate documents, but it was strong enough that by 1927 the Opposition's case could be given to the public only in the form that the leadership chose.

Propaganda played on Lenin. The party built a **cult of Lenin** after his death: the mausoleum on Red Square, Lenin corners in factories and offices, the renaming of Petrograd as Leningrad, Lenin's face on posters and the Lenin Institute that collected his writings. The leaders, who had to rule in his name, competed to be seen as his heirs. Zinoviev started to talk of "Leninism" as a doctrine, and Stalin took this over in his lectures at Sverdlov University in April 1924, which became *The Foundations of Leninism*: a clear, simple summary of Lenin's ideas in the form of a catechism, that served as the party's textbook. By stating Leninism and presenting himself as its guardian, Stalin cast himself as **Lenin's disciple**: not an original thinker, but a faithful pupil who defended the founder's teaching. This was a clever approach. It disarmed those who thought him dull, because he claimed to be only a follower. It made opposition look like a betrayal of Lenin. And it let him label Trotsky's theories as "Trotskyism", an alien heresy, against Lenin's true doctrine.

:::key How "Lenin's disciple" helped Stalin
1. **Legitimacy.** Stalin's authority came from his claim to be Lenin's heir, not from his own record.
2. **A weapon.** Anything that Trotsky or the Opposition said could be called a departure from Leninism, as in "Trotskyism or Leninism?"
3. **Safety.** A "disciple" did not look like a rival, so it hid his ambition.
4. **A base for later.** The Lenin cult supplied the forms (portraits, shrines, slogans, renamed cities) that the Stalin cult would copy.
:::

### The beginning of the cult of personality

By the late 1920s, the Lenin cult began to turn into a Stalin cult. The first big sign was the celebration of Stalin's fiftieth birthday on **21 December 1929**, which the party press marked with long articles, telegrams of congratulation from the whole country and descriptions of Stalin as "Lenin's best pupil" and "the Lenin of today". Articles in 1929 had already begun to alter the record of the Civil War to give Stalin a greater part and to play down Trotsky's. The cult did not yet reach the scale of the 1930s, and Stalin himself affected modesty, but the pattern had begun: the leader's image was a tool of power. It is covered in full in [Stalin's Russia (2)](#hist.d.stalin.2).

## Why did Stalin win? Weighing the reasons

Questions on this topic ask you to rank reasons. Here are the main ones and a way to compare them.

| Reason | The case that it was decisive | The case against |
|---|---|---|
| Stalin's control of the party machine | General Secretary: appointments, delegates, patronage; the party's votes did what he wanted | The machine was built over years with the help of other leaders; it worked because of the ban on factions and the rivals' errors |
| The weaknesses and divisions of his rivals | Trotsky's arrogance and silence, Zinoviev and Kamenev's alliance with Stalin, Bukharin's naivety; each rival helped him defeat the last | Rivals' mistakes counted only because Stalin took the chance; Trotsky might have lost anyway |
| Stalin's skills and personality | Patience, timing, a moderate image, ability to work with each side in turn and then abandon it | His reputation was low; his skills are mostly seen with hindsight |
| Policy and ideology | Socialism in One Country; first the NEP, then industrialisation, each at a popular time | He changed policy to suit; the policies matter less than who held power |
| Repression and propaganda | The ban on factions, expulsions, the OGPU, the Shakhty trial, the Lenin cult and "Lenin's disciple" | No one was killed in the struggle before 1929; the methods were party methods |
| Circumstances | Lenin's death with no successor; the failure of European revolution; party weariness and fear of Bonapartism | These explain the opening, not why Stalin was the one to take it |

A strong answer separates the *conditions* that allowed a leader to win (Lenin's death, the ban on factions, the failure of world revolution, the party's mood) from the *means* he used (the machine, alliances, ideas) and from the *choices of his rivals* (silence, alliance, misjudgement). It then argues which did most. A defensible judgement is that the party machine was the main reason, because it turned every disagreement into a vote that Stalin could win, but that it would not have worked if his rivals had not helped him to build it and then divided against each other, so that the single most important thing about the machine was that it was in the hands of a man who understood it better than they did.

:::note The historians' debate (stretch: useful for A*)
Historians have given several accounts of why Stalin won, and they disagree about how far it was due to Stalin and how far to the situation.
- **Trotsky** himself, writing in exile (*The Revolution Betrayed*, written in 1936 and published in English in 1937), argued that Stalin represented a new privileged bureaucracy, which rose in a backward and exhausted country after the revolutionary wave had ebbed. On that view Stalin was less a brilliant man than the mouthpiece of a social layer.
- **Isaac Deutscher**, *Stalin: A Political Biography* (1949), a biographer who sympathised with Trotsky, shared much of this view. He argued that Stalin was the leader of the party's machine, in an exhausted country, and that Trotsky was handicapped by the party's weariness with revolution.
- **E.H. Carr**, in his many-volume *A History of Soviet Russia* (begun 1950), stressed circumstances and the pressure of events: the policy of Socialism in One Country, in his view, met a real need of a party that had to run an isolated state.
- **Robert Service**, *Stalin: A Biography* (2004), emphasised Stalin's abilities, his drive and his ruthlessness, and his skill in choosing when to move, and presented him as no mediocrity but a leader of great capacity who had also a fierce capacity to nurse grievances.
- **Stephen Kotkin**, *Stalin: Paradoxes of Power, 1878–1928* (2014), made a detailed case for Stalin's ability, hard work and political skill and for the importance of his understanding of the party and its beliefs, and gave less weight to the idea that he was a bureaucrat who happened to be in the right place.
:::

:::exam Technique for "Assess" and "Evaluate" essays on 2.1
1. **Define the criteria first.** For "Assess the reasons for Stalin's victory": criteria could be *necessity* (would he have won without this?), *contribution* (how much did it add?) and *timing* (did it matter at each of the four stages?).
2. **Spend a paragraph on each of three or four reasons**, each ending with a sentence that says how much it mattered and compares it with another reason.
3. **Use precise evidence**: 3 April 1922, 21 January 1924, late January 1925, the Fourteenth Congress in December 1925, expulsions of 12–14 November 1927, about 240,000 workers in the Lenin Enrolment, the 5 of 11 Shakhty executions.
4. **Keep the dates in view.** "1924–29" means the four stages, not the terror of the 1930s or the Great Turn.
5. **Avoid hindsight and narrative.** Do not tell the story from 1924 to 1929 in order; use it as evidence for the argument.
6. **Judge.** End with a view that says which reason mattered most and why, and what would have changed if it had been absent.
:::

## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

A Paper 4 essay is marked on **two tables**, each out of 15, and the total is out of 30. Table A (AO2) rewards analysis, criteria and judgement. Table B (AO1) rewards precise, selected and focused knowledge. The levels are the same for both, from Level 1 (1–3) to Level 5 (13–15).

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: Assess the reasons for Stalin's victory in the power struggle, 1924–29.
**What the levels want.** Level 3 (7–9 on each table): some analysis, an argument that lacks balance, knowledge mostly relevant but descriptive in places. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, mostly analytical, valid criteria, a supported judgement partly substantiated; a good range of mostly precise points, with uneven depth. Level 5 (13–15): full understanding, balanced and sustained argument with wide-ranging criteria, a clear and supported judgement, and detail that is carefully selected, fully focused and precise.

**Plan (5 minutes).**
- Criterion: which reason, if absent, would most probably have prevented Stalin's victory?
- Reason 1: the party machine: General Secretary since 3 April 1922; Orgburo; nomenklatura; delegates at congresses; the Lenin Enrolment (about 240,000 workers in 1924); allies (Molotov, Kaganovich, Ordzhonikidze); Kirov in Leningrad 1926.
- Reason 2: rivals' weaknesses: Trotsky's silence in May 1924 and absence from the funeral; refusal to use the Red Army; Zinoviev and Kamenev's alliance and late opposition; Bukharin's naivety and the Right's refusal to organise.
- Reason 3: skills and tactics: moderate image, alliances in four stages, timing, "Lenin's disciple".
- Reason 4: policy: Socialism in One Country; the NEP in 1925–27 and the switch in 1928.
- Reason 5: repression and propaganda: the ban on factions, expulsions, the OGPU, Shakhty; the Lenin cult.
- Judgement: the machine was the main reason, but it worked because the rivals divided and Stalin knew how to use it.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Stalin won the power struggle because he was General Secretary. This post meant that he could put his friends in important jobs, so he had a lot of support in the party. Also his rivals were weak. Trotsky was arrogant and did not get on with other people, and Zinoviev and Kamenev were not very good at politics. Stalin also had a lot of luck. He was therefore very clever and all of this meant that he won the struggle."

*Commentary.* Relevant and broadly accurate, but general ("friends in important jobs", "a lot of luck") and with no dates, names of posts, numbers or mechanisms. The last sentence asserts a conclusion ("very clever") that the paragraph has not argued. Table A: Level 3 (about 7). Table B: Level 2 to 3 (about 6). To move up: show *how* the post turned into votes, give precise evidence, and test it against another reason.

**A Level 4 paragraph (a reason explained, but no comparison).** "The most important reason for Stalin's victory was his control of the party machine. As General Secretary from April 1922 he sat at the centre of the Secretariat, and with the Orgburo he controlled appointments through the nomenklatura. Regional secretaries who owed their posts to him made sure that delegates to party congresses supported him. In 1924 the Lenin Enrolment, which brought about 240,000 workers into the party, increased the share of members who respected officials and did not take part in debates. By 1925 he had allies such as Molotov and Kaganovich in the apparatus, and in 1926 Kirov replaced Zinoviev in Leningrad. This meant that when Zinoviev and Kamenev opposed him at the Fourteenth Congress in December 1925, only the Leningrad delegation voted with them."

*Commentary.* Precise, focused and well explained, so Table B reaches Level 4 (about 11). It explains one reason in depth. But it never says whether the machine was *more* important than other reasons, and does not notice that the machine would not have been enough had Stalin's rivals united in 1923. Table A: Level 4 (about 10). The next level needs a **comparison and a judgement**.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The reasons for Stalin's victory can be tested by asking which of them, if absent, would most probably have prevented it. On that test the party machine was the main reason, but it was a reason that worked only because of the mistakes of his rivals and Stalin's skill in using it, and so the three cannot be fully separated.

The machine comes first. In April 1922 Stalin became General Secretary, a post that looked administrative but that gave him, with the Orgburo and the nomenklatura, control of who was appointed to every important party job. The regional secretaries so appointed chose the delegates to congresses, which elected the Central Committee, which elected the Politburo, and so a debate among the top leaders could be settled by votes that Stalin could influence. The pattern was clear at the Fourteenth Congress in December 1925, when only the Leningrad delegation, which Zinoviev still controlled, voted for the Opposition. The party's growth made this easier: the Lenin Enrolment of 1924 brought in about 240,000 workers, and membership rose from about 446,000 in January 1924 to over a million two years later, so that a large share of members had joined in the Stalin years and tended to defer to officials. Because the machine turned every dispute among the leaders into a vote, it was a necessary condition of his victory: without it Stalin, a poor speaker with a low reputation, could not have beaten Trotsky, a hero of the Revolution.

But the machine does not explain why the rivals did not use their own strengths, and here the weakness and division of Stalin's opponents were the second great reason. Trotsky's silence at the Thirteenth Congress in May 1924, when the Testament called for Stalin's removal, threw away the best chance to remove him, and his absence from Lenin's funeral made him look indifferent. He refused to use the army, which he commanded until late January 1925, and said that to do so would be a Bonapartist act. Zinoviev and Kamenev were the most culpable, for they helped Stalin to destroy Trotsky in 1923–25 and then found that they had no allies when they turned on Stalin. Bukharin, Rykov and Tomsky, the Right, helped Stalin to beat Zinoviev and Kamenev and then the United Opposition, and when it came to their turn in 1928–29 they would not form a faction, since they had voted for the ban on factions themselves. Each of the rivals had the means to stop Stalin at an early stage, when his power was less secure. They were divided by hatred and ambition and they thought him a minor figure. Their errors were not just accidents. They arose from the party's rules, which made organised opposition illegal, and from a shared belief that Stalin was a tool.

Stalin's skill turned these conditions into victory. He chose his alliances in stages, with Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky, with Bukharin's Right against Zinoviev and Kamenev, and then with a new majority against the Right, and each time he allied with the group whose policy was popular in the party at that moment. He presented himself as a moderate guardian of party unity and as 'Lenin's disciple', and his *Foundations of Leninism* made loyalty to Lenin a test that Trotsky's Permanent Revolution failed. His change of line was shrewd: he attacked the Left for threatening the peasants in 1925–27, and in 1928, after the grain crisis, took over the Left's industrialisation programme and used it against Bukharin. This tactical skill was not a mere extra, because without it the machine could have been used by anyone who held the post.

Repression and propaganda were less important than they might seem. Until 1929 nobody was shot for losing the struggle: Trotsky was exiled, and Zinoviev and Kamenev were readmitted. The ban on factions, expulsions and the OGPU's arrests of Oppositionists in 1927 made the later victories easier, and the Shakhty trial of 1928 showed what was to come, but these were the consequences of power more than its cause. The Lenin cult was useful to Stalin as a weapon, but the other leaders used it too.

Overall, the machine was the main reason, because it was the one condition without which none of the others could have produced Stalin's victory. But it was not enough by itself: it needed rivals who would not unite against Stalin in time, and it needed a man who understood the party's rules better than they did. The strongest judgement is that Stalin won because he held the one post that mattered and because the men who might have stopped him mistook him for a servant."

:::eg Paper 4 [30]: 'Trotsky's mistakes were the main reason for Stalin's rise.' Evaluate this view.
**What the levels want.** This is an "Evaluate this view" question with one named reason. Level 5 asks for criteria and a sustained balance: not "were Trotsky's mistakes important?" but *how much* and *compared with what*, with a judgement that tests the view.

**Plan.**
- Criterion: if Trotsky had made the best choices available to him, would Stalin still have won? This tests whether the mistakes were a *main* reason or a minor one.
- For the view: silence at the Thirteenth Congress (May 1924) when the Testament was read; absence from the funeral (27 January 1924); refusal of Lenin's offer of a deputy chairmanship of Sovnarkom (1922); arrogance and failure to build allies; *Lessons of October* (1924), which united the triumvirate against him; refusal to use the Red Army; the late alliance with Zinoviev and Kamenev (1926); the "Bonaparte" fear he did nothing to calm.
- Against: the ban on factions of 1921, which made organised opposition illegal; the machine (General Secretary, nomenklatura, delegates); his past as a Menshevik; anti-Semitism and the fear of Bonapartism, which were not of his making; the mistakes of Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin, who helped Stalin beat him and then others; Stalin's skill in alliances; the party's mood in favour of Socialism in One Country.
- Judgement: Trotsky's mistakes mattered in 1923–25 and made the victory easier, but they were not the main reason: the structure of the party and the choices of all the rivals made a Stalin victory likely however Trotsky had acted.

**A Level 5 answer (full).**

"The view can be tested by asking whether Stalin would have won if Trotsky had made the best choices open to him. On that test Trotsky's mistakes made Stalin's victory easier and quicker, but they were not the main reason for it, because the structure of the party and the errors of all the other leaders would still have favoured Stalin.

The case for the view is real. In 1922 Trotsky refused Lenin's request to become a deputy chairman of Sovnarkom, which would have given him a position in the government to match Stalin's in the party. In January 1924 he left Moscow for the Caucasus and was away at the moment when Lenin died and the funeral took place, so that the public ceremonies went to Stalin and Zinoviev. In May 1924, at the Thirteenth Congress, the Testament recommended Stalin's removal, and Trotsky sat silent, and historians offer different explanations: that he did not want to seem to seize power over Lenin's grave, or that he doubted that the Central Committee would back him. His arrogance cost him friends: he did not make the efforts that Stalin made to know the officials of the party. *The Lessons of October* (autumn 1924), which reminded the party of Zinoviev's and Kamenev's opposition in 1917, was a mistake of timing because it drove them to join Stalin in the 'literary discussion', in which Trotsky was portrayed as an enemy of Leninism. His refusal to use his command of the Red Army, because he feared being called a Bonaparte, deprived him of the only force that could have resisted the machine. Finally, he allied with Zinoviev and Kamenev only in 1926, when both had already lost their Politburo or Leningrad positions. The sum of these is a man who threw away chances.

Yet the mistakes did not have the weight that the view gives them, because most of them were constrained by a situation that Trotsky did not make. The ban on factions of 1921 meant that any organised criticism could be called factionalism, so that a leader who campaigned for support, as he did in the New Course of December 1923, was breaking the rules. The party machine, which Stalin had run since 1922, made the congress delegates dependable. Trotsky's past, as a Menshevik until 1917, made older Bolsheviks distrustful, and he was a target of anti-Semitism. The charge that he might be a Bonaparte was made by Zinoviev and Kamenev for their own purposes. Even if he had spoken at the Thirteenth Congress, it is likely that the Central Committee, which had a majority that supported the triumvirate, would have kept Stalin, as it did when Zinoviev asked it to. It is not at all certain that a Trotsky who attended the funeral or accepted Lenin's offer would have changed the situation.

Most important, the case ignores the other leaders. Trotsky's defeat was brought about by the triumvirate, and it was Zinoviev and Kamenev who decided to join Stalin against him. Stalin was then free to turn on them with the help of the Right, who helped him to destroy the United Opposition in 1926–27 and then found that they were next. If Trotsky was a poor politician, so were they, since each group helped Stalin to destroy the last and none of them acted until it was too late. The pattern of Stalin's rise, in four stages against four different groups, cannot be explained by the mistakes of one man, since it needed the mistakes of all of them. Stalin's skill in making the alliances, his moderate image and his change of policy in 1928 all counted as well.

In conclusion, Trotsky's mistakes were a real reason for Stalin's rise in the first stage, 1923–25, and they made the victory more complete than it needed to be. But they were not the main reason. The main reason was the combination of a party machine that Stalin controlled, a ban on factions that made opposition illegal and a series of rivals who each helped him to defeat the others. A better Trotsky might have lasted longer, but the likely result would still have been Stalin."

*Commentary.* The essay opens with a **criterion** (the counterfactual test), gives the case for the view in precise detail (the 1922 offer, the funeral, the Thirteenth Congress, *Lessons of October*, the Red Army), and then tests it against structural factors and the mistakes of other rivals. It reaches a judgement that depends on distinguishing the first stage of the struggle from the whole. Table A, Level 5: about 14. The evidence is precise and chosen to prove points (Table B, Level 5: about 13). Total about 27/30. To reach 30 it would need one more precise piece of evidence in the final paragraph.

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph
Question: "Assess the importance of Lenin's Testament in the power struggle after 1924." [30]

A student wrote:

"Lenin's Testament was written in 1922 and said that Stalin was too rude. It also said things about Trotsky and the others. It was important because it was about Stalin, who was the General Secretary. Stalin could have lost his job but he kept it. After this he got stronger and defeated all his rivals, so the Testament was very important."

**The level.** Level 2 on both tables (4–6). The facts are broadly right, but the paragraph **describes** and does not analyse. It says what the Testament contained, but not *what it did*: who saw it, what happened to it, and why it failed to remove Stalin. "Very important" is an assertion, and it is not clear why a document that Stalin survived was important for the struggle. The claim that Stalin "got stronger" after it is a statement of sequence, not of cause.

**The fix.** Make a claim, use the detail as proof, and set a limit. "The Testament was important mainly because of what happened to it. Dictated between 23 December 1922 and 4 January 1923, it criticised Stalin's power and rudeness, but it also criticised Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin and Pyatakov, so no one in the leadership wished it to be read widely. When Krupskaya gave it to the Central Committee in May 1924, it was read only to the heads of delegations at the Thirteenth Congress, and Zinoviev, who feared Trotsky more than Stalin, defended Stalin, and Trotsky said nothing. The danger passed, and Stalin then used his survival to appear to be vindicated. So the Testament shows how weak the opposition to Stalin was in 1924, more than it shows how important Lenin's judgement was: even Lenin's last advice could not remove a General Secretary who had the support of his colleagues." Now each fact is evidence for a point, and there is a limit and a judgement.

:::try Check your knowledge
Q: What posts did Stalin hold in 1924, and why did the General Secretary's post matter?
A: General Secretary (from 3 April 1922), a member of the Orgburo and of the Politburo (since 1919), with Rabkrin and the Commissariat of Nationalities earlier. The Secretariat controlled appointments through the nomenklatura, so he could place supporters in regional posts that chose congress delegates, who elected the Central Committee and Politburo.
Q: What were the main points of Lenin's Testament, and what happened to it?
A: Dictated 23 December 1922 to 4 January 1923: it warned that Stalin had too much power and (in the postscript) was too rude for the post, said Trotsky was very able but too self-confident, said that Zinoviev's and Kamenev's October 1917 opposition was no accident, and judged Bukharin and Pyatakov. Krupskaya gave it to the Central Committee in May 1924, it was read only to heads of delegations at the Thirteenth Congress, the Central Committee kept Stalin in post, and it was not published in the USSR until 1956.
Q: Why was Trotsky not at Lenin's funeral, and why did it matter?
A: He was on a train to the Caucasus to recover from illness and was told that the funeral would be on a day when he could not return; he later claimed Stalin had deceived him about the date. It mattered because Stalin and Zinoviev appeared as Lenin's heirs while Trotsky seemed indifferent.
Q: What was "collective leadership"?
A: The claim that after Lenin the Politburo would lead the party together, with no single successor. In practice it was a struggle between the seven members, and it ended when Stalin dominated the Politburo by 1929.
Q: What was the difference between Permanent Revolution and Socialism in One Country?
A: Trotsky's Permanent Revolution said that revolution in backward Russia could not survive without revolutions in advanced countries. Stalin's Socialism in One Country (1924, adopted 1925) said that the USSR could build socialism on its own, a patriotic and reassuring line that made Trotsky look defeatist.
Q: Who were the "Left" and the "Right" in the 1920s, and where did Stalin stand?
A: The Left (Trotsky, Preobrazhensky, later Zinoviev and Kamenev) wanted to end the NEP and industrialise quickly at the peasants' expense. The Right (Bukharin, Rykov, Tomsky) wanted to keep the NEP. Stalin sided with the Right in 1925–27 and took over the Left's policy in 1928.
Q: What happened at the Fourteenth Congress, December 1925?
A: Zinoviev and Kamenev attacked the party line and Stalin's growing power, but only the Leningrad delegation voted with them, while the rest voted for Stalin and Bukharin. Leningrad was then taken over by Kirov, and Zinoviev was removed from the Politburo in 1926.
Q: Why was the Shakhty trial important?
A: In 1928 53 engineers were accused of sabotage in the Donbas, 11 were sentenced to death and five shot. The charges were false, but it provided a scapegoat for economic problems, scared the old specialists and was the first of the show trials.
Q: How was Stalin's way of winning power different from his way of ruling in the 1930s?
A: Before 1929 he won by votes, appointments, alliances and expulsions: the defeated were exiled, demoted or allowed to recant. In the 1930s he used mass arrest and execution against the party itself.
