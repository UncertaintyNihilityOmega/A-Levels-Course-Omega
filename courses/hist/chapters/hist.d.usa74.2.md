## What is the question really asking?

"How great was the **impact**?" asks you to measure change. The danger is to confuse what the New Conservatives said with what they did, so test every claim against four questions:
1. **Did the policy do what it promised?** Compare the aim with the result (for example, a balanced budget with the deficit).
2. **Who gained and who lost?** The impact on rich and poor, on workers and employers, on different races and regions.
3. **Did the change last?** Did the next President and Congress keep it?
4. **Was it new, or was it already under way?** Deregulation and monetarism began under Carter and Volcker, and the Democrats helped to pass the 1986 tax reform.

The chapter takes the topics in the order of the syllabus: Reaganomics, its impact on Americans, Bush and the election of 1992, and the failure of the Democrats.

## Reaganomics in theory

### The end of the Keynesian consensus

From the 1940s to the 1970s both parties had accepted that the federal government should manage the economy with taxes and spending and look after the poor and the old. Stagflation broke that consensus (see [The USA, 1974–93 (1)](#hist.d.usa74.1)). If unemployment and inflation rose together, a Keynesian cure for one made the other worse, and the intellectual authority of the economists who had run policy since the 1960s collapsed. Three groups offered an alternative: **monetarists** such as Milton Friedman, who said that inflation came from too much money; **supply-siders** such as Arthur Laffer, who said that high taxes discouraged work and investment; and **free-market thinkers** such as Friedrich Hayek (Nobel Prize 1974), who said that the state itself was the obstacle to prosperity.

:::def Neo-liberalism
The set of economic ideas, revived in the 1970s, that favours free markets, low taxes, deregulation, privatisation and a limited role for the state, and that distrusts government intervention. In the USA its supporters were found in the Republican Party and the New Right, and in Britain Margaret Thatcher's government (from 1979) shared many of its ideas.
:::

:::def Reaganomics
The economic programme of Ronald Reagan's presidency: large cuts in tax rates, cuts in the growth of domestic spending, deregulation, a tight monetary policy to end inflation, and a build-up of defence spending. It was a mixture of supply-side, monetarist and free-market ideas, and its critics said that the mixture did not add up.
:::

### The key features

Reagan set out his plan in a televised address to Congress on 18 February 1981, the **Program for Economic Recovery**, which had four aims:
1. **Cut tax rates**, by 30 per cent over three years in the original Kemp–Roth plan (Congress passed 25 per cent), so that workers and companies would work, save and invest more.
2. **Cut the growth of federal spending**, especially on domestic programmes, to balance the budget.
3. **Reduce regulation**, which the administration said raised business costs.
4. **Support a stable money supply** to bring inflation down (here Reagan backed the Federal Reserve under Volcker).

The programme had an unadvertised fifth element: a large rise in **defence spending**, which Reagan believed was needed to face the Soviet Union (see [The USA, 1974–93 (4): changing relations with the USSR](#hist.d.usa74.4)). The members of the team, David Stockman at the Office of Management and Budget, Donald Regan at the Treasury, Murray Weidenbaum at the Council of Economic Advisers, together with Congressman Jack Kemp outside, did not fully agree about what would happen. The central claim, associated with the **Laffer curve**, was that cutting tax rates would speed up growth so much that revenue would not fall by as much as the rate cut, or might not fall at all. The sums only balanced if that claim was largely true.

:::key The theory of Reaganomics in one chain
1. Cut marginal tax rates (the rate paid on the last dollar earned), especially for higher earners and businesses.
2. People and firms work, save and invest more; growth speeds up.
3. Faster growth produces more taxable income, so revenue falls by less than the rate cut.
4. Cuts in domestic spending close the rest of the gap, so the budget is balanced by the mid-1980s.
5. Tight money breaks inflation; deregulation cuts costs.
**Weak links:** link 3 (revenue did not recover as promised), link 4 (Congress would not cut entitlements, spending such as Social Security that the law obliges the government to pay, or defence) and the combination of tax cuts with tight money, which produced a recession first.
:::

## Reaganomics in practice

### 1981: the tax and budget victories

Reagan had a mandate: a landslide, a Republican Senate and a gain of 33 seats in the House. His position was strengthened when he survived an assassination attempt on 30 March 1981, when a gunman, John Hinckley, wounded him outside a Washington hotel; the public sympathy helped him in Congress. He won the key votes with the help of conservative Southern Democrats known as the **Boll Weevils**.

On 13 August 1981 he signed two laws:
- The **Economic Recovery Tax Act** (ERTA) cut income tax rates by 25 per cent over three years (5 per cent in October 1981, then 10 per cent in July 1982 and 10 per cent in July 1983). The top rate on unearned income (income from investments and rents) fell from 70 per cent to 50 per cent, the top rate on capital gains (profit from selling shares or property) from 28 per cent to 20 per cent, business depreciation allowances (tax relief for the wearing out of machines and buildings) were speeded up and, from 1985, tax brackets were to be indexed to inflation, so that "bracket creep" would stop.
- The **Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act** (OBRA) cut federal spending in the 1982 budget on food stamps, school meals, Aid to Families with Dependent Children (AFDC), housing, jobs and training, and gave more control to the states through block grants.

It was one of the greatest legislative victories of any modern President, passed in his first seven months. But the plan was already in trouble. In December 1981 the journalist William Greider published in *The Atlantic* a set of interviews with David Stockman, in which the Budget Director admitted doubts about the supply-side arithmetic. And the economy was going into recession.

### The recession of 1981–82 and the change of course

With Volcker's Federal Reserve holding interest rates very high (the federal funds rate, the interest rate that banks charge each other, which the Fed sets, averaged 19.1 per cent in June 1981), the economy fell into recession in July 1981. Real output fell by 1.8 per cent in 1982, unemployment rose to 9.7 per cent in 1982 and 9.6 per cent in 1983 (annual averages) and reached 10.8 per cent in late 1982, the highest since the Great Depression. Farm incomes fell, steel and car plants closed, and the old industrial states of the Midwest, the "Rust Belt", lost jobs on a huge scale. Reagan's approval rating fell, and the Republicans lost 26 seats in the House in the midterm elections of November 1982.

The recession did what it was meant to do to prices: inflation fell from 13.5 per cent in 1980 to 6.2 per cent in 1982 and 3.2 per cent in 1983. But the tax cuts, a falling economy and the defence build-up now produced very large deficits. Reagan, who had promised a balanced budget by 1984, responded to the figures. In September 1982 he signed the **Tax Equity and Fiscal Responsibility Act** (TEFRA), which closed loopholes and raised taxes, partly reversing ERTA; it is often described as the largest peacetime tax increase in American history to that time. In 1983 he accepted a bipartisan rescue of Social Security, which raised payroll taxes and gradually raised the retirement age. And further tax rises followed in 1984.

:::warn Do not describe Reagan as a President who only cut taxes
Reagan cut taxes in 1981, but he also signed TEFRA (1982), the Social Security rescue (1983), further tax increases (1984) and, in 1986, a reform that closed many loopholes. A good answer says that he was a **pragmatist as well as an ideologue**, which is why the federal government did not shrink as the slogans promised.
:::

### Recovery, 1983–89

From November 1982 the economy recovered. Real output grew by 4.6 per cent in 1983 and 7.2 per cent in 1984, inflation stayed at 3–5 per cent, unemployment fell to 7.5 per cent in 1984 and 5.3 per cent in 1989 (annual averages), and nonfarm payrolls grew from 91.0 million in January 1981 to 108.8 million in December 1989, a gain of about 17.8 million jobs. In 1984 an advertisement for Reagan's re-election campaign proclaimed "Morning in America". The stock market boomed: the Dow Jones index rose until the crash of 19 October 1987 ("Black Monday"), when it fell by 22.6 per cent in a day. The crash did not produce a depression, because the Federal Reserve provided cash to the banks.

Was the recovery caused by Reaganomics? Supporters said that the tax cuts released the energy of the economy; critics said that the recovery was the normal rebound after a deep recession, helped by lower oil prices, by falling inflation after Volcker's policy, and by the huge stimulus of the deficits, which were a Keynesian policy in all but name. The honest answer is that all of these played a part, and that the argument shows how hard it is to separate a President's policy from the cycle.

### Deregulation

Reagan believed that regulation was a burden on business. In February 1981 he issued Executive Order 12291, requiring agencies to weigh the costs and benefits of new rules, and he set up a task force on regulatory relief under Vice President George H. W. Bush. He ended the price controls on oil in January 1981, completing Carter's plan, and in 1982 his Justice Department settled the long antitrust case that led to the break-up of AT&T on 1 January 1984. Deregulation of airlines, trucking and railways, which had begun under Carter, continued.

In finance, Congress passed the **Garn–St Germain Act** in October 1982, which let savings and loan associations (S&Ls, local lenders that took savings and mostly lent for houses) make riskier loans and invest in commercial property with fewer controls. Combined with weak supervision, this contributed to the **savings and loan crisis**: the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation counted about 1,300 savings institutions failing between 1980 and 1994, and the total cost was estimated by the General Accounting Office at about 160 billion dollars, much of it falling on taxpayers. In 1989 Bush signed a rescue law to close the failed institutions.

In environmental policy Reagan appointed advocates of less regulation: James Watt as Secretary of the Interior and Anne Gorsuch (later Burford) as head of the Environmental Protection Agency. Enforcement fell, there was a scandal over the use of the Superfund clean-up money, and Gorsuch resigned in March 1983. Yet the political reaction was strong, and Congress passed the Clean Water Act amendments of 1987 over Reagan's veto.

Read the figures with care:
- **Spending did not fall.** It was 20.7 per cent of GDP in 1980 and 20.3 per cent in 1989, and it rose to 22.3 per cent in the recession of 1982. The government in 1989 was about the same size, relative to the economy, as in 1980.
- **The mix changed.** Defence spending more than doubled in cash terms (national defence spending in the national accounts was 183 billion dollars in 1980 and 391 billion in 1989), and interest on the debt more than tripled, from 52.5 billion to 169.0 billion dollars. Domestic programmes for the poor, such as food stamps, AFDC, housing, training and school meals, were cut. But the largest programmes, Social Security and Medicare, were left largely alone (Reagan did try to cut Social Security in 1981 and withdrew the plan after a storm of protest).
- **Revenue fell.** It dropped from 18.7 per cent of GDP in 1981 to 16.5 per cent in 1983 and did not recover to its 1981 level, which is why the supply-side claim that the tax cuts would pay for themselves failed. The gap was the deficit.
- **The debt tripled.** Total public debt rose from 907.7 billion dollars in 1980 to 2,857.4 billion in 1989, a rise of about 3.1 times.

:::key Extent of the reduction of federal spending: the judgement
1. **Not achieved overall:** federal spending was about 20 per cent of GDP before and after; the deficit was never eliminated.
2. **Achieved in part:** domestic programmes for the poor and for the states were cut, and the *rate of growth* of non-defence spending slowed.
3. **Reversed in other areas:** defence and interest costs rose far faster.
4. **Why:** Reagan would not cut defence or Social Security, Congress (including the Democratic House) protected the programmes that voters valued, and the revenue lost through the tax cuts was larger than any cuts could cover. Stockman's own book, *The Triumph of Politics* (1986), blamed politics for the failure of his plan.
:::

### The Tax Reform Act of 1986

The last major step in the Reagan economic programme was a bipartisan one. The **Tax Reform Act**, signed on 22 October 1986 after two years of negotiation between the White House and Democratic leaders such as Congressman Dan Rostenkowski and Senator Bill Bradley, cut the number of income tax brackets and lowered the top rate: from 70 per cent in 1980 to 50 per cent in 1982 under ERTA, then 38.5 per cent in 1987 and 28 per cent in 1988, with a 33 per cent band for some incomes. To help the poor, it also raised the personal exemption (the part of each person's income that is not taxed), which almost doubled by 1989, and expanded the Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC, a payment to low-paid working families), and it took about six million low-income Americans off the tax rolls. It paid for the lower rates by closing many loopholes and tax shelters, and raised the share of tax paid by corporations relative to individuals. It is often seen as the high point of the neo-liberal approach, a simple tax system with low rates and a wide base, and it was accepted by both parties.

:::note Evidence: Reagan's first inaugural address
*Adapted from President Ronald Reagan, first inaugural address, 20 January 1981, West Front of the Capitol. The extract is shortened and every cut is marked; the words are the speech's own.*

"In this present crisis, government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem. … it is not my intention to do away with government. It is, rather, to make it work … to stand by our side, not ride on our back."

*Provenance and use.* A speech given to the nation and the world on the day Reagan took office, in the middle of stagflation, so it is evidence of his **aims** and of the mood he spoke to, not of what he later did. The first sentence is the most quoted line of the New Conservatism. The later sentences show that he did not promise to abolish government but to limit and redirect it, which helps to explain why federal spending was still about 20 per cent of GDP when he left office. Note also the words "In this present crisis": he was blaming government for the crisis of 1981, not for every problem of the century.
:::

## The impact on the American people

### Tax policies and budget cuts

Reaganomics affected different Americans differently.

- **Taxpayers.** Every income group received a cut in income-tax rates, but the largest cut in rates, and by far the largest in dollars, went to the highest earners (the top rate on unearned income fell from 70 to 50 per cent, and the top rate on capital gains from 28 to 20 per cent). Much of the gain for wage earners was offset by rising Social Security payroll taxes, which fall on wages and not on investment income. Reagan's supporters replied that lower rates raised incomes at all levels, and that the share of income tax paid by the top earners rose after 1986.
- **The poor.** OBRA and later budget laws cut or tightened food stamps, AFDC, school meals, housing assistance and public-service jobs, and a famous attempt to count ketchup as a school-lunch vegetable (1981, withdrawn after protests) became a symbol. Reagan argued that programmes of this kind encouraged dependency, and he spoke of a "safety net" for the "truly needy". Critics said that the cuts fell on families that were already struggling in a recession.
- **Investors and business.** The stock market boomed, companies enjoyed faster depreciation and a lower capital-gains tax, and a wave of mergers and "leveraged buyouts" made fortunes on Wall Street; the figure of the "yuppie" (young urban professional) became a symbol of the new money.

The table shows the central difficulty of judging Reaganomics. Poverty rose sharply in the recession (11.7 per cent in 1979, 15.2 per cent in 1983) and, although it fell during the recovery, it was still 12.8 per cent in 1989, higher than the 1979 level at the same point in the economic cycle. And inequality rose steadily through the decade, from 0.403 in 1980 to 0.431 in 1989, so that the benefits of growth were shared unevenly. Visible homelessness in the cities became a national issue, although estimates of its scale varied widely and were disputed.

### Policy towards trade unions: the air traffic controllers' strike

The Professional Air Traffic Controllers Organization (PATCO) had supported Reagan in 1980. In 1981 it demanded large pay rises and a shorter working week, and the government's offer fell short of them. On 3 August 1981 about 13,000 controllers struck. Federal employees are forbidden by law to strike, and Reagan, speaking in the Rose Garden, gave the strikers 48 hours to return. About 11,300 who did not (sources give 11,345 to 11,359) were dismissed on 5 August and banned from federal service; PATCO was decertified on 22 October 1981. Supervisors, military controllers and non-strikers kept the system working with reduced flights, and it took years to rebuild staffing. The ban on rehiring was lifted by Bill Clinton in 1993.

:::key The PATCO strike: cause or symptom?
1. **A signal.** Reagan showed that the government would enforce the law against a strike by its own employees. Private employers noticed: the use of permanent replacement workers in strikes became more common in the 1980s.
2. **A symptom.** Union membership was already falling because of the decline of manufacturing, foreign competition, the move of industry to the South and West, and employers' opposition. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' first comparable figure, for 1983, is 20.1 per cent of workers (17.7 million), and it kept falling.
3. **Reagan's appointments to the National Labor Relations Board** made the law less favourable to unions.
4. **A balanced view:** the strike hastened and symbolised a decline that had other causes. Its significance lies in the message, not the numbers.
:::

### Social issues: civil rights

Reagan's record on civil rights was controversial and mixed.
- **Opposition to busing and quotas.** He opposed court-ordered busing and affirmative action quotas, and his Justice Department, under Assistant Attorney General William Bradford Reynolds, moved away from the goals and timetables of the 1970s. In January 1982 the administration reversed the Internal Revenue Service's policy and said that it would grant tax exemption to private schools that discriminated; after a protest it retreated, and the Supreme Court held by eight votes to one in *Bob Jones University v. United States* (24 May 1983) that the IRS had been right to deny it.
- **Voting rights.** The Voting Rights Act of 1965 was due to expire in 1982; Reagan's advisers wanted to weaken it, but Congress extended it for 25 years, with a stronger "results" test, and he signed the law on 29 June 1982.
- **Martin Luther King Day.** He disliked the idea at first, partly on grounds of cost, but after the House and Senate passed the bill by large majorities (the Senate 78 to 22) he signed it on 2 November 1983. The first holiday was observed on 20 January 1986.
- **The Civil Rights Commission and the courts.** He replaced critics on the Commission on Civil Rights in 1983–84 and chose Clarence Thomas to chair the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. Congress passed the **Civil Rights Restoration Act** over his veto on 22 March 1988, to reverse a Supreme Court decision (*Grove City College v. Bell*, 1984) that had narrowed federal anti-discrimination laws.
- **Results.** Black unemployment, which was about twice the white rate, peaked at about 20 per cent in 1983 against about 8 per cent for whites. About nine in ten Black voters supported Mondale in 1984. The Reverend Jesse Jackson's campaigns for the Democratic nomination in 1984 and 1988 expressed Black anger at the administration.

Women also saw the change. Reagan opposed the Equal Rights Amendment, and it failed to be ratified by the deadline of 30 June 1982, but he appointed **Sandra Day O'Connor**, the first woman on the Supreme Court, in 1981. A "gender gap" developed in which women were less likely than men to vote Republican.

### The response to AIDS

The first cases of what became AIDS were reported by the Centers for Disease Control on 5 June 1981, and by the end of the year it was clear that it was spreading among gay men and drug users. The disease was named in 1982. The administration's public response was slow: Reagan did not mention AIDS in public until a press conference on 17 September 1985, and did not give a major speech on it until 31 May 1987. By the spring of 1987 about 36,000 Americans had been diagnosed and about 21,000 had died.

Several factors explain the delay and the later change. Many conservatives, including some in the New Right, viewed AIDS in moral terms and resisted frank public education; the budget was under pressure; and Reagan seems to have been moved by the death of his friend, the actor Rock Hudson, on 2 October 1985, the year when Congress doubled research funds. Surgeon General C. Everett Koop, a conservative Christian, published a frank report on AIDS in 1986 that urged sex education and condom use, and a presidential commission under Admiral James Watkins reported in 1988. The first drug to treat HIV, AZT, was approved in 1987. Gay activists formed **ACT UP** in March 1987 to demand faster research and treatment.

The key judgement is that the federal response was **slower and smaller than the scale of the epidemic required**, for reasons of ideology, stigma and money, and that it improved after 1985, partly because of activists, Congress and scientists, as well as the White House.

### Deregulation and the role and scope of the federal government

Reagan tried to change how federal power worked as well as how much of it there was. The **New Federalism** meant returning power to the states: OBRA consolidated dozens of separate federal grants into nine block grants, general revenue sharing with the states was ended in 1986, and federal aid to state and local government fell as a share of their budgets. A bigger 1982 proposal to swap the responsibility for Medicaid and welfare between Washington and the states failed. He also changed the courts: his appointments to the Supreme Court (O'Connor, 1981; William Rehnquist as Chief Justice and Antonin Scalia, 1986; Anthony Kennedy, 1988, after the Senate rejected Robert Bork in October 1987) moved the Court to the right over the following years.

:::key The impact on the American people: winners and losers
1. **Winners:** high earners and investors (lower rates, a booming stock market); most Americans in work after 1983 (falling inflation, 17.8 million new jobs); employers (weaker unions, fewer regulations); the Sun Belt states.
2. **Losers:** the unemployed of 1981–83 (10.8 per cent at the peak); poor families affected by budget cuts (poverty 15.2 per cent in 1983); industrial workers and farmers in the Rust Belt and the Midwest; trade unionists; many Black Americans and AIDS patients, who faced a slower federal response.
3. **A mixed group:** the middle classes, who gained from lower inflation and tax cuts but faced higher payroll taxes and stagnant real wages.
4. **A general judgement:** the decade combined faster growth with greater inequality, and Reaganomics did not "benefit only the wealthy", but it benefited them most.
:::

## Bush's domestic policies, 1989–93

### A different Republican

**George H. W. Bush** had served as Reagan's Vice President, and won the 1988 election, defeating the Democratic Governor of Massachusetts, Michael Dukakis, by 426 electoral votes to 111 (53.4 per cent to 45.6 per cent). He promised continuity and a "kinder, gentler nation", and in his acceptance speech in New Orleans on 18 August 1988 made the pledge that would shape his presidency: "Read my lips: no new taxes." He was a moderate, a former Congressman and CIA director with great foreign-policy experience, but he had little interest in what he called "the vision thing" in domestic politics. He faced a Democratic Congress throughout his term, and vetoed 44 bills; only one veto (on cable television, in 1992) was overridden.

### Fiscal policy and the national debt

Bush inherited a deficit of 152.6 billion dollars in 1989, a national debt of 2.9 trillion dollars, and the bill for the savings and loan collapse. In 1989 he signed the law that set up the Resolution Trust Corporation to close failed S&Ls. In 1990 the recession began (July 1990 to March 1991), the deficit jumped to 221.0 billion dollars and the Gramm–Rudman rules (a 1985 law that forced automatic spending cuts if the deficit passed set limits) threatened automatic cuts in spending.

After months of negotiation with the Democratic leaders, Bush said on 26 June 1990 that "tax revenue increases" would be needed, and on **5 November 1990** he signed a budget law aiming to reduce the deficit by about 500 billion dollars over five years. It raised the top income tax rate from 28 to 31 per cent and the tax on petrol, cut spending and set limits on future spending, with the rule that any new spending or tax cut had to be paid for. It was a courageous and responsible measure that many economists later credited with helping to bring the budget into surplus in the late 1990s, but it split Bush's party: conservatives, led in the House by Newt Gingrich, who had helped to defeat the first plan on 5 October, felt betrayed. His broken pledge was used against him by Pat Buchanan in the 1992 primaries and by Clinton in the general election.

### Social reforms

- The **Americans with Disabilities Act** (26 July 1990) banned discrimination against disabled people in jobs, public services, transport and telecommunications, and required reasonable accommodation. It passed Congress with large bipartisan majorities, and is often called the most important civil rights law since 1964.
- The **Clean Air Act Amendments** (15 November 1990) tightened controls on car exhausts, toxic emissions and the pollution that causes acid rain, and introduced a "cap and trade" market for sulphur dioxide. Bush had promised in 1988 to be "the environmental President".
- **Civil rights.** In October 1990 he vetoed a civil rights bill that he said would lead to quotas, and the Senate failed by one vote to override. A revised **Civil Rights Act of 1991**, signed on 21 November 1991, was passed after the controversy over the appointment of Clarence Thomas to the Supreme Court. He also twice vetoed a bill requiring unpaid family leave (it was signed by Clinton in 1993).
- The **Immigration Act of 1990** raised the numbers of legal immigrants admitted.

These were real achievements, bipartisan and lasting. But they were measures of improvement and not a programme, and by 1992 many voters thought that Bush had no plan for the economy or the cities. The riots in Los Angeles after the acquittal of police officers in the Rodney King case (29 April 1992), in which 63 people died, appeared to show a government without ideas.

## The election of 1992

### From triumph to defeat

In late February 1991, after the quick victory in the Gulf War (the 1991 war in which a US-led coalition drove Iraq out of Kuwait; see [The USA, 1974–93 (4)](#hist.d.usa74.4)), Gallup recorded 89 per cent approval for Bush, the highest ever measured for a President. By July 1992 it was 29 per cent. The economy explains most of it: unemployment, 5.3 per cent in 1989, was 7.5 per cent on average in 1992 and peaked at 7.8 per cent in June, and the recovery from the recession of 1990–91 was slow and "jobless". Bush himself was challenged in the Republican primaries by the conservative commentator Pat Buchanan, who won 37 per cent to Bush's 53 per cent in New Hampshire on 16 February 1992, and at the Republican convention in Houston in August the party's mood was dominated by the culture war (the clash between liberals and social conservatives over family, abortion and values).

### The candidates

- **Bill Clinton**, the 46-year-old Governor of Arkansas, was a "New Democrat" who had chaired the Democratic Leadership Council. He ran on the slogan "Putting People First", promising economic change, a middle-class tax cut, health reform and welfare reform, and he distanced himself from the party's left, as he did when he criticised a rap artist's remarks at a meeting of Jesse Jackson's Rainbow Coalition in June 1992. He chose another young Southern moderate, Senator Al Gore, as his running mate. His campaign team in Little Rock, led by James Carville, kept a sign that listed the message of the campaign: "Change vs. more of the same", "The economy, stupid" and "Don't forget health care". The phrase "It's the economy, stupid" became its slogan. Clinton survived stories about his private life and his draft record with a rapid-response organisation and a talent for town-hall meetings.
- **Dan Quayle**, Bush's Vice President, was a figure of ridicule for many voters after a series of gaffes, but he also became a lightning-rod of the culture war with his speech of 19 May 1992 attacking the television character Murphy Brown for her decision to have a baby on her own. There is little evidence that he cost Bush many votes, but his presence on the ticket did nothing to answer the questions about the economy.
- **Ross Perot**, a Texas billionaire, announced his candidacy on a television talk show on 20 February 1992, led some polls in June, withdrew on 16 July and re-entered on 1 October. His campaign, built on charts about the deficit and opposition to the North American Free Trade Agreement, attracted voters who distrusted both parties.

|---|---|---|---|
| Bill Clinton (Democrat) | 370 | 44.9 million | 43.0% |
| George H. W. Bush (Republican) | 168 | 39.1 million | 37.4% |

Turnout rose by more than five points to 58.1 per cent. Clinton won with only 43 per cent, the second-lowest share for a winner in the twentieth century, and Bush's 37.4 per cent was the lowest for a sitting President since Taft in 1912. Perot's 18.9 per cent was the highest for an independent or third-party candidate since Theodore Roosevelt in 1912.

:::key Reasons for Bush's defeat in 1992
1. **The recession and the slow recovery.** The economy was the main issue, and the voters judged Bush to have no plan. Of all the reasons, this one made the others matter.
2. **The broken pledge.** "Read my lips" cost him the trust of conservatives, who stayed at home or voted for Perot, and gave opponents a ready weapon.
3. **Bush's own weaknesses.** He was seen as out of touch and absorbed in foreign affairs; the high ratings after the Gulf War were not a basis for domestic support.
4. **Perot.** His vote came from Bush and Clinton voters in about equal numbers, according to exit polls, so Bush would probably still have lost without him, though in a closer election.
5. **Clinton's appeal and campaign.** A centrist message, a focused campaign on the economy and an ability to connect with ordinary voters.
:::

The Democrats won both Houses of Congress, so 1993 began with a Democratic President and a Democratic Congress for the first time since Carter. It was the end of twelve years of Republican control of the White House, and to understand why it took so long, we must look at the Democrats.

## The failure of the Democrats in the 1980s

The Democrats lost the presidency in 1980, 1984 and 1988, by 489–49, 525–13 and 426–111 in the Electoral College. Yet they held the House of Representatives throughout the 1980s and won back the Senate in 1986. So the question is why they failed at the presidential level, and why their strength in Congress did not carry over.

| Election | Democratic candidate | Electoral votes (Democrat–Republican) | Democratic share of the vote | What went wrong |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1980 | Jimmy Carter | 49–489 | 41.0% | Inflation, the hostages, a bitter fight with Kennedy, Anderson's 6.6% |
| 1984 | Walter Mondale | 13–525 | 40.6% | A booming economy; Mondale seen as a captive of "special interests"; a promise to raise taxes |
| 1988 | Michael Dukakis | 111–426 | 45.6% | A strong lead in the summer lost to a negative campaign; the "liberal" label; weakness on crime and patriotism |

### The "civil war" between Carter and Kennedy, 1980

Senator **Edward Kennedy** of Massachusetts announced on 7 November 1979, three days after the hostages were seized, that he would challenge the sitting President for the nomination. He represented the liberal wing, which wanted national health insurance and more jobs programmes and thought that Carter had abandoned the party's tradition. At first Kennedy led in the polls. Then the hostage crisis made voters rally round Carter, Kennedy stumbled in a television interview, and Carter won the first contests, in Iowa and New Hampshire. But Kennedy kept fighting, winning large states such as New York, California, New Jersey and Pennsylvania in the later primaries, and took the contest to the convention in New York (11–14 August 1980), where he lost a vote on the rules that would have freed delegates from supporting Carter. Carter won the nomination with about 2,100 delegates to about 1,150, but he emerged weakened.

The consequences were serious: the contest drained Carter's time and money; it highlighted his unpopularity among his own party; it made a Democratic attack on Carter from the left available to Reagan; and Kennedy gave only a lukewarm endorsement, while some liberal Democrats supported the independent candidate Anderson. The contest also left the party divided over what it stood for.

### Divisions in the 1980s: radicals and moderates

The party was an uneasy coalition of trade unions, Black voters, feminists, liberal intellectuals, Northern urban machines and white Southern conservatives. In the 1980s three tensions ran through it.
- **Liberals against moderates.** In the early 1970s the party had changed its rules to give more power to activists in the primaries (the McGovern–Fraser reforms, which produced the nomination of George McGovern in 1972) and, in 1982, created "superdelegates" (elected officials who were automatically delegates) to give the party's leaders more weight. The activists tended to be more liberal than the average voter. Liberals such as Kennedy, Mondale and Jackson wanted to defend the programmes of the New Deal and Great Society; moderates such as Senator Gary Hart (whose 1984 challenge spoke of "new ideas") said that the party must find new policies for a changing economy.
- **The Rainbow Coalition.** The Reverend **Jesse Jackson**, a civil rights leader, ran for the nomination in 1984 and 1988 and built a "Rainbow Coalition" of Black voters, labour and the poor. He won more than 3 million votes in 1984 and nearly 7 million in 1988, winning a number of primaries and caucuses (party meetings that choose delegates, in place of a primary vote). His success showed the party's dependence on Black voters, and the difficulty of keeping them together with more conservative whites.
- **The Democratic Leadership Council.** After the landslide of 1984 a group of moderate and Southern Democrats, including Senator Sam Nunn, Governor Charles Robb and the organiser Al From, founded the **Democratic Leadership Council** in 1985. They argued that the party was seen as too liberal on taxes, crime and defence and too close to "special interests" such as trade unions, and that it should adopt a "New Democrat" agenda of economic growth, opportunity and responsibility. Bill Clinton chaired it in 1990–91 and made its ideas his own in 1992. Jesse Jackson and others on the left attacked it as a betrayal of the party's principles.
- **"Reagan Democrats".** White working-class voters in the North, such as those in Macomb County near Detroit, had voted for Democrats for generations but voted for Reagan in 1980 and 1984. A study of that county in 1985 by the pollster Stanley Greenberg found that many of these voters felt that the Democrats favoured the poor, Black Americans and the unions over people like themselves.

### Mondale and the problem of modernising the party, 1984

**Walter Mondale**, Carter's Vice President and a Minnesota Senator, won the 1984 nomination after a close fight with Gary Hart and Jackson. He was a decent, experienced man who stood for the old New Deal coalition, and his problem was that he seemed to be the candidate of the party's past, linked to Carter's failures and to the interest groups, and not of its future. His choice of **Geraldine Ferraro** as his running mate, announced on 12 July 1984, was a bold step, the first woman on a major party's ticket, but questions about her husband's finances weakened the campaign. In his convention speech he said that Reagan would raise taxes and so would he, intending to show honesty about the deficit; Republicans used it to show that he was a tax-and-spend Democrat. Meanwhile the economy was growing fast, Reagan's "Morning in America" advertisements spoke for the mood of the country, and Reagan won 49 states, 525 electoral votes and 58.8 per cent of the vote. Mondale carried only his home state and the District of Columbia.

### Dukakis, 1988

**Michael Dukakis**, Governor of Massachusetts, ran as a competent manager (the "Massachusetts miracle") and, after the Atlanta convention in July 1988, led in some polls. Bush's campaign managers, Lee Atwater and Roger Ailes, then defined him as a liberal out of touch with ordinary Americans: they attacked his veto of a bill to require the Pledge of Allegiance in schools, his membership of the American Civil Liberties Union, and his state's prison furlough programme, which an independent group used in an advertisement about a convicted murderer, Willie Horton, who committed a rape while on furlough. Dukakis responded late, and a photograph of him in a tank in September made him look ridiculous. He embraced the word "liberal" only in October, and he won 10 states and the District of Columbia. His failure showed that competence alone was not a message.

### Continued weaknesses in the South

White Southerners had begun to leave the Democrats after the Civil Rights Acts of 1964–65. Barry Goldwater carried the Deep South in 1964, George Wallace won five Southern states in 1968, and Nixon carried the whole region in 1972; Carter, a Southerner, won all but Virginia in 1976 but only his home state, Georgia, in 1980. Mondale and Dukakis carried **no** Southern state. The Southern white voters who moved were attracted by the New Right's religion and patriotism, by Reagan's defence policy and by the tax cuts, and resented what they saw as the party's identification with Black interests and federal power. The Democrats could still win Southern seats in Congress, where incumbents were well known, but at the presidential level the old "solid South" was gone. This mattered because the Electoral College gave the Sun Belt more and more votes.

:::key Reasons for the Democrats' presidential failures in the 1980s
1. **Strong Republican opponents in good times.** Reagan's personal popularity and the boom after 1983 made 1984 almost unwinnable. In 1988 the same economy helped Bush.
2. **Weak candidates and campaigns.** Carter had lost faith, Mondale looked like the past and Dukakis ran a poor campaign.
3. **An unclear message.** The party could not decide whether to defend the New Deal state or to modernise, and it was seen as the captive of "special interests".
4. **Division.** The Carter–Kennedy fight of 1980, the quarrels between liberals and moderates, and Jackson's challenges.
5. **The loss of the South and of white working-class voters** to the Republicans, because of race, religion, defence and taxes.
**Ranking:** the Republican strength (reasons 1 and 5) set the conditions, and the party's weak message and candidates (2 and 3) decided each defeat. The party did not recover until it found a moderate Southern candidate in 1992.
:::

The New Conservatism had **its greatest impact on ideas and politics**: it made low taxes and distrust of government the common sense of the age, moved the Democrats to the centre, and shifted the courts. ## Worked examples: Paper 4 essays built level by level

The levels below are the real Cambridge levels for 2027. Table A (analysis and judgement, AO2) and Table B (supporting knowledge, AO1) are each out of 15.

:::eg 'Reaganomics benefited only the wealthy.' Evaluate this view. [30]
**What the levels want.** *Table A (AO2).* Level 3 (7–9): some analysis, argument lacks balance. Level 4 (10–12): good understanding, valid criteria, a supported judgement. Level 5 (13–15): consistently analytical, wide-ranging valid criteria, a sustained and balanced argument, a clear and supported judgement. *Table B (AO1).* Level 3 (7–9): adequate but descriptive in places; Level 4 (10–12): good range, mostly precise; Level 5 (13–15): carefully selected, fully focused, consistently precise.

**Plan (3 minutes).** "Only" makes the claim extreme, so the answer will test it. Criteria: (1) who gained, group by group; (2) on what measure (tax, jobs, prices, incomes); (3) in which period (1981–83 or 1983–89). Support: top rate 70 to 28 per cent, capital gains 28 to 20; payroll taxes up; cuts to food stamps, AFDC and housing; poverty 13.0 per cent (1980), 15.2 per cent (1983), 12.8 per cent (1989); Gini 0.403 to 0.431; the stock-market boom. Challenge: inflation 13.5 to 3.2 per cent, helping everyone; unemployment 10.8 per cent to 5.3 per cent; 17.8 million jobs; the 1986 Act raised the personal exemption and expanded the Earned Income Tax Credit; the poor also gained from falling prices. Judgement: it benefited the wealthy **most**, but not **only** them.

**A Level 3 paragraph.** "Reaganomics benefited the wealthy a lot. Reagan cut the top rate of tax from 70 per cent to 28 per cent so rich people paid much less tax. He also cut spending on food stamps and welfare, which hurt the poor. Inequality went up in the 1980s and there were homeless people in the cities. However, some people did gain because unemployment fell and 17.8 million jobs were created. So it did not benefit only the wealthy, but mostly them."

*Commentary.* Accurate and balanced in shape, with figures, so Table B is Level 3. But the argument is a list on each side followed by a vague "mostly them", with no criteria for judging benefit and no distinction between years. Table A Level 3 (7–9).

**Moving to Level 4: apply a criterion and test the claim.** "If benefit is judged by tax, the claim is strong. The top rate on unearned income fell from 70 to 50 per cent under ERTA in 1981 and then to 28 per cent in 1988, while Social Security payroll taxes, which fall mainly on wages, rose in 1983. But if benefit is judged by prices and jobs, the picture changes: inflation fell from 13.5 per cent in 1980 to 3.2 per cent in 1983, which helped every family on a fixed or low income, and nonfarm employment grew by about 17.8 million between January 1981 and December 1989."

*Commentary.* Two criteria are used and tested against evidence. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs a third criterion (period), an answer on the poorest, and a judgement of what "only" means.

**A full Level 5 answer.**

"The view is too strong. Reaganomics benefited the wealthy most, but it is not true that it benefited only them. I shall judge it by three criteria: the effect of taxes, the effect on jobs and prices, and the effect on the poorest, and I shall distinguish the recession of 1981–83 from the recovery after it.

On taxes, the case for the view is strong. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of August 1981 cut the top rate on unearned income from 70 to 50 per cent and the capital gains rate from 28 to 20 per cent, and by 1988 the top rate was 28 per cent. The cut was largest in rate, and by far the largest in dollars, for the highest earners, and the booming stock market of the mid-1980s enriched those who owned shares. Meanwhile the Social Security rescue of 1983 raised payroll taxes, which fall on wages and not on investment income, so many middle-income families saw most of their income-tax cut returned. The figures on inequality fit this: the Census Bureau's Gini index for households rose from 0.403 in 1980 to 0.431 in 1989.

On jobs and prices, however, the picture is wider. Inflation, which had been 13.5 per cent in 1980, fell to 3.2 per cent in 1983, and a family with no investments gained from stable prices as much as a shareholder did. The recession was deep, with unemployment at 10.8 per cent in late 1982, but after November 1982 the economy grew by 4.6 per cent in 1983 and 7.2 per cent in 1984, unemployment fell to 5.3 per cent by 1989, and about 17.8 million jobs were added. Those jobs, and the lower prices, went to people far down the income scale. The Tax Reform Act of 1986 also raised the personal exemption and expanded the Earned Income Tax Credit, so that many poor working families paid less.

On the poorest, the view has more force, though not all of it. The Omnibus Budget Reconciliation Act of 1981 and later budget laws cut food stamps, school meals, housing and Aid to Families with Dependent Children, and poverty rose from 13.0 per cent in 1980 to 15.2 per cent in 1983, more than 35 million people. But the rise began with the recession and the high interest rates that Volcker's Federal Reserve imposed, and not only with budget cuts, and poverty fell back to 12.8 per cent in 1989. It did not fall back to its 1979 level of 11.7 per cent, so the recovery left the poorest behind.

Overall, the view is not justified in its claim of 'only', because inflation, unemployment and the number of jobs improved for most Americans after 1983. It is justified in a weaker form: the benefits were distributed very unevenly, with the largest gains for those with capital and high incomes, and the poorest bore the cost of the recession and the budget cuts and recovered the least. The fairest judgement is that Reaganomics benefited the wealthy most, the working majority substantially, and the poor least."

*Commentary.* Table A, Level 5 (14): three explicit criteria; period distinguished; both sides tested against each other; a judgement that deals directly with the word "only". Table B, Level 5 (13–14): precise, selected detail (August 1981; 70 to 50 to 28 per cent; 0.403 to 0.431; 13.5 to 3.2; 10.8 per cent; 4.6 and 7.2 per cent; 17.8 million; 13.0, 15.2, 12.8 and 11.7 per cent). Total about 27–28 out of 30. About 700 words, 35 to 40 minutes.
:::

**A Level 2 paragraph.** "The Democrats lost the elections in the 1980s because Reagan was very popular and the economy was doing well. Also their candidates were not very good. Carter had a bad record, Mondale said he would raise taxes and Dukakis was in a tank. The Democrats also had arguments inside the party. So there were lots of reasons for their failure."

*Commentary.* Each reason is named, with a detail, but none is explained, and the last sentence is a statement and not a judgement. Table A Level 2 (4–6); Table B Level 2 (4–6) with some accurate but thin detail.

**Moving to Level 4: explain how each reason worked and compare.** "Reagan's popularity and the boom were the main reason for 1984, when the economy grew by 7.2 per cent and Mondale carried only Minnesota and the District of Columbia. But they do not explain 1980, when the economy was weak and Carter lost because of inflation of 13.5 per cent and the hostages, or 1988, when Dukakis led in the summer and lost because of a campaign that defined him as a liberal. *Commentary.* The reasons are explained and compared against the three elections. This is Level 4. To reach Level 5 it needs a ranking with a clear criterion, the divisions inside the party, and a sustained judgement.

**A Level 5 conclusion** (after paragraphs on each reason).

"Overall, the Democrats failed in the 1980s for reasons that were partly structural and partly their own. The structural reasons were the changing electorate and the strength of the Republicans. White Southerners and white working-class voters in the North had been leaving the party since 1964, and by 1984 the Democrats carried no Southern state, while Reagan's personal appeal and the recovery after 1983 made him almost unbeatable. These reasons set the ceiling on what any Democrat could achieve. The party's own failings decided the outcome of each election: Carter's weakness and the quarrel with Kennedy in 1980, Mondale's identification with the party's past and with a tax rise in 1984, and Dukakis's failure in 1988 to answer the charge that he was a liberal who was out of touch. The criterion of *necessity* shows this clearly. The loss of the South was a necessary condition of every defeat, because without it the party would have won some states in each election. But it was not sufficient, as the Democrats had held their position in Congress, and Clinton, a Southern moderate, showed in 1992 that a different candidate and message could win back enough of the South and the white working class once the economy turned against the Republicans. I therefore judge that the structural loss of the South and the white working class was the most important reason, because it made every defeat possible, and that the party's unclear message and candidates were the reason why the defeats were so large."

*Commentary.* A clear ranking with a stated criterion (necessity and sufficiency) and evidence from all three elections, and an answer that ends with a judgement. Table A Level 5; Table B depends on the detail in the paragraphs before it.

**The level.** Table A Level 2 (4–6): the answer names reasons and picks one as "most important" without a criterion; the claim about Perot is assertion, not evidence. Table B Level 2 (4–6): the figures are accurate, but important knowledge is missing (the recession, the unemployment figures, Bush's approval falling from 89 to 29 per cent) and one claim is wrong: exit polls found that Perot voters would have split about equally between Bush and Clinton.

**The fix.** Test the claim and rank. "Perot's 18.9 per cent was historic, but it is not the main reason for Bush's defeat. Exit polls showed that his voters would have divided almost equally between Bush and Clinton, and state exit polls suggested that he did not change the result in the Electoral College, except possibly in Ohio. The main reason was the economy: unemployment rose from 5.3 per cent in 1989 to a peak of 7.8 per cent in June 1992, and Bush's approval fell from 89 per cent after the Gulf War to 29 per cent in the summer of 1992. The broken tax pledge made it worse, because it persuaded conservatives that he was not one of them, which is why many of them voted for Perot. So Perot was a symptom of Bush's weakness, and not its cause." Now each fact is evidence for a ranked argument.

Q: Name the key features of Reaganomics and the weak links in its theory.
A: Cuts in tax rates, cuts in the growth of domestic spending, deregulation, tight money (Volcker) and a defence build-up. The central claim was the Laffer curve: faster growth would make up most of the lost revenue. Weak links: revenue fell (18.7 per cent of GDP in 1981 to 16.5 per cent in 1983); Congress would not cut Social Security, Medicare or defence; the mixture produced a recession and huge deficits.
Q: What did ERTA and OBRA do, and when?
A: Both were signed on 13 August 1981. ERTA cut income tax rates by 25 per cent over three years (5, 10 and 10 per cent), the top rate from 70 to 50 per cent, capital gains from 28 to 20 per cent, and indexed brackets to inflation from 1985. OBRA cut spending on food stamps, AFDC, school meals, housing and jobs, and created block grants to states.
Q: Why was there a recession in 1981–82, and how did Reagan respond?
A: Volcker's tight money (federal funds rate 19.1 per cent in June 1981) broke inflation but caused a recession from July 1981 (unemployment 10.8 per cent in late 1982). Reagan stayed with the Federal Reserve, but signed TEFRA (3 September 1982), a Social Security rescue (1983) and later tax rises, because deficits were growing. The recovery began in November 1982.
Q: How far did Reagan reduce federal spending? Give figures.
A: Not overall: spending was 20.7 per cent of GDP in 1980 and 20.3 per cent in 1989; the deficit was 73.8 billion dollars in 1980, 212.3 billion in 1985 and 152.6 billion in 1989; debt rose from 907.7 billion to 2,857.4 billion dollars. Domestic programmes for the poor were cut; defence (183 to 391 billion dollars) and interest (52.5 to 169.0 billion) rose.
Q: What was the PATCO strike and why was it important?
A: About 13,000 air traffic controllers struck on 3 August 1981; Reagan gave them 48 hours and dismissed about 11,300 of them on 5 August; PATCO was decertified on 22 October. It signalled that the government would enforce the law, encouraged employers to use replacement workers, and symbolised the decline of the unions (membership 20.1 per cent in 1983), though that decline had other causes.
Q: Give four points about Reagan's policy towards civil rights.
A: He opposed busing and quotas; the IRS/Bob Jones episode (reversed; Supreme Court 8–1, 24 May 1983); he signed the 1982 extension of the Voting Rights Act and the Martin Luther King Day bill (2 November 1983); Congress passed the Civil Rights Restoration Act over his veto (22 March 1988). Black unemployment was about twice the white rate.
Q: What was the response to AIDS and how did it change?
A: First cases reported 5 June 1981; Reagan mentioned it publicly on 17 September 1985 and made his first major speech on 31 May 1987. Reasons for the delay: stigma, the moral views of the New Right, budget pressure. After Rock Hudson's death (2 October 1985), Koop's report (1986), the Watkins Commission (1988) and ACT UP (1987), funding and public education increased.
Q: What happened to Bush's pledge "Read my lips: no new taxes"?
A: He made it on 18 August 1988. The deficit and the recession of 1990–91 led him to accept tax rises in the budget agreement of 5 November 1990 (top rate from 28 to 31 per cent; a 500 billion dollar deficit-reduction target; spending limits). Conservatives felt betrayed, and Buchanan and Clinton used it against him in 1992.
Q: Name two social reforms of Bush's presidency.
A: The Americans with Disabilities Act (26 July 1990) and the Clean Air Act Amendments (15 November 1990), which created a cap-and-trade market for sulphur dioxide. Also the Civil Rights Act of 1991 (signed 21 November 1991) after his veto of the 1990 bill, and the Immigration Act of 1990.
Q: Give the result of the 1992 election and four reasons for Bush's defeat.
A: Clinton 370 electoral votes and 43.0 per cent; Bush 168 and 37.4 per cent; Perot 0 and 18.9 per cent. Reasons: the recession and slow recovery; the broken tax pledge; Bush's lack of a domestic vision; Perot (though he probably did not change the result); Clinton's appeal and his focus on the economy; Quayle was a liability for some.
Q: Why did the Democrats lose the presidency three times in the 1980s? Give four reasons.
A: Reagan's popularity and the economy (1984); weak candidates and campaigns (Carter, Mondale, Dukakis); an unclear message, with the party seen as the prisoner of special interests; division (the Carter–Kennedy contest, Jackson, liberals against the DLC); the loss of Southern and white working-class voters (Reagan Democrats).

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent of the reduction of federal spending under Reagan.
A: Indicative content. Criteria: total spending; the mix; the promise. Spending was 20.7 per cent of GDP (1980) and 20.3 per cent (1989); the deficit was 73.8 billion dollars (1980), 212.3 billion (1985) and 152.6 billion (1989); the debt tripled to 2,857.4 billion dollars; defence rose (183 to 391 billion) and interest (52.5 to 169.0 billion). Some domestic cuts: OBRA 1981 (food stamps, AFDC, housing, school meals), block grants, the end of revenue sharing (1986). Limits: Social Security and Medicare protected (the 1981 proposal withdrawn); Congress resistance; tax cuts reduced revenue; Stockman's *The Triumph of Politics*. Level 5 distinguishes the rate of growth of domestic spending (slowed) from the size of government (unchanged) and judges that the reduction was limited to certain programmes.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Reaganomics succeeded in achieving its aims.' Evaluate this view.
A: Indicative content. Aims: end stagflation; faster growth; lower taxes; smaller government; a balanced budget. Achieved: inflation 13.5 to 3.2 per cent (1980–83); growth 4.6 and 7.2 per cent (1983–84); unemployment 5.3 per cent (1989); about 17.8 million jobs; top rate 70 to 28 per cent. Not achieved: balanced budget (deficit 152.6 to 290.3 billion dollars between 1989 and 1992); federal spending unchanged at about 20 per cent of GDP; the 1981–82 recession; rising inequality; the Laffer claim failed (revenue 18.7 to 16.5 per cent). Level 5 uses the stated aims as criteria and judges which were more important to the supporters and the voters.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the impact of Reagan's policies on trade unions.
A: Indicative content. PATCO (3–5 August 1981; about 11,300 dismissed; decertified 22 October); the message to employers (replacement workers); NLRB appointments; membership 20.1 per cent (1983), still falling; recession and loss of manufacturing jobs in the Rust Belt; deregulation of airlines and trucking weakened unions' bargaining power; real wages stagnant. Limits: the decline began before 1981 (foreign competition, the shift to the South and to services); unions still had political strength in the Democratic Party; the PATCO strike was illegal and had been chosen by the union. Level 5 judges the effect as symbolic and accelerating more than causal.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the response of the Reagan administration to the AIDS epidemic.
A: Indicative content. Slow response: first cases 5 June 1981; no public mention until 17 September 1985; first major speech 31 May 1987; stigma, moral views of the New Right, budget pressure; the budget requests were repeatedly cut by the administration and increased by Congress. Improvement: Hudson's death (2 October 1985); Koop's report (1986); the Watkins Commission (1988); AZT approved (1987); ACT UP (1987). Level 5 uses criteria such as speed, scale and effectiveness and balances ideology against the real difficulty of a new disease; it judges that the response was inadequate before 1985 and better, but still limited, afterwards.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Reagan's domestic policies did more to change the language of politics than the reality of government.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare what Reagan said with the figures on spending, debt and the size of government.
H: Then ask what really changed: taxes, unions, the courts, the Democrats.
A: Indicative content. Support: "government is the problem" (20 January 1981); spending 20.7 per cent of GDP (1980) and 20.3 per cent (1989); debt tripled; TEFRA and other tax rises; Social Security untouched; the Democrats moved to the centre (the DLC, Clinton). Challenge: real changes in the top tax rate (70 to 28 per cent), PATCO, deregulation, the shift in the courts, cuts in programmes for the poor, the end of the Keynesian consensus; Wilentz and Gerstle on the political order. Level 5 weighs the evidence by area and judges that rhetoric and the political agenda changed most, but real change in taxes and in the balance of power at work were also considerable.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Bush's domestic policies were a failure.
H: What did he achieve in 1990 that was lasting?
H: Judge them by what he promised in 1988 and by what he left in 1992.
A: Indicative content. Failure: broken tax pledge; slow response to the recession; deficit 290.3 billion dollars (1992); no domestic vision; vetoes of the 1990 civil rights bill and family leave; the Los Angeles riots (1992); the election defeat. Success: the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990); the Clean Air Act (1990); the budget agreement of 1990, which many economists credit with later surpluses; the S&L rescue; the Civil Rights Act of 1991; a Democratic Congress limited him (44 vetoes). Level 5 uses the criteria of promises, results and lasting effects, and judges that the policies were more successful than the voters' verdict suggests.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Ross Perot's candidacy was the main reason for Bush's defeat in 1992.' Evaluate this view.
H: Where did Perot's voters come from, according to exit polls?
H: Compare the effect of Perot with that of the economy.
A: Indicative content. Support: Perot's 18.9 per cent (19.7 million votes); Bush's 37.4 per cent was the lowest for a sitting President since Taft; Perot's attack on the deficit and NAFTA attracted Republicans. Challenge: exit polls showed that his voters would have divided about equally; state exit polls suggested that he did not change the Electoral College result (except possibly Ohio); the recession (unemployment 7.8 per cent in June 1992); approval 89 to 29 per cent; the broken pledge; Clinton's campaign and the "It's the economy, stupid" message. Level 5 judges that Perot was a symptom of Bush's weakness more than a cause of the defeat.
Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the impact of Reagan's policies on civil rights for African Americans.
A: Indicative content. Opposition to busing and quotas; the IRS and Bob Jones episode (1982–83); the Civil Rights Commission; Black unemployment about twice the white rate (almost 20 per cent in 1983); poverty; but also the Voting Rights Act extension (29 June 1982), Martin Luther King Day (2 November 1983) and the Civil Rights Restoration Act passed over his veto (22 March 1988); the courts. Level 5 uses criteria (law, economic position, political representation) and judges that the effect was limited and often negative in practice, with real changes mainly forced by Congress.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The New Conservatism changed American politics more than it changed American life.' Evaluate this view.
H: List the changes in politics (the parties, the courts, the language of debate) and the changes in life (jobs, incomes, unions, health).
H: Ask whether the political changes were themselves a change in life, because they changed what government did.
A: Support: the Democrats' move to the centre (the DLC; Clinton's 1992 campaign; his statement in the State of the Union address of 23 January 1996 that "the era of big government is over", showing a Democratic President accepting Reagan's argument); low taxes as common sense; the Supreme Court; the realignment of the South and the white working class; government as large as before. Challenge: inflation down and 17.8 million jobs; inequality up (Gini 0.403 to 0.431); union membership down; PATCO; deregulation and the S&L collapse; AIDS. A* judgement: the distinction is partly false, since political change altered life through taxes, unions and regulation, but the New Conservatism's reach was greatest on the way Americans thought about government.
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The failures of the Democrats in the 1980s were caused more by their candidates than by their ideas.' Evaluate this view.
H: Which of the three candidates was beaten mainly by the economy, which by the party's divisions and which by a poor campaign?
H: What did Clinton change in 1992, the candidate or the ideas?
A: Support: Carter's record and the Kennedy quarrel; Mondale's tax pledge and association with interest groups; Dukakis's response to Bush's attack and the tank photograph; Clinton, a better candidate, won in 1992 with similar issues. Challenge: the ideas also mattered: the party was thought to be tied to unions and welfare, soft on crime and defence (the DLC's argument); Clinton changed the ideas as well as the candidate (welfare reform, the death penalty, "Putting People First"); the loss of the South was structural. A* judgement: candidates decided each defeat, but ideas explain why each defeat was so large and why the party had to change in 1992.
