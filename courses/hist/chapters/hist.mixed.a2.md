:::why
Papers 3 and 4 are the A Level papers that ask you to do what historians do: judge an argument, and make one. Paper 3 gives you a historian's extract and asks what you can learn from it; Paper 4 gives you a big question on your depth study and asks for a sustained, balanced judgement. Both reward the same habit: decide your answer before you start writing, and use only the knowledge that serves it. This chapter gives you unseen questions to practise that habit, mixed across topics and options, closed-book.
:::

:::exam How to use this chapter
**You need:** the chapters for your own topics and option, a timer and paper. Do the Paper 3 section for the topic you study (or all three for extra practice of the skill) and the Paper 4 questions for the topics you have studied. You study at least two topics of one Paper 4 option; do the three questions for each.

**Method, for every question:**
1. Plan, then write closed-book. For Paper 3, a plan has four parts: the main interpretation (usually two aspects), the sub-messages, the approach, and the knowledge you will use to test each. For Paper 4, a plan has a judgement, three or four criteria or factors, and the evidence for each side.
2. Mark yourself against the levels (below), then open the answer. The indicative content is a guide: "accept any other valid responses" is on every real mark scheme.
3. Add a line to your error log: the level reached, what would raise it, and the fact you did not know.

**Timing.** Paper 3 is 75 minutes for one question of 40 marks: about 10 minutes reading and planning the extract, then about 60 minutes writing and 5 minutes checking. Paper 4 is 105 minutes for two questions of 30 marks: about 50 minutes each, including 5 minutes of planning, and a few minutes at the start to choose your questions from the three in your option.
:::

## Paper 3 practice: interpretations

### How the practice extracts are made

The real Paper 3 prints one extract of at most 600 words, with no author named, and you are never asked to identify the author. The practice extracts below are **summaries written for practice, in the course's own words**, of the arguments of named historians: each says which at the end, so that you can check what you read. They are not quotations. In your answers do not name the historian unless the extract does; write "the historian". Labels (traditionalist, revisionist, intentionalist, structuralist and so on) are credited only when the extract supports them and you say why.

**The question for every extract:** *What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of [the origins of the First World War / the Holocaust / the origins and development of the Cold War] to explain your answer.* [40]

### The origins of the First World War

:::note Practice extract A (about 180 words)
The war of 1914 was not the work of one guilty government. Europe was a system of five great powers and, in the Balkans, of small states with ambitions of their own, and the leaders in Vienna, Belgrade, St Petersburg, Berlin, Paris and London each took decisions that seemed prudent to them. Taken together, those decisions closed the road to peace. Serbian policy and the murder at Sarajevo opened the crisis; Russia's backing for Serbia and its mobilisation widened it; Berlin's promise of support to Vienna was one choice among many. The men who made them were not schemers but anxious statesmen, afraid for the safety of their states and ignorant of how others would act. To ask who was to blame is to look for a culprit in a crowd. The better question is how a crisis that could have been settled became a war, and the answer lies in following the decisions as they were made, with the uncertainty that the men themselves felt. That is the task of the historian, not to pass sentence.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Christopher Clark, The Sleepwalkers: How Europe Went to War in 1914 (2012). The words are not Clark's.*
:::

:::try Paper 3 practice: First World War, extract A
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract A (in the box above). What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer.
H: Find the contrast words ("not the work of one", "closed the road", "not … to pass sentence") and use them to state the main interpretation in one sentence with two aspects.
H: Test each aspect with knowledge: where does the evidence fit (several capitals, uncertainty) and where does it strain (the blank cheque, the German war aims)?
H: For the approach, ask what the historian is interested in, and what is left out or played down.
A: **Plan.** *Main interpretation (two linked aspects):* (1) the war came from the interaction of many powers in a system and of decisions taken in several capitals, not from a plan of one government; (2) responsibility is therefore shared and "who is to blame?" is the wrong question, the right one being how it happened. *Sub-messages:* Serbian policy and Sarajevo opened the crisis; Russia's support and mobilisation widened it; Berlin's support for Vienna was one decision among many; the leaders were anxious rather than aggressive. *Approach:* the whole European system, not one nation; the sequence of decisions in July 1914; sympathy with the decision-makers' uncertainty; a refusal to judge or blame; it implies a reply to the view that Germany was responsible.

*Knowledge to use (Table B).* **Supporting the interpretation:** Sarajevo (28 June); Austria-Hungary's fear of Serbia and its ultimatum (23 July, a reply on 25 July, war declared on 28 July); Russia's partial mobilisation (28–29 July) and general mobilisation (30 July), made before Germany's own; the Franco-Russian alliance, the Entente and the alliance systems (Dual Alliance 1879, Triple Alliance 1882, Entente Cordiale 1904, Anglo-Russian Convention 1907); war plans and mobilisation timetables (the Schlieffen–Moltke plan through Belgium, Plan XVII) that made delay dangerous; Britain's hesitation until the invasion of Belgium (4 August). **Testing it:** the blank cheque of 5–6 July, in which Germany encouraged Austria to act against Serbia knowing it might lead to wider war; the German war council of 8 December 1912 and the September Programme of 1914, which Fischer used to argue that Germany sought a continental war; the German ultimatums of 31 July and the declarations on Russia (1 August) and France (3 August); the Navy Laws and *Weltpolitik* as a cause of Anglo-German rivalry. A Level 6 answer explains the "system" argument and tests it: the knowledge shows many capitals acting, but also that Germany's choices of 5–6 July and 31 July were more decisive than the extract suggests.

*Time and place (Level 6 approach).* Clark's account belongs to the post-Cold War period and the centenary of 2014, when historians were less inclined to assign a verdict of guilt and more interested in the multi-polar system; it is also a reply to the Fischer view that dominated from the 1960s. Do not guess motives: say how the time and place may have shaped the questions. **Level 3** (9–11 for Table A) finds sub-messages and treats them as the interpretation, for example "the historian says Russia mobilised"; **Level 4** (12–14) identifies elements without explaining the whole; **Level 5** (15–17) gives a sound account of the interpretation, with some sub-messages treated as equal to the whole; **Level 6** (18–20) explains both aspects and the approach and tests them. For Table B, a Level 6 answer's knowledge is accurate, detailed and always used to test something in the extract.
:::

:::note Practice extract B (about 170 words)
A continental war became a world war because Britain chose to enter it. The German government feared the growth of Russian power, and in 1914 that fear was not irrational; but it is a mistake to read the plans of September 1914 back into the years before, because those plans were made once the war had begun and in the hope of an early victory. The war was not bound to come. Britain had no treaty obliging it to fight for France or Russia, and Sir Edward Grey, the Foreign Secretary, had allowed the military talks with France to build an expectation that he would not disown. His fears of German power were exaggerated. Had Britain stayed neutral, a short continental war would probably have ended with Germany the strongest state on the Continent, a result that Britain could have lived with and that would have spared Europe a long war, a revolution in Russia and the disasters that followed. The decision of the British to intervene was the great mistake.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Niall Ferguson, The Pity of War (1998). The words are not Ferguson's.*
:::

:::try Paper 3 practice: First World War, extract B
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract B (in the box above). What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins of the First World War to explain your answer.
H: What does the historian say turned a continental war into a world war, and what does he say did not make the war inevitable?
H: The extract is about a decision and a counterfactual. How does that show an approach?
A: **Plan.** *Main interpretation:* (1) Britain's decision to enter the war turned a continental war into a world war, and it need not have done so; (2) the war was not inevitable or the result of a long German plan, and the British intervention was a mistake that harmed Britain and Europe. *Sub-messages:* Germany's fear of Russia was rational; the September Programme belongs to the war, not before it; Britain had no treaty duty; Grey encouraged an expectation of help; a German victory in Europe would have been tolerable. *Approach:* the focus on British decisions and a willingness to ask "what if?" (a counterfactual); scepticism towards the view that Germany planned the war; a judgement that this was a mistake, which implies a reply to Fischer and the argument from the 1960s.

*Knowledge to use.* **Supporting:** the Anglo-French military conversations (1906 onwards) and the naval arrangements of 1912, of which many in the cabinet knew little; the Entente Cordiale (1904) as a friendship, not a treaty of alliance; Britain's decision on 4 August after the invasion of Belgium, whose neutrality was guaranteed in 1839, and Grey's speech to the Commons on 3 August; the September Programme (9 September 1914), drawn up when the war was already a month old. **Testing:** the Haldane Mission of February 1912 and Germany's naval challenge (Navy Laws of 1898 and 1900, HMS *Dreadnought* 1906), which gave Britain a strategic reason to fear German power; the Schlieffen–Moltke plan, which made the invasion of Belgium part of Germany's war plan, so Britain's intervention was almost forced by the plan; the German ultimatums of 31 July; and Fischer's evidence of the war council of December 1912 and the blank cheque. A Level 6 answer sees that the extract is a counterfactual about Britain and treats the claim about the September Programme as a part, not the whole.

*Level guidance.* **Level 3** summarises the extract as "the war was Britain's fault", which gets the main claim wrong; **Level 4** sees that Britain's role is important but not that the war's inevitability is also denied; **Level 5** gets both aspects but treats the counterfactual as a side-issue; **Level 6** explains the two aspects, the approach (counterfactual, a reply to the German-guilt thesis) and tests both with accurate knowledge. A common mistake in Table B is a list of long-term causes with no link to what the extract claims.
:::

### The Holocaust

:::note Practice extract C (about 160 words)
The men of Reserve Police Battalion 101 were not chosen for their beliefs. They were about five hundred reservists, mostly middle-aged working men from Hamburg, who had never been soldiers and were not the Nazi elite. In July 1942 they were sent to a Polish town and told to shoot Jews. Their commander offered the older men the chance to step out, and a few did; most did not. The explanation is not a deep hatred of Jews, shared by all Germans, nor simply fear of punishment, since those who refused were not punished. The men conformed to their group, did not want to seem weaker than their comrades, and in time grew used to killing. Orders, careerism and the habit of obedience did the rest. Ideology and propaganda played a part, but the men were ordinary, and ordinary men, in the right circumstances, can become killers. This is the most disturbing conclusion, and it is the one that history must face.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Christopher R. Browning, Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland (1992). The words are not Browning's.*
:::

:::try Paper 3 practice: the Holocaust, extract C
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract C (in the box above). What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer.
H: What does the historian reject as the explanation for the killers' behaviour, and what does he offer instead?
H: The extract focuses on one battalion. How does that choice, and the phrase "ordinary men", show the approach?
A: **Plan.** *Main interpretation:* (1) the perpetrators of the shootings were ordinary men, not especially fanatical Nazis or men chosen for their beliefs; (2) their behaviour is explained mainly by conformity, group pressure, obedience, careerism and habituation, not by a deep, shared hatred of Jews or by fear of punishment. *Sub-messages:* the battalion's make-up (middle-aged, working-class, from Hamburg); the offer to step out; those who refused were not punished; ideology had a part but was not enough. *Approach:* a close study of a small group's behaviour; an interest in the psychology and sociology of the killers, not only in Hitler and the leadership; a conclusion that is deliberately disturbing, and a rejection of the view that the Germans were uniquely prone to genocidal antisemitism (an implied reply to those, like Goldhagen, who wrote later). The label "functionalist" does not fit well, because the extract is about perpetrators, not the decision-making structure; use a label only if you can explain it.

*Knowledge to use.* **For:** the Einsatzgruppen from 22 June 1941, the Order Police and Wehrmacht units that took part; Babi Yar (29–30 September 1941; 33,771 killed in two days); about a third of the six million Jewish victims were shot; at Józefów in July 1942 the commander, Major Trapp, offered men the chance to step out; the Reinhard camps (1942) and the camp staff. **Against:** *Mein Kampf* (1925–26) and the antisemitism of the Nazi movement; the effect of years of propaganda and the Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935); the minority of eager killers in the battalion itself and the local collaborators (in Lithuania, Romania and Poland), which show that not only Germans took part; Goldhagen's reply (1996) that German antisemitism was "eliminationist"; the fact that most Germans were not perpetrators. **Level 6** explains both aspects, notes the approach, and uses accurate, detailed knowledge to test them: for example, Babi Yar's scale and the voluntary killers support the idea that ordinary men could kill, while the unusual commitment of some shows the role of ideology.

*Level guidance.* Level 3 treats the extract as "it says people obeyed orders", which misses that the historian rejects the claim that they had to. Level 5 explains the main interpretation but not the approach. At Level 6, a student must not rely on moral judgement: it is a historian's argument about causation to be tested, not a position to be admired or rejected.
:::

:::note Practice extract D (about 175 words)
The murder of the Jews of Europe was not the carrying out of a plan made by Hitler at the start. It grew in stages out of the working of the Nazi state itself. Hitler's regime was not a smooth machine but a mass of rival agencies, in the party, the SS, the army and the civil administration in the occupied east, each competing to carry out what it believed the Führer wanted. Hitler spoke often of destroying the Jews, but he rarely gave precise orders; he approved, encouraged and left the details to others. The policy moved from forced emigration to expulsion and then, when the plans for deportation to the east and to Madagascar failed and the ghettos filled with people they could not feed, to killing. Local officials looked for their own solutions, and each step made the next more likely. The war, and above all the war against the Soviet Union, removed the last restraints. The Final Solution was the result of a process of cumulative radicalisation, not of a blueprint.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of the structuralist school, in the manner of Hans Mommsen, "The Realization of the Unthinkable: The 'Final Solution of the Jewish Question' in the Third Reich" (1983). The words are not Mommsen's.*
:::

:::try Paper 3 practice: the Holocaust, extract D
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract D (in the box above). What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the Holocaust to explain your answer.
H: State what the historian denies and what he claims in its place.
H: Which label fits, and which phrases in the extract justify it?
A: **Plan.** *Main interpretation:* (1) the Final Solution was not the execution of a plan fixed by Hitler from the start; (2) it developed step by step from the competition and improvisation of the Nazi state's many agencies, under the conditions of war: a process of cumulative radicalisation. *Sub-messages:* Nazi rule was polycratic; Hitler encouraged but did not specify; policies of emigration, expulsion, Madagascar and the ghettos failed; local initiative mattered; war in the east removed restraints. *Approach:* focus on structures and institutions rather than on Hitler's ideas; sceptical of intentions as an explanation; a history of process; the label "structuralist" fits because of "grew in stages out of the working of the state", "cumulative radicalisation" and "not … a blueprint" (give the reason).

*Knowledge to use.* **Supporting:** the Nisko plan (October 1939) and the Madagascar plan (1940) that failed; the ghettos (Łódź April 1940, Warsaw November 1940) and their overcrowding; competing agencies and local proposals; T4 (from October 1939; about 70,000 killed by August 1941) as a source of the men and methods; the Einsatzgruppen (from 22 June 1941); Chełmno (8 December 1941); Wannsee (20 January 1942), which co-ordinated agencies and counted over 11 million Jews. **Testing:** *Mein Kampf* (1925–26), the 1919 letter and Hitler's statement of 30 January 1939 that war would mean the destruction of the Jews, which an intentionalist would use; Göring's written authorisation to Heydrich in July 1941; the lack of a surviving written order from Hitler (which supports the extract, but a synthesis historian would say decisions were taken in 1941 with Hitler's approval); the timing, which suggests a link to the war in the east, not only to structures. **Level 6** explains both aspects, and sees that the extract can lose Hitler and ideology if taken too far; at Level 5 the student explains the first claim but not the second.

*Level guidance.* Level 3 treats a sub-message ("the ghettos were overcrowded") as the interpretation. A student who calls the historian an "intentionalist" has misread the extract, and the label would fail the test: the extract says the opposite.
:::

### The origins and development of the Cold War

:::note Practice extract E (about 160 words)
The Cold War was the outcome of Soviet policy. Stalin's Russia was ruled by a Marxist-Leninist ideology which taught that the capitalist world was an enemy, and by a dictator whose suspicion of everyone made any lasting partnership impossible. At Yalta the Soviet Union promised free elections in Eastern Europe; within three years it had built one-party states there, by force and fraud, in Poland, Hungary, Romania and Czechoslovakia. The West did not seek a quarrel. It had demobilised, it hoped for a peaceful post-war order, and it answered Soviet pressure only when it could no longer ignore it, with the Truman Doctrine and the Marshall Plan. American leaders made mistakes, in part because they thought the Soviets could be trusted, but they were responding to the expansion of Soviet power, not creating it. The Cold War could not have been avoided by American concessions; it could have been avoided only if the Soviet Union had been a different kind of state.

*A summary, written for practice, in the manner of the traditional view put by Arthur M. Schlesinger Jr, "The Origins of the Cold War", Foreign Affairs (1967). The words are the course's own and are not Schlesinger's.*
:::

:::try Paper 3 practice: the Cold War, extract E
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract E (in the box above). What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins and development of the Cold War to explain your answer.
H: Which side does the historian blame, and what are the two reasons given?
H: What is the one thing the historian says the West did wrong, and how much weight does it have?
A: **Plan.** *Main interpretation:* (1) the Soviet Union, because of its ideology and Stalin's character and its expansion in Eastern Europe, caused the Cold War; (2) the West's actions were a response and the conflict could not have been avoided by Western concessions. *Sub-messages:* Marxist-Leninist ideology; Stalin's suspicion; broken promises at Yalta; one-party states by force; Western demobilisation; the Truman Doctrine and Marshall Plan as reactions; some American mistakes. *Approach:* centred on Soviet behaviour and ideology, and on political rather than economic causes; sympathetic to American decision-makers; an interest in intentions and responsibility; an implied reply to those who blame the USA. "Traditionalist" fits because of the Soviet blame and the Western response; say why.

*Knowledge to use.* **Supporting:** Yalta (February 1945) and its Declaration on Liberated Europe; Soviet control of Romania (March 1945), Poland (1947), Hungary (1947), Bulgaria (1947) and the Czech coup of February 1948; the Cominform (September 1947); the Long Telegram (22 February 1946) and Churchill's Fulton speech (1946); the Berlin Blockade (24 June 1948 to 12 May 1949); the Truman Doctrine (12 March 1947; 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey) and the Marshall Plan (June 1947; about 13 billion dollars); NATO (April 1949). **Testing:** the Percentages Agreement (October 1944), which shows that the Western powers also accepted spheres of influence; Stalin's caution in Greece; the atomic bomb (August 1945), the ending of Lend-Lease and the failure to agree a reconstruction loan for the USSR, which revisionists use; the Soviet fear of Germany and the huge Soviet losses in the war (estimates of about 27 million dead); the Novikov telegram of 1946. **Level 6** explains both aspects and the approach and uses knowledge on both sides; it notes that the extract's claim that the West did not seek a quarrel is tested by the evidence that it also used economic power.

*Level guidance.* Level 3 may say "the historian blames the Soviets" but explain only one reason. Level 4 identifies the Soviet blame and the Western response without explaining that the extract says concessions could not have avoided the conflict.
:::

:::note Practice extract F (about 175 words)
The Soviet Union was a ruined country after 1945 and the United States was the strongest state in the world. The Cold War is best understood from Stalin's side as the story of a dictatorship that needed a hostile world, and from the evidence of Moscow's own archives, which have now been opened. They show a leader who was cautious about risk but who wanted security through a sphere of influence in Eastern Europe, and who used the ideology of Marxism-Leninism to justify his control at home. Stalin was not a gambler, but he was not a man who would settle for less. The Americans, meanwhile, built their influence in Western Europe not simply by force but at the invitation of governments that feared Soviet power and wanted American money and protection. The new evidence does not clear the West of every mistake, but it shows that the origin of the conflict lay in the nature of Stalin's regime and in the interaction of two systems, with the Soviet leader's aims the more decisive.

*A summary, written for practice, of the argument of John Lewis Gaddis, We Now Know: Rethinking Cold War History (1997). The words are not Gaddis's.*
:::

:::try Paper 3 practice: the Cold War, extract F
Q: [40 marks] ★★★ Read Practice extract F (in the box above). What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it? Use the extract and your knowledge of the origins and development of the Cold War to explain your answer.
H: How is the extract's use of "the evidence of Moscow's own archives" a clue to the approach?
H: What does it say about the West, and does that make it simply "traditionalist"?
A: **Plan.** *Main interpretation:* (1) the Cold War arose mainly from the nature of Stalin's regime and its aims (security through a sphere of influence, ideology, the need for an enemy); (2) the conflict was also the result of an interaction between two systems, with the West's influence in Western Europe often by invitation, and the Soviet aims more decisive. *Sub-messages:* the USSR was ruined; Stalin was cautious but uncompromising; archives are now open; the US influence was by invitation; the West was not blameless. *Approach:* reliance on Soviet archives and a new evidence base; attention to the nature of the Soviet system and its leader; a balance that still gives more weight to the Soviet side; written after the end of the Cold War. The label "post-revisionist" can fit because of the interaction of two systems, and "the new Cold War history" because of the archives; each is credited only if you explain it from the extract.

*Knowledge to use.* **Supporting:** the Soviet losses and ruin of 1945; Stalin's caution (the Percentages Agreement of October 1944, the failure to support the Greek communists, the withdrawal from northern Iran in 1946); Soviet control of Eastern Europe from 1945 to 1948 and Tito's split (June 1948); American influence in Western Europe through the Marshall Plan (about 13 billion dollars to sixteen countries and West Germany) and NATO (4 April 1949) at European request; the Berlin Blockade (June 1948 to May 1949) and the Airlift. **Testing:** the revisionists' points (the atomic bomb, Lend-Lease, the Open Door), the Novikov and Long telegrams as two theories of the other's hostility; the Soviet bomb of August 1949; the Czech coup (February 1948). **Level 6** sees that the extract is not a simple blame of Stalin (aspect 2) and notes the effect of the opening of the archives after 1991 on the approach. Level 4 treats the archive point as a sub-message and misses the interaction argument.

*Level guidance.* The commonest weakness in Table B is a narrative from 1941 to 1949 with no link to the claims in the extract. Select from the list above only what tests an element of the extract.

## Paper 4 practice: depth study

### How the questions are set

Each Paper 4 question is worth 30 marks: 15 for analysis and judgement (AO2) and 15 for knowledge (AO1). The real paper has three questions in each option, one on each topic, and you answer two. The questions below follow the board's forms: "Assess …" or "'…' Evaluate this view." You get three for each of the nine topics, spread across the key questions. If you study two topics of an option, do all six; if you study three, do all nine and then sit one of the three questions from each topic as a timed pair.

**What a top answer does (Level 5, 13–15 on each table).** It sets valid criteria and judges against them throughout, weighs both sides, reaches a judgement that is sustained to the end and supported, and uses precise, carefully selected evidence that is wholly focused on the argument. The answers below give the indicative content; any other valid argument earns the same marks.

### European option: Mussolini's Italy

:::try Paper 4 practice: Mussolini's Italy, 1919–41
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The main reason why Mussolini became Prime Minister in October 1922 was the weakness of Italy's liberal governments.' Evaluate this view.
H: Separate the conditions that made it possible from the events that decided it.
H: Whose decision on 28 October 1922 was the most important?
A: **For (weakness).** Six governments in 40 months (Nitti twice, Giolitti, Bonomi, Facta twice); proportional representation (1919) produced parliaments in which the Socialists (156 seats) and Popolari (100 of 508) could not be brought together; the governments failed to answer the post-war crisis (about 650,000 dead, a huge debt, inflation, the "mutilated victory" and Fiume); Giolitti brought the Fascists into his electoral blocs in 1921 (about 35 seats); Facta hesitated in October 1922. **Against (other reasons).** Fascist violence: *squadrismo* from 1920 under the local *ras* (Balbo, Farinacci, Grandi), the breaking of strikes in August 1922; fear of socialism after the *Biennio Rosso* and the factory occupations of September 1920 (about 400,000 workers), which made the middle classes and many elites welcome Fascist force; the March on Rome (27–28 October; about 25,000–30,000 blackshirts against about 28,000 troops) as a bluff the army could have broken; the decisive act: on 28 October the king, Victor Emmanuel III, refused to sign the state of siege, then appointed Mussolini on 31 October, aged 39, at the head of a coalition. **Level 5.** Criteria: necessary conditions, triggers, decisive decision. Liberal weakness was a necessary condition, but not sufficient: Fascist violence made the threat credible and the elites' fear of the left gave Mussolini his allies; the king's refusal was the decisive act. A Level 3 answer gives a list of causes in the order of the chapter with no weighing.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'By 1940 Mussolini had created a totalitarian state.' Evaluate this view.
H: Mussolini's own aim was "everything within the State" (28 October 1925). Test it in four ways: reach, behaviour, belief, limits.
A: **For.** The laws of 1925–26 (Head of Government law, 24 December 1925; the press laws; the banning of parties and the Special Tribunal, November 1926) made him responsible to the king alone in form; the police state under Bocchini and OVRA, with informers and *confino* for over 10,000; propaganda (the press instructions, EIAR radio, LUCE newsreels, the Ministry of Popular Culture in 1937, the cult of the Duce); the Balilla (1926) and the *Dopolavoro* (1925; close to four million members); a single textbook (1929) and the professors' oath of 1931 (about 12 of about 1,200 refused); the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations (1939). **Against.** Power was shared with the king (who dismissed him in July 1943 after the Grand Council vote of 24–25 July, 19 to 7), the army, the Church (the Lateran Treaties of 1929; Catholic Action's defiance in 1931) and big business; fewer killings than in Germany or the USSR; only about a million radio licences in 1939; patchy commitment among the young, weaker in the south; reforms of customs such as the Roman salute were mocked; the party under Starace was a source of ridicule; support fell after 1938. **Level 5.** The test of reach, behaviour, belief and limits gives the judgement: totalitarian in aim and in the form of its laws, authoritarian with a dual power in practice; and Mussolini himself did not claim to have finished the job. A Level 3 answer lists the controls and concludes "totalitarian" or "not totalitarian" without a test.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which Mussolini's economic policies achieved their aims in the years 1922–40.
A: **Aims:** power, prestige, self-sufficiency and order; policy was driven more by politics than economics. **Success.** The Battle for Grain (launched 20 June 1925) raised output by about a third by 1935 and cut imports; land reclamation (Littoria 1932, Sabaudia 1934) was real if small; the state rescued banks and industry in the Depression through the IMI (1931) and IRI (1933), so that by 1939 it controlled most shipping, much of pig iron and nearly half of steel, and the IRI was the most lasting achievement; the Corporate State gave control of labour (Vidoni Pact 1925, Rocco Law 1926). **Failure.** The revaluation of the lira (the Pesaro speech of 18 August 1926; Quota 90 reached by December 1927) brought deflation, wage cuts and weaker exports; the grain campaign made bread dearer and harmed other crops, helping large landowners; autarky after sanctions (November 1935 to July 1936) was costly and ineffective; registered unemployment rose from about 300,000 (1929) to about one million (1932–33); real wages fell in the 1920s; the Corporate State was weak as a "Third Way". **Level 5.** Judge aim by aim: success for control and prestige, and for the state's role through the IRI; failure for self-sufficiency and living standards. Conclusion: more successful as politics and propaganda than as economics.

### European option: Stalin's Russia

:::try Paper 4 practice: Stalin's Russia, 1924–41
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons why Stalin defeated his rivals in the struggle for power in the years 1924–29.
H: Rank at least four reasons and say which carried the most weight at each stage.
A: **Reasons.** (1) *The party machine*: Stalin was General Secretary from 3 April 1922, with the Orgburo and the *nomenklatura*, so he controlled appointments; the Lenin Enrolment brought about 240,000 workers into the party in 1924, loyal to the machine. (2) *The rivals' errors*: Lenin's Testament criticised all the leaders (it was read to the delegations in May 1924 and not published until 1956); Trotsky, Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin each underestimated Stalin and quarrelled with each other. (3) *Tactics*: the triumvirate with Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky (removed as Commissar for War in January 1925); then Stalin and the Right (Bukharin, Rykov, Tomsky) against Zinoviev and Kamenev (Fourteenth Congress, December 1925); then against the United Opposition (expulsions 12–14 November 1927); finally against the Right (condemned April 1929). (4) *Ideas*: Socialism in One Country (1924, adopted April 1925) appealed to a party tired of waiting for world revolution, against Trotsky's Permanent Revolution. (5) *Propaganda and repression*: the Lenin cult and "Lenin's disciple"; the ban on factions; Trotsky exiled in January 1928 and deported in February 1929, though no leading rival was shot before 1929. **Level 5.** The machine was the main reason, but it worked because the rivals divided and underestimated him and because he used it with skill; ideas and propaganda gave the machine a justification. A Level 3 answer narrates the stages without ranking.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Collectivisation was a political success but an economic failure.' Evaluate this view.
A: **Political success.** The state gained control of the countryside and the grain: about three fifths of households were collectivised by 1932 and about nine tenths by 1937; dekulakisation (about 1.8 million deported in 1930–31) removed rural opposition (the "kulak" was a loose political label); grain procurement fed the cities, whose population rose from about 26 million (1926) to about 56 million (1939), and paid for industrial imports. **Economic failure.** The number of cattle fell from about 70.5 million (1928) to about 38.4 million (1933), sheep and goats from about 146.7 to about 50.2 million; the famine of 1932–33 caused about 5 to 8 million excess deaths (Davies and Wheatcroft: about 5.7 million), Ukraine about 3.5 to 4 million and Kazakhstan about 1.5 million; the law of 7 August 1932, blacklists and internal passports made it worse; Stalin's "Dizzy with Success" (2 March 1930) showed a retreat when about 58 per cent of households had been collectivised and the figure fell to about 23 per cent by June; the household plots (allowed in 1935) were more productive than the kolkhoz. **Complication.** The human cost is not only economic: historians agree that policy caused the famine and disagree about intention. **Level 5.** The view is half right: the political aims (control, procurement, a labour supply for industry) were achieved; but the economic cost was severe and long-lasting, and the same policy produced the political result and the economic damage, so they cannot be separated.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons for the Terror in the Soviet Union in the years 1934–38.
A: **Reasons.** *Stalin's role*: his suspicion and his approval of the execution lists; the murder of Kirov on 1 December 1934 and the decree that same day on speedy trials, a trigger; the wish to remove rivals and old Bolsheviks (the show trials of August 1936, January 1937 and March 1938, with Bukharin, Rykov and Yagoda among the 18 sentenced to death in 1938). *Security and fear*: fear of war and of spies (Tukhachevsky and seven generals shot on 12 June 1937), the need for scapegoats for economic failures, and the ideology of the enemy within. *The system*: quotas (Order 00447 of 30 July 1937 set targets of 75,950 to be shot and 193,000 to the camps; about 767,000 sentenced and about 387,000 shot under it), denunciation and the ambitions of local bosses; the NKVD under Yagoda, Yezhov and Beria. About 1.6 million were arrested and 681,692 shot in 1937–38; the decree of 17 November 1938 ended it. **Level 5.** Distinguish the trigger (Kirov), Stalin's decisions (the lists and orders), and the system that gave the scale (quotas, denunciation); judge that Stalin's decisions were the central cause, but the scale needs the system. A Level 3 answer lists the show trials only.

### European option: Hitler's Germany

:::try Paper 4 practice: Hitler's Germany, 1929–41
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Hitler came to power in January 1933 mainly because of the Great Depression.
H: Put the Depression, Nazi methods and the decisions of the elite on one scale and say which was necessary and which decisive.
A: **For.** The Nazis won 2.6 per cent in 1928 and 18.3 per cent in 1930, 37.3 per cent (230 seats) in July 1932; unemployment rose from about 1.9 million (1929) to about 5.6 million (1932); American loans were withdrawn and production fell by about 40 per cent; Müller's coalition fell on 27 March 1930 and Brüning ruled by Article 48 and deflation (30 March 1930 to 30 May 1932), which deepened the despair; the Communists (16.9 per cent in November 1932) and Nazis together held a majority of seats. **Against.** Nazi methods and appeal: Hitler's leadership, the SA (445,000 by August 1932), Goebbels's propaganda with tailored promises; the structure of Weimar (proportional representation, a President who could appoint chancellors and use Article 48); above all the decisions of the elite: Papen (June–December 1932) and Schleicher, and the Cologne meeting of 4 January 1933, then Hindenburg's appointment of Hitler on 30 January 1933 with only three Nazis in a cabinet of eleven. The Nazi vote fell in November 1932 (33.1 per cent, 196 seats), so there was no inevitability. **Level 5.** The Depression was the necessary condition (without it the Nazis would have stayed a small party), but it was not sufficient; the appointment of January 1933 was the decision of a small group who believed they could control Hitler.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ 'Nazi economic policy in the 1930s served the needs of war more than the needs of the German people.' Evaluate this view.
A: **For.** Schacht at the Reichsbank (from 17 March 1933) financed hidden rearmament through Mefo bills (about 12 billion marks, 1934 to March 1938); the Four Year Plan (September 1936, under Göring) and the Reichswerke Hermann Göring (July 1937) aimed at autarky for war; military spending rose from about 3 to about 23 per cent of output between 1933 and 1939; the self-sufficiency of food rose from about 68 to about 83 per cent; the unions were destroyed (2 May 1933), the DAF replaced them, hourly pay was frozen and hours rose to 43–47 or more; rationing began in late August 1939. **Against.** Unemployment fell from about 5.6 million (1932) to about 0.9 million (1937), through the autobahn, the Labour Service (compulsory from 26 June 1935) and rearmament; KdF holidays and the Volkswagen savings scheme (about 330,000 savers) offered consumer benefits; but the figures left out women, Jews and Labour Service men, and conscription began on 16 March 1935. **Level 5.** Criteria: whose needs; short-term and long-term; the claim that jobs helped the people but the pattern of spending served the military: success in jobs and in rearmament, bought with debt and shortages, and not in lasting prosperity or readiness for a long war.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which the Nazi regime transformed the lives of young people and women in Germany in the years 1933–39.
A: **Young people.** The Hitler Youth grew from about 108,000 (1932) to 2.3 million (1933), 5.4 million (1936) and 8.7 million (1939); the law of 1 December 1936 made it a state youth organisation and the decree of 25 March 1939 enforced the Youth Service Duty; the BDM, military training and the reshaping of biology and history in schools (the National Socialist Teachers' League; *The Poisonous Mushroom*, 1938); the Napolas and Adolf Hitler Schools; limits: Swing Youth, Edelweiss Pirates, boredom and evasion (the work of Kater and Peukert). **Women.** *Kinder, Küche, Kirche*; the marriage loans (1 June 1933, up to 1,000 marks, a quarter cancelled for each child); the Mother's Cross (16 December 1938), Lebensborn (1935); the birth rate rose from 14.7 (1933) to 20.3 per thousand (1939). But about 14 million women were working by 1939; the loan condition on leaving work was dropped in 1937, and the *Pflichtjahr* (1938) put young women into work, because the economy needed them. **Level 5.** Compare: the transformation was greatest for the young, through organisation and schooling, and limited for women, whose work was needed; the most persistent changes were in what the state controlled, the least in what people believed.

### American option: post-war USA, 1945–61

:::try Paper 4 practice: Truman, Eisenhower and post-war USA, 1945–61
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which Truman's domestic policies were successful in the years 1945–53.
A: **Success.** Truman won the 1948 election (303 electoral votes to Dewey's 189 and Thurmond's 39) and rebuilt the New Deal coalition; he vetoed the Taft–Hartley Act (it passed on 20 and 23 June 1947 over his veto); the Fair Deal (5 January 1949) won the Housing Act (15 July 1949; 810,000 units), a minimum wage of 75 cents (from 40) and Social Security extended in 1950 to about 10 million more people; Executive Orders 9980 and 9981 (26 July 1948) began desegregation of federal employment and the armed forces. **Failure.** After the 1946 midterms the conservative coalition of Republicans and Southern Democrats blocked health insurance, aid to schools, the Brannan Plan and the Columbia Valley Administration; Taft–Hartley was never repealed; the post-war strikes of 1946 and the end of price controls (14 October 1946) hurt him; no civil rights laws were passed; the Loyalty Program (21 March 1947) fed the Red Scare that McCarthy later exploited (Wheeling, 9 February 1950). **Level 5.** Use criteria (policy achieved, politics, effect on people): real but limited success; strongest where he acted alone (vetoes, executive orders, the campaign), weakest where Congress and the Cold War limited him; the Loyalty Program is the main mark against him.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'Eisenhower's greatest achievement as President was to maintain peace and prosperity.' Evaluate this view.
H: Test "peace" and "prosperity" separately, then set them against civil rights, poverty and the cities.
A: **Peace.** The Korean armistice (27 July 1953); no intervention at Dien Bien Phu (7 May 1954); the Geneva Summit (July 1955); restraint over Hungary and the Suez crisis (autumn 1956). But the "New Look" relied on nuclear threats ("massive retaliation", 12 January 1954), and covert action overthrew governments in Iran (19 August 1953) and Guatemala (27 June 1954). **Prosperity.** Growth and consumer spending; the Interstate Highway Act (29 June 1956; 41,000 miles); but three recessions (1953–54, 1957–58, 1960–61), unemployment about 7.5 per cent in 1958 and about 22.4 per cent of Americans in poverty in 1959. **Other achievements.** "Modern Republicanism": Social Security extended (1954), HEW (April 1953) and a minimum wage of $1 (1956); the Court's *Brown* decision (17 May 1954) which he did not push, but he sent troops to Little Rock (24 September 1957); the Civil Rights Acts of 1957 and 1960; his warning against the military-industrial complex (17 January 1961). He won 442 to 89 (1952) and 457 to 73 (1956). **Level 5.** Very effective by his own aims (peace, prosperity, order), less effective by the country's needs (race, poverty, cities); on civil rights, the advance came mainly from the Court and the movement.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which Eisenhower's foreign policy differed from Truman's in the years 1945–61.
A: **Continuity.** The same aim, containment: Truman's Long Telegram (22 February 1946), the Truman Doctrine (12 March 1947; 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey), the Marshall Plan (about 13 billion dollars, 1948–52), NATO (4 April 1949), NSC-68 (1950), the Korean War, and the Berlin airlift; Eisenhower kept NATO, added SEATO (8 September 1954) and supported West Germany's entry to NATO (9 May 1955); both leaders saw communism as a single threat. **Change.** Eisenhower's "New Look" (30 October 1953) cut conventional forces and relied on nuclear weapons and "massive retaliation"; covert action in Iran and Guatemala; refusal to intervene at Dien Bien Phu and over Hungary; opposition to Britain and France at Suez (29 October 1956); the Eisenhower Doctrine for the Middle East (5 January 1957); summit diplomacy (Geneva 1955, Camp David September 1959) until the U-2 affair (1 May 1960) and the collapse of the Paris summit (16 May 1960). **Level 5.** Compare using tests: aims, means, places and risks, results. Conclusion: the same aim and structure, different means and a wider range; continuity in strategy, change in method.

### American option: a time of challenges, 1961–74

:::try Paper 4 practice: A time of challenges, 1961–74
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Lyndon Johnson was a more effective domestic president than John F. Kennedy.
A: **For Johnson.** Kennedy faced a conservative coalition, won a narrow election in 1960 (303 to 219 electoral votes; a margin of 112,827 popular votes) and passed little: a minimum wage of $1.25, the Area Redevelopment Act (May 1961) and the Housing Act (June 1961), the Equal Pay Act (10 June 1963); Medicare was defeated 52 to 48 (17 July 1962) and the civil rights bill (19 June 1963) was not passed before his death. Johnson, after the landslide of 3 November 1964 (61.1 per cent; 486 to 52), passed the Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964), the Economic Opportunity Act (20 August 1964), the Elementary and Secondary Education Act (11 April 1965), Medicare and Medicaid (30 July 1965), the Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965) and the Fair Housing Act (11 April 1968); poverty fell from 19.0 per cent (1964) to 11.1 per cent (1973). **For Kennedy.** He set the agenda and gave the moral lead (the address of 11 June 1963); Johnson used the sympathy for the dead President and the huge Democratic majorities won in 1964. **Limits on Johnson.** The cost of the Great Society and of Vietnam, the riots, the white backlash and the loss of 47 seats in 1966. **Level 5.** Criteria: achievement against intention, scale, permanence and context. Kennedy was the agenda-setter and Johnson the achiever, but Johnson's achievements rested on circumstances Kennedy had helped create.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons why the USA escalated its involvement in the Vietnam War in 1964–65.
H: Separate the long-term policy, the means used to get Congress's support, and the triggers in 1965.
A: **Long-term.** Containment and the domino theory (stated by Eisenhower on 7 April 1954); SEATO; commitment to Diem, with about 900 advisers (1960) rising to about 16,300 (1963); the failure of the Strategic Hamlets and the coup against Diem (1 November 1963) left the USA with a weak ally. **Means.** The Tonkin incidents (2 and 4 August 1964) and the Resolution (7 August 1964; 416 to 0 in the House and 88 to 2 in the Senate), which gave Johnson wide powers. **Triggers.** The attack at Pleiku (7 February 1965), Operation Rolling Thunder (from 2 March 1965), the Marines at Da Nang (8 March), the decision of 28 July 1965 to send 125,000 troops, about 184,000 by the end of 1965. **Johnson's own reasons.** Credibility, the fear of being the first President to lose a war, advisers who believed that air power and attrition would work, and the wish to protect the Great Society. **Level 5.** Rank: the commitment of the 1950s created the problem; the Resolution gave the means; Pleiku and the weakness of the South gave the occasion; Johnson's decision turned an advisory commitment into a war.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that the federal government, rather than the civil rights movement, did most to bring about progress in civil rights between 1961 and 1968.

### American option: the USA, 1974–93

:::try Paper 4 practice: The USA, 1974–93
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons why Jimmy Carter lost the 1980 presidential election.
A: **The economy.** Stagflation: inflation of 13.5 per cent in 1980 and a "misery index" of 20.6; the second oil shock (1979); Volcker's high interest rates (6 October 1979). **Leadership.** Carter's difficult relations with Congress; his "crisis of confidence" speech (15 July 1979), after which his approval, about 75 per cent in 1977, had fallen to about 28 per cent; the challenge from Edward Kennedy (7 November 1979) within his own party. **Foreign affairs.** The seizure of the embassy in Tehran (4 November 1979; 52 Americans held for 444 days), the failed rescue (Eagle Claw, 24–25 April 1980, eight dead), Afghanistan (December 1979) and the failure to ratify SALT II. **Reagan and the New Right.** Reagan's appeal, supply-side ideas, the Moral Majority (1979), Proposition 13 (1978) and the conservative shift in the South and among working-class whites. Result: Reagan 489, Carter 49, with 50.7, 41.0 and 6.6 per cent (Anderson). **Level 5.** Rank: stagflation and the hostage crisis were decisive; Carter's weak leadership turned problems into blame; the New Right turned the vote into a change of direction. A Level 3 answer lists the hostage crisis and the economy with no ranking.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the extent to which Reagan's economic policies benefited the USA in the 1980s.
A: **Benefits.** Tax cuts and spending cuts (ERTA and OBRA, 13 August 1981); inflation fell; after the recession of July 1981 to November 1982 (unemployment 10.8 per cent) growth recovered (4.6 per cent in 1983, 7.2 per cent in 1984); about 17.8 million jobs; the Tax Reform Act (22 October 1986; top rate 28 per cent in 1988); a shift in the balance of ideas towards the market. **Costs.** Deficits of 73.8 (1980), 212.3 (1985) and 152.6 billion dollars (1989), with debt from 907.7 billion to 2,857.4 billion dollars; defence spending rising from 183 to 391 billion; poverty 13.0 per cent (1980), 15.2 (1983) and 12.8 (1989); inequality rising (Gini 0.403 to 0.431); the PATCO strike (3–5 August 1981; about 11,300 dismissed) and union membership falling to 20.1 per cent (1983); the savings and loan crisis and Black Monday (19 October 1987). Federal spending was 20.7 per cent of GDP in 1980 and 20.3 per cent in 1989, so government did not shrink. **Level 5.** Judge by group and by time: the greatest benefit was to those on high incomes and to confidence in the market; the impact on the economy was moderate; the effect on the size of government was least.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons for the improvement in relations between the USA and the USSR in the 1980s.
H: Think about who changed: Reagan, Gorbachev, or both. Separate the improvement in relations from the end of the Cold War.

### International option: the Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe

:::try Paper 4 practice: The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953–91
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the extent to which Soviet control of Eastern Europe in the years 1953–68 depended on force.
H: List the occasions when force was used, and then the other means of control. Which were present every time?
A: **Force.** East Germany, 16–17 June 1953: a rising ended by Soviet tanks, at least 55 dead; Hungary, 1956: Nagy's withdrawal from the Warsaw Pact (1 November) was followed by Operation Whirlwind on 4 November, with about 2,500–3,000 Hungarians and about 700 Soviet soldiers killed, about 200,000 refugees and Nagy hanged on 16 June 1958; the Berlin Wall (13 August 1961), built because about 2.7 million had left East Germany in 1949–61; Czechoslovakia: the invasion of 20–21 August 1968 by the USSR, Poland, Bulgaria and Hungary (137 Czechs and Slovaks killed), the Moscow Protocol (26 August) and Husák's replacement of Dubček (17 April 1969); the Brezhnev Doctrine justified it. **Other means.** The party's monopoly and the *nomenklatura*; secret police (the Stasi from 1950, the ÁVH, the UB and SB, the StB); censorship (partly offset by Western radio); closed borders; Comecon (1949) and the Warsaw Pact (14 May 1955; a Joint Command in Moscow); some flexibility: Moscow accepted Gomułka in the Polish October of 1956 and living standards rose slowly after 1953. **Level 5.** Criteria: how often, how much, and why it was enough. Control rested on the party monopoly, the Soviet army and the Pact, and the will to use force, never on consent; but force was used only when the party itself lost control, and the daily work was done by the police and the party.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Evaluate the view that the Brezhnev years (1964–82) were a period of stability rather than stagnation.
A: **Stability.** Brezhnev's policy of "stability of cadres" and "developed socialism"; détente (SALT I, 26 May 1972; Helsinki, 1 August 1975) which secured frontiers, parity and trade; slowly rising living standards; the satellites stayed quiet after 1968 (Husák's normalisation, Gierek's debt-funded boom, Honecker's social policy, Kádár's New Economic Mechanism). **Stagnation.** The 1965 Kosygin reform was blocked; growth fell from about 4.9 per cent a year (1960s) to about 2.5 (1970s) and about 1.8 (1980–85) on Western estimates; the Tenth Five-Year Plan (1976–80) missed its targets; defence took about 9–17 per cent of GNP; a second economy, corruption (the cotton affair) and imports of oil and grain filled the gap; a gerontocracy (Suslov 79, Kosygin 76 and Ustinov 76 at death); détente ended with Angola, the SS-20s and the invasion of Afghanistan (24–27 December 1979); the satellites relied on debt instead of reform. **Level 5.** The two descriptions are two sides of one system: the stability of the elite produced the stagnation of the economy. Judge by criteria (politics, economy, foreign policy, satellites): stable in politics and the empire for a time, stagnant in the economy, and the label itself is debated (Bacon and Sandle, Kotkin, Brown).

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The collapse of Soviet control in Eastern Europe in 1989 was mainly the result of Gorbachev's decisions.' Evaluate this view.
H: Compare 1989 with 1956 and 1968: what was different, and who decided?
A: **For.** Gorbachev's "new thinking" and his UN speech (7 December 1988), the speech at Strasbourg (6 July 1989) and the so-called "Sinatra Doctrine" (25 October 1989): the satellites could choose their own way; he refused to send troops, unlike Hungary in 1956 and Czechoslovakia in 1968, so each regime that fell in 1989 (Hungary opening its border on 11 September; the Wall, 9 November; Czechoslovakia, 17 November; Bulgaria, 10 November) did so without Soviet force. **Against.** Solidarity (the Gdańsk Agreement, 31 August 1980, about 10 million members by September 1981; martial law, 13 December 1981) was the origin and model: the Round Table (6 February to 5 April 1989) and the elections of 4 June 1989 (161 and 99 seats) began the sequence; the economic failure and the debt of the satellites (Poland's debt of more than 20 billion dollars); the Church and John Paul II; Helsinki, Western radio and Ostpolitik; the courage of the people in the streets of Leipzig, Prague and Timisoara; and the fact that Romania's regime (Ceaușescu shot on 25 December) used force and fell anyway. **Level 5.** Distinguish conditions from decisions: economic failure and the example of Solidarity were the conditions, Gorbachev's decision not to use force was the necessary cause in 1989, and popular action decided the timing in each country.

### International option: end of minority rule

:::try Paper 4 practice: End of minority rule in South Africa and Zimbabwe, 1948–94
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that Sharpeville in 1960 was a turning point in opposition to apartheid.
H: A turning point in what: the methods of the opposition, the response of the state, or the attitude of the world?
A: **For.** On 21 March 1960 police shot into a crowd at Sharpeville during the PAC's pass campaign (69 dead by the police count, at least 91 on later research), and at Langa; a state of emergency was declared on 30 March; the ANC and PAC were banned on 8 April and Sobukwe was jailed; the failure of peaceful protest led to the armed struggle (*Umkhonto we Sizwe* launched on 16 December 1961; Mandela arrested on 5 August 1962; the Rivonia raid of 11 July 1963; life sentences on 12 June 1964); the UN Security Council passed Resolution 134 (1 April 1960); South Africa left the Commonwealth (1961) and the Anti-Apartheid Movement grew. **Against.** Resistance had a long run-up (the Defiance Campaign, 1952; the Freedom Charter, 1955; the women's march, 1956; the Treason Trial, 1956–61); in the 1960s repression worked in the short term and separate development went on (Verwoerd, Transkei self-government 1963); the greater turning points came later: Black Consciousness (SASO, July 1969; Biko), Soweto (16 June 1976) and the UDF (1983). **Level 5.** Sharpeville was a turning point in method and in outside attention, but not in the balance of power; Soweto was the turning point in mass resistance; resistance grew and changed form while the state's power stayed intact for decades.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'International pressure was the main reason why apartheid ended in 1990–94.' Evaluate this view.

International pressure was a main reason, but not the main reason.

Q: [30 marks] ★★ Assess the reasons why white minority rule in Rhodesia ended in 1980.

### International option: the route to independence

:::try Paper 4 practice: The route to independence: Malaysia and Indonesia, c.1909–67
Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Evaluate the view that British rule did more to divide than to unite the peoples of Malaya before 1941.
H: Separate what the British intended from what the system did.
A: **Divide.** Three kinds of rule (the Straits Settlements, a Crown colony from 1867; the Federated Malay States with Residents, 1895–96; the Unfederated Malay States with Advisers, the north from the Bangkok Treaty of 10 March 1909 and Johor from 1914); separate jobs, schools and places for Malays, Chinese and Indians; the protection of Malays (the Malay Reservation Enactment, 1913); the census of 1947 showed 49.5 per cent Malays, 38.4 per cent Chinese and 10.8 per cent Indians living largely in different economic worlds. **Unite.** A common administration in the Federated States, railways and roads, English-language schooling for some, and Clementi's plan (High Commissioner 1930–34; decentralisation at Sri Menanti in August 1931) as a step towards a wider union, which failed because of opposition from business (85 of 111 on the customs union), Chinese opinion and the Unfederated rulers. **Nationalism.** Before 1941 it was narrow and communal (*Kaum Muda*, 1906–08; the Kesatuan Melayu Singapura, 1926; the KMM, 1938, with about sixty members by 1945). **Level 5.** The effect of the system was division, but much of it came from protection, the economy and habit more than from a plan to divide; the British made no serious attempt at unity before 1941 because it cost too much and threatened the Malay rulers.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ Assess the reasons why Malaya achieved independence in 1957.
A: **British decolonisation and costs.** After the failure of the Malayan Union (1 April 1946), which Malay opposition under UMNO (May 1946) forced the British to drop, the Federation of Malaya (1 February 1948) restored the sultans; British aims shifted to leaving on terms that protected their interests. **The Emergency** (June 1948 to 1960): the Briggs Plan (about 500,000 resettled in about 450 New Villages), Templer's "hearts and minds" (from 1952) and the promise of independence took the nationalist cause away from the communists, who had about 7,000 fighters, about 90 per cent of them Chinese. **The Alliance.** The UMNO–MCA partnership (1952), joined by the MIC (1954), won 51 of 52 seats on 81.7 per cent of the vote (27 July 1955) and showed that Malays, Chinese and Indians could co-operate under Tunku Abdul Rahman; the London talks (18 January to 6 February 1956), the Reid Commission and the Merdeka Constitution (Agong, Islam, Malay, citizenship, Article 153) were the bargain; Merdeka on 31 August 1957. **Level 5.** The causes were linked: British decolonisation and the fear of communism gave the opportunity; the Alliance's inter-communal bargain made independence safe to grant; the Emergency showed that the Alliance was the alternative to the communists.

Q: [30 marks] ★★★ 'The Japanese occupation was the main reason why Indonesia gained its independence.' Evaluate this view.
H: Ask what Indonesia had before 1942, and what it lacked.
A: **For.** The occupation (8–9 March 1942) broke Dutch prestige and banned Dutch; it promoted Indonesian and put Indonesians into office; it gave organisation and arms (Putera, March 1943; Peta, 3 October 1943, with 69 battalions); Koiso's promise of independence (7 September 1944), the BPUPK (inaugurated 28 May 1945) and the PPKI (7 August 1945) prepared the way; the surrender (15 August 1945) left a vacuum, and Sukarno and Hatta proclaimed independence on 17 August 1945; the *romusha* (forced labour) and the famine of 1944–45 created grievances. **Against.** Ideas, leaders and symbols existed before: Budi Utomo (1908), Sarekat Islam (1912), the PKI (1920), the PNI (1927) with Sukarno, the Youth Pledge (28 October 1928) with its common language; Hatta and Sjahrir; and independence in 1949 was won by a revolution after the Japanese left: the British landing, the battle of Surabaya (10 November 1945), the Dutch police actions (21 July 1947 and 19 December 1948), the UN's Good Offices, pressure from India, Australia and the USA (the Marshall Plan threat), Dutch weakness, and the Round Table agreement (23 August to 2 November 1949; sovereignty on 27 December 1949). **Level 5.** The ideas, leaders and symbols were made before 1942, and the revolution decided the outcome; the occupation was the decisive accelerator, giving organisation, arms and the opportunity. It was the main reason for the timing, not the sole reason for independence.
:::

## Mixed recall round

Twelve questions across the options. One point for an answer with the date or name and the reason; 9 or more means the facts are secure. Each question links to the chapter to revise.

:::try Twelve questions across the topics
Q: (#hist.d.italy.4) What did the Lateran Treaties of 11 February 1929 settle?
A: The "Roman Question": Italy recognised the Vatican City as a sovereign state, paid 1,750 million lire and signed a Concordat; the Church recognised the Italian state. The result was support for Mussolini among Catholics (the plebiscite of 24 March 1929 had over 98 per cent yes), not total control, as 1931 (Catholic Action) and 1938 (the racial laws) showed.
Q: (#hist.d.stalin.3) What was "Dizzy with Success"?
A: Stalin's article of 2 March 1930 blaming local officials for excesses in collectivisation, which led to a retreat: from about 58 per cent of households in the collectives in March to about 23 per cent in June 1930, before the drive was resumed.
Q: (#hist.d.hitler.1) Why was the Enabling Act of 23 March 1933 important?
A: It let the government make laws without the Reichstag or the President for four years (it passed 444 to 94), the legal basis of the dictatorship; the Social Democrats alone voted against; the Communists were arrested or excluded.
Q: (#hist.d.usa45.4) What was the Truman Doctrine?
A: A commitment announced on 12 March 1947 to support free peoples resisting subjugation, with 400 million dollars for Greece and Turkey; it began containment as an American policy.
Q: (#hist.d.usa61.2) How did the Cuban Missile Crisis end?
A: The USSR agreed on 28 October 1962 to withdraw its missiles from Cuba; the USA promised not to invade and, secretly, to remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey; a hot line (1963) and the Partial Test Ban Treaty (5 August 1963) followed.
Q: (#hist.d.usa74.3) What did the Camp David Accords (1978) achieve?
A: A framework for peace between Egypt and Israel (5–17 September 1978), turned into the treaty of 26 March 1979, with Sinai returned by April 1982; nothing for the Palestinians, and Sadat was murdered on 6 October 1981.
Q: (#hist.d.soviet.1) What did the Brezhnev Doctrine claim?
A: That the USSR had the right to intervene in any socialist state where socialism was threatened; it justified the invasion of Czechoslovakia (Pravda, 26 September 1968).
Q: (#hist.d.safrica.3) What happened on 2 February 1990 and 11 February 1990?
A: De Klerk announced the unbanning of the ANC, PAC and the Communist Party and the release of political prisoners; Mandela was freed from prison on 11 February.
Q: (#hist.d.malaysia.2) Why did Singapore leave Malaysia in 1965?
A: Conflict between the PAP's "Malaysian Malaysia" and the Alliance's Malay special rights; riots in 1964 (21 July: 23 dead; 2 September: 13 dead); economic disputes; separation on 9 August 1965 (Parliament voted 126 to 0).
Q: (#hist.interp.ww1.2) Which historian argued in 1961 that German war aims were continuous with pre-war ambitions?
A: Fritz Fischer (*Griff nach der Weltmacht*, 1961), who used the September Programme of 1914 and the war council of 1912; his critics argued that it was a wartime document and that Germany's aim in July 1914 was a calculated risk.
Q: (#hist.interp.holocaust.1) What was decided at Wannsee on 20 January 1942?
A: A meeting of officials (chaired by Heydrich) co-ordinating the departments for the Final Solution; the list counted over 11 million Jews; it did not "decide" the killing, which had begun in 1941, but organised the agencies.
Q: (#hist.interp.coldwar.1) What were the two superpowers' responses to the Marshall Plan?
A: The USA offered about 13 billion dollars over four years (the Economic Cooperation Act of 3 April 1948); the Soviet Union refused it for itself and its satellites, and answered with the Molotov Plan, Comecon and the Cominform's "two camps" doctrine.
:::

## Repair two weak paragraphs

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph (Paper 4)
**Question.** Assess the reasons for the Terror in the Soviet Union in the years 1934–38. [30]

**A student's paragraph.** "The Terror began when Kirov was murdered in 1934. After that Stalin held show trials in 1936, 1937 and 1938 where old Bolsheviks like Bukharin were put on trial and shot. The army was purged and Tukhachevsky was shot. Millions of people were arrested by the NKVD and sent to the Gulag. The Terror ended when Yezhov was replaced by Beria. So the reason for the Terror was that Stalin wanted to get rid of his enemies."

**The level.** About Level 2 on the analysis table (4–6) and Level 3 on knowledge (7–9). The facts are mainly accurate and relevant, but the paragraph tells the story in order and states one reason at the end with no support. There are no criteria, no other reasons (fear of war, the system), no weighing, and "millions" is imprecise.

**The fix.** "The Terror had a trigger, a cause and a mechanism, and the third explains its scale. The trigger was the murder of Kirov on 1 December 1934, after which a decree of the same day allowed trials without defence. The cause was Stalin himself: he approved the execution lists, wanted to remove old rivals (Bukharin and Rykov were among 18 sentenced to death in March 1938) and feared that the generals and spies would let in a foreign enemy (Tukhachevsky and seven generals were shot on 12 June 1937). But Stalin's wishes alone do not explain the numbers: Order 00447 of 30 July 1937 set regional quotas (75,950 to be shot) which local NKVD chiefs asked to raise, and the arrests, about 1.6 million in 1937–38 with 681,692 shot, depended on denunciation and on officials who feared being called weak. The Terror therefore resulted from Stalin's decisions working through a system that rewarded excess, and it ended only when he chose to stop it (17 November 1938)."

**Why this is Level 5 in method.** It sets a structure (trigger, cause, mechanism), ranks Stalin's role against the system's, uses precise figures and gives a supported conclusion.
:::

:::eg Spot the weak paragraph (Paper 3, knowledge)
**Extract D (above).** A student wrote: "The historian says the Final Solution grew in stages. This is true because first the Nazis boycotted Jewish shops in 1933, then there were the Nuremberg Laws in 1935, then Kristallnacht in 1938, then the war began and the camps were built. So the historian is right that it happened gradually."

**The level.** About Level 3 on both tables (9–11 each). The knowledge is accurate but is a general chronology, and it is used to agree with a sub-message ("in stages"), not to test the interpretation. The student has not said what the historian denies (a plan from the start) or tested that claim, has not used the extract's evidence of failed plans and rival agencies, and has no approach.

**The fix.** "The historian argues that the Final Solution was not the carrying out of a plan, but the result of 'cumulative radicalisation' in a state of rival agencies. Some evidence supports this: the Nisko plan of 1939 and the Madagascar plan of 1940 failed, the ghettos in Łódź (April 1940) and Warsaw (November 1940) became overcrowded and starving, and local officials asked for solutions; the killing in the east began only in 1941, with the Einsatzgruppen from 22 June 1941, and Chełmno started on 8 December 1941. However, an intentionalist could answer that Hitler declared on 30 January 1939 that war would bring the destruction of the Jews, and that Göring's authorisation to Heydrich in July 1941 shows a central decision. The extract downplays these, which is part of its approach: it is interested in structures and process, not in Hitler's intentions."

**Why this is stronger:** it states what the claim is, tests it with evidence on both sides, and links the choice to the approach.
:::

## Plan your practice

:::steps
1. **Week 1: Paper 3.** Plan all six extracts in 10 minutes each (main interpretation with two aspects, sub-messages, approach, knowledge). Compare your plan with the answer's plan before you write anything. Then write one full answer in 60 minutes.
2. **Week 2: Paper 4, one option.** Do the questions for your option as 5-minute plans first, then write two of them as a timed pair (100 minutes).
3. **Week 3: gaps.** Do the recall round and the quiz without notes. Rewrite one answer you marked at Level 3 so that it reaches Level 4 or 5, using your error log.
4. **Week 4: the mocks.** Take [Mock Paper 1](#hist.mock.p1) and [Mock Paper 2](#hist.mock.p2) for the AS papers, then [Mock Paper 3](#hist.mock.p3) and [Mock Paper 4](#hist.mock.p4), under exam conditions, and use the real past papers on the Cambridge site.
:::

:::warn Three habits that cost marks in Paper 3 and Paper 4
- **Paper 3: summarising instead of explaining.** "The historian says … and then says …" is Level 2. Say what the whole extract argues, then what it assumes and leaves out.
- **Paper 3: using knowledge as a story.** Every fact must test a claim in the extract. If you cannot say which claim, cut the fact.
- **Paper 4: a list of factors with no judgement.** The paper is "Assess" or "Evaluate": the top marks go to the answer that says, from its first paragraph, how far and why, and keeps saying it.
:::
