### hist.d.italy.1 · Mussolini's Italy (1): how Mussolini gained control (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, European option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 1 Mussolini’s Italy, 1919–41
- Key question 1.1 How did Mussolini gain control of Italy?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Problems facing Italy after the First World War, including: Impact of war on Italy; Responses to the post-war settlement: the ‘Mutilated Victory’; The seizure of Fiume by d’Annunzio; Economic challenges: unemployment and inflation; Government instability and unpopularity; Biennio Rosso
  - Mussolini’s appointment as prime minister in 1922, including: The development of policy programmes; Fasci de Combattimento; Squadrismo; Establishment of a dual policy and the March on Rome; The role of Victor Emmanuel III and of the political and economic elites; The nature of the National government
  - The establishment of a dictatorship, 1922–26, including: Formation of the Fascist Grand Council and Militia; Acerbo Election Law; The murder of Matteotti; The fascist movement and law on powers of Head of Government
  - Extent of fascist control by 1926, including: Levels of support; Opposition and dissent
- **What to cover and stress.** Italy after 1918: 600,000 or more dead (check), debts, inflation, unemployment, the 'mutilated victory' (Treaty of London 1915 promises vs 1919 gains), d'Annunzio's Fiume (September 1919–December 1920), liberal governments' instability (Nitti, Giolitti, Bonomi, Facta; proportional representation 1919; PSI and PPI), Biennio Rosso (1919–20; factory occupations September 1920). Fasci di Combattimento (23 March 1919, San Sepolcro programme), squadrismo (from 1920; Po valley; Balbo, Grandi), the PNF (November 1921), dual policy (legal and violent), March on Rome (27–28 October 1922), Victor Emmanuel III's refusal to sign martial law, elites (army, industrialists, Church, landowners), the coalition national government (October 1922). Dictatorship 1922–26: Fascist Grand Council (December 1922), MVSN militia (January 1923), Acerbo Law (July 1923; two-thirds of seats to the list with 25 per cent), April 1924 election (about 65 per cent), Matteotti's murder (June 1924) and the Aventine secession, Mussolini's speech of 3 January 1925, Law on the powers of the Head of Government (December 1925), decrees of 1926 (opposition parties banned, press controlled, Special Tribunal). Extent of control by 1926: support (middle classes, elites) and opposition (communists, socialists, Catholic popolari, Liberals).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Paper 4 has no sources, but use short quotations you are sure of as evidence (e.g. Mussolini's 3 January 1925 speech taking responsibility).
- **Worked examples and practice.** Assess the reasons for Mussolini's appointment as prime minister in 1922 (the 2024 paper asked this: write a differently worded question, e.g. 'The weakness of liberal Italy was the main reason Mussolini came to power in 1922.' Evaluate this view). 'The Matteotti crisis strengthened Mussolini's position.' Assess this view. One full Level 5 answer.
- **Historians you may name.** Denis Mack Smith, Mussolini (1981); R.J.B. Bosworth, Mussolini (2002) and Mussolini's Italy (2005); Adrian Lyttelton, The Seizure of Power (1973); Renzo De Felice (biography, 1965–97).
- **Pitfalls.** The March on Rome was a bluff that worked because of the king: do not describe a military seizure. Mussolini arrived by train after being appointed.
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of election results 1919, 1921, 1924 (check sources); timeline.

### hist.d.italy.2 · Mussolini's Italy (2): how effectively Mussolini governed Italy (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, European option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 1 Mussolini’s Italy, 1919–41
- Key question 1.2 How effectively did Mussolini govern Italy?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The terror state and coercion, including: Methods to deal with opposition and dissent: OVRA, MVSN and special tribunals; Antisemitic laws, 1938
  - Propaganda and censorship, including: Attempts to link fascist Italy with Ancient Rome; Personality cult; Ministry of Popular Culture; Control of mass media: newspapers, radio and cinema; Rallies; Use of art and culture; Sporting success; Extent, impact and success of propaganda
  - The nature of fascist government, including: Fascist institutions: Duce, PNF and Fascist Grand Council; Central government: king, cabinet, parliament, police, civil service and judiciary; Prefects and podestas
  - Mussolini’s foreign policy, including: Aims: restoring Italy’s international prestige, control of the Mediterranean and expansion of empire in Africa; Methods: international diplomacy and alliances, Corfu, Albania and the invasion of Abyssinia; Extent of success: individual successes and failures and the overall achievement of aims
- **What to cover and stress.** Terror and coercion: OVRA (secret police, from 1927), MVSN, Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State (1926; about 5,000 political prisoners sentenced; check), confino (internal exile), the 1938 racial laws (Manifesto of Race July 1938; laws from September–November 1938). Propaganda and censorship: romanità (Ancient Rome, the fasces, the 'Roman salute', the Mostra Augustea della Romanità 1937), cult of the Duce ('Mussolini is always right'), Ministry of Popular Culture (MinCulPop, 1937), press (Il Popolo d'Italia), radio (EIAR), cinema (LUCE, Cinecittà 1937), rallies, art and architecture, sport (World Cups 1934 and 1938, Primo Carnera); extent and success. Fascist government: Duce, PNF (Starace), Grand Council; king, cabinet, parliament (Chamber of Fasces and Corporations 1939), police, civil service, judiciary; prefects and podestà (1926). Foreign policy: aims (prestige, Mediterranean 'mare nostrum', African empire), methods (Corfu 1923, Fiume 1924, Locarno, Albania protectorate 1926 and invasion April 1939, Stresa 1935, Abyssinia 1935–36, Spain, Axis, Pact of Steel, non-belligerence and war June 1940), success.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Use precise facts and figures as evidence; quotations only if certain.
- **Worked examples and practice.** Evaluate the view that Mussolini's government relied more on consent than on coercion. Assess the success of Mussolini's foreign policy in the years 1922–40. Full Level 5 answer for one.
- **Historians you may name.** De Felice's 'years of consensus' (1929–36) and its critics; Bosworth; Mack Smith; Philip Morgan, Italian Fascism (1995).
- **Pitfalls.** Fascist Italy was less totalitarian than Nazi Germany or Stalin's USSR: the king, Church and army kept power. Racial laws: sensitive and factual.
- **Figures and widgets.** SVG of the structure of fascist government (Duce, Grand Council, PNF, king, prefects).
