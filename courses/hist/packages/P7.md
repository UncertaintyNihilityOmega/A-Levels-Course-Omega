### hist.us.civilwar.1 · Civil War and Reconstruction (1): growing tension between North and South before 1860 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS American option (Section B). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2029**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2028**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 4 The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77
- Key question 4.1 Why was there increasing tension between the North and the South before 1860?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The differing social and economic structures of the South and North, including: Industry versus agriculture; Tariff issues; Missouri Compromise, 1820
  - Impact of territorial expansion, including: Westward expansion; The absorption of Texas and the Mexican War, 1846–48; The Wilmot Proviso; Emergence of Free Soil ideology
  - Problems arising from the implementation of the Compromise of 1850, including: Fugitive Slave Act; Kansas–Nebraska Act; ‘Bleeding Kansas’; Brooks-Sumner incident; Lecompton Constitution
  - Changes in the party-political system, including: The decline of the Whig Party; The rise of the Republican Party; The Lincoln–Douglas debates; Splits within the Democratic Party
- **What to cover and stress.** North and South compared (population, industry, cities, railways, immigration; cotton and slavery: about 4 million enslaved people by 1860); tariffs (1828 'Tariff of Abominations', the Nullification crisis 1832–33); Missouri Compromise (1820, 36°30′); westward expansion and Manifest Destiny; Texas annexation (1845) and the Mexican War (1846–48; Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo); Wilmot Proviso (1846); Free Soil ideology and party (1848); Compromise of 1850 (California free, popular sovereignty in Utah and New Mexico, slave trade banned in DC, the Fugitive Slave Act); Kansas–Nebraska Act (1854; Douglas; repealed 36°30′); Bleeding Kansas (1854–59); Brooks beats Sumner in the Senate (22 May 1856); Lecompton Constitution (1857–58); decline of the Whigs (after 1852), Know-Nothings, rise of the Republicans (1854; Frémont 1856); Lincoln–Douglas debates (1858; Freeport Doctrine); Democratic splits (1860).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** The Wilmot Proviso (1846); John C. Calhoun's speech on the Compromise (March 1850) and Daniel Webster's Seventh of March speech (1850); the Fugitive Slave Act (1850); Charles Sumner's 'The Crime against Kansas' (May 1856); cartoons of the Brooks–Sumner attack (visual); Lincoln's 'House Divided' speech (16 June 1858); the Lincoln–Douglas debates.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a) (specimen asked about Dred Scott: that is KQ 4.2; choose another): Explain why the Kansas–Nebraska Act increased sectional tension. P2(b): 'Territorial expansion was the main cause of sectional tension before 1860.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How far did the Compromise of 1850 settle the issue of slavery? P1 on Bleeding Kansas.
- **Historians you may name.** David Potter, The Impending Crisis 1848–1861 (1976); James McPherson, Battle Cry of Freedom (1988); Eric Foner, Free Soil, Free Labor, Free Men (1970); Michael Holt on the party system.
- **Pitfalls.** Sensitive: slavery. Use 'enslaved people'; never imply slavery was benign. Do not reduce sectionalism to tariffs: slavery was central (as the secession declarations themselves say).
- **Figures and widgets.** Schematic SVG map of the Missouri Compromise line and the 1850 and 1854 settlements, or a table of the compromises and what each decided.

### hist.us.civilwar.2 · Civil War and Reconstruction (2): why the Civil War broke out in April 1861 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS American option (Section B). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2029**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2028**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 4 The Civil War and Reconstruction, 1820–77
- Key question 4.2 Why did the Civil War break out in April 1861?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Growing strength of abolitionism and support for secession, including: The impact of ‘Uncle Tom’s Cabin’; John Brown and Harper’s Ferry
  - Emerging notions of states’ rights and ‘slave power’, including: The Dred Scott judgement
  - The election campaigns of 1860 and reactions to the election results
  - The failure of compromise and the attack on Fort Sumter, including: The stages of secession before and after Lincoln’s inauguration
- **What to cover and stress.** Abolitionism: Garrison, Frederick Douglass, Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852; Harriet Beecher Stowe) and its impact; John Brown's raid on Harper's Ferry (16–18 October 1859; executed 2 December 1859) and Northern and Southern reactions; states' rights and 'slave power' conspiracy; Dred Scott v. Sandford (6 March 1857; Taney: Black people not citizens; Missouri Compromise unconstitutional); election of 1860 (Lincoln, Douglas, Breckinridge, Bell; Lincoln won with about 40 per cent of the popular vote and no Southern electoral votes); secession (South Carolina 20 December 1860; seven states by February 1861; Confederacy and Jefferson Davis); failure of compromise (Crittenden); Lincoln's inaugural (4 March 1861); Fort Sumter (12–13 April 1861) and the call for 75,000 volunteers; four more states secede; border states.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) and Southern replies; John Brown's last speech to the court (2 November 1859) and his note of 2 December 1859; Chief Justice Taney's opinion in Dred Scott (1857); South Carolina's Declaration of the Immediate Causes of Secession (24 December 1860); Lincoln's First Inaugural Address (4 March 1861); Alexander Stephens's 'Cornerstone Speech' (21 March 1861).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why Lincoln's election led to secession. P2(b): 'The attack on Fort Sumter was the main reason the Civil War broke out in April 1861.' How far do you agree? P2(b): To what extent was the Dred Scott judgement a turning point? (The specimen (a) used Dred Scott: write new wording.) P1(a) compare South Carolina's declaration and Lincoln's inaugural as evidence about the causes of secession.
- **Historians you may name.** Potter; McPherson; Kenneth Stampp, And the War Came (1950).
- **Pitfalls.** The 'states' rights' explanation is contradicted by the secession documents' own emphasis on slavery: show the evidence. Lincoln did not campaign to abolish slavery in the states in 1860.
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of the 1860 election (candidates, parties, popular vote %, electoral votes) with checks recomputing percentages from cited totals; secession timeline.
- **Next line.** `Next: [Civil War and Reconstruction (3): why the Civil War lasted four years](#hist.us.civilwar.3)`
