### hist.interp.skills · How historians disagree: reading an interpretation (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 3** (one extract, one [40] question; FACTS.md has the levels).
- **What to cover and stress.** What Paper 3 asks (FACTS.md): one unseen extract (at most 600 words, author not named), one 40-mark question 'What can you learn from this extract about the interpretation and approach of the historian who wrote it?'. Interpretation = the historian's main claims and conclusions; approach = what they bring (the questions they ask, their interests, ideology and beliefs). Why historians differ (the four syllabus reasons: fragmentary evidence; selection and interpretation; the passage of time and new evidence; the time and place they work in) and how approaches differ (questions, ideology, the link between approach and interpretation). Method: read the whole extract twice; find the main interpretation (usually two linked aspects, as the specimen mark scheme shows); test each paragraph against it; separate sub-messages; use knowledge to explain and test the interpretation (AO1 is half the marks); use labels only when the extract supports them; write about the whole extract. Levels: Table A (AO4) and Table B (AO1), each 20 (FACTS.md). Practice on a neutral example before the topics: e.g. two short paraphrased summaries of famous arguments about the causes of the French Revolution (Lefebvre vs Cobban), clearly labelled as summaries.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** No extracts copied from past papers. Write short, clearly labelled paraphrased summaries of a named historian's argument (e.g. 'A summary, written for practice, of the argument of Alfred Cobban, The Social Interpretation of the French Revolution, 1964').
- **Worked examples and practice.** Worked example: a full answer to 'What can you learn from this extract…' on a practice summary, with a Level 3 answer (sub-messages), a Level 5 answer (one aspect), a Level 6 answer (both aspects), and commentary. Exam practice: three short practice summaries with tasks (identify the main interpretation; identify sub-messages; explain the approach; which knowledge tests it).
- **Historians you may name.** E.H. Carr, What Is History? (1961); Geoffrey Elton, The Practice of History (1967); Richard Evans, In Defence of History (1997): use for the nature of history, briefly.
- **Pitfalls.** The command is not 'evaluate the interpretation' or 'compare with other historians': it is what you can learn about this historian's interpretation and approach. Never guess the author's name: the mark scheme does not reward it.
- **Figures and widgets.** Build the hist-sort widget here (see Widgets), with a demo set: statements to sort into 'main interpretation', 'sub-message', 'approach', 'not in the extract'. An SVG of the Paper 3 method (read, find, test, explain).

### hist.interp.ww1.1 · Origins of the First World War (1): the road to war, c.1890–1914 (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 3** (one extract, one [40] question; FACTS.md has the levels).
- **What to cover and stress.** Context c.1890–1914 (AO1, the knowledge half of Paper 3): Great Power tensions (Germany's rise; Weltpolitik; Anglo-German antagonism; Franco-German enmity after 1871; Austria-Hungary's nationalities; Russia after 1905), alliance system (Dual Alliance 1879, Triple Alliance 1882, Franco-Russian 1894, Entente Cordiale 1904, Anglo-Russian 1907), militarism and the arms race (naval race; army laws 1912–13; Russia's Great Programme 1913), Balkan instability (Bosnian crisis 1908–09; Balkan Wars 1912–13; Serbia), war plans (Schlieffen–Moltke, Plan XVII, Plan 19/G, Austria's plans), Sarajevo (28 June 1914), the July Crisis (blank cheque 5–6 July; ultimatum 23 July; Serbian reply 25 July; Austrian declaration 28 July; Russian general mobilisation 30 July; German ultimatums and declarations 1 and 3 August; Belgium; Britain 4 August). Each event tagged with which interpretations use it.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Documents historians argue over: the blank cheque (5–6 July 1914); the Austrian ultimatum (23 July); the 'Willy–Nicky' telegrams; the 'War Council' of 8 December 1912 (Admiral von Müller's diary); Bethmann Hollweg's September Programme (9 September 1914); Grey's speech of 3 August 1914; Article 231.
- **Worked examples and practice.** Worked examples: Paper 3-style knowledge tests: 'Which events would a historian who blames Germany emphasise, and why?'; a model paragraph using July Crisis knowledge to test an interpretation. Also quick 'explain the significance of …' answers.
- **Historians you may name.** Name only for context here (debate chapter has them): Fischer, Clark, MacMillan, Joll.
- **Pitfalls.** Old Style vs New Style dates for Russia in 1914: use New Style (Western) dates with a note. Do not narrate everything: tag events by what they mean for responsibility.
- **Figures and widgets.** hist-timeline (if built) of the July Crisis with lanes per power; table of alliances; SVG of the alliance blocs.

### hist.interp.ww1.2 · Origins of the First World War (2): the historians' debate (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 3** (one extract, one [40] question; FACTS.md has the levels).
- **What to cover and stress.** All twelve syllabus bullets: tension likely to lead to war?; key individuals; Article 231 and the war-guilt debate (German Foreign Office's 'innocence campaign', document collections such as Die Grosse Politik); 'shared responsibility' (Lloyd George's 'slithered over the brink' in his War Memoirs; Sidney Fay, The Origins of the World War, 1928; Harry Elmer Barnes; appeasement-era views); the Second World War's effect (1951 Franco-German historians' agreement on shared responsibility); the Fischer thesis (Germany's Aims in the First World War, 1961 German; War of Illusions, 1969: Germany planned a war of aggression; September Programme; 'War Council' 1912; Primat der Innenpolitik); the German reaction (Gerhard Ritter; the 'Fischer controversy' at the 1964 Historikertag); challenges to Fischer (Egmont Zechlin, Karl Dietrich Erdmann and the Riezler diaries; Wolfgang Mommsen); modern interpretations of other nations' responsibility (Samuel Williamson on Austria-Hungary 1991; Sean McMeekin on Russia 2011; Niall Ferguson, The Pity of War 1998, on Britain; Christopher Clark, The Sleepwalkers 2012: shared, Serbia and the Entente's decisions); long vs short-term (James Joll's 'unspoken assumptions', 1984; A.J.P. Taylor, War by Timetable 1969); the centenary (Clark, MacMillan The War That Ended Peace 2013, Max Hastings Catastrophe 2013, renewed German debate); contingency over motive (Holger Afflerbach and David Stevenson (eds), An Improbable War? 2007).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Paper 3 extracts must be practice summaries clearly labelled as paraphrases of a named historian's published argument; never invented quotations.
- **Worked examples and practice.** One full worked answer (Levels 3, 5 and 6) on a practice summary of, e.g., Fischer's thesis or Clark's; exam practice with three more summaries from different schools; a stretch question on how the time and place of writing shaped an interpretation (Fischer in 1960s West Germany).
- **Historians you may name.** As in cover; only with title and year as given.
- **Pitfalls.** Specimen Paper 3 Section A extract rejects Lloyd George's 'slide to war' thesis: do not reuse it; write your own. Labels (revisionist, anti-revisionist) only when the extract supports them (specimen mark scheme).
- **Figures and widgets.** Use hist-sort with statements to sort by school (Fischerite, shared responsibility, contingency, other nations). A timeline table of the historiography (1919–2014).

## Widgets (this package builds them; one file each in widgets/<name>.js; kit/WIDGETS.md)

- `hist-sort`: Generic sorting exercise. Attributes: data-title, data-cats (comma list, 2–5 categories), data-items ('statement | correct category | short explanation ;; …'). Each statement shows with one button per category (aria-pressed); 'Check' marks each right or wrong with a word and symbol (not colour alone) and shows the explanation; 'Reset' and 'Shuffle'; a score readout. Keyboard only, no dragging. First use in hist.interp.skills (sorting main interpretation vs sub-message vs approach) and hist.interp.ww1.2 (schools of thought).
- Probe each (`node kit/zta.js widget <name> '<attrs>' --probe`) until clean; look once; use it with the keyboard only; test its logic with a small Node script.
