- Syllabus topic: 8 International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation
- Key question 8.3 How and why did international tensions remain high after the Versailles settlement?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Crises and tensions, including: Fiume; Corfu; The Ruhr crisis; German hyperinflation and the reparations issue; Ethnic, political and economic problems in the successor states
  - Aims and impact of international treaties and conferences, including: Little Entente, 1921; Washington Conference, 1921–22; Genoa Conference, 1922; Rapallo Pact, 1922; Treaty of Lausanne, 1923
  - Changing relations between the major powers, including: European nations: Britain, France, Germany and Italy; The Soviet Union and the challenge of Communism; The USA: isolation or involvement; Emergence of Japan: internal issues and the effect on international relations
- **What to cover and stress.** Crises: Fiume (d'Annunzio 1919–20; Treaty of Rapallo 1920; annexed 1924), Corfu (August–September 1923; Tellini murder; Conference of Ambassadors; Greece pays), Ruhr occupation (from 11 January 1923; passive resistance; hyperinflation peaking November 1923; Stresemann ends resistance September 1923), reparations disputes, successor states' ethnic, political and economic problems (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Austria, Hungary). Treaties and conferences: Little Entente (1920–21; Czechoslovakia, Romania, Yugoslavia, backed by France), Washington Conference (1921–22: Four-Power, Five-Power naval treaty with 5:5:3:1.75:1.75 capital-ship ratio, Nine-Power treaty), Genoa (April–May 1922), Rapallo (16 April 1922, Germany–Soviet Russia), Lausanne (24 July 1923, Turkey). Changing relations: Britain and France disagree (Ruhr, reparations), Germany, Italy under Mussolini (from October 1922), the USSR and the Comintern (1919), US isolation (yet Dawes, Washington, loans), Japan (internal issues, Washington, 1924 US immigration act).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** The Washington Naval Treaty (1922); the Treaty of Rapallo (1922); French and German posters on the Ruhr (visual); Mussolini's ultimatum to Greece (1923); League and Conference of Ambassadors statements on Corfu; Keynes or Poincaré on reparations.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why France occupied the Ruhr in 1923. P2(b): 'The Corfu crisis showed the League could not deal with a Great Power.' How far do you agree? (Ensure you treat Corfu as a KQ 8.3 crisis.) P2(b): How far was the Washington Conference a success? P1 (the 2024 Paper 1 asked whether the Ruhr occupation was justified: use other sources) on the successor states.
- **Historians you may name.** Steiner; Sally Marks, The Illusion of Peace (1976); Conan Fischer, The Ruhr Crisis 1923–1924 (2003).
- **Pitfalls.** Two treaties of Rapallo (1920 Italy–Yugoslavia; 1922 Germany–Russia): the syllabus means 1922.

### hist.int.1919.4 · International relations, 1919–29 (4): how and why relations improved, 1924–29 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS International option (Section C). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2028**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 8 International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation
- Key question 8.4 How and why did international relations improve, 1924–29?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Economic recovery and improved relations, including: Dawes Plan: proposals and effect on international relations; Stresemann and German recovery; German entry into the League of Nations; Young Plan
  - Aims and impact of international proposals, treaties and conferences, including: Geneva Protocol, 1924: support and opposition; Locarno Treaties, 1925: achievements and failures; Kellogg–Briand Pact, 1928: purpose and limitations; Hague Conference, 1929 and the dispute over evacuation of the Rhineland
  - Developing relations between the major powers, including: France and Germany: the role of Stresemann and Briand; Italy and the impact of Mussolini’s ambitions on international relations; Shifting relations with the Soviet Union
  - Improved relations: reality or illusion, including: European powers; Japan and USA; Immediate impact of the Wall Street Crash 9 International history 1929–39: the rise of extremism and the road to war
- **What to cover and stress.** Economic recovery: Dawes Plan (August 1924: reparations rescheduled, US loans, Rentenmark and Reichsmark), Stresemann's 'fulfilment' and recovery, German entry to the League (September 1926; permanent Council seat), Young Plan (1929: total cut to about 112 billion marks over 59 years; check). Treaties: Geneva Protocol (1924; British rejection by Baldwin's government 1925), Locarno (October 1925, signed December 1925: Rhineland Pact guaranteed by Britain and Italy, arbitration treaties, eastern borders not guaranteed; 'spirit of Locarno'), Kellogg–Briand Pact (27 August 1928; renounced war, no enforcement; over 60 states eventually), Hague Conference (August 1929) and early Rhineland evacuation (completed June 1930). Relations: Briand and Stresemann (Nobel Peace Prize 1926), Mussolini's ambitions (Albania, Corfu, Locarno), the USSR (Berlin Treaty 1926; British recognition 1924 and break 1927). Reality or illusion: European powers, Japan and the USA, immediate impact of the Wall Street Crash (October 1929; US loans recalled; Stresemann's death 3 October 1929).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Stresemann's letter to the former Crown Prince (7 September 1925) on his aims; the Locarno treaties (1925); Briand's speech welcoming Germany to the League (10 September 1926); the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928); cartoons on Locarno (visual).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why Germany joined the League of Nations in 1926. P2(b): 'The improvement in international relations in the years 1924–29 was an illusion.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How far was the Dawes Plan the main reason for improved relations? P1 on Locarno.
- **Historians you may name.** Jonathan Wright, Gustav Stresemann (2002); Steiner; Marks.
- **Pitfalls.** Locarno did not guarantee Germany's eastern borders: this is the key limitation. Stresemann's motives are debated: use the 1925 letter carefully.
- **Figures and widgets.** Timeline widget 1924–29 if built; table of agreements.
