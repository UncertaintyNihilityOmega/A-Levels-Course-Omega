### hist.d.soviet.1 · The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (1): the USSR and the satellite states to 1968 (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, International option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 7 The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953–91
- Key question 7.1 How did the Soviet Union’s relationship with the satellite states develop in the period up to 1968?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The formation of the Warsaw Pact
  - Reasons for formation
  - The nature of government in the satellite states including: Czechoslovakia; Poland; East Germany; Hungary
  - The political influence of the Communist Party: Repressive measures including: Censorship, State security forces such as the Stasi, Policies towards the Church, Restrictions on emigration; Social and economic conditions in the satellite states including: The impacts of economic centralisation, Changes in living standards; Threats to the stability of Soviet control including: Uprisings in the 1950s in Poland and Germany, Hungarian uprising in 1956: causes, course and impact, Problems in Berlin and the building of the Berlin Wall, Prague Spring in 1968: causes, course and impact
  - Soviet reactions to dissent, including: Political change and repression; Military actions; Introduction of the Brezhnev Doctrine, 1968
- **What to cover and stress.** Warsaw Pact (14 May 1955; after West German entry to NATO; reasons: security, legitimacy, control). Government in Czechoslovakia (Gottwald, Novotný), Poland (Bierut, Gomułka from 1956), East Germany (Ulbricht), Hungary (Rákosi, Nagy, Kádár); party influence; repression: censorship, security forces (Stasi 1950, ÁVH, UB/SB, StB), policies towards churches (Cardinal Wyszyński arrested 1953; Mindszenty), restrictions on emigration. Social and economic: centralisation, Comecon (1949), heavy industry, collectivisation, living standards. Threats: East German rising (17 June 1953), Poznań (June 1956) and the Polish October (1956), Hungarian uprising (23 October–November 1956; Soviet invasion 4 November; Nagy executed 1958; about 2,500 Hungarians killed and 200,000 refugees; check), Berlin (emigration of about 2.7 million 1949–61; check) and the Wall (13 August 1961), Prague Spring (January–August 1968; Dubček; Action Programme; invasion 20–21 August 1968), Soviet responses: repression, military action, Brezhnev Doctrine (September–November 1968).
- **Worked examples and practice.** Specimen Q7 asked whether the Warsaw Pact was formed to keep control of the satellites: write a different question, e.g. 'The Hungarian uprising of 1956 was the greatest threat to Soviet control before 1968.' Evaluate this view. Assess the reasons for the Soviet invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968. Full Level 5 answer.
- **Historians you may name.** Mark Kramer; Anne Applebaum, Iron Curtain (2012); Vladislav Zubok, A Failed Empire (2007); Timothy Garton Ash.
- **Pitfalls.** Hungary 1956 and Poland 1956 had different outcomes: explain why.
- **Figures and widgets.** Schematic SVG map of the Warsaw Pact states (labelled), or a table of the satellite states and their leaders.

### hist.d.soviet.2 · The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe (2): stagnation, 1964–85 (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, International option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 7 The Soviet Empire in Eastern Europe, 1953–91
- Key question 7.2 Why did the period 1964–85 become characterised by political and economic stagnation?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Political leadership in the period from 1964–85: Brezhnev’s aims, Andropov and Chernenko; Corruption and the nomenklatura system; Development of the gerontocracy
  - The nature and extent of the problems facing the Soviet economy: Attempts to reform and the 10th Five Year Plan; Impact of defence spending; Development of stagnation
  - Foreign relations: Détente with the West; The invasion of Afghanistan and its impact on the Soviet Union; The Second Cold War
  - Developments in the satellite states
  - Economic reforms
  - Social policies and developments
  - Challenges to Soviet control, including: Influence of the West; Political activism; Influence of the Church
- **What to cover and stress.** Leadership 1964–85: Brezhnev (stability of cadres, aims), Kosygin reforms (1965), Andropov (1982–84: discipline, anti-corruption), Chernenko (1984–85); corruption (the 'cotton affair' in Uzbekistan), nomenklatura, gerontocracy (average Politburo age about 70 by 1982; check). Economy: reform attempts, 10th Five-Year Plan (1976–80; quality and efficiency targets missed), defence spending (estimates 15–25 per cent of GNP; give range), stagnation (growth slowing; grain imports), the 'second economy'. Foreign relations: détente (SALT I, Helsinki), Afghanistan (December 1979–February 1989; about 15,000 Soviet dead; check), Second Cold War. Satellites: debt (Poland, Hungary 'goulash communism'), Comecon, Ostpolitik, social policy and consumerism, challenges: Western influence (radio, Helsinki groups: Charter 77 January 1977), political activism (KOR 1976), the Church (John Paul II elected October 1978; visit to Poland June 1979).
- **Worked examples and practice.** 'The Brezhnev era was one of stability rather than stagnation.' Evaluate this view. Assess the impact of the war in Afghanistan on the Soviet Union. Full Level 5 answer.
- **Historians you may name.** Edwin Bacon and Mark Sandle (eds), Brezhnev Reconsidered (2002); Stephen Kotkin, Armageddon Averted (2001); Archie Brown.
- **Pitfalls.** Statistics on Soviet growth are estimates (CIA, Soviet official, later reconstructions): give the source.
- **Figures and widgets.** hist-chart (if built): Soviet GNP growth by five-year period from a cited estimate.
