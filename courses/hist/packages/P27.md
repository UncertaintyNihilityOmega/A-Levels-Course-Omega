### hist.d.hitler.3 · Hitler's Germany (3): how successful Nazi economic policies were (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, European option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 3 Hitler’s Germany, 1929–41
- Key question 3.3 How successful were Nazi economic policies?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Responses to the Great Depression, including: Schacht’s deficit financing schemes and Mefo Bills; Public works programmes such as the autobahn network; Reich Labour Service; Law for Reduction of Unemployment
  - Agricultural policies, including: The use of, and impact of, tariffs; Reich Food Estate; Reich Entailed Farm Law
  - Rearmament and autarky, including: Schacht’s New Plan, 1934; Goering’s Four Year Plan and the creation of a war economy; Development of key war industries; The debate about guns or butter
  - Living standards, including: Employment rates; Working hours; Rates of pay; Labour Front (DAF) and loss of trade union representation; Strength through Joy (KdF) and Beauty of Work
- **What to cover and stress.** Depression responses: Schacht (Reichsbank 1933, economics minister 1934–37), deficit finance and Mefo bills (1934–38), public works (autobahn programme from September 1933), Reich Labour Service (RAD; compulsory 1935), Law for the Reduction of Unemployment (June 1933; marriage loans), unemployment fell from about 6 million (1932) to about 0.3 million by 1939 (check; note hidden unemployment). Agriculture: tariffs, Reich Food Estate (Darré, 1933), Reich Entailed Farm Law (1933). Rearmament and autarky: New Plan (1934, bilateral trade), Four-Year Plan (1936; Göring; synthetic fuel and rubber, Reichswerke Hermann Göring 1937), war industries, 'guns or butter', Schacht's resignation (1937). Living standards: employment, hours (rising), pay (real wages flat; check), DAF (May 1933; Ley; unions abolished), KdF (cruises, Volkswagen savings scheme), Beauty of Labour.
- **Worked examples and practice.** 'Nazi economic policy was a success.' Evaluate this view. Assess the extent to which the German economy was prepared for war by 1939. Full Level 5 answer.

### hist.d.hitler.4 · Hitler's Germany (4): how far the Nazis transformed German society (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, European option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 3 Hitler’s Germany, 1929–41
- Key question 3.4 How far did the Nazis transform German society?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Impact on women of Nazi policies, including: Views on women’s domestic roles: Kinder, Küche, Kirche; Attempts to reduce women’s involvement in the workplace; Efforts to increase the birthrate such as Motherhood Cross and Marriage Loans; Changes to attitudes to women in work from 1937
  - Impact on young people of Nazi policies, including: Education: syllabus and textbook changes, control of teachers, creation of new schools and university education; Youth groups: Hitler Youth, BDM, indoctrination, military training, levels of attendance and compulsion
  - Religious policies, including: Concordat with Catholic Church; Reich Church, Bishop Müller and German Christians; German Faith Movement
  - Persecution of Jews and other minorities, including: Nazi race theories: Social Darwinism and eugenics; Other reasons for persecution of Jews, such as The First World War and the Great Depression; Ways in which Jews were persecuted: boycotts, Nuremberg Laws and Kristallnacht; Other minorities: homosexuals, Jehovah’s Witnesses, Roma, mentally and physically disabled, reasons for persecution and examples of persecution
- **What to cover and stress.** Women: Kinder, Küche, Kirche; reducing women in work (1933 marriage loans with condition to leave work), Mother's Cross (1938; check date: introduced December 1938), Lebensborn (1935), birth rate (rose to 1939; check); reversal from 1937 (labour shortage). Youth: education (curriculum: race science, history, biology; textbooks; teachers' National Socialist Teachers' League; Adolf Hitler Schools 1937, Napolas 1933; universities), Hitler Youth and BDM (membership compulsory from 1936 law and 1939 decree; check), indoctrination, military training, attendance. Religion: Concordat (20 July 1933), Reich Church (Ludwig Müller, Reich Bishop 1933), German Christians, Confessing Church, German Faith Movement (Hauer), encyclical Mit brennender Sorge (1937). Persecution: race theory (Social Darwinism, eugenics), other reasons (scapegoating for 1918 and the Depression); boycotts (1 April 1933), Nuremberg Laws (15 September 1935), Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938; about 90 or more killed, 30,000 men sent to camps; check); other minorities: gay men (Paragraph 175 strengthened 1935), Jehovah's Witnesses, Roma and Sinti, disabled people (sterilisation law July 1933; Aktion T4 from 1939), asocials.
- **Worked examples and practice.** 'Nazi policies towards young people were successful.' Evaluate this view. Assess the reasons for the persecution of minorities in Nazi Germany, 1933–39. Full Level 5 answer.
- **Historians you may name.** Jill Stephenson, Women in Nazi Germany (2001); Michael Kater, Hitler Youth (2004); Richard Evans, The Third Reich in Power (2005); Saul Friedländer.
- **Pitfalls.** Sensitive: persecution; respectful, factual, victims as people. Overlap with Paper 3 Holocaust: here 1933–39 policy, not the genocide.
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of anti-Jewish measures 1933–39 (date, measure, effect).
