### hist.int.1929.3 · International history, 1929–39 (3): appeasement: why, and with what effects (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS International option (Section C). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2029**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2028**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 8 International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation
- Key question 9.3 Why, and with what effects, did Britain and France pursue a policy of appeasement?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Impact of economic, military and social considerations on foreign policy, including: Britain: effects of Great Depression, 10-year rule, Oxford ‘King and Country’ debate of 1933 and the Peace Ballot, 1934–35; Fascism as a bulwark against Bolshevism, and sympathy for Nazi policies; France: economic problems, political divisions, threat of communism and elections of 1936; French Grand Strategy: creation of the Maginot Line
  - Actions taken to appease Hitler, including: Response to rearmament, Anglo-German Naval Agreement, 1935; Inaction over the Rhineland; Acceptance of the Anschluss; The Sudetenland Crisis; The Munich conference and its outcome; Responses to Hitler’s occupation of Prague
  - Successes of appeasement, including: War postponed; Buying time for rearmament; Gaining popular consent
  - Failure of appeasement, including: Increased resources of dictators; Increased morale of dictators; Growing opposition to the policy
- **What to cover and stress.** Britain: Depression and budgets, the Ten-Year Rule (1919–32), Oxford Union 'King and Country' debate (9 February 1933), Peace Ballot (1934–35; over 11 million votes), fear of bombing, the Dominions, the empire's overstretch (three potential enemies), fascism as a bulwark against Bolshevism and some sympathy for Nazi policies; France: economic problems, political divisions, fear of communism, the Popular Front (1936 elections, Blum), Maginot Line (built from 1929). Actions: response to rearmament (March 1935), Anglo-German Naval Agreement (June 1935, 35 per cent), Rhineland (7 March 1936), Anschluss (12–13 March 1938), Sudetenland crisis (Berchtesgaden 15 September, Godesberg 22–23 September, Munich 29–30 September 1938), Munich Agreement and Chamberlain's paper ('peace for our time'), occupation of Prague (15 March 1939) and the end of appeasement (guarantee to Poland 31 March 1939). Successes: war postponed, time to rearm (radar, fighters), popular consent and Dominion support. Failures: resources gained by dictators (Czech arms and Škoda works), dictators' morale, the USSR alienated, opposition (Churchill, Eden's resignation February 1938, Duff Cooper).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Chamberlain's broadcast of 27 September 1938 ('a quarrel in a far away country'); the Munich Agreement (30 September 1938) and the Anglo-German declaration; Churchill's Commons speech (5 October 1938); results of the Peace Ballot; David Low's cartoons (visual); the Hossbach Memorandum (5 November 1937) as context; Daily Express headlines.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why Britain did not oppose the remilitarisation of the Rhineland. P2(b): 'Appeasement was a sensible policy.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How far was fear of communism the main reason for appeasement? P1 on Munich.
- **Historians you may name.** 'Cato', Guilty Men (1940); R.A.C. Parker, Chamberlain and Appeasement (1993); Robert Self, Neville Chamberlain (2006); Frank McDonough, Neville Chamberlain, Appeasement and the British Road to War (1998).
- **Pitfalls.** 'Peace for our time' (not 'in our time') was Chamberlain's phrase on 30 September 1938. Appeasement was British and French policy: give France its own causes.
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of Hitler's actions 1935–39 and the British and French responses.

### hist.int.1929.4 · International history, 1929–39 (4): why war broke out in 1939 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS International option (Section C). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2029**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2028**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 8 International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation
- Key question 9.4 Why did war break out in 1939?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Aims and impact of Hitler’s expansionist policies, including: Underlying aims of Hitler in foreign policy; Rearmament and Anglo-German Naval Agreement, 1935; Remilitarisation of the Rhineland, 1936; Anschluss, 1938; Sudetenland, 1938; Czechoslovakia, 1939
  - British rearmament in response to Germany’s expansionism, including: Development of aircraft; Preparation for air raids; Targets and progress by 1939
  - Development of German alliances, including: Anti-Comintern Pact; Pact of Steel; Nazi–Soviet Pact; Long-term and short-term motives of participants
  - Attack on Poland, including: German–Polish issues including Danzig and the Polish Corridor; The end of Appeasement and the Anglo-French guarantee to Poland, March 1939; Disagreement between Hitler and the German High Command; Hitler’s intentions
- **What to cover and stress.** Hitler's aims (Mein Kampf, Lebensraum, revision of Versailles, anti-communism; the Hossbach Memorandum 1937; the debate: programme or opportunism); stages: rearmament and conscription (March 1935), Anglo-German Naval Agreement, Rhineland (7 March 1936), Anschluss (1938), Sudetenland (1938), Czechoslovakia (March 1939), Memel (March 1939). British rearmament: aircraft (Spitfire, Hurricane, the shift to fighters 1937–38 under Inskip), radar (Chain Home), air raid precautions (ARP, gas masks, evacuation plans), targets and progress by 1939. Alliances: Anti-Comintern Pact, Pact of Steel (22 May 1939), Nazi–Soviet Pact (23 August 1939; secret protocol dividing Poland and the Baltic); long- and short-term motives of Hitler and Stalin (Stalin's distrust after Munich, time, territory; Hitler's avoidance of a two-front war). Poland: Danzig and the Corridor, the guarantee (31 March 1939), Anglo-Polish alliance (25 August 1939), disagreements between Hitler and the High Command (e.g. Beck's resignation 1938; the generals' worries), Hitler's intentions; invasion 1 September 1939; British and French declarations of war 3 September 1939.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** The Hossbach Memorandum (1937); the Pact of Steel (1939); the Nazi–Soviet Pact and its secret protocol (1939); David Low's cartoon 'Rendezvous' (Evening Standard, 20 September 1939; visual); Chamberlain's broadcast of 3 September 1939; the British guarantee to Poland (31 March 1939).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a) (specimen asked why the Nazi–Soviet Pact was formed: write another): Explain why Britain guaranteed Poland in March 1939. P2(b): 'Hitler's foreign policy was the main cause of war in 1939.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How far was the Nazi–Soviet Pact responsible for the outbreak of war? (The 2024 paper asked this: rephrase or choose another.) P1 on the Pact.
- **Historians you may name.** A.J.P. Taylor, The Origins of the Second World War (1961: Hitler an opportunist); Hugh Trevor-Roper's reply (1961); Richard Overy; Ian Kershaw.
- **Pitfalls.** The A.J.P. Taylor debate is stretch, not Paper 3. Dates of the declarations: Britain 11 am, France 5 pm, 3 September 1939.
- **Figures and widgets.** Schematic SVG map of German expansion 1935–39 (Rhineland, Austria, Sudetenland, Bohemia-Moravia, Memel, Danzig and the Corridor), labelled schematic; or a table.
