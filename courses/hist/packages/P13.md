## Chapters

### hist.int.empire.1 · Imperialism and world powers (1): why imperialism was a significant force in Africa from 1870 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS International option (Section C). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 7 Imperialism and the emergence of world powers, c.1870–1918
- Key question 7.1 Why was imperialism a significant force in Africa from 1870?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Motives for the Scramble for Africa by European countries, including: Economic; Religious and cultural, ‘the civilising mission’; National and strategic rivalry
  - Process of colonisation of Africa, including: Expansion and government of colonies: direct and indirect; Activities of European explorers, including: Barth, Livingstone, Stanley; Influence of the advance of technology/science; Development of the Suez Canal
  - Impact of colonialism on Africa, including: Imposition of arbitrary boundaries by colonising powers; Human exploitation and abuse: the Belgian Congo; Conversion to Christianity, healthcare and education; Infrastructure development; Causes and impact of African resistance, including: The Anglo-Zulu war of 1879, Ndebele rebellion, The Battle of Adwa, Herero War
- **What to cover and stress.** Motives: economic (raw materials, markets, investment; Hobson and Lenin's theories), religious and cultural ('civilising mission', missionaries), national and strategic rivalry (prestige, Suez and the route to India, Egypt 1882). Process: direct rule (France, Belgium, Germany) and indirect rule (Britain; Lugard in Northern Nigeria); explorers Barth, Livingstone (d. 1873), Stanley ('Dr Livingstone, I presume', 1871; Congo for Leopold 1879–84); technology and science: steamships, railways, telegraph, quinine, Maxim gun; Suez Canal (opened 17 November 1869; British share purchase 1875). Impact: arbitrary boundaries; the Congo Free State (1885–1908; red rubber, forced labour, hand amputations, population loss: estimates vary widely, say so; Casement Report 1904, E.D. Morel; Belgian annexation 1908); Christianity, healthcare, education (mission schools); infrastructure built for extraction; African resistance: Anglo-Zulu War (1879; Isandlwana and Rorke's Drift, Ulundi), Ndebele rebellion (1893 war and 1896–97 rising), Battle of Adwa (1 March 1896; Menelik II defeats Italy), Herero War (1904–08; von Trotha's order of October 1904; genocide of the Herero and Nama, recognised by Germany in 2021).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Jules Ferry's speech to the Chamber of Deputies (28 July 1885) on colonial expansion; Rudyard Kipling, 'The White Man's Burden' (1899); Cecil Rhodes's 'Confession of Faith' (1877); the Casement Report (1904); E.D. Morel's writings; Mark Twain, King Leopold's Soliloquy (1905); Menelik II's circular letter to European powers (1891); cartoons such as 'The Rhodes Colossus' (Punch, 1892; visual).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why European powers wanted colonies in Africa after 1870. P2(b): 'Technology made the Scramble for Africa possible.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How far did African resistance limit European control? P1 (specimen used German attitudes to colonies: choose another): sources on the Congo Free State.
- **Historians you may name.** J.A. Hobson, Imperialism (1902); Lenin, Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism (1917); Ronald Robinson and John Gallagher, Africa and the Victorians (1961: strategic and 'peripheral' motives); Thomas Pakenham, The Scramble for Africa (1991); Adam Hochschild, King Leopold's Ghost (1998).
- **Pitfalls.** Sensitive: atrocities in the Congo and against the Herero and Nama: factual and respectful; African people as actors, not only victims. Avoid period racist terms except in quoted sources, which need context.
- **Figures and widgets.** Schematic SVG map of Africa in 1914 by colonial power (labelled regions, not precise borders) or a table of powers and main colonies; mark Ethiopia and Liberia as independent.
- **Next line.** `Next: [Imperialism and world powers (2): imperial expansion and international relations to 1914](#hist.int.empire.2)`

### hist.int.empire.2 · Imperialism and world powers (2): imperial expansion and international relations to 1914 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS International option (Section C). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 7 Imperialism and the emergence of world powers, c.1870–1918
- Key question 7.2 What was the impact of imperial expansion on international relations?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The Berlin Conference, 1884–85: Purpose, including: Pressures on Bismarck, Intentions of different powers; Outcome, including: Principle of ‘effective occupation’, Recognising King Leopold’s Congo claim, End of slavery, Free trade and navigation
  - Rivalries between colonial powers, including: Between Britain and Germany, Weltpolitik and naval rivalry, and tension over South Africa; Between France and Britain over the Sudan: the Fashoda Incident; Between France and Germany over Morocco
  - Attempts to resolve tensions between imperial nations after 1900, including: The end of Britain’s ‘splendid isolation’ and the Anglo-French Entente; The Algeciras conference; The convention of November 1911 that ended the Agadir crisis
  - The situation in 1914, including: The extent of European empires; Influence on European alliances; Contemporary views and verdicts
- **What to cover and stress.** Berlin Conference (15 November 1884–26 February 1885): Bismarck's motives (colonial lobby, elections, relations with France and Britain), powers' intentions; General Act: effective occupation, Leopold's Congo recognised, end of slavery and the slave trade, free trade and navigation on the Congo and Niger. Rivalries: Britain–Germany (Weltpolitik from 1897, Tirpitz's Navy Laws 1898 and 1900, Dreadnought race from 1906; Kruger Telegram 1896; South Africa and the Boer War 1899–1902), France–Britain (Fashoda, September 1898; Marchand and Kitchener), France–Germany (Morocco: Tangier 1905, Algeciras 1906, Agadir 1911). Resolutions: end of 'splendid isolation' (Anglo-Japanese Alliance 1902), Entente Cordiale (8 April 1904), Anglo-Russian Convention (1907), Algeciras, the convention of 4 November 1911 (Morocco to France, part of the French Congo to Germany). Situation in 1914: extent of empires (a table: Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Italy, Russia), influence on alliances (Triple Entente vs Triple Alliance), contemporary views (Hobson, Lenin; pro-imperial opinion).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** The General Act of the Berlin Conference (1885), Articles 6 and 35; Bismarck's closing speech (the specimen used it: choose another extract); Bülow's 'place in the sun' speech (6 December 1897); the Kruger Telegram (3 January 1896); the Kaiser's Tangier speech (31 March 1905); Lloyd George's Mansion House speech (21 July 1911); the Crowe Memorandum (1 January 1907); cartoons of Fashoda (visual).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why the Berlin Conference was held in 1884–85. P2(b): 'Colonial rivalry was the main cause of tension between the Great Powers before 1914.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How far did the Moroccan crises strengthen the Entente? P1 on Fashoda.
- **Historians you may name.** Robinson and Gallagher; Paul Kennedy, The Rise of the Anglo-German Antagonism (1980); Christopher Clark, The Sleepwalkers (2012) for 1911.
- **Pitfalls.** Overlap with Paper 3 (origins of the First World War): keep the focus on imperial rivalry. Agadir is 1911 (second Moroccan crisis).
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of the crises (date, powers, outcome, effect on alliances).
- **Next line.** `Next: [Imperialism and world powers (3): why Japan emerged as a world power](#hist.int.empire.3)`
