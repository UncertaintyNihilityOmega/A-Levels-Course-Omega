# P47: Connections: the big-picture timeline and dictatorships compared

One Writer's package. Read, in this order: COURSE.md (rules, conventions, chapter structure, widget formats, the
verified links), kit/STYLE.md, the gold chapter chapters/hist.eu.france.1.md (match its structure, depth, tone and the
level-by-level model answers), then this file. Grep FACTS.md for the paper facts and level descriptors (never invent
exam facts). Write each chapter whole, then checks/<id>.py, then `node kit/zta.js check <id>`.

Why this package exists (user's request, 2026-11-11): a history student who read the course said that topics split by
country (Italy, Germany, Russia studied separately) hide how they connect, and that the course starts at the French
Revolution with nothing before. The syllabus really is set by topic (each exam question is on one topic, and a student
takes one option per paper), so the topic chapters stay; these two chapters join them up. Both chapters are
read by every student, whatever their options.

Rules that matter most here:
- Every fact exact; where you are not sure, leave it out. Items marked "check" are ones to verify in a reliable
  source before you use the number; if you cannot, write a rounded figure with "about" or leave it out.
- Sources: real documents only, provenance line in italics, "Adapted from" when shortened. Never an invented quotation.
  Historians: named only with work and year, as in these notes.
- Link generously to the topic chapters (`[Title](#id)`; only ids in course.json), and say what each linked chapter
  teaches in more depth. Do not repeat their content at their depth: summarise and point.
- Never claim the exam asks comparative questions. Paper 4 sets one question per topic; comparison is used as context
  and to support a judgement. Practice questions use the real command forms only (COURSE.md).

## Chapters

### hist.f.eras · History from zero (2): the big picture, from before 1789 to 1991 (`@level pre`)

- Examined: not directly. It is the map every option sits on; it helps every paper by giving context.
- **What to cover and stress.**
  - Opening: why a syllabus is split by country and topic, and why historians still think in eras and global
    phenomena (revolutions, nationalism, industrialisation, imperialism, total war, dictatorship, decolonisation, the
    Cold War) that cross borders. Students study only their own options' chapters; this chapter shows where the rest fit.
  - **Before 1789 (not examined, clearly badged; about a sixth of the chapter).** The world of the 18th century in
    plain words: monarchies and absolutism (Louis XIV, divine right); the great empires (Habsburg, Ottoman, Russian,
    Qing China, the Mughals in decline, the British and Spanish Atlantic empires); the Atlantic slave trade; the
    Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment (Locke, Montesquieu, Rousseau, Voltaire: one idea each); the Seven
    Years' War (1756–63) and its debts; the American Revolution (1775–83; Declaration of Independence 1776) as the
    model and the debt that led into France's crisis; early industrialisation in Britain. Then a short "even earlier"
    paragraph: the very long story (ancient, medieval, early modern) in five or six lines, with `:::links` for further
    reading (verify each new link: https, a 200 response, a reputable site such as Khan Academy world history, Crash
    Course World History, the BBC or a university page; end with the "Links checked" line).
  - **The eras of the course**, one section each: what happened across the world, which course chapters belong to it
    (by option and paper), and 2–4 concrete connections between them:
    1. Age of revolutions, 1774–1849: France 1789–1815; Napoleon redraws Germany; 1848 across Europe (France, the
       German states, Austria, Italy). Links: hist.eu.france.*, hist.eu.germany.1–2.
    2. Nations, industry and empire, 1850–1914: German unification; Civil War and Reconstruction; the Gilded Age;
       Tsarist Russia's crises; the scramble for Africa; Japan and the USA as new powers; the road to 1914.
       Links: hist.eu.germany.3–4, hist.eu.russia.1–2, hist.us.civilwar.*, hist.us.gilded.*, hist.int.empire.*,
       hist.interp.ww1.*.
    3. World war and its aftermath, 1914–29: the First World War as the hinge (the student's own suggestion: read
       1910–1920 with a global view); 1917 in Russia; the peace settlements; Mussolini's rise; the League; the 1920s.
       Links: hist.interp.ww1.*, hist.eu.russia.3–4, hist.int.1919.*, hist.d.italy.1, hist.d.stalin.1.
    4. Depression, dictatorship and the road to war, 1929–41: the Crash spreads (USA, Germany, Japan); Hitler's rise;
       Stalin's revolution from above; the New Deal; Manchuria, Abyssinia, appeasement; 1939; the Holocaust.
       Links: hist.us.depression.*, hist.d.hitler.*, hist.d.stalin.*, hist.d.italy.*, hist.int.1929.*,
       hist.interp.holocaust.*, hist.d.compare.
    5. Cold War and decolonisation, 1945–91: the breakdown of the wartime alliance; the USA at home and abroad;
       Soviet control of Eastern Europe and its collapse; the end of empires in Asia and Africa; apartheid.
       Links: hist.interp.coldwar.*, hist.d.usa45.*, hist.d.usa61.*, hist.d.usa74.*, hist.d.soviet.*,
       hist.d.safrica.*, hist.d.malaysia.*.
  - **Phenomena across borders** (a short section): nationalism, liberalism, socialism and communism, fascism,
    imperialism, total war, each with 2–3 places it shows up in the course (link the chapters).
  - **Reading by era**: three ready-made reading paths in chronological order across options (e.g. 1900–45: the
    origins of WW1 → the 1917 revolutions → the peace settlements → Mussolini → Stalin → the Depression → Hitler → the
    1930s crises → the Holocaust → the Cold War), with a note that a student revising for the exam reads their own
    options first.
- **Worked examples and practice.** Worked examples: put 8 events from different countries in order and explain two
  causal links that cross a border; turn a one-country explanation into one with international context (e.g. why
  Hitler gained support in 1930–32: the Depression came from the USA's crash). Try questions throughout (ordering,
  "which came first", "what connects X and Y"), with full answers. No [30] essays here.
- **Historians you may name.** E.J. Hobsbawm, The Age of Revolution 1789–1848 (1962), The Age of Empire 1875–1914
  (1987) and Age of Extremes (1994): one idea each (his "long nineteenth century" and "short twentieth century").
  C.A. Bayly, The Birth of the Modern World 1780–1914 (2004): global connections.
- **Pitfalls.** Level pre: plain language, define every term; do not overload with dates (the timeline widget holds
  them). Before-1789 material is badged "not examined" (`:::note Not examined`). Keep era dates as conventions, not
  facts ("historians often use…").
- **Figures and widgets.**
  - An SVG "one axis" chart, 1770–2000: one horizontal bar per course topic (France 1774–1814, Germany 1815–71,
    Russia 1881–1924, Civil War 1840s–77, Gilded Age 1870–1920, Depression 1920s–41, Imperialism 1870–1914,
    International 1919–39, WW1 origins, Holocaust, Cold War origins, Italy 1919–41, Stalin 1924–41, Hitler 1929–41,
    the Paper 4 American and International topics) grouped by paper and option, with era bands shaded behind them;
    take each bar's dates from the topic titles in course.json and FACTS.md (check). Check coordinates and label
    positions with Python; it must read at 360 px (wrap or stack the labels; a horizontal scroll is acceptable only
    inside the figure).
  - `hist-timeline` with lanes (e.g. `data-lanes="Europe,Americas,Asia and Africa,International"`), 30–45 key events
    from 1756 to 1991, each one sentence, so a student can filter by region.

### hist.d.compare · Dictatorships compared: Italy, the Soviet Union and Germany (`@level a2`)

- Examined: supports A Level **Paper 4** European option (Italy, Stalin, Hitler) and gives context for Paper 3 (the
  Holocaust, the Cold War) and AS International 1929–39. No exam question is comparative: say so early.
- **What to cover and stress.** Organise by the four key questions the three topics share, so a student who studies
  any two of them sees the parallels and the differences:
  1. Gaining power: crisis after 1918 (mutilated victory, revolution and civil war, Weimar's crises); legal vs
     revolutionary routes (March on Rome 1922 and the King's appointment; the Bolshevik seizure of power 1917 and
     Stalin's rise inside the party 1924–29; Hitler appointed 1933 and the Enabling Act); the role of the elites, the
     army and violence (squadristi, SA, the Cheka/OGPU). Mussolini as a model: Hitler's Beer Hall Putsch (Nov 1923)
     followed the March on Rome (Oct 1922).
  2. Governing: terror (OVRA, NKVD and the Great Terror, SS/Gestapo; scale differs greatly: give verified figures
     only, on the same basis); propaganda and the leader cult; the party and the state; how far each was
     "totalitarian" (Friedrich and Brzezinski's model; Arendt; the "weak dictator" and "working towards the Führer"
     debates; Mussolini's compromises with King, Church and army).
  3. Economies: the corporate state and the battles (grain, lira, births); the Five-Year Plans and collectivisation;
     Schacht's New Plan and the Four-Year Plan; autarky and rearmament in all three; who paid.
  4. Societies: women, youth, workers, peasants, religion, minorities; how far each regime changed society.
  - **Links between the regimes**: Italy and Germany over Austria (1934) and Abyssinia; the Rome–Berlin Axis (1936),
    the Anti-Comintern Pact (Germany–Japan 1936; Italy 1937), the Pact of Steel (1939), the Nazi–Soviet Pact (Aug
    1939), Barbarossa (June 1941); fascism vs communism as rival ideologies; the Spanish Civil War. Check every date.
  - A `:::cols` comparison table per key question (Italy / USSR / Germany), and a "how to use comparison in a Paper 4
    essay" section.
- **Worked examples and practice.**
  - `:::eg` A Level 5 paragraph for a single-topic Paper 4 question (e.g. on how effectively Mussolini governed Italy)
    that uses one comparative sentence about Hitler or Stalin as context to support the judgement, with the
    comparison kept short and relevant; then the same paragraph spoiled by too much comparison (it drifts off the
    question), with the level and the fix.
  - `:::eg` A full Level 5 answer plan for "Assess the reasons why …" on one regime, showing where context from the
    others strengthens the evaluation.
  - `:::try` recall questions (which regime did what, when), comparison grids to fill, and exam practice in the real
    single-topic Paper 4 forms only, 2–3 per regime, each answer showing where a comparison helps. Label any
    comparative discussion question "for understanding, not an exam question type".
- **Historians you may name.** Hannah Arendt, The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951); Carl Friedrich and Zbigniew
  Brzezinski, Totalitarian Dictatorship and Autocracy (1956); Ian Kershaw and Moshe Lewin (eds), Stalinism and Nazism:
  Dictatorships in Comparison (1997); Richard Overy, The Dictators: Hitler's Germany and Stalin's Russia (2004);
  R.J.B. Bosworth, Mussolini's Italy (2005). Check each title and year before use.
- **Pitfalls.** Do not treat the three as the same (different ideologies, aims, scale of terror and death toll);
  do not rank suffering crudely. The Holocaust's distinct character: link hist.interp.holocaust.1 rather than
  summarise it. Every number on the same base when compared (COURSE.md). Summarise the topic chapters, never copy them.
- **Figures and widgets.** SVG timeline of the three regimes on one axis, 1917–1945 (Bolshevik revolution, March on
  Rome, Stalin's rise, Five-Year Plans, Hitler appointed, Great Terror, Axis, Nazi–Soviet Pact, Barbarossa, Mussolini's
  fall 1943), checked with Python, readable at 360 px. hist-chart only if one comparable verified series exists on
  the same basis (otherwise none).
- **Next line.** `Next: [Post-war USA, 1945–61 (1): Truman and the internal issues of post-war America](#hist.d.usa45.1)`
