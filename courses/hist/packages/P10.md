### hist.us.gilded.3 · Gilded Age and Progressive Era (3): why the Progressive Movement developed: aims and policies (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS American option (Section B). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 5 The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920
- Key question 5.3 Why did the Progressive Movement develop and what were its aims and policies?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The reasons for the growth of the reform movement, including: The work of the muckrakers; Temperance movement; The frequency of banking ‘panics’ and the concentration of wealth in the hands of the few; The impact of rapid and uncontrolled urbanisation and endemic corruption; The demand by women’s groups for radical social change and their participation in society and government
  - Reforms in cities, states, Congress, Federal Government and political parties, including: Widening the franchise and reforming elections; Primary elections and the direct election of Senators; Civil Service reform
  - The regulation of banking, business and industry, including: The recognition of labor unions; Regulation of working conditions; New controls over banks, insurance companies, trusts and the Stock Market
- **What to cover and stress.** Muckrakers (Tarbell, Steffens, Sinclair, Riis; Roosevelt named them in 1906); temperance (WCTU 1874, Anti-Saloon League 1893); banking panics and concentration of wealth; urbanisation and corruption; women's groups (settlement houses, Jane Addams' Hull House 1889; NAWSA 1890; General Federation of Women's Clubs; women's social reform roles). Reforms in cities (commission and city-manager government), states (La Follette's Wisconsin Idea; initiative, referendum, recall; secret ballot; primaries; direct election of senators before the 17th Amendment), Congress and federal government, parties; civil service reform (Pendleton Act 1883, after Garfield's assassination). Regulation: unions recognised (limited; Clayton Act 1914 later), working conditions (state laws on hours, Muller v. Oregon 1908, Lochner 1905), controls on banks, insurance (Armstrong investigation 1905), trusts and the stock market.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Ida Tarbell, The History of the Standard Oil Company (1904); Lincoln Steffens, The Shame of the Cities (1904); Upton Sinclair, The Jungle (1906); Theodore Roosevelt's 'The Man with the Muck-Rake' speech (14 April 1906); Jane Addams, Twenty Years at Hull-House (1910); temperance cartoons (visual).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why the muckrakers were important to the Progressive Movement. P2(b): 'The Progressive Movement was a middle-class movement.' How far do you agree? P2(b): To what extent was corruption the main reason for the growth of the reform movement? P1 on the muckrakers.
- **Historians you may name.** Richard Hofstadter, The Age of Reform (1955: status anxiety); Robert Wiebe, The Search for Order (1967); Gabriel Kolko, The Triumph of Conservatism (1963: business shaped regulation).
- **Pitfalls.** Progressivism was many movements, not one party. The Progressive (Bull Moose) Party of 1912 is only part of it.
- **Figures and widgets.** Table: problem, reform, level (city, state, federal), date.

### hist.us.gilded.4 · Gilded Age and Progressive Era (4): how successful the Progressive Movement was, 1890–1920 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS American option (Section B). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 5 The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920
- Key question 5.4 How successful was the Progressive Movement from 1890 to 1920?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The role of the Presidents, Roosevelt, Taft and Wilson, including: The changing role of the presidency; The increasing intervention by the Federal Government in social and economic affairs
  - The growth of regulation by city, state and federal governments, including: Transport, food and drugs and environmental protection; Working and living conditions; Banks, businesses, the stock market, monopolies and trusts; Anti-corruption measures
  - The amendments to the Constitution: 16th amendment; 17th amendment; 18th amendment; 19th amendment
  - The impact of pressure groups and the role played by women
  - The remaining problems of the Progressive movement, including: The nature and extent of the legislation; Minorities; Gender inequality; Rural deprivation
- **What to cover and stress.** Presidents: Theodore Roosevelt (1901–09: Square Deal, trust-busting Northern Securities 1904, coal strike 1902, Hepburn Act 1906, Meat Inspection and Pure Food and Drug Acts 1906, conservation), Taft (1909–13: more antitrust suits, Mann–Elkins Act 1910, Payne–Aldrich Tariff, Ballinger–Pinchot), Wilson (1913–21: New Freedom; Underwood Tariff and income tax 1913, Federal Reserve Act 1913, Clayton Act and FTC 1914, Keating–Owen child labour act 1916, struck down 1918); the stronger presidency. Regulation by city, state, federal; transport, food and drugs, environment (national forests, Antiquities Act 1906), working and living conditions, banks and trusts, anti-corruption. Amendments: 16th (income tax, 1913), 17th (direct election of senators, 1913), 18th (Prohibition, ratified 1919), 19th (women's suffrage, 1920). Pressure groups and women (NAWSA, Alice Paul's National Woman's Party, the Anti-Saloon League). Limits: legislation weak or struck down; minorities (segregation, Plessy 1896, Wilson's federal segregation; NAACP 1909); gender inequality; rural deprivation.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Roosevelt's 'New Nationalism' speech (Osawatomie, 31 August 1910); Wilson's first inaugural (4 March 1913); the 16th–19th Amendments; NAACP's The Crisis (Du Bois, from 1910); suffrage posters (visual); Booker T. Washington's Atlanta speech (1895) as background.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why the 18th Amendment was passed. P2(b): 'Theodore Roosevelt was the most successful Progressive president.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How successful was the Progressive Movement in improving living and working conditions? P1 on women's suffrage.
- **Historians you may name.** Hofstadter; Kolko; Michael McGerr, A Fierce Discontent (2003).
- **Pitfalls.** Ratification dates differ from passage dates: give ratification. The Progressive Era was worse for African Americans in many ways: do not leave minorities out of 'success'.
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of the four amendments; a 'scorecard' table of successes and limits by group.
