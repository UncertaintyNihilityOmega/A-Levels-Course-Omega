### hist.us.gilded.1 · Gilded Age and Progressive Era (1): why there was rapid industrialisation after 1870 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS American option (Section B). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 5 The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920
- Key question 5.1 Why was there rapid industrialisation after 1870?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The impact of the Civil War on industrialisation, including: Opening of the West; Railroad expansion; Industrial expansion
  - Population growth, including: Migration of emancipated slaves North and West; Mass immigration into the United States
  - Availability of natural resources: Raw materials and food; Energy resources; Access to export markets
  - Lack of regulation and support from State and Federal Governments, including: Tariffs; Money supply and availability of capital; Banking systems
  - Technological innovation, including: Electrical power and the internal combustion engine; The typewriter and the telephone; US system of patents; Developments in steel production
  - Contemporary social attitudes towards commercial growth and success
- **What to cover and stress.** Civil War effects: Homestead Act (1862), Pacific Railroad Acts (1862), national banking, tariffs; transcontinental railroad (10 May 1869); railroad mileage (about 35,000 miles in 1865 to about 190,000–200,000 by 1900; cite); Great Migration's beginnings (Exodusters 1879); immigration (about 25 million 1870–1916; new immigrants from southern and eastern Europe; Chinese Exclusion Act 1882); resources (coal, iron ore of the Mesabi range, oil, timber, land and food), energy, export markets; laissez-faire, tariffs (McKinley Tariff 1890), money supply and banking (gold standard debates, private banks, J.P. Morgan); innovation: electricity (Edison's Pearl Street station 1882), internal combustion engine, typewriter (1870s), telephone (Bell 1876), patents (about 640,000 patents 1860–1900; check), Bessemer and open-hearth steel (Carnegie); social attitudes: Social Darwinism (Spencer, Sumner), the 'gospel of wealth' (Carnegie 1889), Horatio Alger stories.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Andrew Carnegie, 'Wealth' (1889, the 'Gospel of Wealth'); William Graham Sumner, What Social Classes Owe to Each Other (1883); Frederick Jackson Turner, 'The Significance of the Frontier in American History' (1893); immigration posters and cartoons; census statistics.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why railroad expansion was important to industrialisation after 1870. P2(b): 'Technological innovation was the main reason for rapid industrialisation after 1870.' How far do you agree? (The 2024 Paper 2 asked about electric power: do not reuse.) P1 on attitudes to wealth.
- **Historians you may name.** Alfred Chandler, The Visible Hand (1977); Richard White, Railroaded (2011); Robert Gordon, The Rise and Fall of American Growth (2016).
- **Pitfalls.** Numbers: give sources and round honestly. 'Lack of regulation' and 'support from government' both appear in the syllabus: government helped (tariffs, land grants) while not regulating.
- **Figures and widgets.** hist-chart (if built): railroad mileage and steel output 1870–1900 from a cited source (e.g. Historical Statistics of the United States).
- **Next line.** `Next: [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (2): the consequences of rapid economic growth](#hist.us.gilded.2)`

### hist.us.gilded.2 · Gilded Age and Progressive Era (2): the consequences of rapid economic growth (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS American option (Section B). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 5 The Gilded Age and the Progressive Era, 1870–1920
- Key question 5.2 What were the consequences of the rapid economic growth after 1870?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Industrial expansion and economic recessions, including: The nature and extent of industrial growth; The reasons for, and consequences of, the ‘Panics’
  - The social impact of industrialisation and urbanisation, including: Living and working conditions; Housing; Health; The ‘Boss’ system
  - The growth of trusts and monopolies, including: Impact on political parties, states and legislatures; Impact on the Federal government and the economy; The Robber Barons
  - The rise of organised labour and the growth of populist movements, including: The National Labor movement; The Knights of Labor and the American Federation of Labor; Farm organisations and the Granger Movement; The Greenback Party
- **What to cover and stress.** Nature and extent of growth; Panics of 1873 and 1893 (and 1907 for context): causes (railroad speculation, Jay Cooke; silver, gold reserves) and consequences (unemployment, strikes, populism). Social impact: tenements, immigrant neighbourhoods, health (cholera, tuberculosis), child labour, dangerous work; the 'Boss' system (Tammany Hall, Boss Tweed). Trusts and monopolies: Standard Oil (Rockefeller), Carnegie Steel, U.S. Steel (1901), railroads (Vanderbilt), banks (Morgan); influence on parties and legislatures (Senate as 'millionaires' club'); Interstate Commerce Act (1887), Sherman Anti-Trust Act (1890) and its weak enforcement (E.C. Knight 1895; used against unions); Robber Barons vs captains of industry. Organised labour: National Labor Union (1866), Knights of Labor (Powderly; Haymarket 1886), AFL (1886; Gompers; craft unions), Great Railroad Strike (1877), Homestead (1892), Pullman (1894); farmers: Grange (1867), Granger laws, Munn v. Illinois (1877), Farmers' Alliances, Greenback Party (1870s), People's Party (Omaha Platform 1892; 1896 election and Bryan's 'Cross of Gold').
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Jacob Riis, How the Other Half Lives (1890; text and photographs); Joseph Keppler's cartoon 'The Bosses of the Senate' (Puck, 1889; visual); the Sherman Anti-Trust Act (1890); the Omaha Platform (1892); Henry George, Progress and Poverty (1879); newspaper reports of Homestead and Pullman.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why the Knights of Labor declined after 1886. P2(b) (the 2024 paper asked how significant organised labour was: write another): To what extent did the growth of trusts harm the American economy? P1 (specimen used the Sherman Act and trusts: choose another issue): the sources on the Homestead strike.
- **Historians you may name.** Matthew Josephson, The Robber Barons (1934); Chandler; Richard White; Lawrence Goodwyn, The Populist Moment (1978).
- **Pitfalls.** The specimen Paper 1 American question is on this topic (trusts, Sherman Act): practise similar skills on other issues, never copy it. Distinguish Knights (all workers) and AFL (skilled crafts).
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of the major strikes (date, industry, cause, outcome).
- **Next line.** `Next: [Gilded Age and Progressive Era (3): why the Progressive Movement developed: aims and policies](#hist.us.gilded.3)`
