### hist.int.1919.1 · International relations, 1919–29 (1): dissatisfaction with the peace settlements of 1919–20 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS International option (Section C). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2028**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 8 International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation
- Key question 8.1 Why was there such extensive dissatisfaction with the peace settlements of 1919–20?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The negotiations at Versailles, including: Key issues in agreeing the Treaties, including: The aims of the Big Three, Representation of other powers; Key terms of the Treaties of Versailles, Trianon, Neuilly, Saint Germain and Sèvres
  - Difficulties created by the treaties, including: Boundaries created by the settlements; Inconsistent application of national self-determination
  - Reactions of victors and defeated powers: France, Britain, USA, Italy, Japan and Germany; Position of Russia
  - Reparations: The question of ‘war guilt’; The issue of size and payment
- **What to cover and stress.** Paris Peace Conference (from 18 January 1919): Big Three aims (Clemenceau security and reparations; Lloyd George compromise and trade; Wilson the Fourteen Points and the League), Orlando and Italy, other powers' representation (small states, colonies, Japan's racial equality proposal; Germany and Russia excluded). Treaties: Versailles (28 June 1919; Germany: territory about 13 per cent, colonies, army 100,000, Rhineland, Article 231, reparations set in 1921 at 132 billion gold marks), Saint-Germain (Austria, 1919), Neuilly (Bulgaria, 1919), Trianon (Hungary, 4 June 1920; about two-thirds of territory lost), Sèvres (Ottoman Empire, 1920; replaced by Lausanne 1923). Difficulties: boundaries, minorities, inconsistent self-determination (Sudeten Germans, Anschluss forbidden, South Tyrol, Danzig and the Corridor). Reactions: France (too soft), Britain (Keynes; guilt), USA (Senate rejects), Italy (mutilated victory, Fiume), Japan (Shantung, racial equality), Germany (Diktat, 'stab in the back'), Russia's position. Reparations: war guilt, size, payment, 1921 schedule (A, B and C bonds).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Wilson's Fourteen Points (8 January 1918); Lloyd George's Fontainebleau Memorandum (25 March 1919); Brockdorff-Rantzau's speech at Versailles (7 May 1919); the Deutsche Zeitung's 'Vengeance!' front page (28 June 1919); Will Dyson's cartoon 'Peace and Future Cannon Fodder' (Daily Herald, 1919; visual); Keynes, The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919); Article 231.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why Germany objected to the Treaty of Versailles. P2(b): 'The peace settlements of 1919–20 were a fair compromise.' How far do you agree? P2(b): To what extent did the principle of self-determination cause problems in the peace settlements? P1 on reparations.
- **Historians you may name.** Keynes; Margaret MacMillan, Peacemakers (2001); Sally Marks, 'The Myths of Reparations' (1978); Zara Steiner, The Lights that Failed (2005).
- **Pitfalls.** Article 231 does not use the words 'war guilt': quote it accurately or paraphrase. Reparations sums: the 132 billion gold marks figure and the C bonds that were never expected to be paid.
- **Figures and widgets.** Schematic SVG map of German territorial losses, or a table of the five treaties (date, defeated state, main terms).
- **Next line.** `Next: [International relations, 1919–29 (2): the League of Nations: creation and challenges in the 1920s](#hist.int.1919.2)`

### hist.int.1919.2 · International relations, 1919–29 (2): the League of Nations: creation and challenges in the 1920s (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS International option (Section C). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2028**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 8 International relations 1919–29: conflict and cooperation
- Key question 8.2 Why was the League of Nations created and what challenges did it face in the 1920s?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Creation of the League, including: Wilson’s 14 points; Inclusion in all the Treaties; Aims and structure
  - Weaknesses of the League, including: Restricted membership; Leadership; Voting processes; Enforcement difficulties
  - Collective security and the League’s involvement in the resolution of post-war disputes, including: The Aaland Islands; Teschen; Polish seizure of Vilna; The Upper Silesian Coalfields dispute; Memel; Greco-Bulgarian border dispute
  - Role and impact of the Agencies, including: Commission for Refugees; International Labour Organisation (ILO); Slavery Commission; Disarmament Commission; Mandates Commission
- **What to cover and stress.** Creation: Wilson's Fourteen Points (point 14), Covenant included in all the treaties (Part I), aims (collective security, disarmament, cooperation), structure (Assembly, Council with permanent and non-permanent members, Secretariat at Geneva, Permanent Court of International Justice 1922, agencies). Weaknesses: membership (USA never joined; Germany until 1926; USSR until 1934), leadership by Britain and France with different aims, unanimity voting, enforcement (moral pressure, sanctions, no army; Article 16). Disputes: Aaland Islands (1920–21, to Finland with autonomy for the Swedish-speaking population), Teschen (1920, divided; settled by the Conference of Ambassadors), Vilna (seized by Poland October 1920; League failed), Upper Silesia (plebiscite 1921, partition), Memel (seized by Lithuania 1923; Memel Convention 1924), Greco-Bulgarian incident (October 1925; League success). Agencies: Refugees (Nansen; Nansen passports 1922), ILO (1919; Albert Thomas), Slavery Commission (Sierra Leone 1927; slavery convention 1926), Disarmament Commission, Mandates Commission (A, B, C mandates).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** The Covenant of the League (Articles 10, 12, 16); the US Senate debate and Lodge's reservations (1919); Leonard Raven-Hill's cartoon 'The Gap in the Bridge' (Punch, December 1919; visual); Nansen's reports on refugees; ILO conventions; League reports on the disputes.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a) (specimen asked about Aaland: write another): Explain why the League failed to resolve the Vilna dispute. P2(b) (specimen compared agencies and peacekeeping: write another): 'The absence of the USA was the League's greatest weakness in the 1920s.' How far do you agree? P1 on the Corfu crisis (belongs to KQ 8.3: use only lightly here).
- **Historians you may name.** Ruth Henig, The League of Nations (2010); Susan Pedersen, The Guardians (2015, on mandates); F.P. Walters, A History of the League of Nations (1952).
- **Pitfalls.** The specimen Paper 2 International question is on this KQ: write original questions. Teschen was settled by the Conference of Ambassadors (1920), not by League action alone: be precise.
- **Figures and widgets.** SVG of the League's structure (Assembly, Council, Secretariat, Court, agencies).
- **Next line.** `Next: [International relations, 1919–29 (3): why tensions remained high after Versailles](#hist.int.1919.3)`
