### hist.eu.russia.1 · Russia (1): the challenges facing Tsarism, 1881–94 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS European option (Section A). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2029**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2028**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 3 Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924
- Key question 3.1 What challenges faced the Tsarist regime in the years 1881–94?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - How Russia was ruled in the period 1881–94: Autocracy: the role of the Tsar, State Council and key ministries, the army, and the Church; Reform and repression, including: Alexander III’s policies, Russification, Repression
  - The development of the Russian economy in the period 1881–94: Economic developments, including: Bunge’s policies, Industrialisation, Economic problems, including taxation and famines
  - Social change in the period 1881–94: Social development, urban growth and development of the middle class
  - The role of opposition: The development of opposition from the peasants and urban workers; Growth of Marxism
- **What to cover and stress.** Assassination of Alexander II (1 March 1881, Old Style) by the People's Will; Alexander III's Manifesto (April 1881); autocracy: Tsar, State Council, Committee of Ministers, ministries (Interior), the Okhrana, army, Orthodox Church and the Holy Synod (Pobedonostsev, Over-Procurator); 'Orthodoxy, Autocracy, Nationality'; counter-reforms: Statute on Measures for the Protection of State Order (August 1881; 'temporary' emergency powers), land captains (1889), restrictions on zemstvos (1890) and universities (1884); Russification (Poles, Finns, Baltic peoples, Ukrainians); antisemitic pogroms (1881–82) and the May Laws (1882); repression and exile. Economy: Bunge (finance minister 1881–86; abolished the poll tax, Peasant Land Bank 1883, factory inspectorate), Vyshnegradsky (exporting grain: 'we may not eat enough, but we will export'), Witte (from 1892, gold standard 1897 and railway building fall in KQ 3.2 but begin here), industrial growth, taxation of peasants, famine of 1891–92 (estimates of deaths around 400,000; give the range). Society: urban growth, workers, the small middle class, the zemstvo liberals. Opposition: peasants (redemption payments), workers' strikes (Morozov 1885), Populism, Marxism (Plekhanov, Emancipation of Labour group 1883), Lenin's brother executed (1887).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Alexander III's Manifesto of 29 April 1881; Pobedonostsev's writings (e.g. Reflections of a Russian Statesman, 1896); the May Laws (1882); Tolstoy's writings on the 1891 famine; Plekhanov's programmes; government statistics on industrial output.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why Alexander III followed a policy of repression after 1881. P2(b): How far did the Russian economy develop successfully between 1881 and 1894? P2(b): 'Russification strengthened the Tsarist regime.' How far do you agree? P1 on the famine of 1891–92 and the government's response.
- **Historians you may name.** Hugh Seton-Watson, The Russian Empire 1801–1917 (1967); Richard Pipes, Russia under the Old Regime (1974); Orlando Figes, A People's Tragedy (1996); Geoffrey Hosking, Russia: People and Empire (1997).
- **Pitfalls.** Dates: Russia used the Julian (Old Style) calendar, 12 days behind in the nineteenth century, 13 after 1900: say which. Witte's main policies are after 1892 and belong mostly in KQ 3.2.
- **Figures and widgets.** Build the hist-chart widget here: Russian industrial growth 1880–1900 (coal, pig iron, railway kilometres) from a cited standard source (e.g. figures quoted in Seton-Watson or in a statistical abstract), with the source shown. Also a structure-of-autocracy SVG.

### hist.eu.russia.2 · Russia (2): the 1905 Revolution and its outcomes to 1914 (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS European option (Section A). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2029**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2027** and **2028**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 3 Russia from autocracy to revolution, 1881–1924
- Key question 3.2 What were the causes and outcomes of the 1905 Revolution up to 1914?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - The causes of the 1905 Revolution: Discontent with the regime of Nicholas II, including: Resentment caused by the lack of political reform; Political opposition, The Socialist Revolutionaries, The Social Democrats; Economic problems, including: Bad harvests, Unemployment, Witte’s policies, Taxation; Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War
  - The events and consequences of the 1905 Revolution: Bloody Sunday, strikes and unrest in 1905; The reactions of Nicholas II to the 1905 revolution, including: October Manifesto, The formation of the Duma
  - Reasons for the survival of the Tsarist regime: Nature and extent of opposition; The Fundamental Laws and the Dumas; Repression
  - The extent of changes in Russia between 1905 and the start of the First World War: Stolypin’s agrarian reforms and their impact; Developments in industry and their impact
- **What to cover and stress.** Nicholas II (1894; 'senseless dreams' speech, January 1895); Witte's industrialisation (1892–1903): gold standard 1897, foreign loans and investment, Trans-Siberian Railway (begun 1891), heavy taxes and tariffs; industrial depression 1900–03; bad harvests; SRs (1901; terrorism; Plehve assassinated 1904) and Social Democrats (1898; split 1903 into Bolsheviks and Mensheviks); liberals (Union of Liberation 1904; banquet campaign); Russo-Japanese War (February 1904–September 1905; Port Arthur, Mukden, Tsushima May 1905; Treaty of Portsmouth). 1905: Bloody Sunday (9 January 1905; Gapon), strikes, peasant risings, Potemkin mutiny (June), general strike (October), St Petersburg Soviet (Trotsky); October Manifesto (17 October 1905); Duma; Fundamental Laws (April 1906: Article 4 'supreme autocratic power'); the four Dumas (1906–17), the electoral law of 3 June 1907; repression (Stolypin's field courts, 'Stolypin's necktie'); why the regime survived (army loyal, opposition divided, October Manifesto split liberals, peace with Japan, foreign loan 1906). Stolypin's agrarian reforms (1906–11; consolidated holdings, Peasant Land Bank, migration to Siberia; by 1914 perhaps a fifth to a quarter of households had left the commune; check figures) and assassination (September 1911); industry 1907–14 (growth, Lena goldfields 1912, strikes rising to 1914).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Gapon's petition (January 1905); the October Manifesto (17 October 1905); the Fundamental Laws (April 1906); Stolypin's speeches to the Duma; Witte's memoirs (to evaluate); cartoons of Nicholas II; Lenin, What Is to Be Done? (1902).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why there was a revolution in Russia in 1905. P2(b): 'The October Manifesto was the main reason the Tsarist regime survived 1905.' How far do you agree? P2(b): How successful were Stolypin's agrarian reforms? P1 on Bloody Sunday.
- **Historians you may name.** Figes; Abraham Ascher, The Revolution of 1905 (1988–92); Sheila Fitzpatrick, The Russian Revolution (1982).
- **Pitfalls.** Bloody Sunday was 9 January (Old Style), 22 January (New Style). The Duma was not a parliament with control of government. Do not assume Stolypin's reforms 'failed' or 'succeeded' without evidence: weigh.
- **Figures and widgets.** hist-chart (from P5) for strikes 1905–14 or industrial output; table of the four Dumas.
