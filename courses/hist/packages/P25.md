### hist.d.stalin.3 · Stalin's Russia (3): how successful Stalin's economic policies were (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, European option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 2 Stalin’s Russia, 1924–41
- Key question 2.3 How successful were Stalin’s economic policies?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Reasons for the Great Turn, including: Security from invasion; Achievement of self-sufficiency; Creation of a fully socialist society and economy; Enhancing Stalin’s control
  - Agricultural policies, including: Reasons for collectivisation; Voluntary and forced collectivisation; State farms; The impact and extent of success: forced requisitioning, famine and agricultural yields; Dekulakisation
  - Industrial policies, including: Reasons for industrialisation; Gosplan; The organisation, aims and results of the first three Five Year Plans; Constructions, industrial centres and major projects; Stakhanovite movement
  - Living standards, including: The living and working conditions of managers and workers in urban areas; Wage differentials and incentives; Workplace discipline; Housing and consumer items
- **What to cover and stress.** Reasons for the Great Turn (1928–29): security (war scare 1927), self-sufficiency, socialism, Stalin's control, the grain procurement crisis (1927–28) and the Urals–Siberian method. Collectivisation: voluntary then forced (from late 1929; 'liquidation of the kulaks as a class', December 1929), 'Dizzy with Success' (March 1930), kolkhozes and sovkhozes, MTS, requisitioning, famine 1932–33 (Ukraine (Holodomor), Kazakhstan, Volga; estimates of deaths about 5–7 million overall; cite and give range), yields (grain output and livestock fell; check), dekulakisation (deportations of about 1.8 million in 1930–31; check). Industrialisation: Gosplan, Five-Year Plans (first 1928–32, declared complete in four years; second 1933–37; third 1938–41 interrupted), targets and results (coal, steel, oil, electricity: table with cited figures), projects (Magnitogorsk, Dnieprostroi, Belomor Canal by Gulag labour, Moscow Metro), Stakhanovite movement (31 August 1935; 102 tonnes of coal in a shift). Living standards: managers and workers, wage differentials, incentives, workplace discipline (labour books 1932, 1938–40 laws), housing (communal apartments), consumer goods shortages.
- **Worked examples and practice.** Specimen Q2 asked about reasons for the Great Turn: write a different question, e.g. 'Collectivisation was a complete failure.' Evaluate this view. Assess the success of the first three Five-Year Plans (2024 asked 'Evaluate the extent to which the Five Year Plans achieved Stalin's objectives': reword). Full Level 5 answer.
- **Historians you may name.** Alec Nove, An Economic History of the USSR (1969); R.W. Davies; Robert Conquest, The Harvest of Sorrow (1986); Lynne Viola, Peasant Rebels under Stalin (1996); Stephen Kotkin, Magnetic Mountain (1995).
- **Pitfalls.** Sensitive: the famine. Holodomor recognition debate: present it factually. Soviet statistics were inflated: say so and use Western reconstructions where possible.
- **Figures and widgets.** hist-chart (if built) of output 1928–40 with cited sources; table.

### hist.d.stalin.4 · Stalin's Russia (4): how far Stalin transformed Soviet society (`@level a2`)

- Examined: A Level **Paper 4**, European option: [30] essays, 'Assess' and 'Evaluate' (FACTS.md).
- Syllabus topic: 2 Stalin’s Russia, 1924–41
- Key question 2.4 How far did Stalin transform soviet society?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Women and the family, including: The Great Retreat and policies towards marriage, divorce and childbirth; Women’s role in the workplace: opportunities and limitations; Treatment of national groups within the Soviet Union
  - Youth and education, including: Education reforms and impact relating to primary, secondary and higher education; 1936 Great Retreat and reversal of earlier reforms
  - Cultural changes, including: Cultural Revolution; Socialist Realism in art, music, literature and cinema and their aims and impact
  - Religious policies, including: Repression towards organised religion; Impact and continued influence of the Church on society
- **What to cover and stress.** Women and the family: early Bolshevik liberalisation (1918 and 1926 codes), the Great Retreat: 1936 family law (abortion banned, divorce harder), Motherhood medals (1944 is later: avoid), women in work (share of workforce rising to about 40 per cent by 1940; check), double burden, limits. National groups: korenizatsiya in the 1920s then Russification, deportations of peoples (Koreans 1937; Poles, Germans 1941), purges of national elites. Youth and education: early experiments, the 1930s return to discipline (uniforms, exams, history textbooks 1934–37), literacy, higher education expansion, Komsomol, Young Pioneers. Culture: Cultural Revolution (1928–31; RAPP), Socialist Realism (1932–34 Union of Writers; Gorky; Zhdanov), music (Shostakovich, Lady Macbeth condemned January 1936), film (Eisenstein), art; aims and impact. Religion: League of the Militant Godless (1925), church closures, priests arrested, 1937 census results on belief (about 57 per cent declared believers; check), continued influence.
- **Worked examples and practice.** 'Stalin's policies towards women were a Great Retreat.' Evaluate this view. Assess the extent to which Stalin transformed Soviet culture. Full Level 5 answer.
- **Historians you may name.** Nicholas Timasheff, The Great Retreat (1946); Sheila Fitzpatrick, Everyday Stalinism (1999); Wendy Goldman, Women, the State and Revolution (1993).
- **Pitfalls.** Precise dates for laws; avoid wartime policies (after June 1941) except as a closing note.
- **Figures and widgets.** Table of policies toward women, 1917–41: change and continuity.
