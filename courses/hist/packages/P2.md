### hist.eu.france.3 · France (3): governing France, 1793–99: the Terror and the Directory (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS European option (Section A). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 1 France, 1774–1814
- Key question 1.3 How well was France governed in the period 1793–99?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Instability, 1793–95: Problems facing France after the execution of Louis XVI, including: The influence of the sans-culottes, Disagreement within the Convention, The effects of war on France in 1793; New constitution, 1793 and the Committee of Public Safety; Robespierre and the reign of terror; Economic problems; The White Terror, 1794–95; The Parisian risings, 1795
  - The Directory, 1795–99: The Constitution of the Year III; Aims of the Directory; Problems facing the Directory; Success and failures of the Directory, including: Financial, Military, Political
- **What to cover and stress.** January 1793 to November 1799. Execution of Louis XVI (21 January 1793); First Coalition; levée en masse (23 August 1793); Vendée revolt (from March 1793) and federalist revolts (Lyon, Marseille, Toulon) after the purge of the Girondins (2 June 1793); sans-culottes, the Enragés and Hébertists; the Constitution of 1793 (never applied); Committee of Public Safety (April 1793; Robespierre from July 1793); Revolutionary Tribunal; Law of Suspects (17 September 1793); General Maximum (29 September 1793); dechristianisation and the Cult of the Supreme Being (8 June 1794); Law of 22 Prairial (10 June 1794); Great Terror; Thermidor (27–28 July 1794); scale of the Terror (about 17,000 official death sentences; many more died in prison or in the Vendée; give ranges and say estimates differ). Economic problems: assignat collapse, the Maximum, food shortage. White Terror (1794–95); Germinal and Prairial risings (April and May 1795); Vendémiaire (5 October 1795, Bonaparte's 'whiff of grapeshot'). Directory: Constitution of Year III (two councils: Five Hundred and Ancients; five Directors; property franchise; two-thirds decree); aims (stability, end of Terror and royalism); problems (finances, war, left and right); Babeuf's Conspiracy of Equals (1796); Fructidor coup (4 September 1797); Floréal (1798); Prairial (1799); successes (bankruptcy of two-thirds 1797 then a more stable currency, Bonaparte's Italian victories, local administration) and failures (coups, corruption, reliance on the army).
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Robespierre, 'On the Principles of Political Morality' (5 February 1794: virtue and terror); the decree of the levée en masse (23 August 1793); the Law of Suspects (17 September 1793); the Law of 22 Prairial (10 June 1794); sans-culotte petitions; prints of the Terror and of Thermidor; the Constitution of Year III (1795).
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why the Committee of Public Safety introduced the Terror in 1793. P2(b): 'The Directory was a failure.' How far do you agree? P2(b): To what extent was war the main reason for the Terror? P1(a) compare Robespierre's speech and a sans-culotte petition as evidence about the purpose of the Terror; P1(b) how far the sources show that the Directory brought stability.
- **Historians you may name.** Albert Soboul (the sans-culottes, 1958); R.R. Palmer, Twelve Who Ruled (1941); Martyn Lyons, France under the Directory (1975); Peter McPhee, Robespierre: A Revolutionary Life (2012).
- **Pitfalls.** Sensitive: the Terror and the Vendée repression; factual, give numbers with sources and ranges, no gore. 'Reign of Terror' dates: usually September 1793 to July 1794. Do not call the Directory 'the Directoire constitution of 1793'. The Directory's failures must be weighed against real financial and administrative recovery.
- **Figures and widgets.** A table of the governments 1792–99 (Convention, Committee of Public Safety, Directory) with dates; an SVG of the Directory's structure (two councils, five Directors).

### hist.eu.france.4 · France (4): the rise and fall of Napoleon Bonaparte (`@level as`)

- Examined: AS European option (Section A). **Paper 1** (sources) in **2027**; **Paper 2** (essays) in **2028** and **2029**. Teach both styles (COURSE.md). Opening `:::exam Where this is examined` box says this.
- Syllabus topic: 1 France, 1774–1814
- Key question 1.4 What caused the rise and fall of Napoleon Bonaparte?
- Content (cover all of it; 'including' items in full):
  - Napoleon Bonaparte’s military reputation and political ambitions
  - The coup of 1799
  - Napoleon’s initiatives as first consul
  - Establishing authoritarian control: Means used, including: Setting up new ministries and a Council of State, Reform of the police force and the judiciary, Propaganda and censorship, Control of elections, Emperor Napoleon, 1804
  - Napoleon’s domestic reforms: Legal: the Code Napoleon; Educational; Financial and economic, including: Bank of France, Taxation, Bread prices; Religious: the Concordat with the Roman Catholic Church
  - Reasons for Napoleon’s fall from power: Declining popularity at home, including: Conscription and deteriorating economy; Effects of Napoleon’s failure to defeat Britain, including: The failure of the Continental System, Outcome of the Peninsular War, 1808–14; Growth of nationalism in the Empire, The extent of his Empire by 1812; Outcome of the failure of the campaign against Russia; Impact of the defeat at Leipzig, 1813; Allies capture of Paris, including: Abdication, 1814
- **What to cover and stress.** Bonaparte's military reputation (Toulon 1793, Italy 1796–97, Egypt 1798–99) and ambition; coup of Brumaire (9–10 November 1799; Sieyès, Lucien Bonaparte); Constitution of Year VIII (First Consul; Council of State, Tribunate, Legislative Body, Senate); plebiscites (1800, 1802 consul for life, 1804 emperor; figures doctored, e.g. 1800); initiatives as First Consul: prefects (1800), Bank of France (1800), the franc germinal (1803), Concordat (1801, published 1802), amnesty for émigrés, peace of Lunéville (1801) and Amiens (1802). Authoritarian control: Fouché and the police; censorship (Paris newspapers cut from about 73 to 13 in 1800, then 4 by 1811); propaganda (bulletins, paintings, the Imperial Catechism 1806); control of elections; coronation 2 December 1804. Reforms: Civil Code (21 March 1804; equality before the law, property, patriarchal family), lycées (1802) and the Imperial University (1808), taxation (indirect taxes, droits réunis), bread prices and subsistence. Fall: conscription and draft-dodging, economic decline from 1810–11, Continental System (Berlin Decree 1806, Milan 1807) and smuggling, Peninsular War 1808–14 ('the Spanish ulcer'), nationalism in Spain and Germany, the empire at its height in 1812, Russia 1812 (of over 600,000 men only a fraction returned; give the range), Leipzig (16–19 October 1813), allies in Paris (31 March 1814), abdication (6 April 1814; Fontainebleau). The Hundred Days are outside the dates: one sentence only.
- **Real sources for Paper 1 practice and quotation.** Bonaparte's proclamation of 19 Brumaire; the Constitution of Year VIII; the Concordat (1801); articles of the Civil Code (e.g. Article 213 on a wife's obedience); the Imperial Catechism (1806); the Berlin Decree (1806); Goya's The Third of May 1808 (painted 1814, a visual source); Napoleon's bulletins; police reports on public opinion.
- **Worked examples and practice.** P2(a): Explain why Napoleon signed the Concordat. P2(b): 'Napoleon's rule was based on force rather than consent.' How far do you agree? P2(b): To what extent was the failure of the Continental System the main reason for Napoleon's fall? P1(a) compare two sources as evidence about attitudes to the Civil Code; P1(b) how far the sources agree that Napoleon's power depended on propaganda.
- **Historians you may name.** Pieter Geyl, Napoleon: For and Against (1949); Geoffrey Ellis, Napoleon (1997); Andrew Roberts, Napoleon the Great (2014); Michael Broers on the empire.
- **Pitfalls.** The syllabus does not want military campaigns in detail: explain their impact inside France (conscription, taxation, morale, prestige). Brumaire was not a smooth military coup: it nearly failed on 19 Brumaire. Do not call the Code 'liberal' for women.
- **Figures and widgets.** Schematic SVG map of the empire at its greatest extent (1812): France, satellite states, allies; or a table instead if a map cannot be kept accurate. A table of the plebiscites.
