:::why
Many students take A Levels privately: home-educated students, students whose school does not offer A Levels or a particular subject, students resitting, and adults returning to study. It is entirely possible, and universities treat the results exactly like any others. But without a school, all the organisation is your job. The most common disaster is not a bad exam. It is a missed entry deadline, or a subject that no centre nearby can run.
:::

## What being a private candidate means

:::def Private candidate
A candidate who takes exams at an approved exam centre without being a student of that centre, and who is responsible for their own preparation and exam entries.
:::

:::def Exam centre
A school, college or organisation approved by an exam board to enter candidates for its exams and to run those exams under the board's rules.
:::

You prepare on your own, or with tutors or an online school. You then register with a centre that accepts private candidates. The centre enters you for the exams, supervises them, and passes on your results. You pay the centre's fees.

## Step 1: choose your board

For Mathematics, Business and Economics, every major board assesses you by written exams only, so choose by syllabus, resources and exam dates. Subjects with practical work or coursework need more care, because a centre must supervise that part. **The sciences are the main example**, because of how practical skills are assessed.

| | Cambridge International A Level | Pearson Edexcel International A Level (IAL) | UK A Levels (AQA, OCR, Edexcel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exam series | May/June and October/November | January, May/June and October/November | Usually May/June only |
| Science practical | **Paper 3** is a hands-on laboratory exam; Paper 5 is written | **Units 3 and 6** are written papers testing practical skills | Written papers, plus a separate **practical endorsement** assessed by teachers over the course |
| What a private candidate needs | A centre with a laboratory that will run Paper 3 for private candidates | Any centre that runs IAL exams | A centre willing to supervise the required practical activities |
| Structure | AS can be taken first and carried forward to the full A Level | Modular: units can be sat in different series and resat one at a time | Linear: all exams at the end |

Because the Cambridge science Paper 3 is a practical exam, you must find a centre that has a suitable laboratory and agrees to run it for private candidates, and many centres do not. Edexcel IAL tests practical skills in written papers (Units 3 and 6), so no laboratory session at the centre is needed. This is one reason many private candidates choose Edexcel IAL for the sciences. It is not a shortcut: the written practical papers test planning, measurement, data analysis and the core practicals in detail, so you still need to understand experiments well, ideally by doing some.

For UK A Levels in the sciences, the practical endorsement is reported separately from the grade. Private candidates often find it hard to arrange, and some university science and medicine courses expect a pass.

:::warn Do not mix boards within one subject
An AS result from one board cannot be combined with A Level papers from another board. Choose your board for each subject before you start studying, because syllabuses, textbooks and paper styles differ. You can take different subjects with different boards.
:::

Before you commit, check that the universities and courses you are interested in accept your combination, especially for medicine, where some universities have rules about resits and modular exams.

## Step 2: find a centre

- **In Turkey**, the British Council runs school exams for private candidates in **Istanbul and Ankara**. Some schools registered with Cambridge or Pearson also accept private candidates.
- **Anywhere**, use the centre search tools on the Cambridge International and Pearson websites, contact your local British Council office, and ask international schools near you directly.

Ask every centre the same questions, and keep the answers in writing:

- Do you accept private candidates for my board, subjects and series?
- (Cambridge sciences) Can you run the practical Paper 3 for private candidates?
- What is your entry deadline for the series I want, and is there a late-entry option?
- What are your fees, per subject or component, and are there administration charges?
- What identification do you need, and where exactly will the exams take place?
- Can you arrange access arrangements, such as extra time, if I am eligible?
- How and when will I receive my results and certificates?

## Step 3: entry deadlines and fees

**Centres set their own entry deadlines**, and they are usually much earlier than the board's own deadline, often months before the exams. Late entries, where accepted at all, cost considerably more. Put the deadline in your calendar the day you learn it, with a reminder a month before.

**Fees are set by each centre.** They usually combine the board's entry fee for each subject or component with the centre's own charge, so they vary a lot between centres and countries. Ask for a full written breakdown before you enter.

When you enter, check the exact **entry options**: for example AS only or full A Level, and the right components. With Cambridge, an AS result can be carried forward to the full A Level within 13 months, so you can sit AS in one year and complete the A Level the next.

## Predicted grades and references without a school

When you apply through UCAS, universities expect a **reference** and **predicted grades**. Without a school, you still need someone who knows your academic work.

- **Referee:** a tutor, a former teacher, or the head of an online school who has taught you. Family members and friends must not write it.
- **Predicted grades:** these must come from someone who has seen and marked your work. Sit timed past papers regularly and have a tutor mark them, and keep a dated record of scores. That record is the evidence behind a credible prediction.
- **Apply after your results:** many private candidates apply once their grades are known, often taking a gap year. Your offers are then based on real results, which removes the predicted-grade problem completely.

See [University applications and admissions tests](#road.university) for the full process.

## A self-study plan

Cambridge designs each A Level around roughly **360 guided learning hours** per subject (about 180 for AS). On your own, plan for at least that much, plus revision. Over two years, that means a steady weekly routine, not bursts.

| Stage | Roughly when | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Set up | First month | Choose subjects and boards, download syllabuses, buy textbooks, contact centres |
| AS content | Months 2 to 9 | Learn the AS topics in order, with questions after every chapter |
| AS consolidation | Months 10 to 12 | Timed AS past papers; sit AS exams now if you are taking them separately |
| A Level content | Months 13 to 19 | Learn the A Level topics and keep revisiting AS topics with mixed questions |
| Exam preparation | Months 20 to 24 | Full timed papers, error logs and final revision |

To turn this into a week-by-week plan around your exam dates, use [Build your personal study plan](#road.planner) and the methods in [Study methods that actually work](#road.methods).

## Tutors, online schools and study partners

**Tutors** are most valuable for the things you cannot do alone: marking long answers against the mark scheme, explaining a topic you are stuck on, setting mock exams, and writing a reference. Choose one with experience of your specific board, ask for a trial lesson, and avoid paying large sums in advance.

**Online schools** can provide a full course, regular feedback and a reference. Check what they actually include: most do not enter you for exams, so you still need a centre.

**Study partners** keep you accountable. An online partner taking the same subject can quiz you, compare answers and share the motivation.

## Exam-day logistics

- **Identification:** private candidates normally have to show valid photo identification, such as a passport or national identity card, at every exam. The name must match your entry exactly.
- **Statement of entry:** when the centre sends your list of entries and timetable, check every subject, component, date and the spelling of your name. Report any error immediately.
- **Venue and travel:** the exam may be held at a different address from the centre's office. Visit or plan the route in advance and aim to arrive at least 30 minutes early.
- **Equipment:** black pens, pencil, ruler, eraser and an approved calculator in a clear pencil case. Check the board's calculator rules; calculators with computer algebra are not allowed.
- **Results and certificates:** you receive results through your centre. Certificates arrive later; collect them and keep them safe, because universities and employers may ask for them.

## Your checklist

:::steps
1. Choose your subjects and check that they fit the university courses you are considering.
2. Choose a board for each subject, thinking carefully about the practical papers in the sciences.
3. Download each syllabus and specimen paper, and get one endorsed textbook per subject.
4. Find a centre that accepts private candidates for your subjects (and runs Paper 3 if you choose Cambridge sciences).
5. Write down the centre's entry deadline and fees, and set reminders.
6. Build a weekly study plan, and find a tutor or teacher who can mark your work and act as referee.
7. Enter before the deadline and check your statement of entry carefully.
8. Sit timed mock exams and keep the marked papers as evidence for predicted grades.
9. Prepare identification, travel and equipment for each exam day.
10. Collect your results and certificates, and plan any resits early.
:::

:::recap
- A private candidate studies independently and sits exams at an approved centre that accepts private candidates.
- Cambridge science Paper 3 is a hands-on laboratory exam, so you need a centre that can run it; Edexcel IAL Units 3 and 6 test practical skills in written papers, one reason many private candidates choose IAL for sciences.
- Do not mix boards within one subject; choose before you start.
- Centres set their own entry deadlines, often months ahead, and their own fees. Get everything in writing.
- For UCAS you need a referee who knows your work and evidence-based predicted grades; applying after results avoids predictions.
- Plan for about 360 guided learning hours per subject over the course, plus revision.
- Bring valid photo identification to every exam and check your statement of entry carefully.
:::
