@summary Who sets A Level exams, how Cambridge International, Pearson Edexcel International and the boards in England organise them, when exams and results happen, and how papers, mark schemes and grade boundaries work.

:::why
Two students can both be "doing A Level Physics" and sit completely different exams: different papers, different months, different resit rules. Knowing your board and its rules tells you which syllabus to follow, which past papers to practise and when your deadlines fall.
:::

## What an exam board does

An exam board writes the syllabus, sets the papers, marks them and awards the grades. All boards teach broadly the same subjects at the same level, but the details differ: the exact topics, the number and style of papers, the exam months and the resit rules.

:::def Exam board
An organisation that writes the syllabus for a qualification, sets and marks the exams, and awards grades and certificates. Also called an awarding body.
:::

:::def Syllabus
The official document listing exactly what can be examined in a subject, how the exams are structured and what each assessment objective is worth. Cambridge calls it a syllabus; Pearson and the UK boards call it a specification.
:::

## The main boards

| Board | Qualification | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Cambridge International (CAIE) | Cambridge International AS & A Level | International schools in many countries; private candidates at approved centres |
| Pearson Edexcel International | International Advanced Level (IAL) | International schools; widely used by private candidates, including at British Council centres |
| AQA | AS and A Level | Schools in England |
| OCR | AS and A Level | Schools in England |
| Pearson Edexcel (UK) | AS and A Level | Schools in England, and some British schools abroad |
| WJEC / Eduqas | AS and A Level | WJEC in Wales; Eduqas is the same organisation's brand in England |

Northern Ireland has its own board, CCEA, and OxfordAQA offers international A Levels in some countries. Cambridge International and OCR both belong to Cambridge University Press & Assessment, but they set different qualifications. AQA, OCR, Pearson Edexcel and Eduqas follow the same rules for A Levels in England, set by the regulator Ofqual, so their A Levels share one structure.

## Cambridge International: AS first, or everything at the end

A Cambridge A Level is made of several papers, which Cambridge calls **components**. The AS papers make up the AS Level and also half of the full A Level; the A Level adds more papers on second-year content. You can take them in three ways:

:::steps
1. **AS Level only.** Sit the AS papers and receive an AS qualification.
2. **Staged route.** Sit the AS papers in one series and the remaining A Level papers in a later one. Your AS result is carried forward and combined with them.
3. **Linear route.** Sit every paper, AS and A Level, in the same series at the end of the course.
:::

An AS result can be carried forward to complete the A Level **within 13 months**. In practice, an AS taken in June can be completed in the following June series.

:::warn The 13-month rule
If you sit AS in June 2026, you must complete the A Level by the June 2027 series for the AS to count. If you wait longer, you would have to sit all the papers, AS ones included, in a single series.
:::

A real example is Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics (9702). Chemistry (9701) and Biology (9700) follow the same pattern.

| Paper | Type | Time | Marks | Share of A Level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Multiple choice (AS) | 1 h 15 min | 40 | 15.5% |
| 2 | AS structured questions | 1 h 15 min | 60 | 23% |
| 3 | Advanced practical skills (laboratory exam) | 2 h | 40 | 11.5% |
| 4 | A Level structured questions | 2 h | 100 | 38.5% |
| 5 | Planning, analysis and evaluation | 1 h 15 min | 30 | 11.5% |

Papers 1–3 form the AS (50% of the A Level); Papers 4 and 5 are the second-year papers.

Cambridge runs two main **exam series** each year: **June** (exams from roughly late April to mid-June) and **November** (October and November). A third, March, series is available in India only. June results come out in August (the June 2026 A Level results were released on 11 August 2026), and November results in January.

:::def Exam series
A period in which a board runs a full set of exams, named after its main month, such as the June series or the November series.
:::

## Pearson Edexcel International: units taken in stages

An IAL is **modular**: it is split into **units**, each examined by one paper. IAL sciences have six units, three for the International AS and three more for the full IAL. IAL Mathematics also has six: four Pure units and two applied units (mechanics, statistics or decision mathematics).

:::def Modular qualification
A qualification split into separately examined units that can be taken at different times, with the results combined at the end. Edexcel International A Levels are modular; A Levels from the boards in England are linear.
:::

- There are three sittings a year: **January**, **May/June** and **October/November**. Most units are offered in every sitting.
- You can spread units over several sittings, for example Pure 1 in January and Pure 2 in June.
- You can **resit** a unit to improve it, and the better result counts.
- Each unit's raw mark is converted to a **uniform mark** so that units from different sittings can be added fairly ([how this works](#start.grades)).
- When you have all the units you need, your centre claims the qualification (called "cashing in") and your overall grade is calculated.
- In the sciences, practical skills are tested in written papers (Units 3 and 6), not in a laboratory exam.

## The boards in England: linear A Levels

A Levels from AQA, OCR, Pearson Edexcel and Eduqas are **linear**:

- Every exam that counts is sat in May and June at the end of Year 13.
- The AS is a separate qualification whose marks never count towards the A Level, so many schools no longer enter students for it.
- There is one exam series a year, in the summer. To improve a grade, you resit all the exam papers the following summer; coursework marks can usually be carried forward.
- In Biology, Chemistry and Physics you also receive a **Practical Endorsement**, reported separately as Pass or Not classified, based on at least 12 practical activities assessed by your teacher. Many universities expect a Pass.
- Some subjects include coursework, officially called **non-exam assessment (NEA)**: typically 20% in History, Geography, English Literature and Computer Science, and more in subjects such as Design and Technology and Art.

## The three systems side by side

| | Cambridge International | Edexcel International (IAL) | Boards in England |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | Papers; AS papers form half the A Level | Units; AS units form half the IAL | Linear: all papers at the end |
| Does the AS count? | Yes, if carried forward within 13 months | Yes | No |
| Exam series | June and November (March in India only) | January, May/June, October/November | Summer only |
| Improving a grade | Retake in a later series | Resit single units; the better result counts | Resit all papers the next summer |
| Science practicals | Laboratory exam plus a planning paper | Written practical-skills papers | Practical Endorsement plus practical questions in written papers |
| Results | August (June series), January (November series) | Roughly two months after each sitting | Mid-August |

:::board
WJEC A Levels in Wales and CCEA A Levels in Northern Ireland are unitised: the AS counts for 40% of the A Level. Scotland uses its own qualifications (Highers and Advanced Highers). Rules change from time to time, so always check the current syllabus for your board.
:::

## What the papers look like

| Paper type | What you do | Common in |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice | Answer 30–40 questions, each with four options | Cambridge sciences, Economics, Accounting |
| Structured questions | Answer a question in parts (a), (b), (c): short answers, calculations, explanations | Every subject |
| Extended response | Write a long answer, marked by "levels of response" | Business, Economics, English, History |
| Data response or case study | Answer questions on unseen data, a text or a business case | Business, Economics, Geography |
| Practical exam | Carry out experiments in a laboratory under exam conditions | Cambridge sciences |
| Planning and analysis | Plan an experiment and analyse given results, on paper | Cambridge and Edexcel International sciences |
| Coursework (NEA) | Complete a project over several weeks, marked by teachers and checked by the board | Some subjects at the boards in England |

## Mark schemes, examiner reports and grade boundaries

After each series, boards publish three documents that are gold for revision:

- **Mark schemes** show exactly what earned each mark. In Maths they use M (method), A (accuracy) and B (independent) marks; in essay subjects they describe levels of response.
- **Examiner reports** explain, question by question, what candidates did well and where they lost marks.
- **Grade boundaries** (Cambridge calls them grade thresholds) give the minimum mark for each grade. They are set after marking, for each paper in each series. [Grades, marks and UCAS points](#start.grades) explains why they move.

Cambridge puts a selection of past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports on its public website, and schools get more through its School Support Hub. Pearson, AQA and OCR publish theirs on their qualification pages, although the most recent papers are sometimes released to teachers first.

:::exam Use mark schemes from day one
Do not wait for "past-paper season". After every topic, try two or three past-paper questions on it and mark them yourself with the official scheme. You will quickly learn which words and steps examiners need to see. [Phase 4: Past papers and mark schemes](#road.pastpapers) shows a full method.
:::

## Entries and deadlines

You cannot simply turn up on exam day. Your school, or your exam centre if you are a private candidate, must **enter** you for each paper months before the series. For a June series, the deadline usually falls early in the same year, and many centres set their own earlier deadline. Late entries, if accepted at all, cost extra. Fees are set by each centre and vary, so ask for the full price per subject, including any centre charges.

:::warn Confirm four things before you start studying
Check with your centre: the board, the syllabus code (for example 9702 for Cambridge Physics), the exam series, and the entry deadline. A year spent studying the wrong syllabus is an expensive mistake.
:::

:::recap
- An exam board writes the syllabus, sets and marks the papers and awards grades. Always study the syllabus with the correct code for your exam year.
- Cambridge: papers called components; AS papers form half the A Level; take AS only, AS first (carried forward within 13 months), or everything at the end.
- Cambridge series: June (results in August) and November (results in January); March in India only.
- Edexcel International: modular units, sittings in January, May/June and October/November, single-unit resits, and written practical-skills papers.
- Boards in England: linear, all exams in May–June of Year 13, AS does not count, Practical Endorsement in sciences, NEA in some subjects.
- Mark schemes, examiner reports and grade boundaries are published after each series: use them throughout the course.
- Entries close months before the exams, so confirm your board, syllabus code, series and deadline early.
:::
