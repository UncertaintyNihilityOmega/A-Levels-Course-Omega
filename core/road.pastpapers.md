:::why
Past papers are the closest thing to the real exam that exists. They show you the style of the questions, how long you really need, and the exact ideas that earn marks. Students who do many past papers, mark them honestly and learn from every mistake usually outscore students who simply know more content.
:::

## Where to find them

Use the official sources first. Each board publishes four kinds of document for each exam series: **question papers**, **mark schemes**, **examiner reports** (what candidates did well and badly) and **grade thresholds** or grade boundaries (the raw marks needed for each grade).

| Board | Where to look | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cambridge International | The syllabus page on [cambridgeinternational.org](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org) | Cambridge publishes some past papers on its website. The full archive is on the School Support Hub, which registered schools use; private candidates can ask their exam centre |
| Pearson Edexcel (IAL) | Your subject's page on [qualifications.pearson.com](https://qualifications.pearson.com) | Papers, mark schemes and examiner reports under the exam materials; grade boundaries on their own page |
| AQA | [aqa.org.uk](https://www.aqa.org.uk) | Past papers and mark schemes for each specification |
| OCR | [ocr.org.uk](https://www.ocr.org.uk) | Past papers in the assessment section of each qualification |

With every board, the newest series is sometimes available only to teachers for a while. Many other websites copy the official files. If you use one, make sure each paper comes with its matching mark scheme.

### Reading Cambridge file names

Cambridge files have names like **9709_s25_qp_12**. Once you can read them, finding the right paper takes seconds.

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 9709 | The syllabus code (Mathematics) |
| s25 | The series and year: s = May/June, w = October/November, m = February/March; 25 = 2025 |
| qp, ms, er, gt | Question paper, mark scheme, examiner report, grade thresholds |
| 12 | Paper 1, variant 2 |

Cambridge sets several versions (**variants**) of most papers in each series for different time zones. That is why there are so many papers to practise with: a few recent years give you 20 or more versions of some components.

## How many, and in what order?

Aim for **8–12 full papers per component** by exam day, plus topic questions, and more for components you find hard. Use them in this order:

1. **Older papers** (about 6–10 years old): cut into topic questions during phase 3.
2. **Middle years:** full timed papers in phase 4.
3. **The two or three most recent series:** saved for full mocks in phase 5.

Before you start, read the "changes to the syllabus" page at the front of your syllabus. Papers written before a syllabus change can include topics that are no longer examined, and miss new ones. Skip those questions, or replace them with questions from this guide.

## Sitting a paper under timed conditions

:::steps
1. Choose the same time of day as the real exam, a quiet desk, and only the permitted equipment: pens, pencil, ruler, an allowed calculator, and the formula list or data booklet your board provides (for Cambridge Mathematics, the list called MF19).
2. Put your phone in another room and set a timer for the real length of the paper.
3. Read the front cover as if it were the real exam.
4. Work to time. Cambridge Mathematics Paper 1 has 75 marks in 1 hour 50 minutes, so allow about 1.5 minutes per mark.
5. When time runs out, draw a line. You may finish in a different colour to see what more time would give, but count only the marks above the line.
:::

## Marking honestly

Mark the paper the same day, or the next, with the official mark scheme. Be strict: if your answer does not clearly contain the point the mark scheme describes, do not give yourself the mark. Generous self-marking only hides the problems you need to fix.

Mathematics mark schemes use a short code. Learn it, because it tells you exactly how marks are earned:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| M1 | A method mark, for a correct method applied to the problem |
| A1 | An accuracy mark: only possible if the related M mark is earned |
| B1 | An independent mark for a correct answer or statement |
| FT | Follow through: credit for correct work that uses your own earlier wrong answer |
| CAO | Correct answer only |
| OE | Or equivalent: another correct form of the answer is accepted |
| AWRT | Answer which rounds to the value given |
| AG | Answer given in the question: every step must be shown |

Science mark schemes list the acceptable answers for each mark point, and essay mark schemes describe **levels**: what an answer must show to reach each band of marks. Then turn your raw mark into a grade with the grade thresholds for that series and variant. Thresholds change from series to series because some papers are harder than others, so always use the matching one. Aim to be comfortably above your target grade, not just on it.

:::exam Why the method mark matters
An A mark depends on its M mark. A correct final answer from a wrong or invisible method can score nothing, while a wrong final answer from a correct method can still score most of the marks. In "show that" questions, the answer is printed (the mark scheme labels it AG), so all the marks are for the steps. Always write your working where the examiner can follow it.
:::

## Read the examiner report

For every paper you sit, read the examiner report's comments on it. Examiners describe the common errors, the misunderstandings they saw most often, and what the best answers did. Ten minutes with the report often explains a lost mark better than the mark scheme does. Copy anything that applies to you into your error log.

## Topic by topic, or full papers?

| | Topic-by-topic questions | Full papers |
|---|---|---|
| When | Phase 3 and the start of phase 4 | Most of phase 4, and all of phase 5 |
| What it trains | Depth in one topic; finding gaps quickly | Timing, stamina, switching between topics, choosing methods |
| The risk | The topic heading tells you which method to use | One paper cannot cover every topic |

Use both. Start phase 4 with one full paper per component per week, plus topic sets for your weakest areas. Build up to two or three full papers a week in the last month of the phase.

## The redo cycle

:::steps
1. **Sit** the paper, timed.
2. **Mark** it honestly, the same day.
3. **Log** every lost mark in your error log, with its type and a rule for next time.
4. **Relearn:** for a knowledge gap, go back to the chapter; for a method gap, do three to five similar questions.
5. **Redo** every question that lost marks, from a blank page, a week later.
6. **Track** your scores for each component, with the grade thresholds next to them, so you can see the trend.
:::

:::warn Do not memorise mark-scheme wording without understanding it
Mark schemes are written for examiners, not as model answers. Phrases learned by heart without understanding end up in the wrong context, where they earn nothing. Questions are reworded every year, and the mark is for the idea used correctly. Learn why each point earns its mark, then practise explaining it in your own words while keeping the key technical terms.
:::

:::tip Keep the newest papers for later
Do not use the two or three most recent series before phase 5. They are the best guide to your own exam, so save them for full mocks under real conditions.
:::

:::recap
- Use official sources: Cambridge (public website and the School Support Hub through your centre), Pearson, AQA and OCR, with mark schemes, examiner reports and grade thresholds.
- Read Cambridge file names: syllabus, series and year, document type, paper and variant (9709_s25_qp_12).
- Aim for 8–12 full papers per component: old papers as topic questions, middle years timed, newest saved for mocks.
- Sit papers in real conditions and to time: about 1.5 minutes per mark in Cambridge Maths Paper 1.
- Mark strictly; learn M, A, B, FT and AG; compare your mark with the thresholds for that exact paper.
- Read the examiner report for every paper, and follow the redo cycle: sit, mark, log, relearn, redo, track.
- Understand mark-scheme points; never memorise their wording blindly.
:::
