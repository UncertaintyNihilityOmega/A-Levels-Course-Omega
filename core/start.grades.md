:::why
Your grades open university doors, so it pays to know exactly how they are calculated. Understanding grade boundaries also changes how you study: it shows why every single mark matters, and why a hard paper is not a disaster.
:::

## The grades

| Qualification | Pass grades, highest first |
|---|---|
| A Level | A*, A, B, C, D, E |
| AS Level | a, b, c, d, e at Cambridge; A, B, C, D, E at UK boards |

A result below the lowest pass grade is reported as ungraded (U), and no qualification is awarded in that subject. Your certificate shows a grade for each subject, not a percentage.

## From raw marks to a grade

Each paper is marked and you receive a **raw mark**. Your raw marks are combined, using each paper's weighting, and the total is compared with the **grade boundaries** for that series.

:::def Raw mark
The number of marks you actually score on a paper, before any conversion; for example, 52 out of 75.
:::

:::def Grade boundary
The minimum mark needed for a particular grade on a paper or qualification in a given exam series. Boundaries are set after marking and can change from series to series. Cambridge calls them grade thresholds.
:::

### Why boundaries move

No two papers are equally difficult. If this year's paper is harder than last year's, the same student scores fewer marks. To keep an A worth the same every year, senior examiners study the papers, samples of marked scripts and statistics, and then set the boundaries. A harder paper gets lower boundaries; an easier paper gets higher ones.

| Mark needed (out of 100) | Series 1: harder paper | Series 2: easier paper |
|---|---|---|
| Grade A | 68 | 76 |
| Grade C | 47 | 55 |

These numbers are invented to show the idea; each board publishes the real boundaries for every series. Three lessons follow:

1. **There is no fixed percentage for an A.** "70% is an A" is a myth.
2. **A hard paper is hard for everyone.** If a paper feels brutal, keep collecting marks: the boundaries will reflect it.
3. **Every mark counts.** Neighbouring grades are often separated by only a handful of marks on each paper, so one extra method mark here and there can lift a whole grade.

:::tip Estimating your grade from a past paper
Mark the paper with the official mark scheme, then compare your total with the published boundaries for **that same series**, not this year's or an average. Do this for papers from several different years to get a fair picture.
:::

## How the A* is awarded

- **Cambridge International** awards A* for the full A Level only, based on your total across all the papers. There is no A* at AS.
- **Edexcel International** requires an A overall plus an especially high score on the A2 (second-year) units, so an A* reflects strength in the hardest material.
- **The boards in England** set the A* as the top boundary on your total mark, like any other grade.

## Uniform marks at Edexcel International

In a modular system, you might sit one unit in a January with a hard paper and another in a June with an easy one. Simply adding the raw marks would be unfair. Pearson solves this with **uniform marks**.

:::def Uniform mark
A raw unit mark converted onto a fixed scale, so that results from papers of different difficulty, taken in different sittings, can be added fairly. The grade boundaries on the uniform scale stay the same every series.
:::

The conversion uses each sitting's grade boundaries: a raw mark exactly on the A boundary always becomes the same uniform mark, however hard the paper was. Your uniform marks from all the units are then added, and the total decides your overall grade. Pearson's results show the uniform mark for each unit, which tells you how close you were to the next grade.

:::analogy
Uniform marks work like currency exchange. Raw marks from different sittings are like dollars, euros and lira: you cannot add them directly. Converting everything into one currency first lets you add them fairly.
:::

## UCAS tariff points

**UCAS** (the Universities and Colleges Admissions Service) handles applications to UK universities. Its **tariff** turns grades into points so that different qualifications can be compared.

:::def UCAS tariff points
A points system that converts qualifications and grades into numbers so that different qualifications can be compared. Some universities state their entry requirements in tariff points.
:::

| Grade | A Level | AS Level | Extended Project (EPQ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A* | 56 | no A* | 28 |
| A (AS: a) | 48 | 20 | 24 |
| B (AS: b) | 40 | 16 | 20 |
| C (AS: c) | 32 | 12 | 16 |
| D (AS: d) | 24 | 10 | 12 |
| E (AS: e) | 16 | 6 | 8 |

An EPQ is worth exactly half an A Level at every grade. An AS that you later complete as a full A Level in the same subject does not count separately: only the A Level counts.

:::eg Adding up tariff points
A student has A Levels in Mathematics (A*), Physics (A) and Chemistry (B), an AS in Further Mathematics (grade b) and an EPQ (grade A). How many UCAS tariff points does she have?

A Levels: \(56 + 48 + 40 = 144\)

AS Further Mathematics, grade b: \(16\)

EPQ, grade A: \(24\)

Total: \(144 + 16 + 24 = 184\) points.
:::

:::warn Points are not everything
AAA and A*AB are both worth 144 points, but a course asking for "A*AA including Mathematics" accepts neither. Most selective universities make offers in **grades** and name the subjects. Use points only when a course actually asks for them.
:::

## What offers look like

You apply through UCAS to up to five courses (four at most in medicine, dentistry or veterinary science). Each university replies with an offer or a rejection. Typical offers read like this:

- **"AAA"**: three A grades, sometimes excluding certain subjects.
- **"A*AA, including A* in Mathematics and A in Physics"**: grades plus named subjects, as for many engineering courses.
- **"ABB including Biology and Chemistry"**: a common pattern for life sciences.
- **"112 UCAS points, including a C in Business"**: a tariff offer, which BBC would meet (\(40 + 40 + 32 = 112\)).
- **"AAB, or ABB with an A in the EPQ"**: some universities lower an offer for a strong EPQ.

:::def Conditional offer
A university place that becomes yours only if you meet stated conditions, usually particular grades. An unconditional offer has no grade conditions, usually because you already have your results.
:::

From your offers you choose one **firm** choice and, if you wish, one **insurance** choice with lower grades as a backup. On results day, the university confirms your place if you meet the conditions. If you miss both, **Clearing** matches students without a place to courses that still have vacancies. Some courses also set admissions tests or interviews; see [University applications and admissions tests](#road.university).

:::key UCAS deadlines for 2027 entry
- **15 October 2026**: Oxford, Cambridge, and most courses in medicine, dentistry and veterinary science.
- **13 January 2027**: deadline for equal consideration for most other courses.
:::

## Predicted grades

Universities usually decide before your final exams, so they rely on predictions.

:::def Predicted grades
The grades your teachers expect you to achieve, entered on your UCAS application. Universities make most offers on the basis of predicted grades, because final results arrive after most decisions are made.
:::

Teachers predict from evidence: classwork, mock exams, AS results if you have them, and the progress you have shown. A prediction below a course's typical offer usually means no offer, so the evidence you give your teachers matters. Private candidates still need predicted grades and a reference; a tutor, a former teacher or a school can provide them. [Studying as a private candidate](#road.private) explains how.

:::exam Earn your predictions
Do your mocks properly, under timed conditions, and keep a record of your past-paper scores. A strong AS result, on Cambridge's staged route or at Edexcel International, is the most convincing evidence of all.
:::

:::recap
- A Level grades run A* to E, AS grades a to e (Cambridge) or A to E (UK boards); U is not a pass.
- Raw marks are compared with grade boundaries set after marking. Harder papers get lower boundaries, so there is no fixed percentage for an A.
- Cambridge awards A* for the full A Level only. Edexcel International requires an A overall plus a very high score on the A2 units.
- Edexcel International converts raw unit marks into uniform marks so that units from different sittings add up fairly.
- UCAS tariff: A* 56, A 48, B 40, C 32, D 24, E 16; AS a 20, b 16, c 12, d 10, e 6; EPQ A* 28 down to E 8.
- Selective universities mostly make grade offers that name subjects; some use tariff points.
- Offers are based on predicted grades, so mocks and past-paper scores matter. For 2027 entry, UCAS deadlines are 15 October 2026 and 13 January 2027.
:::
