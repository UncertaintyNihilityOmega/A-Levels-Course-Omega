## What is biology?

Biology asks how life works, at every scale. At the smallest scale it studies molecules: the proteins that act as machines inside cells, the DNA that stores instructions, the enzymes that make reactions fast enough for life. One step up are **cells**, the smallest units that are alive. Then come tissues and organs, whole organisms, populations, and finally **ecosystems**, where living things and their surroundings affect each other.

Two big ideas hold it all together. The first is that **structure fits function**: the shape of a red blood cell, a leaf or an antibody explains what it does. The second is **evolution by natural selection**, which explains why living things are so well suited to where they live, and why all life shares the same genetic code.

## What do biologists do?

- **Medicine and health.** Doctors, nurses, pharmacists and biomedical scientists use biology every day: how drugs act on cells, how infections spread, how the immune system can be trained by vaccines.
- **Research.** Laboratory scientists work out how diseases start, how genes are switched on and off, and how to design new treatments such as gene therapy.
- **Food and farming.** Plant and animal scientists breed crops that resist drought and disease and study soil, pests and yields.
- **Environment and conservation.** Ecologists count and protect species, restore habitats and measure the effects of climate change.
- **Biotechnology.** Companies use enzymes, microorganisms and genetic engineering to make medicines, vaccines, biofuels and food ingredients.

## Why it matters

Many of the largest problems of this century are biological: pandemics, antibiotic resistance, feeding a growing population, and the loss of species and habitats. Biology also helps you make good personal decisions about health, diet and medicine, and judge the claims you read in the news.

## What the A Level covers

The guide follows **Cambridge International AS & A Level Biology (9700)**, the syllabus for exams in 2025–2027. The course in this guide is planned in these parts:

- **Cells and molecules:** cell structure, microscopy, biological molecules, enzymes and cell membranes.
- **Genetics at AS:** the mitotic cell cycle, DNA, RNA and protein synthesis.
- **Transport and exchange:** transport in plants and mammals, and gas exchange.
- **Infection and immunity:** infectious diseases, antibiotics and the immune system.
- **Energy:** respiration and photosynthesis (A Level).
- **Control and coordination:** homeostasis, nerves, hormones and plant responses (A Level).
- **Inheritance and evolution:** meiosis, inheritance, selection and evolution (A Level).
- **Ecology and genetic technology:** classification, biodiversity, conservation and genetic technology (A Level).
- **Skills and exam readiness:** practical skills, data handling, statistics, paper guides and mock papers.

## Is it for you?

Biology suits you if you are curious about living things and happy to learn a lot of precise vocabulary. It needs clear writing: many marks go to explaining a process in the right order with the right terms. It also needs numbers: rates, percentages, magnifications, simple statistics and reading graphs. You will also do practical work: microscopy, measuring and planning experiments.

It combines well with **Chemistry** (most medicine, dentistry and veterinary degrees ask for both), and with Maths, Physics, Psychology or Geography. Check the subject requirements of any degree you are interested in.
