@summary Practice turns understanding into marks. This phase uses retrieval practice, spaced repetition, flashcards and mixed questions, with an error log that makes sure each mistake happens only once.

:::why
You can understand a topic perfectly on Monday and be unable to answer a question on it by Friday. Forgetting is normal, and it happens to everyone. The cure is not more reading: it is testing yourself, spread out over time. This phase runs alongside phase 2 from the first week, and becomes your main activity once all the content is learned.
:::

## Retrieval practice: test, don't reread

:::def Retrieval practice
Learning by recalling information from memory, for example by answering questions or writing down everything you remember, instead of reading the material again.
:::

Every time you pull an idea out of memory, the memory becomes stronger and easier to find next time. Rereading does not do this, because the answer is already in front of you. A large 2013 review of ten common study techniques rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful, and rereading and highlighting among the least useful.

Ways to practise retrieval:

- **Blank page:** write everything you remember about a topic on a blank sheet, then check it against your notes and add what you missed in a different colour.
- **Flashcards**, answered aloud or on paper before you turn the card over.
- **Quizzes:** the quick check at the end of each chapter, and the mixed [practice quizzes](#practice.quiz).
- **Exam-style questions**, from your textbook, this guide and past papers.

## Spaced repetition: when to review

:::def Spaced repetition
Reviewing material at increasing intervals (for example after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days), so that each review happens just as the memory starts to fade.
:::

Review a topic about **1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days** after you first learn it, then once a month until the exam. For example, for a topic learned on Monday 12 October 2026:

| Review | Day | Date | What to do (5–15 minutes) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 1 | Tuesday 13 October | Blank page, then check |
| 2nd | 3 | Thursday 15 October | Flashcards for the topic |
| 3rd | 7 | Monday 19 October | 3–4 exam-style questions |
| 4th | 14 | Monday 26 October | Quiz or mixed questions |
| 5th | 30 | Wednesday 11 November | A past-paper question, timed |

Every review is a test, never a reread. If a review goes badly, restart the cycle for that topic from day 1. Write the review dates in your diary on the day you learn the topic, so you never have to remember them.

## Flashcards that work

The **Leitner system** organises paper flashcards in five boxes. Box 1 is reviewed every day, box 2 every 3 days, box 3 every week, box 4 every 2 weeks and box 5 every month. A card you answer correctly moves up one box; a card you get wrong goes back to box 1. Flashcard apps such as Anki do the same scheduling automatically.

- **Answer before you flip.** Say or write the full answer first. A vague "I knew that" counts as wrong.
- **Be strict.** Half right is wrong for a definition, because the mark scheme wants the key words.
- **Mix card types:** definitions, formulas, "why" questions, and blank diagrams to label.

## Interleaving: mix your topics

:::def Interleaving
Practising a mixture of different topics or question types in one session, so that you must decide which method each question needs, instead of practising one method many times in a row.
:::

| | Blocked practice | Interleaved practice |
|---|---|---|
| What it looks like | 20 questions on completing the square | 12 questions mixing quadratics, functions, series and trigonometry |
| How it feels | Smooth and fast | Slower and harder |
| What it trains | Repeating a method you already know is needed | Choosing the right method, which is what the exam demands |
| When to use it | The first practice on a new topic | Everything after that |

Exam questions never tell you which chapter they come from. Once you have learned three or four topics, make most of your practice interleaved.

## Topic questions

After each topic, climb this ladder: textbook exercises, then the practice questions in this guide, then **past-paper questions on that topic**. Collections of Cambridge questions sorted by topic ("topical past papers") are widely available, and they let you practise a topic in exam style long before you sit full papers. Aim for at least 10–15 exam-style questions on each topic, more for big topics, before you call it green.

## The error log

The error log is the most valuable document you will make. Every mark you lose goes into it, with the reason and a rule to stop it happening again.

| Date | Question | Topic | Marks lost | What went wrong | Type | Rule for next time | Redo on |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 Oct | Maths practice Q4 | Quadratics | 2 | Used \(-b = -4\) when \(b = -4\) | Careless slip | Write \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) with their signs before substituting | 19 Oct |
| 14 Oct | Physics topic test Q2 | Kinematics | 2 | Did not convert \(\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) to \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) | Method | Convert every quantity to SI units first | 21 Oct |
| 15 Oct | Chemistry worksheet Q5 | Moles | 3 | Ignored the 1 : 2 ratio in the equation | Knowledge | Always use the mole ratio from the balanced equation | 22 Oct |
| 20 Oct | Business 12-mark question | Motivation | 4 | No judgement at the end | Exam technique | End with a justified conclusion that answers the question | 27 Oct |

Use five types: **knowledge** (you did not know it), **method** (you knew the topic but chose or applied the wrong method), **reading** (you misread the question), **careless slip**, and **exam technique** (timing, missing working, no evaluation). Once a week, read the whole log and count the types. If most are knowledge errors, go back to phase 2 for those topics. If most are careless slips, slow down and check every answer. Then redo each logged question on its redo date, without looking at your old answer.

## Calculation subjects and essay subjects

:::cols
:::col Calculations (Maths, Physics, Chemistry)
- Do many questions: volume matters.
- Write full working, as in the exam: method marks need visible steps.
- Check units and significant figures at the end of every answer.
- Redo wrong questions from a blank page, not by reading the solution.
- Time yourself from early on: about 1.5 minutes per mark in Cambridge Maths.
:::
:::col Essays (Business, Economics, long-answer Biology)
- Plan every answer for 3–5 minutes before writing.
- Build each paragraph: knowledge, application, analysis, evaluation.
- Write timed answers, then mark them against the mark scheme's levels.
- Collect strong chains of reasoning ("this leads to… which means…").
- See [Writing top-band business answers](#bus.writing).
:::
:::

## Measuring readiness

Test each topic with mixed questions a week or more after learning it, and record your score.

| Score | Meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 80% or more | Exam-ready | Mark it green; review it once a month |
| 60–79% | Nearly there | Mark it amber; work through its error-log entries and do more topic questions |
| Below 60% | Not secure yet | Mark it red; go back to the chapter (phase 2) |

You are ready to give past papers most of your time when your average across mixed sets is 70% or more, no topic is red, and the same mistakes have stopped appearing in your error log.

:::tip Start every session with retrieval
Spend the first 10 minutes of each study session on questions or flashcards from older topics, before any new work. It warms you up, and it builds your spaced reviews into every day without extra planning.
:::

:::warn Looking at the answer too soon
When a question gets hard, it is tempting to look at the solution after a minute. Struggle for at least 5–10 minutes first: the effort of trying is what makes the method stick. If you do look, close the solution and redo the whole question from a blank page.
:::

:::exam Practise in exam format from the start
Write practice answers exactly as you would in the exam: full working, units, sentences rather than notes, and diagrams labelled. Habits formed in practice are the ones you will use under pressure, so make them exam habits from the first week.
:::

:::recap
- Retrieval practice (testing yourself) beats rereading; use blank pages, flashcards, quizzes and exam-style questions.
- Review each topic about 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days after learning it, then monthly; every review is a test.
- Use a Leitner box or a flashcard app, and be strict with yourself.
- Interleave: once you know a few topics, mix them in every practice set.
- Keep an error log with a type and a rule for every lost mark, and redo each question a week later.
- Green at 80% or more on mixed questions a week after learning; move on to past papers at a 70% average with no red topics.
:::
