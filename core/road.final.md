:::why
The last eight weeks decide more grades than any other part of the course. By now you know most of the content; this phase makes it reliable under pressure. Done well, it is calm and organised: you know exactly what to do each day, and each week you can see the gaps closing.
:::

## The last eight weeks, week by week

Count the weeks back from your **first** paper. If you have only six weeks, combine weeks 8 and 7, and weeks 6 and 5.

| Weeks to go | Main focus | Full papers per subject | Also this week |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | Audit: update your tracker, list every red and amber topic, plan the eight weeks | 1, as a baseline | Get the final exam timetable and mark every paper on your plan |
| 7 | Red topics, first half: relearn, then practise | 1 | Condensed notes for each red topic |
| 6 | Red topics, second half | 1–2 | Start daily formula and definition drills |
| 5 | Mock week: a full set of papers in exam conditions | A full set | Mark everything; update the error log |
| 4 | Fix the mock: relearn every topic that lost marks, then amber topics | 2 | Finish a one-page summary for every topic |
| 3 | Timed papers from recent series | 2–3 | The redo cycle after every paper |
| 2 | Final mocks with the newest papers, in the order of your exams | 2–3 | Shift your sleep to fit the exam start times |
| 1 | Light review: summaries, flashcards, error-log rules; nothing new | Parts of papers only | Logistics: route, equipment, ID, statement of entry |

## The last two weeks

- **Follow the timetable.** The subject you sit first gets the first mocks and the most review time in the final days.
- **Sit papers at exam times.** If your papers start in the morning, do your mocks in the morning, so your brain is used to working hard at that hour.
- **Read your rules daily.** Read the "rule for next time" column of your error log every day. It is a personal list of the traps you fall into.
- **Make one sheet per paper:** the formulas, definitions and mistakes that matter most for that paper. Read it the evening before.
- **Plan the gaps between papers.** With three days between two papers, for example: one timed paper on day 1, weak topics on day 2, light review on day 3.

## Daily timetable templates

On a full revision day (study leave, weekends, or every day for a private candidate):

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 08:30–09:00 | Flashcards and error-log rules |
| 09:00–10:50 | A timed paper (subject A) |
| 10:50–11:10 | Break, away from your desk |
| 11:10–12:30 | Mark the paper and log every lost mark |
| 12:30–13:30 | Lunch, away from screens |
| 13:30–15:00 | Relearn the topics the morning's paper exposed |
| 15:00–15:20 | Break and some movement |
| 15:20–16:50 | Subject B: topic questions on a weak topic |
| 16:50–19:00 | Exercise, free time, dinner |
| 19:00–20:00 | Subject C: condensed notes and flashcards |
| From 20:00 | Free time; screens off an hour before bed |

That is about 7.5 hours of real work. More than this, day after day, usually means less is learned per hour.

On a school day, add about two hours after lessons:

| Time | Activity |
|---|---|
| 17:00–17:45 | Topic questions on a red or amber topic |
| 18:00–18:45 | One timed section of a past paper |
| 19:45–20:30 | Mark it, update the error log, 20 minutes of flashcards |

## Running your own mocks

If your school runs mock exams, treat them as the real thing. If you study alone, run your own:

- the same length, the same time of day and the same rules as the real exam;
- a clear table, no notes, phone in another room, and if possible someone in the room acting as the invigilator;
- the whole set of papers over a few days, in the same order as your real timetable;
- marking within 24 hours, followed by the redo cycle from [Phase 4](#road.pastpapers).

A disappointing mock is useful: it shows you the problems while there is still time to fix them. Treat the score as information, not as a judgement of you.

## Condensing your notes

Condensing works because every step forces you to choose what matters and to recall it.

1. **Chapter notes** from phase 2.
2. **One page per topic:** write it from memory first, then check and fill the gaps.
3. **One page per paper:** only the formulas, definitions and traps for that paper.
4. **A final card per paper** to read on the evening before.

## Formulas, definitions and data

Get a clean copy of what your board provides in the exam: for Cambridge, the formula list MF19 for Mathematics, the data and formulae pages printed in each Physics paper, and the Data Booklet for Chemistry. Learn what is on it, and where, so you never waste time searching. Then **memorise everything else.** Each day in the last three weeks, write out every formula and definition for one paper on a blank page in five minutes, check it, and correct it. Our sheets help: [Maths](#tools.f.math), [Physics](#tools.f.phys), [Chemistry](#tools.f.chem), [Biology](#tools.f.bio) and [Business, economics and accounting](#tools.f.bus).

## Sleep, food and exercise

Your brain stores the day's learning while you sleep, so sleep is part of revision, not time taken away from it. Most teenagers need 8–10 hours a night. An all-nighter before a paper makes you slower and more error-prone the next day. Two weeks before the exams, move your bedtime and wake-up time so that you are alert at the time your papers start. A daily walk or other exercise helps you sleep and manage stress, and avoiding caffeine after mid-afternoon protects your sleep. There is more in [Motivation, sleep and stress](#road.wellbeing).

## What not to do

| Don't | Do instead |
|---|---|
| Start new textbooks, courses or apps | Stick to your notes, this guide and past papers |
| Reread your notes for hours | Test yourself: papers, flashcards, blank pages |
| Revise only your favourite topics | Work from your red and amber list |
| Sit papers without marking them | Mark every paper within 24 hours |
| Use the newest papers too early | Save them for the last three weeks |
| Stay up late to "finish" a topic | Keep the same bedtime every night |
| Compare hours with friends or on social media | Compare your scores with your own scores last week |

:::tip Revise by question, not by chapter
In the final weeks, start from questions rather than from your notes. Do a question; if you cannot, open the chapter, fix that one gap, and try a similar question. Your time goes exactly where your marks are being lost.
:::

:::warn The last-week panic switch
In the final week, many students abandon their plan and start rereading everything from the beginning. It feels safe but achieves little, and it leaves no time for the subject you sit first. Trust the plan you made when you were calm: light review, error-log rules, sleep.
:::

:::exam Turn your mock into a timing plan
After each mock, note how long each question took. Then write a timing plan for each paper: for Cambridge Mathematics Paper 1 (75 marks in 1 hour 50 minutes), about 1.5 minutes per mark, so roughly 45 minutes for the first 30 marks. Practise the plan in your final mocks so that pacing feels automatic on the day.
:::

:::recap
- Count back from your first paper; use the 8-week table, merging weeks if you have only six.
- Weeks 8–6 for red topics, week 5 a full mock, week 4 fixing the mock, weeks 3–2 timed papers and final mocks, week 1 light review.
- Sit papers at real exam times, mark within 24 hours, and read your error-log rules daily.
- Condense: chapter notes, one page per topic, one page per paper, one card per paper.
- Know what is on your formula list or data booklet, and memorise everything else.
- Sleep 8–10 hours; shift your bedtime to the exam timetable two weeks ahead.
- No new resources, no all-nighters, no last-week panic.
:::
