@summary Research shows that some popular study habits, such as rereading and highlighting, do very little. This chapter shows you the methods that do work (testing yourself, spacing and mixing your practice) and how to build them into a normal week.

:::why
Two students can study for the same hours and get very different grades. Usually the difference is method, not talent. Rereading feels productive but builds familiarity, not memory. Testing yourself feels harder, and that difficulty is what makes knowledge stay. Change how you study and every hour from now on is worth more.
:::

**Before you start**, read [The preparation road at a glance](#road.overview) so you know where these methods fit. They are the engine of [Phase 2: Learn the content](#road.learn) and [Phase 3: Practise and test yourself](#road.practice).

## What the research says

In 2013 the psychologist John Dunlosky and four colleagues reviewed the research on ten popular learning techniques and rated how useful each one is for real students.

| Rating | Technique | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Practice testing | Recalling or answering questions without looking |
| **High** | Distributed practice | Spreading study of a topic over days or weeks |
| Moderate | Elaborative interrogation | Asking yourself "why is this true?" |
| Moderate | Self-explanation | Explaining each step of a solution to yourself |
| Moderate | Interleaved practice | Mixing problem types in one session |
| Low | Summarising | Writing shorter versions of a text |
| Low | Highlighting | Marking key words while reading |
| Low | Keyword mnemonics | Linking new words to memorable images |
| Low | Imagery for text | Picturing what you read |
| Low | Rereading | Reading the same notes again |

"Low" does not mean useless; it means a small benefit for the time it takes. Yet rereading and highlighting are the most popular study habits of all. They feel good because familiar words make your brain mistake "I recognise this" for "I know this", an **illusion of competence**. The exam gives you a blank space, not the page, so producing knowledge from memory is what you must practise.

## Retrieval practice: test yourself first

:::def Retrieval practice
Learning by recalling information from memory (for example by answering questions or writing everything you remember on a topic) instead of rereading it. Each successful recall makes the memory stronger and easier to find later.
:::

Retrieval works even when you get answers wrong, as long as you check and correct them straight away. Good ways to retrieve:

- **Blank-page recall ("blurting").** Close your notes and write everything you remember about a topic for 10 minutes. Then fill the gaps from your notes in a different colour. The coloured parts are your revision list.
- **Chapter questions.** Do the practice questions at the end of each chapter here without looking back.
- **Flashcards** (see below) and **past-paper questions**, which are retrieval in the exact form the exam uses. See [Phase 4: Past papers and mark schemes](#road.pastpapers).

:::tip Turn your notes into questions
After you finish a section, write three questions it answers in the margin, for example "What does the discriminant tell you?" Next time, cover the notes and answer the questions. Your notes become a self-test instead of something to reread.
:::

## Spacing: little and often beats one long session

:::def Spaced practice
Spreading study of the same material over several sessions separated by gaps of days or weeks, instead of doing it all at once. Also called distributed practice.
:::

Memory fades quickly after you first learn something, and each recall after a gap makes it fade more slowly. A useful rule of thumb is to review a new topic about **1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks and 1 month** after first learning it. The exact gaps matter less than the habit of coming back.

The opposite is **cramming**: everything in one long session just before a test. It can get you through tomorrow's test, but most of it is gone weeks later, and A Levels examine two years of content at the end.

:::warn Rewatching is rereading
Watching the same video lesson twice is the video version of rereading. Watch it once, carefully, then close it and test yourself. Rewatch only the part you could not recall.
:::

## Interleaving: mix your practice

:::def Interleaving
Mixing different topics or types of problem within one practice session (for example differentiation, vectors and probability), so that you must decide which method each question needs.
:::

When you do twenty questions on one method in a row (**blocked practice**), you know the method before you read the question. In the exam, nobody tells you, and interleaving trains that choice. Use blocked practice for a brand-new topic, then switch to mixed sets once the basic questions feel easy.

## Making ideas stick: elaboration, examples and pictures

**Elaboration** means connecting a new idea to what you already know by asking "why?" and "how?". Why does a higher temperature speed up a reaction? How does that link to activation energy? Every connection is another route your memory can use.

**Concrete examples** make abstract ideas easy to picture. "Price elastic demand" is hard to remember; "when one brand of crisps raises its price, many buyers switch brands" is easy. Collect one or two examples of your own for every definition.

**Dual coding** means combining words with visuals. Draw blood flow through the heart, a supply and demand diagram or a flowchart of protein synthesis, and label it from memory. Words and pictures give you two routes to the same idea.

## Worked examples, then fade them

When you are a beginner, solving problems alone can overload you, so study a **worked example** first. Then remove the support step by step:

| Stage | What you do |
|---|---|
| 1. Study | Read a full worked example. Explain each step to yourself: why this step, why now? |
| 2. Complete | Try a similar example where the last steps are hidden. Finish it. |
| 3. Solo | Do a similar question with no help, then check against a full solution. |
| 4. Mixed | Do the same type of question inside a mixed set, under time pressure. |

Once you reach stage 4 easily, stop studying examples for that topic and spend your time on questions.

## The Feynman technique

The physicist Richard Feynman was famous for explaining hard ideas in simple words. This technique finds exactly where your understanding breaks.

:::steps
1. Choose one concept, for example "moles" or "opportunity cost".
2. Explain it out loud or on paper as if teaching a younger student. Use plain words and an example.
3. Notice where you hesitate, use jargon you cannot explain, or say "it just is". Those are your gaps.
4. Go back to your notes or the chapter and fix those gaps only.
5. Explain it again, more simply. Finish with the exact syllabus definition.
:::

:::tip If English is your second language
Explain the idea first in your own language to check you understand it. Then explain it again in English, using the precise syllabus terms. Examiners reward the correct technical words, so collect them in a personal word list with a short meaning next to each.
:::

## Spaced-repetition flashcards

Flashcards combine retrieval and spacing. A **spaced-repetition** app such as **Anki** (free on computers, Android and the web) shows each card just before you are likely to forget it: cards you know appear rarely, and cards you miss come back soon.

- **One fact per card.** "State the units of the Planck constant" is a good card; "quantum physics" is not.
- **Make your own cards.** Writing them is learning, and shared decks often do not match your syllabus.
- **Use cards for exact facts:** definitions, equations, units, reagents and conditions. Use diagrams and questions for processes and problem-solving.
- **Review every day,** and add only about 10 to 20 new cards a day so reviews do not pile up.

On paper, use the **Leitner system**: review box 1 daily, box 2 every three days and box 3 weekly. A correct card moves up a box; a wrong card goes back to box 1.

## Focus: your phone is the biggest problem

Every time you check a message, it takes minutes to regain full concentration, and a phone on the desk distracts you even when it is silent.

- Put the phone in another room, or use a focus mode that blocks apps while you study.
- Choose one specific task before you start: "Questions 1 to 6 on the discriminant", not "do maths".
- Study at the same place and time when you can. Routine makes starting easier.

The **Pomodoro technique** makes this easy: 25 minutes of full focus, then a 5-minute break; after four rounds, a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. ## Maths and science versus essay subjects

The same principles apply everywhere, but what you practise is different.

| Subject type | Main thing to practise | Best retrieval tool | Typical trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | Many questions, in mixed sets | Questions with worked answers | Reading solutions before trying |
| Physics and Chemistry | Definitions, equations, calculations, practical skills | Flashcards; past-paper questions | Learning calculations but not explanations |
| Biology | Precise terms, processes, data analysis | Diagrams from memory; long answers | Vague words such as "it affects" |
| Business and Economics | Definitions, diagrams, evaluation | Timed paragraph plans; case studies | Describing the case instead of analysing it |

For essay subjects, write full answers regularly and compare them with the mark scheme's levels; see [Writing top-band business answers](#bus.writing).

## Studying with friends

Group study works when everyone tests and explains, and fails when it turns into chatting. Keep groups to two to four people, agree a topic and a finishing time, and use the time to quiz each other, take turns teaching a topic (the Feynman technique with a real audience), mark each other's answers with the official mark scheme, or compare essay plans. If you study alone as a private candidate, an online study partner gives the same benefits.

## Using AI tools responsibly

AI chat tools can be excellent tutors or terrible ones. The rule: **use AI to explain and to check, never to do the practice for you.** Learning happens when your own brain retrieves and works things out. If a tool does that work, you get none of the benefit, and you still meet the exam alone.

| Good uses | Bad uses |
|---|---|
| "Explain the discriminant in a different way, with an example." | "Solve these ten homework questions for me." |
| "Here is my answer and the mark scheme. Which marks did I miss?" | Copying an AI answer into your notes without trying first |
| "Quiz me on Group 2 trends, one question at a time." | Trusting AI calculations or facts without checking |
| "Translate this sentence from the question into simpler English." | Submitting AI-written work in coursework or applications |

:::warn AI can be confidently wrong
AI tools sometimes give wrong facts, wrong numbers or answers for a different syllabus, in a confident tone. Check against your syllabus, a trusted textbook or the mark scheme. In coursework or non-exam assessment, presenting AI-generated work as your own is malpractice and can lead to disqualification.
:::

## A sample weekly schedule

Here is a realistic week for a student taking Mathematics, Physics and Chemistry, with about two hours of independent study on weekdays. Each block is two Pomodoros (about 50 minutes). Swap in your own subjects; the structure is what matters. For a plan built around your exam dates, use [Build your personal study plan](#road.planner).

| Day | Block 1 | Block 2 | Short review (15 min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Maths: learn a new topic | Physics: questions on last week's topic | Flashcards |
| Tuesday | Chemistry: learn a new topic | Maths: questions on Monday's topic | Flashcards + blank-page recall of Monday's maths |
| Wednesday | Physics: learn a new topic | Chemistry: questions on Tuesday's topic | Flashcards |
| Thursday | Maths: mixed question set | Physics: questions on Wednesday's topic | Flashcards + update error log |
| Friday | Chemistry: mixed question set | Maths: redo last week's wrong questions | Flashcards |
| Saturday | One timed past-paper section (rotate the subject each week) | Mark it with the mark scheme | Add mistakes to error log |
| Sunday | Rest | Plan next week (10 minutes) | Optional light flashcards |

Each subject returns several times (spacing), new content is tested on a later day (retrieval), Thursday and Friday mix topics (interleaving), and Saturday is a test. Sunday's rest protects your motivation; see [Motivation, sleep and stress](#road.wellbeing).

:::recap
- Test yourself instead of rereading: retrieval practice and spaced practice were the two techniques rated high utility in the 2013 review.
- Review new topics after gaps of about 1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks and 1 month. Avoid cramming.
- Learn new methods with blocked practice, then switch to mixed (interleaved) question sets.
- Connect ideas by asking "why?", collect concrete examples, and draw diagrams from memory.
- Study worked examples first, then fade the support until you can solve questions alone.
- Use flashcards with spaced repetition (such as Anki) for facts that must be exact.
- Put your phone in another room and work in focused blocks such as 25-minute Pomodoros.
- Use AI to explain and check, never to do your practice or your coursework.
:::
