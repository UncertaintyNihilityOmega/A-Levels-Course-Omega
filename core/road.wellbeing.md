:::why

Students who sleep well, move every day and take real rest usually learn more in fewer hours than students who study until they are exhausted. None of this is about being "strong". It is about working with your body instead of against it.

## Motivation follows action

Many students wait to feel motivated before they start. But motivation is unreliable: it rises and falls with mood, sleep and the weather. The good news is that it usually **follows** action. Start a small task, make some progress, and the motivation to continue arrives.

So do not rely on motivation. Rely on **habits** and **process goals**.

:::def Process goal
A goal about an action you control, such as "do ten mixed maths questions every weekday", rather than about a result you cannot fully control, such as "get an A*" (an outcome goal).
:::

| Outcome goal (the destination) | Process goals (what you do each day) |
|---|---|
| A* in Mathematics | Ten mixed questions every weekday; one timed paper every Saturday |
| A in Chemistry | 15 minutes of flashcards daily; one past-paper question per topic learned |
| A place to study medicine | Read one article on health science each week; prepare for the admissions test 3 hours a week |

Outcome goals give direction. Process goals give you something to do today, and you can tick them off, which feels good and builds momentum.

## Building habits

A habit is something you do with little thought because it is attached to a regular cue. To build a study habit:

- **Link it to a cue.** Use a specific plan: "After dinner, at my desk, I will do 20 minutes of flashcards." Plans in the form "when X, I will do Y" are much more likely to happen than "I will study more".
- **Make it small.** Start with a version so easy you cannot say no, even five minutes. You can increase it once the habit is fixed.
- **Make it visible.** Mark each day you do it on a calendar. A growing chain of marks is motivating.
- **Never miss twice.** Missing one day is normal. Missing two starts a new habit of not doing it.

## Beating procrastination

Procrastination is not laziness. It is usually a way of avoiding a bad feeling: a task feels too big, too vague, too boring or too frightening. Fix the feeling and the task becomes easier to start.

| Why you are avoiding it | What to do |
|---|---|
| It feels too big ("revise organic chemistry") | Break it down; do only the first 25-minute piece |
| It is vague | Write the exact first action: "open the June paper, question 1" |
| You fear doing badly | Treat the first try as practice; mistakes show you what to fix |
| Something more fun is nearby | Phone in another room; block distracting sites |
| You are tired | Drink water and walk for 10 minutes, then start; if you are exhausted, rest properly and plan the morning |

:::tip The five-minute rule
Tell yourself you will work for just five minutes and then you are allowed to stop. Starting is the hardest part. Most of the time, once five minutes have passed, you will keep going.
:::

## Sleep: when memories are saved

Sleep is not wasted study time. While you sleep, your brain **consolidates** what you learned during the day: it strengthens new memories and links them to old ones. Students who cut sleep to study more often remember less of what they studied.

Teenagers need roughly **8 to 10 hours** of sleep a night. Teenage body clocks also run later than adults', so falling asleep early can be hard. The most useful habit is a **regular wake-up time**, kept within about an hour on weekends too.

- Stop using screens 30 to 60 minutes before bed, and charge your phone outside the bedroom.
- Avoid caffeine (coffee, strong tea, energy drinks) from the early afternoon: it stays in your body for many hours.
- Keep your room cool, dark and quiet.
- If you nap, keep it to about 20 minutes and not late in the day.

:::warn All-nighters do not work
Staying up all night before an exam damages exactly what the exam tests: recall, reasoning and concentration. The night before an exam, a full night's sleep usually does more for your marks than a few extra hours of revision.
:::

## Exercise and food

The World Health Organization recommends that young people aged 5 to 17 do an average of **60 minutes a day** of moderate to vigorous physical activity. Exercise improves mood, sleep and concentration. You do not need a gym: a brisk walk, a game of football or a bike ride all count. A 10 to 20 minute walk is also one of the best breaks between study blocks.

There are no magic "brain foods". What helps is ordinary good eating: regular meals including breakfast, plenty of water, fruit and vegetables, and not living on sugar and energy drinks, which give a short boost followed by a dip. On exam days, eat a familiar breakfast and take water into the exam if your centre allows it.

## Exam nerves: making them work for you

Some nervousness before an exam is normal and useful: it makes you alert. It becomes a problem when it blocks your thinking. These techniques are simple and backed by evidence.

- **Slow breathing.** Breathe in for a count of 4 and out for a count of 6 to 8. A longer out-breath calms your heart rate. Repeat five times.
- **Rename the feeling.** A racing heart feels the same whether you are anxious or excited. Telling yourself "this is energy that will help me focus" can improve performance.
- **Write the worry down.** In a well-known 2011 study, anxious students who spent ten minutes writing about their worries just before an exam scored better than those who did not. Write the worry, then put it away.
- **Practise under real conditions.** Timed past papers at a desk make the real exam feel familiar, and familiar situations are less frightening.

:::steps
1. If your mind goes blank in the exam, put your pen down.
2. Take three slow breaths, with a long out-breath.
3. Move to a question you know you can do, and do it.
4. Come back to the hard question and write anything relevant: a formula, a definition, a diagram.
:::

## Perfectionism

High standards are good. **Perfectionism** is different: it means feeling that anything less than perfect is failure. Warning signs include spending hours making notes beautiful, not starting until conditions are perfect, avoiding past papers in case you score badly, and feeling worthless after one poor mark.

Remember that top grades never require perfection. Grade thresholds are set below full marks, so you can drop marks on every paper and still reach an A*. Early past-paper scores are supposed to be low: they show you what to fix. Aim for progress, keep notes "good enough", and talk to yourself the way you would talk to a friend who made the same mistake.

## Burnout: know the signs

**Burnout** is exhaustion that comes from too much pressure for too long. Watch for:

- tiredness that a night's sleep does not fix;
- dreading study, or feeling "what is the point?";
- working longer hours but achieving less;
- irritability, headaches, stomach aches or getting ill often.

If you notice these, the answer is not to push harder. Reduce your workload for a week, protect your sleep, take a full day off, and talk to someone. Then adjust your plan to be sustainable.

:::analogy
Athletes do not get fitter during training; they get fitter while they recover from it. Training without rest leads to injury. Study is the same: the learning settles during rest and sleep.
:::

## Rest is part of the plan

Schedule rest the way you schedule study. Take at least one full day, or two half-days, off each week. Make breaks real: a walk, a meal with family, sport or a hobby, not an hour of scrolling. Keep seeing friends. During school holidays, lighten the load for a few days before building up again.

## When to get help

Everyone has bad days. But talk to someone if worry, low mood or poor sleep lasts more than about two weeks, if you have panic attacks, if you stop eating properly, or if stress stops you doing normal things.

- **A trusted adult:** a parent, relative or teacher you feel comfortable with.
- **Your school counsellor or guidance teacher**, if you are at a school. Private candidates can ask their exam centre or tutor who to contact.
- **A doctor:** your family doctor can help with sleep problems, anxiety and low mood, and can refer you to specialist support.

If your exam performance is affected by illness or a serious event, your exam centre can apply to the board for **special consideration**. If you have a diagnosed condition, **access arrangements** such as extra time must be applied for in advance through your centre. See [Phase 6: Exam day, results and resits](#road.examday).

:::warn If you are in danger
If you ever think about harming yourself, or feel unsafe, tell someone straight away. Call emergency services (112 in Turkey and across Europe; 999 or 112 in the UK) or go to the nearest hospital emergency department. In the UK and Ireland you can also call Samaritans free, at any time, on 116 123.
:::
