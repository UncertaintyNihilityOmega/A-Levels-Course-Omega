:::why
Phase 2 is the longest part of the road, and the easiest to get wrong. Reading a chapter is not the same as learning it. You have learned a topic when you can explain it and use it without looking, a week later. Everything in this phase is designed to get you to that point with as little wasted time as possible.
:::

## How to study one chapter actively

Passive study (reading, highlighting, copying) feels comfortable because the material looks familiar while it is in front of you. Active study makes your brain produce the ideas itself, which is harder in the moment and far more effective. Use this routine for every chapter in this guide or your textbook.

:::steps
1. **Preview (5 minutes).** Read the summary, the headings and the key-term cards. Ask yourself what the topic is for.
2. **Study one section at a time.** At the end of each section, look away and explain it aloud or on paper in your own words.
3. **Work through each worked example with the solution covered.** Attempt each step before you reveal it.
4. **Answer the practice questions without notes**, then check them against the full answers and mark honestly.
5. **Write your notes from memory**, then compare them with the chapter and fill the gaps in a different colour.
6. **Finish with the quick check**, and make flashcards from the key terms and formulas.
:::

Work in focused blocks of 25–50 minutes with a short break between them, and keep your phone in another room. The timer below helps.

## Turn the syllabus into a checklist

The syllabus for your exam year is the official list of everything that can be examined. Download it free from your board's website. In the subject content section, each topic is broken into numbered **learning outcomes**, short statements of what you must be able to do. Copy them into a tracker, in a notebook or a spreadsheet, and colour each one as you go.

| Learning outcome (Quadratics, Cambridge Paper 1, in our words) | Learned | Practised | Exam-ready |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete the square and use it to find the vertex | Yes | Yes | Green |
| Use the discriminant to decide how many real roots there are | Yes | Yes | Amber |
| Solve quadratic inequalities | Yes | No | Red |
| Solve one linear and one quadratic equation simultaneously | No | No | Red |

- **Red:** not learned yet, or you cannot do it even with notes.
- **Amber:** you can do it with notes, or slowly.
- **Green:** you can do it without notes, under time pressure, at least a week after learning it.

The tracker becomes the backbone of your whole preparation. In phase 5 it tells you exactly which topics need work.

## Making notes that help

Good notes are short, written partly from memory, and built for testing yourself later. Three formats work well. Use whichever suits the topic.

| Method | How it works | Best for |
|---|---|---|
| Cornell notes | A page split into a cue column, a notes column and a summary strip | First notes on a topic: definitions, explanations, methods |
| Concept map | The main idea in the centre, with branches and labelled links | Connections: organic reactions, the cardiovascular system, macroeconomic policy |
| One-page summary | One sheet per topic: key terms, formulas, one diagram, one worked example, common mistakes | The end of a topic, and final revision |

:::warn Notes are not the goal
Beautiful notes with colours and perfect handwriting take hours and teach little. If you are copying sentences from the textbook, stop. Close the book, write what you remember, then check. Notes are a tool for testing yourself, not a product.
:::

## Choosing a textbook

One good textbook per subject, this guide and the past papers are enough. Collecting more resources feels productive, but it is usually a way of avoiding practice.

- **Cambridge:** look for books that carry Cambridge's endorsement mark on the cover. Cambridge lists endorsed resources on its website, from publishers such as Cambridge University Press and Hodder Education.
- **Edexcel IAL:** Pearson publishes its own student books for the IAL specifications.
- **AQA and OCR:** each board approves or endorses textbooks from several publishers for its own specifications.
- **Check the syllabus years.** A book written for an older syllabus can miss new topics or include ones that were removed. Compare its contents with the current syllabus before you buy, especially second-hand.

## Pacing: how fast should you go?

Your plan from the [study planner](#road.planner) gives you a number of learning weeks. Divide the topics by the weeks to get your weekly target. For Cambridge, the number of topics in the syllabus is:

| Subject (Cambridge) | Topics or sections | Topics per week with 20 learning weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematics 9709 (Pure 1, Pure 3, Mechanics, Probability & Statistics 1) | 8 + 9 + 5 + 5 = 27 | about 1.4 |
| Physics 9702 | 25 (1–11 AS, 12–25 A Level) | about 1.3 |
| Chemistry 9701 | 37 (1–22 AS, 23–37 A Level) | about 1.9 |
| Biology 9700 | 19 (1–11 AS, 12–19 A Level) | about 1 |

Topics differ in size, so treat these as averages. Study all your subjects every week rather than one subject per month: switching between subjects is tiring at first, but it gives each topic time to settle and keeps every subject fresh. If a topic takes more than one and a half times its budget, mark it amber, move on, and come back to it in your buffer week.

## Flashcards as you go

Make flashcards on the day you finish each chapter, while you still know what matters. Aim for 5–15 cards per chapter:

- **one fact per card**, written as a question: "What is the discriminant of \(ax^2 + bx + c\)?"
- **definitions** word for word on the back, exactly as the syllabus uses them;
- **"why" questions** as well as "what" questions: "Why does the rate of reaction increase with temperature?"

Paper cards in a box or a free flashcard app both work. How and when to review them is in [Phase 3](#road.practice).

## The weekly review

Once a week, for 30–45 minutes, step back from new content.

:::steps
1. Test yourself for 15 minutes, without notes, on this week's topics and one older topic.
2. Update the colours in your tracker.
3. Read your error log: is any mistake appearing again and again?
4. Compare where you are with your plan, and write next week's topics into your timetable.
:::

## When you are stuck

Being stuck is a normal part of learning, not a sign that you cannot do the subject. Work down this list.

1. **Slow down.** Reread the section, then copy out the worked example line by line, asking why each step follows from the one before.
2. **Go back one step.** Most stuck points are really an earlier gap. Follow the "Before you start" link at the top of the chapter.
3. **Find a different explanation:** your textbook, a video, or a friend who explains it in other words.
4. **Ask a precise question.** "I don't understand moles" is hard to answer; "I don't see why we divide by 2 in line 3" is easy. Teachers, study groups and online forums all respond better to precise questions.
5. **Set a time limit.** After 30 minutes stuck, mark the topic red, move on, and return in two days. Sleep often does the work for you.

:::tip Explain it to a younger student
If you can explain a topic simply to someone two years younger, without notes, you understand it. If you get stuck halfway, you have found exactly the part to study again.
:::

:::exam Learn definitions word for word
Definitions are quick marks, and mark schemes look for precise key words. "Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity" scores; "acceleration is how fast something speeds up" does not. Put every definition from the syllabus and from this guide's key-term cards onto flashcards, and practise writing them out exactly.
:::

:::recap
- Learning means being able to explain and use an idea without looking, a week later.
- Study each chapter actively: preview, explain each section, attempt worked examples before reading them, answer questions without notes.
- Turn the syllabus learning outcomes into a red/amber/green tracker.
- Use Cornell notes, concept maps or one-page summaries; write notes from memory.
- Use one endorsed textbook per subject that matches your syllabus years.
- Pace yourself: divide topics by learning weeks, and study every subject every week.
- Make flashcards on the day you finish a chapter, and review your week every weekend.
:::
