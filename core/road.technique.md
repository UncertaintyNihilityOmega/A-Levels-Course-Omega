@summary Knowing the content is half the job; the other half is turning it into marks. This chapter covers reading questions, timing, working, accuracy and units, and how to answer every type of question, from multiple choice to essays and practical papers.

:::why
Examiners can only mark what is on the page. Every year, examiner reports repeat the same message: many candidates lose marks they could have earned by misreading the question, leaving out units, rounding too early or running out of time. These are skills, and skills can be practised. The habits in this chapter are worth a grade or more to many students.
:::

**Before you start**, it helps to know what each instruction word means: see [Command words decoded](#road.commands). Practise every habit here with real papers, as described in [Phase 4: Past papers and mark schemes](#road.pastpapers).

## Read the question, then read it again

Most lost marks start in the first ten seconds, when a student sees a familiar topic and starts writing the answer to a question they expected, not the one printed. Use the same routine for every question:

:::steps
1. Read the whole question, including any introduction, data or diagram. Information is there because you need it.
2. Underline the **command word** (describe, explain, calculate, evaluate…) and the **topic**.
3. Circle any **conditions**: "two reasons", "exact", "to 3 significant figures", "with reference to the data", "using your answer to (a)".
4. Look at the **marks** and plan that many separate points.
5. Answer, then reread the question to check you answered what was asked.
:::

## Marks tell you how much to write and how long to spend

As a rough guide, **one mark needs one correct, separate point**. A 3-mark "explain" question needs three linked steps of reasoning, not one long sentence. The number of answer lines is a second clue: if you have filled two lines for a 6-mark question, you have probably missed something.

Marks also set your time budget. Divide the time by the total marks:

| Cambridge paper | Time | Marks | Time per mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics 9709 Paper 1 or 3 | 1 h 50 min | 75 | about 1.5 min |
| Mathematics 9709 Paper 4, 5 or 6 | 1 h 15 min | 50 | 1.5 min |
| Sciences Paper 1 (multiple choice) | 1 h 15 min | 40 questions | about 1 min 50 s per question |
| Sciences Paper 2 (AS structured) | 1 h 15 min | 60 | 1.25 min |
| Sciences Paper 4 (A Level structured) | 2 h | 100 | 1.2 min |
| Business 9609 Paper 2 | 1 h 30 min | 60 | 1.5 min |
| Economics 9708 Paper 2 | 2 h | 60 | 2 min |

So an 8-mark maths question deserves about 12 minutes. If you have spent 20, move on and come back later.

:::tip Write checkpoints on the front page
At the start, write two or three checkpoint times, for example "halfway marks by 10:00". A quick glance at the clock then tells you whether you are on pace, without constant calculation.
:::

## Show your working

In mathematics and in science calculations, many marks are for **method**. In mark schemes, M marks are for a correct method, A marks are for accuracy and depend on the method mark, and B marks are independent. If your final answer is wrong but your method is visible, you can still earn many of the marks. A bare wrong answer earns nothing.

Examiners also use **error carried forward** (often called "follow through"): if you make a mistake in part (a) and then use your wrong answer correctly in part (b), you can usually still earn the part (b) marks. That only works if they can see what you did.

Use the same pattern every time: **formula, substitution, answer with unit**.

\[E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times 4.0^2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{J}\]

:::exam "Show that" questions
When the answer is printed in the question, the marks are for the route, not the result. Every step must be visible, with no jumps. Then use the given result in the next part, even if you could not prove it: the later marks are still available.
:::

## Significant figures, units and rounding

- **Do not round in the middle of a calculation.** Keep the full value on your calculator (use the memory or ANS key) and round only the final answer.
- **Mathematics:** give answers to 3 significant figures unless told otherwise, and angles in degrees to 1 decimal place. If the question says **exact**, leave answers as surds, fractions or in terms of \(\pi\) or \(\ln\).
- **Sciences:** give the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data, usually 2 or 3. Never fewer.
- **Units:** write a unit on every final answer and check the prefix. A missing or wrong unit often costs a mark on its own.

:::warn Early rounding changes the answer
A ball of mass \(0.65\,\mathrm{kg}\) falls \(1.8\,\mathrm{m}\) from rest. Take \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Its speed is \(v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 1.8} = 5.9427\ldots\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). If you round \(v\) to 5.9 and then calculate \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\), you get \(11.3\,\mathrm{J}\). Keeping the full value gives \(11.5\,\mathrm{J}\), which matches \(mgh = 0.65 \times 9.81 \times 1.8\). One early rounding lost the accuracy mark.
:::

## Multiple choice

Cambridge science Paper 1 has **40 questions in 1 hour 15 minutes**: about 1 minute 50 seconds each. Economics Papers 1 and 3 are also multiple choice. Edexcel IAL and the UK boards mix multiple-choice questions into some written papers instead.

- **Answer before you look.** Cover the options, work out your answer, then find it. This protects you from attractive wrong options.
- **Eliminate.** Cross out options you know are wrong. With two options left, even a guess has a 50% chance.
- **Watch for NOT, FALSE and INCORRECT,** usually in bold. Underline them.
- **Check powers of ten and units.** Wrong options are often your answer with a factor of 10, 1000 or 2 missing.
- **Never leave a blank.** There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so guess any question you cannot do.
- **Transfer carefully.** If the paper uses a separate answer sheet, check every answer is on the correct line.

## Structured questions

Structured questions are split into parts: (a), (b)(i), (b)(ii). They usually build on each other, so an answer to (a) is often a hint for (b). Keep answers precise: use the technical term, give one idea per mark, and do not copy out the question. "With reference to the data" or "use the information" means quote figures from the question, with units, in your answer.

## Extended writing

For long answers in any subject, build each paragraph with **Point, Evidence, Explain**: make one clear point, support it with evidence or data, and explain why it matters to the question.

In Business and Economics, the mark scheme rewards four skills in order: **knowledge** (correct terms and theory), **application** (using the business or economy in the case), **analysis** (chains of reasoning: "this means that… so…") and **evaluation** (a supported judgement). The top levels require evaluation, so a long, accurate answer with no judgement cannot reach full marks. See [Writing top-band business answers](#bus.writing).

## Data, graphs and diagrams

When you **describe data**, give the overall trend, quote key values with units, describe any change in the rate of change, and point out anomalies. "Sales rose from 1200 to 1800 units, an increase of 50%" earns more than "sales went up".

When you **draw a graph**:

- label both axes with quantity and unit, for example "time / s";
- choose a scale so that the points cover at least half the grid in each direction;
- plot points with small, precise crosses;
- draw a single best-fit line or smooth curve, never dot-to-dot;
- find a gradient using a large triangle, and show the values you read.

In Business and Economics diagrams, label the axes (price, quantity), every curve (D₁, D₂, S), the equilibrium points, and show shifts with arrows. Then refer to the diagram in your writing.

## Practical and planning papers (Cambridge Papers 3 and 5)

Cambridge Physics, Chemistry and Biology each have two practical-skills papers.

| | Paper 3: Advanced Practical Skills (AS) | Paper 5: Planning, Analysis and Evaluation (A Level) |
|---|---|---|
| Format | 2 h, 40 marks, hands-on in a laboratory | 1 h 15 min, 30 marks, written |
| What you do | Follow instructions, take measurements, record, graph, calculate | Plan an experiment, analyse given data, evaluate conclusions |
| Key marks | Tables with "quantity / unit" headings; readings to the precision of the instrument; repeats; good graphs; specific improvements | Clear variables (independent, dependent, controlled); a workable method; safety; how to analyse (what to plot); uncertainties and error bars |

In Paper 3, improvements must be specific: "use a video camera with a timer to measure the oscillation time" earns marks, "be more careful" never does. Edexcel IAL tests practical skills in written papers instead (Units 3 and 6). The subject guides go further: [Physics practical skills](#phys.practical), [Chemistry practical skills](#chem.practical) and [Biology practical skills](#bio.practical).

## Checking and getting unstuck

Leave about 5 to 10 minutes at the end. Check that every question has an answer, every final answer has a unit and the right significant figures, and that each answer matches its command word. Ask "is this sensible?": a car is not travelling at \(3000\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

When you are stuck:

- Mark the question, move on, and come back with fresh eyes.
- Write what you know: the relevant formula, a definition, a labelled diagram. These can earn marks on their own.
- Use every piece of given information; if you have not used something, that is probably the key.
- Write inside the answer space. If you need more room, use the extra page provided and label the question number clearly.

## Common ways marks are lost

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Describing when asked to explain | Underline the command word before writing |
| Missing or wrong units | Write the unit with every final answer |
| Rounding too early | Keep full values; round only at the end |
| Bare answers with no working | Formula, substitution, answer |
| Vague words such as "it affects" | State the direction and size of the change |
| Ignoring the context in a case study | Use the business's name and its data |
| One-sided evaluation | Weigh both sides, then make a justified judgement |
| Running out of time | Use your time per mark and checkpoints |

## Weak and strong answers compared

**A science "explain" question.** *Explain why the rate of a reaction increases when the temperature is increased.* [3 marks]

:::cols
:::col Weak answer (about 1 mark)
The particles move faster, so they collide more and the reaction goes faster.

This repeats the question ("goes faster") and never mentions activation energy or successful collisions.
:::
:::col Strong answer (3 marks)
At a higher temperature the particles have more kinetic energy. [B1] So a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy. [B1] Therefore the frequency of successful collisions increases. [B1]
:::
:::

**A business "evaluate" question.** *Leyla's Bakery made a net profit of $18,000 last year. Leyla needs $40,000 to open a second shop. Evaluate whether she should finance it with a bank loan.* [12 marks]

:::cols
:::col Weak paragraph
A bank loan is good because you get money quickly and do not lose control. But you have to pay interest. Overall she should take the loan.

Correct knowledge, but nothing about Leyla's bakery, no chain of reasoning, and a judgement with no reason.
:::
:::col Strong paragraph
A loan lets Leyla keep full ownership and control (**knowledge**). Her $18,000 profit suggests she could afford repayments from current earnings (**application**). However, a new shop may take months to build sales, while repayments are fixed, so cash flow could turn negative and put both shops at risk (**analysis**). A loan is the better choice only if her forecast for the new shop is realistic and she keeps a cash reserve; if sales are uncertain, a partner sharing the risk may be safer (**evaluation**).
:::
:::

The strong paragraph is not much longer. It earns more because every sentence does a job: it uses the case, builds a chain of reasoning and ends with a judgement that depends on clear conditions.

:::recap
- Underline the command word and topic, circle conditions, and check the marks before you write.
- One mark is roughly one separate point. Use time per mark (for example about 1.5 minutes per mark in Cambridge Maths Paper 1) and move on if a question overruns.
- Show formula, substitution and answer. Method marks and follow-through marks reward visible working.
- Round only at the end: 3 significant figures in maths unless told otherwise; match the data in science. Always give units.
- In multiple choice, answer before looking at the options, eliminate, and never leave a blank.
- For extended writing, build point, evidence, explain paragraphs; in Business and Economics show knowledge, application, analysis and a justified judgement.
- In practical papers, use "quantity / unit" headings, good graph technique and specific improvements.
:::
