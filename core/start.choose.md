@summary How to choose A Level subjects that fit the degree or career you want: typical requirements for popular courses, how many subjects to take, strong combinations, entry requirements, honest workload notes and the mistakes to avoid.

:::why
Your A Level subjects decide which degrees you can apply for. At most universities you cannot study medicine without Chemistry, or engineering without Mathematics. An evening of research now protects options you may want in two years' time.
:::

## Work backwards from your goal

The safest way to choose is to start at the end:

:::steps
1. Write down two or three degree courses you might want to study, even if you are unsure.
2. Look up each course's subject requirements on the websites of a few universities you like.
3. Choose subjects that satisfy all of them, so you keep every option open.
4. Fill any remaining place with a subject you enjoy and can do well in.
:::

University websites separate subjects that are required from those that are only preferred, and the difference matters.

:::def Required subject
An A Level subject that a course insists on; without it, an application is normally rejected. Universities also list "preferred" or "recommended" subjects, which strengthen an application but are not compulsory.
:::

## Typical requirements for popular courses

| If you want to study | Usually required | Recommended or useful | Also note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medicine | Chemistry, and usually Biology | Maths or Physics | UCAT at most schools; interviews; apply by 15 October |
| Dentistry | Chemistry and Biology | Maths or Physics | UCAT at most schools; interviews; apply by 15 October |
| Veterinary science | Chemistry and Biology | Maths or Physics | Animal work experience expected; apply by 15 October |
| Engineering | Maths, and usually Physics | Further Maths, strongly recommended for top courses; Chemistry for chemical engineering | ESAT at some universities (e.g. Cambridge, Imperial, Oxford) |
| Computer science | Maths | Further Maths; Physics or Computer Science | The A Level of the same name is rarely required; TMUA at some universities |
| Mathematics or physics | Maths (and Physics for physics) | Further Maths, often expected at top universities | Admissions tests at some universities |
| Economics | Maths at most selective universities | Further Maths for the most competitive courses | The A Level of the same name is rarely required; TMUA at some universities |
| Business, management, accounting | Usually none | Maths, especially for finance and accounting | A Level Business is not required |
| Law | None in the UK | Essay subjects such as History, English Literature or a language | LNAT at several universities |
| Architecture | Often none | Art or Design; Maths or Physics | A portfolio of creative work is usually required |
| Psychology | Often none | Biology or Maths; some courses want one science | A psychology degree contains a lot of statistics |
| Natural sciences | Two or more of Maths, Physics, Chemistry and Biology | Maths for any physical science | Requirements depend on which sciences you plan to take |

These are typical UK requirements. Every university sets its own, they change from year to year, and universities in other countries have different rules, so confirm the details on each course page for your year of entry. [University applications and admissions tests](#road.university) explains UCAT, LNAT, TMUA and ESAT.

## How many subjects?

**Three** A Levels is the standard, and university offers are almost always based on three. A fourth makes sense mainly when it is Further Mathematics, or when you are strong enough to take it without your other grades suffering. A fourth A Level rarely earns you a lower offer, but a weaker grade in one of your main three can cost you a place.

:::tip The "3 + 1" option
If you cannot decide between four subjects, start all four. At Cambridge or Edexcel International you can sit the AS in one of them at the end of the first year and stop there, keeping a useful extra qualification. An AS at the boards in England is also possible, but it is a separate exam and many schools no longer offer it.
:::

## Strong combinations and keeping doors open

| Combination | Keeps these doors open |
|---|---|
| Maths, Physics, Chemistry | Engineering, physics, chemistry, natural sciences, computer science |
| Maths, Further Maths, Physics | Engineering, mathematics, physics, computer science: the strongest choice for the most competitive courses |
| Biology, Chemistry, plus Maths or Physics | Medicine, dentistry, veterinary science, pharmacy, biomedical sciences, and still some physical sciences |
| Biology, Chemistry, Psychology | Medicine at universities that accept any third subject, psychology, life sciences |
| Maths, Economics, plus History or Further Maths | Economics, finance, management, politics |
| English Literature, History, plus a language or Politics | Law, history, politics, languages, journalism |
| Maths, Physics, Art | Architecture, design engineering |

If you are not sure yet what you want to study, choose subjects that keep the most doors open:

- **Mathematics** is the subject most often required or preferred: engineering, physics, computer science, economics and many sciences ask for it, and it helps with the statistics in psychology and biology.
- **Chemistry** opens medicine, dentistry, veterinary science, pharmacy, biochemistry and chemical engineering.
- **One essay subject** keeps law, the humanities and the social sciences possible.
- It is easier to move from sciences to a humanities degree than the other way round, because most humanities degrees do not require particular subjects.

## Entry requirements: are you ready?

Exam boards set no formal entry requirements, but schools do, and so does common sense: A Level builds directly on IGCSE. Typical school requirements look like this:

| A Level subject | Typical GCSE/IGCSE grade needed |
|---|---|
| Mathematics | 7 (A), sometimes 6 (B) |
| Further Mathematics | 8 or 9 (A*), and a confident start in A Level Maths |
| Physics, Chemistry, Biology | 6 or 7 (B or A) in that science, and at least 6 in Maths |
| Essay subjects (History, English Literature) | 6 (B) in English and a related subject |
| New subjects (Economics, Psychology, Law) | Good grades in English and Maths; no earlier study needed |

If you are teaching yourself and never took the IGCSE, read what the syllabus recommends as prior learning, then fill gaps with the foundation chapters: [Algebra from scratch](#math.f.algebra), [Physics from zero](#phys.f.basics), [Chemistry from zero](#chem.f.basics) and [Biology from zero](#bio.f.basics). [Phase 1: Fix your foundations](#road.foundations) gives a checklist.

## Honest workload notes

No A Level is easy at A* standard, but subjects are hard in different ways.

- **Mathematics** demands fluent algebra and a lot of practice. Answers are right or wrong, so it needs less extended writing, which helps if English is your second language.
- **Further Mathematics** roughly doubles your maths load. Take it only if you are strong at Maths and enjoy it; it also makes ordinary Maths feel easier.
- **Physics** needs confident Maths and clear explanations of ideas, not just formulas.
- **Chemistry** combines a large amount of content with calculations, and later topics build directly on earlier ones, so falling behind early is costly.
- **Biology** has a large amount of content and precise vocabulary, with many longer written answers.
- **Essay subjects** (History, English Literature, Economics, Business) need wide reading and plenty of writing practice. If English is not your first language, start practising timed writing early; it is entirely possible to reach the top grades.
- **Private candidates** should check that their centre can run every part of the course, such as a science laboratory exam or coursework, before choosing.

## Common mistakes

:::warn Choosing for the wrong reasons
- Picking a subject because your friends take it or because you like one teacher.
- Dropping Mathematics without checking: it is required or preferred for engineering, computer science, physics, economics and more.
- Assuming you need the A Level of the same name: law, economics, psychology, business and computer science degrees rarely require it.
- Taking Further Maths when your Maths is not yet secure.
- Taking four or five subjects "to impress" and getting lower grades in all of them.
- Choosing two very similar subjects, such as Business and Economics, without checking: some selective universities prefer them not to be two of your three.
:::

:::exam Grades matter as much as subjects
A university looks at both what you studied and how well you did. A subject you enjoy and can score an A in is usually a better choice than a "prestigious" one in which you would get a C, provided the required subjects for your course are covered.
:::

:::recap
- Work backwards: list possible degrees, check their required subjects, then choose subjects that satisfy all of them.
- Medicine, dentistry and veterinary science need Chemistry and usually Biology; engineering needs Maths and usually Physics, with Further Maths strongly recommended.
- Computer science and economics usually need Maths but rarely the A Level of the same name; law usually needs no particular subject.
- Three A Levels is standard; a fourth makes most sense as Further Mathematics.
- Maths plus one or two sciences keeps the most options open.
- Schools usually expect a 6 or 7 (B or A) at GCSE/IGCSE in a subject, and higher for Further Maths.
- Requirements differ between universities and change over time: always check the course page for your entry year.
:::
