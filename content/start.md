The next twenty minutes will save you months later.

## A Levels in plain English

**A Level** is short for **Advanced Level**. An A Level is a qualification in **one subject**, such as Mathematics, Chemistry or Business. Most students study three of them at the same time, over two years, between the ages of 16 and 18. At the end you receive one grade for each subject, from A* (the highest) down to E.

:::def A Level
An Advanced Level qualification in a single subject, normally studied over two years after GCSE, IGCSE or O Level and graded A* to E. It is the main qualification for university entry in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and schools in many other countries offer it too.
:::

A Levels follow the exams most students take at about 16: **GCSE** (General Certificate of Secondary Education) in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, **IGCSE** (International GCSE) in international schools, or **O Level** (Ordinary Level), still offered by Cambridge in some countries.

In England, the two A Level years are **Year 12** and **Year 13**, together called the **sixth form**. In Turkey they match the ages of Grades 11 and 12 of *lise*. Scotland has its own system (Highers and Advanced Highers), so A Levels are less common there.

## Two halves: AS and A2

An A Level course splits naturally into two halves of about one year each.

:::def AS Level
Advanced Subsidiary Level: a qualification covering roughly the first half of an A Level, normally studied in one year. Cambridge International grades it a to e (lower-case letters); UK boards use A to E. There is no A* at AS Level.
:::

The second half is usually called **A2**, and this guide labels first-year content "AS" and second-year content "A2".

Whether your AS result counts depends on the exam board:

| Exam board | Does the AS count towards the full A Level? |
|---|---|
| Cambridge International | Yes, on the "staged" route: AS first, then the result is carried forward. You can also sit everything at the end. |
| Pearson Edexcel International (IAL) | Yes: the first-year units make up half the qualification. |
| AQA, OCR, Pearson Edexcel and Eduqas in England | No. The AS is a separate qualification; all A Level exams come at the end of Year 13. |
| WJEC (Wales) and CCEA (Northern Ireland) | Yes. The AS is worth 40% of the A Level. |

## How many subjects, and which grades?

Most students take **three A Levels**. Students who choose Further Mathematics usually take four, because Further Maths is studied alongside Mathematics, not instead of it. Some schools ask everyone to start four subjects and drop one at the end of the first year, keeping it as an AS.

| Qualification | Pass grades, highest first |
|---|---|
| A Level (all boards) | A*, A, B, C, D, E |
| AS Level (Cambridge International) | a, b, c, d, e |
| AS Level (UK boards) | A, B, C, D, E |

A result below E is recorded as U (ungraded), which is not a pass. Universities usually make offers in grades, such as "AAB", or in UCAS tariff points, which turn grades into numbers. [Grades, marks and UCAS points](#start.grades) explains both.

## How much work is an A Level?

:::def Guided learning hours
The time a student spends being taught, or doing study directed and supervised by a teacher, for a qualification. Cambridge International plans for about 360 guided learning hours for each A Level and about 180 for each AS Level. Private study comes on top.
:::

Two school years have about 36 teaching weeks each, so 360 hours works out at about **5 hours of teaching per subject per week**. Add homework, revision and practice, and a normal A Level week looks like this:

| Activity | Hours per subject per week | For three subjects |
|---|---|---|
| Lessons (or, if you study alone, working through chapters) | 4–5 | 12–15 |
| Homework and reviewing notes | 2–3 | 6–9 |
| Practice questions, and later past papers | 1–2 | 3–6 |
| **Total** | **7–10** | **21–30** |

Three A Levels are close to a full-time job. In school, that means lessons in each subject on most days, homework in every subject, and a short test or practical most weeks. If you study alone, you are teacher and student at once: lesson time becomes reading, working through examples and checking answers.

:::tip A self-study week that works
Give each subject at least three separate sessions a week rather than one long one: short, regular sessions beat a weekend marathon because you forget less in between. Keep one session a week for older topics. [Build your personal study plan](#road.planner) turns your exam date into a week-by-week timetable.
:::

## Why depth matters

At IGCSE you probably studied eight to ten subjects. At A Level you study three, and each goes much deeper: A Level Chemistry, for example, explains *why* reactions happen and asks you to predict ones you have never seen.

Because of this depth, examiners test understanding more than memory. Only part of the marks are for recalling facts; the rest reward applying ideas to unfamiliar situations, handling data, and analysing or evaluating.

:::def Assessment objectives (AOs)
The skills an exam is designed to reward, listed with their weightings in every syllabus. Typical AOs are knowledge and understanding, application, and analysis and evaluation; science subjects also have one for experimental skills.
:::

:::analogy
IGCSE checks that you can follow a recipe. A Level checks whether you can cook a good meal from whatever is in the fridge. You still need to know the recipes, but the marks go to people who understand why each step works.
:::

Most students find the first term the hardest: the pace is faster, the ideas are more abstract, and you are expected to work more independently. This is normal, and it is why every chapter in this guide starts from zero and builds up in small steps. The same depth is why universities value A Levels: two years of serious work on a few hard subjects is excellent preparation for a degree.

## Who uses A Levels?

A Levels are the standard entry qualification for UK universities, and universities in many other countries accept them too. Each university sets its own requirements, so always check the entry requirements for international students on each course page.

Several organisations, called **exam boards**, write and mark A Levels. Schools in the UK use boards such as AQA, OCR, Pearson Edexcel, WJEC/Eduqas and CCEA. International schools mostly use **Cambridge International** (often shortened to CAIE) or **Pearson Edexcel International** (IAL), and OxfordAQA also offers international A Levels. UK universities accept A Levels from all the major boards. This guide follows Cambridge International and points out where the others differ.

## How A Levels compare with other systems

| | A Levels | IB Diploma | US Advanced Placement (AP) | National systems (e.g. Turkey) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Length | 2 years | 2 years | Usually 1 year per course | Final years of school, then an entrance exam |
| Subjects | Usually 3, in depth | 6 (3 Higher Level, 3 Standard Level) plus a core | Any number of single-subject exams | A broad school curriculum |
| Extra parts | Optional Extended Project (EPQ) at UK boards | Theory of Knowledge, Extended Essay, CAS | None | Varies by country |
| Grading | A* to E per subject | 1 to 7 per subject, 45 points maximum | 1 to 5 per exam | School grades plus an exam score |
| Main use | University entry in the UK and worldwide | University entry worldwide | US college admission and credit | Entry to that country's universities |

None of these is "better". The **IB Diploma** suits students who want breadth (CAS stands for creativity, activity, service). **AP** exams are taken alongside an American high school diploma, and UK universities usually ask for several AP scores as well as the diploma. **National systems**, such as Turkey's *lise* diploma plus the **YKS** entrance exam run by ÖSYM, are designed mainly for entry to that country's own universities. A Levels suit students who are ready to specialise.

:::note Studying in Turkey?
A Levels do not replace the YKS for most places at Turkish universities. Students in Turkey usually take A Levels to apply abroad, and many UK universities ask applicants from Turkey for A Levels, the IB, AP results or a foundation year rather than the national diploma alone. To keep both routes open, check the current rules with ÖSYM and with each university.
:::

## Can anyone take A Levels?

Yes. There is no age limit, and you do not need to be at a school. Adults, home-schooled students and school students adding a subject their school does not teach can all enter as a **private candidate**.

:::def Private candidate
A student who enters for exams through an approved exam centre without being taught there. Private candidates sit the same papers and receive the same grades and certificates as school students.
:::

Private candidates register with an exam centre that accepts external students, pay the fees and sit the exams there. In Turkey, the British Council (in Istanbul and Ankara) and some approved schools act as centres. Sciences need care: Cambridge's science A Levels include a laboratory exam that your centre must be able to run, while Edexcel International tests practical skills in written papers, one reason many private candidates choose it. [Studying as a private candidate](#road.private) explains the process.

@summary Who sets A Level exams, how Cambridge International, Pearson Edexcel International and the boards in England organise them, when exams and results happen, and how papers, mark schemes and grade boundaries work.

:::why
Two students can both be "doing A Level Physics" and sit completely different exams: different papers, different months, different resit rules. Knowing your board and its rules tells you which syllabus to follow, which past papers to practise and when your deadlines fall.
:::

## What an exam board does

An exam board writes the syllabus, sets the papers, marks them and awards the grades. All boards teach broadly the same subjects at the same level, but the details differ: the exact topics, the number and style of papers, the exam months and the resit rules.

:::def Exam board
An organisation that writes the syllabus for a qualification, sets and marks the exams, and awards grades and certificates. Also called an awarding body.
:::

:::def Syllabus
The official document listing exactly what can be examined in a subject, how the exams are structured and what each assessment objective is worth. Cambridge calls it a syllabus; Pearson and the UK boards call it a specification.
:::

## The main boards

| Board | Qualification | Where it is used |
|---|---|---|
| Cambridge International (CAIE) | Cambridge International AS & A Level | International schools in many countries; private candidates at approved centres |
| Pearson Edexcel International | International Advanced Level (IAL) | International schools; widely used by private candidates, including at British Council centres |
| AQA | AS and A Level | Schools in England |
| OCR | AS and A Level | Schools in England |
| Pearson Edexcel (UK) | AS and A Level | Schools in England, and some British schools abroad |
| WJEC / Eduqas | AS and A Level | WJEC in Wales; Eduqas is the same organisation's brand in England |

Northern Ireland has its own board, CCEA, and OxfordAQA offers international A Levels in some countries. Cambridge International and OCR both belong to Cambridge University Press & Assessment, but they set different qualifications. AQA, OCR, Pearson Edexcel and Eduqas follow the same rules for A Levels in England, set by the regulator Ofqual, so their A Levels share one structure.

## Cambridge International: AS first, or everything at the end

A Cambridge A Level is made of several papers, which Cambridge calls **components**. The AS papers make up the AS Level and also half of the full A Level; the A Level adds more papers on second-year content. You can take them in three ways:

:::steps
1. **AS Level only.** Sit the AS papers and receive an AS qualification.
2. **Staged route.** Sit the AS papers in one series and the remaining A Level papers in a later one. Your AS result is carried forward and combined with them.
3. **Linear route.** Sit every paper, AS and A Level, in the same series at the end of the course.
:::

An AS result can be carried forward to complete the A Level **within 13 months**. In practice, an AS taken in June can be completed in the following June series.

:::warn The 13-month rule
If you sit AS in June 2026, you must complete the A Level by the June 2027 series for the AS to count. If you wait longer, you would have to sit all the papers, AS ones included, in a single series.
:::

A real example is Cambridge International AS & A Level Physics (9702). Chemistry (9701) and Biology (9700) follow the same pattern.

| Paper | Type | Time | Marks | Share of A Level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Multiple choice (AS) | 1 h 15 min | 40 | 15.5% |
| 2 | AS structured questions | 1 h 15 min | 60 | 23% |
| 3 | Advanced practical skills (laboratory exam) | 2 h | 40 | 11.5% |
| 4 | A Level structured questions | 2 h | 100 | 38.5% |
| 5 | Planning, analysis and evaluation | 1 h 15 min | 30 | 11.5% |

Papers 1–3 form the AS (50% of the A Level); Papers 4 and 5 are the second-year papers.

Cambridge runs two main **exam series** each year: **June** (exams from roughly late April to mid-June) and **November** (October and November). A third, March, series is available in India only. June results come out in August (the June 2026 A Level results were released on 11 August 2026), and November results in January.

:::def Exam series
A period in which a board runs a full set of exams, named after its main month, such as the June series or the November series.
:::

## Pearson Edexcel International: units taken in stages

An IAL is **modular**: it is split into **units**, each examined by one paper. IAL sciences have six units, three for the International AS and three more for the full IAL. IAL Mathematics also has six: four Pure units and two applied units (mechanics, statistics or decision mathematics).

:::def Modular qualification
A qualification split into separately examined units that can be taken at different times, with the results combined at the end. Edexcel International A Levels are modular; A Levels from the boards in England are linear.
:::

- There are three sittings a year: **January**, **May/June** and **October/November**. Most units are offered in every sitting.
- You can spread units over several sittings, for example Pure 1 in January and Pure 2 in June.
- You can **resit** a unit to improve it, and the better result counts.
- Each unit's raw mark is converted to a **uniform mark** so that units from different sittings can be added fairly ([how this works](#start.grades)).
- When you have all the units you need, your centre claims the qualification (called "cashing in") and your overall grade is calculated.
- In the sciences, practical skills are tested in written papers (Units 3 and 6), not in a laboratory exam.

## The boards in England: linear A Levels

A Levels from AQA, OCR, Pearson Edexcel and Eduqas are **linear**:

- Every exam that counts is sat in May and June at the end of Year 13.
- The AS is a separate qualification whose marks never count towards the A Level, so many schools no longer enter students for it.
- There is one exam series a year, in the summer. To improve a grade, you resit all the exam papers the following summer; coursework marks can usually be carried forward.
- In Biology, Chemistry and Physics you also receive a **Practical Endorsement**, reported separately as Pass or Not classified, based on at least 12 practical activities assessed by your teacher. Many universities expect a Pass.
- Some subjects include coursework, officially called **non-exam assessment (NEA)**: typically 20% in History, Geography, English Literature and Computer Science, and more in subjects such as Design and Technology and Art.

## The three systems side by side

| | Cambridge International | Edexcel International (IAL) | Boards in England |
|---|---|---|---|
| Structure | Papers; AS papers form half the A Level | Units; AS units form half the IAL | Linear: all papers at the end |
| Does the AS count? | Yes, if carried forward within 13 months | Yes | No |
| Exam series | June and November (March in India only) | January, May/June, October/November | Summer only |
| Improving a grade | Retake in a later series | Resit single units; the better result counts | Resit all papers the next summer |
| Science practicals | Laboratory exam plus a planning paper | Written practical-skills papers | Practical Endorsement plus practical questions in written papers |
| Results | August (June series), January (November series) | Roughly two months after each sitting | Mid-August |

:::board
WJEC A Levels in Wales and CCEA A Levels in Northern Ireland are unitised: the AS counts for 40% of the A Level. Scotland uses its own qualifications (Highers and Advanced Highers). Rules change from time to time, so always check the current syllabus for your board.
:::

## What the papers look like

| Paper type | What you do | Common in |
|---|---|---|
| Multiple choice | Answer 30–40 questions, each with four options | Cambridge sciences, Economics, Accounting |
| Structured questions | Answer a question in parts (a), (b), (c): short answers, calculations, explanations | Every subject |
| Extended response | Write a long answer, marked by "levels of response" | Business, Economics, English, History |
| Data response or case study | Answer questions on unseen data, a text or a business case | Business, Economics, Geography |
| Practical exam | Carry out experiments in a laboratory under exam conditions | Cambridge sciences |
| Planning and analysis | Plan an experiment and analyse given results, on paper | Cambridge and Edexcel International sciences |
| Coursework (NEA) | Complete a project over several weeks, marked by teachers and checked by the board | Some subjects at the boards in England |

## Mark schemes, examiner reports and grade boundaries

After each series, boards publish three documents that are gold for revision:

- **Mark schemes** show exactly what earned each mark. In Maths they use M (method), A (accuracy) and B (independent) marks; in essay subjects they describe levels of response.
- **Examiner reports** explain, question by question, what candidates did well and where they lost marks.
- **Grade boundaries** (Cambridge calls them grade thresholds) give the minimum mark for each grade. They are set after marking, for each paper in each series. [Grades, marks and UCAS points](#start.grades) explains why they move.

Cambridge puts a selection of past papers, mark schemes and examiner reports on its public website, and schools get more through its School Support Hub. Pearson, AQA and OCR publish theirs on their qualification pages, although the most recent papers are sometimes released to teachers first.

:::exam Use mark schemes from day one
Do not wait for "past-paper season". After every topic, try two or three past-paper questions on it and mark them yourself with the official scheme. You will quickly learn which words and steps examiners need to see. [Phase 4: Past papers and mark schemes](#road.pastpapers) shows a full method.
:::

## Entries and deadlines

You cannot simply turn up on exam day. Your school, or your exam centre if you are a private candidate, must **enter** you for each paper months before the series. For a June series, the deadline usually falls early in the same year, and many centres set their own earlier deadline. Late entries, if accepted at all, cost extra. Fees are set by each centre and vary, so ask for the full price per subject, including any centre charges.

:::warn Confirm four things before you start studying
Check with your centre: the board, the syllabus code (for example 9702 for Cambridge Physics), the exam series, and the entry deadline. A year spent studying the wrong syllabus is an expensive mistake.
:::

@chapter start.grades
@title Grades, marks and UCAS points
@level start
@time 25 minutes
@summary How raw marks become grades, why grade boundaries move, how the A* is awarded, and how UCAS tariff points, university offers and predicted grades work.

:::why
Your grades open university doors, so it pays to know exactly how they are calculated. Understanding grade boundaries also changes how you study: it shows why every single mark matters, and why a hard paper is not a disaster.
:::

## The grades

| Qualification | Pass grades, highest first |
|---|---|
| A Level | A*, A, B, C, D, E |
| AS Level | a, b, c, d, e at Cambridge; A, B, C, D, E at UK boards |

A result below the lowest pass grade is reported as ungraded (U), and no qualification is awarded in that subject. Your certificate shows a grade for each subject, not a percentage.

## From raw marks to a grade

Each paper is marked and you receive a **raw mark**. Your raw marks are combined, using each paper's weighting, and the total is compared with the **grade boundaries** for that series.

:::def Raw mark
The number of marks you actually score on a paper, before any conversion; for example, 52 out of 75.
:::

:::def Grade boundary
The minimum mark needed for a particular grade on a paper or qualification in a given exam series. Boundaries are set after marking and can change from series to series. Cambridge calls them grade thresholds.
:::

### Why boundaries move

No two papers are equally difficult. If this year's paper is harder than last year's, the same student scores fewer marks. To keep an A worth the same every year, senior examiners study the papers, samples of marked scripts and statistics, and then set the boundaries. A harder paper gets lower boundaries; an easier paper gets higher ones.

| Mark needed (out of 100) | Series 1: harder paper | Series 2: easier paper |
|---|---|---|
| Grade A | 68 | 76 |
| Grade C | 47 | 55 |

These numbers are invented to show the idea; each board publishes the real boundaries for every series. Three lessons follow:

1. **There is no fixed percentage for an A.** "70% is an A" is a myth.
2. **A hard paper is hard for everyone.** If a paper feels brutal, keep collecting marks: the boundaries will reflect it.
3. **Every mark counts.** Neighbouring grades are often separated by only a handful of marks on each paper, so one extra method mark here and there can lift a whole grade.

:::tip Estimating your grade from a past paper
Mark the paper with the official mark scheme, then compare your total with the published boundaries for **that same series**, not this year's or an average. Do this for papers from several different years to get a fair picture.
:::

## How the A* is awarded

- **Cambridge International** awards A* for the full A Level only, based on your total across all the papers. There is no A* at AS.
- **Edexcel International** requires an A overall plus an especially high score on the A2 (second-year) units, so an A* reflects strength in the hardest material.
- **The boards in England** set the A* as the top boundary on your total mark, like any other grade.

## Uniform marks at Edexcel International

In a modular system, you might sit one unit in a January with a hard paper and another in a June with an easy one. Simply adding the raw marks would be unfair. Pearson solves this with **uniform marks**.

:::def Uniform mark
A raw unit mark converted onto a fixed scale, so that results from papers of different difficulty, taken in different sittings, can be added fairly. The grade boundaries on the uniform scale stay the same every series.
:::

The conversion uses each sitting's grade boundaries: a raw mark exactly on the A boundary always becomes the same uniform mark, however hard the paper was. Your uniform marks from all the units are then added, and the total decides your overall grade. Pearson's results show the uniform mark for each unit, which tells you how close you were to the next grade.

:::analogy
Uniform marks work like currency exchange. Raw marks from different sittings are like dollars, euros and lira: you cannot add them directly. Converting everything into one currency first lets you add them fairly.
:::

## UCAS tariff points

**UCAS** (the Universities and Colleges Admissions Service) handles applications to UK universities. Its **tariff** turns grades into points so that different qualifications can be compared.

:::def UCAS tariff points
A points system that converts qualifications and grades into numbers so that different qualifications can be compared. Some universities state their entry requirements in tariff points.
:::

| Grade | A Level | AS Level | Extended Project (EPQ) |
|---|---|---|---|
| A* | 56 | no A* | 28 |
| A (AS: a) | 48 | 20 | 24 |
| B (AS: b) | 40 | 16 | 20 |
| C (AS: c) | 32 | 12 | 16 |
| D (AS: d) | 24 | 10 | 12 |
| E (AS: e) | 16 | 6 | 8 |

An EPQ is worth exactly half an A Level at every grade. An AS that you later complete as a full A Level in the same subject does not count separately: only the A Level counts.

:::eg Adding up tariff points
A student has A Levels in Mathematics (A*), Physics (A) and Chemistry (B), an AS in Further Mathematics (grade b) and an EPQ (grade A). How many UCAS tariff points does she have?

A Levels: \(56 + 48 + 40 = 144\)

AS Further Mathematics, grade b: \(16\)

EPQ, grade A: \(24\)

Total: \(144 + 16 + 24 = 184\) points.
:::

AAA and A*AB are both worth 144 points, but a course asking for "A*AA including Mathematics" accepts neither. Most selective universities make offers in **grades** and name the subjects. Use points only when a course actually asks for them.

## What offers look like

You apply through UCAS to up to five courses (four at most in medicine, dentistry or veterinary science). Each university replies with an offer or a rejection. Typical offers read like this:

- **"AAA"**: three A grades, sometimes excluding certain subjects.
- **"A*AA, including A* in Mathematics and A in Physics"**: grades plus named subjects, as for many engineering courses.
- **"ABB including Biology and Chemistry"**: a common pattern for life sciences.
- **"112 UCAS points, including a C in Business"**: a tariff offer, which BBC would meet (\(40 + 40 + 32 = 112\)).
- **"AAB, or ABB with an A in the EPQ"**: some universities lower an offer for a strong EPQ.

:::def Conditional offer
A university place that becomes yours only if you meet stated conditions, usually particular grades. An unconditional offer has no grade conditions, usually because you already have your results.
:::

From your offers you choose one **firm** choice and, if you wish, one **insurance** choice with lower grades as a backup. On results day, the university confirms your place if you meet the conditions. If you miss both, **Clearing** matches students without a place to courses that still have vacancies. Some courses also set admissions tests or interviews; see [University applications and admissions tests](#road.university).

:::key UCAS deadlines for 2027 entry
- **15 October 2026**: Oxford, Cambridge, and most courses in medicine, dentistry and veterinary science.
- **13 January 2027**: deadline for equal consideration for most other courses.
:::

## Predicted grades

Universities usually decide before your final exams, so they rely on predictions.

:::def Predicted grades
The grades your teachers expect you to achieve, entered on your UCAS application. Universities make most offers on the basis of predicted grades, because final results arrive after most decisions are made.
:::

Teachers predict from evidence: classwork, mock exams, AS results if you have them, and the progress you have shown. A prediction below a course's typical offer usually means no offer, so the evidence you give your teachers matters. Private candidates still need predicted grades and a reference; a tutor, a former teacher or a school can provide them. [Studying as a private candidate](#road.private) explains how.

:::exam Earn your predictions
Do your mocks properly, under timed conditions, and keep a record of your past-paper scores. A strong AS result, on Cambridge's staged route or at Edexcel International, is the most convincing evidence of all.
:::

Your A Level subjects decide which degrees you can apply for. At most universities you cannot study medicine without Chemistry, or engineering without Mathematics. An evening of research now protects options you may want in two years' time.

## Work backwards from your goal

The safest way to choose is to start at the end:

:::steps
1. Write down two or three degree courses you might want to study, even if you are unsure.
2. Look up each course's subject requirements on the websites of a few universities you like.
3. Choose subjects that satisfy all of them, so you keep every option open.
4. Fill any remaining place with a subject you enjoy and can do well in.
:::

University websites separate subjects that are required from those that are only preferred, and the difference matters.

:::def Required subject
An A Level subject that a course insists on; without it, an application is normally rejected. Universities also list "preferred" or "recommended" subjects, which strengthen an application but are not compulsory.
:::

## Typical requirements for popular courses

| If you want to study | Usually required | Recommended or useful | Also note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Medicine | Chemistry, and usually Biology | Maths or Physics | UCAT at most schools; interviews; apply by 15 October |
| Dentistry | Chemistry and Biology | Maths or Physics | UCAT at most schools; interviews; apply by 15 October |

| Engineering | Maths, and usually Physics | Further Maths, strongly recommended for top courses; Chemistry for chemical engineering | ESAT at some universities (e.g. Cambridge, Imperial, Oxford) |

## How many subjects?

**Three** A Levels is the standard, and university offers are almost always based on three. A fourth makes sense mainly when it is Further Mathematics, or when you are strong enough to take it without your other grades suffering. A fourth A Level rarely earns you a lower offer, but a weaker grade in one of your main three can cost you a place.

:::tip The "3 + 1" option
If you cannot decide between four subjects, start all four. At Cambridge or Edexcel International you can sit the AS in one of them at the end of the first year and stop there, keeping a useful extra qualification. An AS at the boards in England is also possible, but it is a separate exam and many schools no longer offer it.
:::

## Strong combinations and keeping doors open

| Combination | Keeps these doors open |
|---|---|
| Maths, Physics, Chemistry | Engineering, physics, chemistry, natural sciences, computer science |
| Maths, Further Maths, Physics | Engineering, mathematics, physics, computer science: the strongest choice for the most competitive courses |
| Biology, Chemistry, plus Maths or Physics | Medicine, dentistry, veterinary science, pharmacy, biomedical sciences, and still some physical sciences |
| Biology, Chemistry, Psychology | Medicine at universities that accept any third subject, psychology, life sciences |
| Maths, Economics, plus History or Further Maths | Economics, finance, management, politics |
| English Literature, History, plus a language or Politics | Law, history, politics, languages, journalism |
| Maths, Physics, Art | Architecture, design engineering |

If you are not sure yet what you want to study, choose subjects that keep the most doors open:

- **Mathematics** is the subject most often required or preferred: engineering, physics, computer science, economics and many sciences ask for it, and it helps with the statistics in psychology and biology.
- **Chemistry** opens medicine, dentistry, veterinary science, pharmacy, biochemistry and chemical engineering.
- **One essay subject** keeps law, the humanities and the social sciences possible.
- It is easier to move from sciences to a humanities degree than the other way round, because most humanities degrees do not require particular subjects.

## Entry requirements: are you ready?

Exam boards set no formal entry requirements, but schools do, and so does common sense: A Level builds directly on IGCSE. Typical school requirements look like this:

| A Level subject | Typical GCSE/IGCSE grade needed |
|---|---|
| Mathematics | 7 (A), sometimes 6 (B) |
| Further Mathematics | 8 or 9 (A*), and a confident start in A Level Maths |
| Physics, Chemistry, Biology | 6 or 7 (B or A) in that science, and at least 6 in Maths |
| Essay subjects (History, English Literature) | 6 (B) in English and a related subject |
| New subjects (Economics, Psychology, Law) | Good grades in English and Maths; no earlier study needed |

If you are teaching yourself and never took the IGCSE, read what the syllabus recommends as prior learning, then fill gaps with the foundation chapters: [Algebra from scratch](#math.f.algebra), [Physics from zero](#phys.f.basics), [Chemistry from zero](#chem.f.basics) and [Biology from zero](#bio.f.basics). [Phase 1: Fix your foundations](#road.foundations) gives a checklist.

## Honest workload notes

No A Level is easy at A* standard, but subjects are hard in different ways.

- **Mathematics** demands fluent algebra and a lot of practice. Answers are right or wrong, so it needs less extended writing, which helps if English is your second language.
- **Further Mathematics** roughly doubles your maths load. Take it only if you are strong at Maths and enjoy it; it also makes ordinary Maths feel easier.
- **Physics** needs confident Maths and clear explanations of ideas, not just formulas.
- **Chemistry** combines a large amount of content with calculations, and later topics build directly on earlier ones, so falling behind early is costly.
- **Biology** has a large amount of content and precise vocabulary, with many longer written answers.
- **Essay subjects** (History, English Literature, Economics, Business) need wide reading and plenty of writing practice. If English is not your first language, start practising timed writing early; it is entirely possible to reach the top grades.
- **Private candidates** should check that their centre can run every part of the course, such as a science laboratory exam or coursework, before choosing.

## Common mistakes

:::warn Choosing for the wrong reasons
- Picking a subject because your friends take it or because you like one teacher.
- Dropping Mathematics without checking: it is required or preferred for engineering, computer science, physics, economics and more.
- Assuming you need the A Level of the same name: law, economics, psychology, business and computer science degrees rarely require it.
- Taking Further Maths when your Maths is not yet secure.
- Taking four or five subjects "to impress" and getting lower grades in all of them.
- Choosing two very similar subjects, such as Business and Economics, without checking: some selective universities prefer them not to be two of your three.
:::

:::exam Grades matter as much as subjects
A university looks at both what you studied and how well you did. A subject you enjoy and can score an A in is usually a better choice than a "prestigious" one in which you would get a C, provided the required subjects for your course are covered.
:::

Here is what three of them look like.

:::def Mark scheme
The document examiners use to award marks. It lists the accepted answers and shows exactly where each mark is earned, including method (M), accuracy (A) and independent (B) marks in Mathematics.
:::

:::eg A rectangle is \((x + 3)\) cm long and \(x\) cm wide. Its area is \(40\,\mathrm{cm^2}\). Find \(x\).
Form the equation: \(x(x + 3) = 40\), so \(x^2 + 3x - 40 = 0\). [B1]

Factorise: \((x + 8)(x - 5) = 0\). [M1]

So \(x = 5\); reject \(x = -8\), because a length cannot be negative. [A1]

**Check:** a rectangle 8 cm long and 5 cm wide has area \(40\,\mathrm{cm^2}\).
:::

The mark labels follow the system used in Cambridge and Edexcel mathematics mark schemes:

- **M (method)**: for a correct method applied to the problem, even if you then make a slip.
- **A (accuracy)**: for a correct answer or step. You can only earn it if you have earned the method mark it depends on.
- **B (independent)**: for a correct result or statement that does not depend on a method.

So a correct answer with no working may not earn full marks, while a wrong answer with a sound method can still earn some. You will meet problems like this one in [Quadratics](#math.p1.quadratics).

:::warn Reading is not the same as doing
Following a worked solution feels easy because every step is already there. The real test is whether you can produce it yourself with the page covered. Always try first, then compare.
:::
