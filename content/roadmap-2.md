@summary Research shows that some popular study habits, such as rereading and highlighting, do very little. This chapter shows you the methods that do work (testing yourself, spacing and mixing your practice) and how to build them into a normal week.

:::why
Two students can study for the same hours and get very different grades. Usually the difference is method, not talent. Rereading feels productive but builds familiarity, not memory. Testing yourself feels harder, and that difficulty is what makes knowledge stay. Change how you study and every hour from now on is worth more.
:::

**Before you start**, read [The preparation road at a glance](#road.overview) so you know where these methods fit. They are the engine of [Phase 2: Learn the content](#road.learn) and [Phase 3: Practise and test yourself](#road.practice).

## What the research says

In 2013 the psychologist John Dunlosky and four colleagues reviewed the research on ten popular learning techniques and rated how useful each one is for real students.

| Rating | Technique | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **High** | Practice testing | Recalling or answering questions without looking |
| **High** | Distributed practice | Spreading study of a topic over days or weeks |
| Moderate | Elaborative interrogation | Asking yourself "why is this true?" |
| Moderate | Self-explanation | Explaining each step of a solution to yourself |
| Moderate | Interleaved practice | Mixing problem types in one session |
| Low | Summarising | Writing shorter versions of a text |
| Low | Highlighting | Marking key words while reading |
| Low | Keyword mnemonics | Linking new words to memorable images |
| Low | Imagery for text | Picturing what you read |
| Low | Rereading | Reading the same notes again |

"Low" does not mean useless; it means a small benefit for the time it takes. Yet rereading and highlighting are the most popular study habits of all. They feel good because familiar words make your brain mistake "I recognise this" for "I know this", an **illusion of competence**. The exam gives you a blank space, not the page, so producing knowledge from memory is what you must practise.

## Retrieval practice: test yourself first

:::def Retrieval practice
Learning by recalling information from memory (for example by answering questions or writing everything you remember on a topic) instead of rereading it. Each successful recall makes the memory stronger and easier to find later.
:::

Retrieval works even when you get answers wrong, as long as you check and correct them straight away. Good ways to retrieve:

- **Blank-page recall ("blurting").** Close your notes and write everything you remember about a topic for 10 minutes. Then fill the gaps from your notes in a different colour. The coloured parts are your revision list.
- **Chapter questions.** Do the practice questions at the end of each chapter here without looking back.
- **Flashcards** (see below) and **past-paper questions**, which are retrieval in the exact form the exam uses. See [Phase 4: Past papers and mark schemes](#road.pastpapers).

:::tip Turn your notes into questions
After you finish a section, write three questions it answers in the margin, for example "What does the discriminant tell you?" Next time, cover the notes and answer the questions. Your notes become a self-test instead of something to reread.
:::

## Spacing: little and often beats one long session

:::def Spaced practice
Spreading study of the same material over several sessions separated by gaps of days or weeks, instead of doing it all at once. Also called distributed practice.
:::

Memory fades quickly after you first learn something, and each recall after a gap makes it fade more slowly. A useful rule of thumb is to review a new topic about **1 day, 3 days, 1 week, 2 weeks and 1 month** after first learning it. The exact gaps matter less than the habit of coming back.

The opposite is **cramming**: everything in one long session just before a test. It can get you through tomorrow's test, but most of it is gone weeks later, and A Levels examine two years of content at the end.

:::warn Rewatching is rereading
Watching the same video lesson twice is the video version of rereading. Watch it once, carefully, then close it and test yourself. Rewatch only the part you could not recall.
:::

## Interleaving: mix your practice

:::def Interleaving
Mixing different topics or types of problem within one practice session (for example differentiation, vectors and probability), so that you must decide which method each question needs.
:::

When you do twenty questions on one method in a row (**blocked practice**), you know the method before you read the question. In the exam, nobody tells you, and interleaving trains that choice. Use blocked practice for a brand-new topic, then switch to mixed sets once the basic questions feel easy.

## Making ideas stick: elaboration, examples and pictures

**Elaboration** means connecting a new idea to what you already know by asking "why?" and "how?". Why does a higher temperature speed up a reaction? How does that link to activation energy? Every connection is another route your memory can use.

**Concrete examples** make abstract ideas easy to picture. "Price elastic demand" is hard to remember; "when one brand of crisps raises its price, many buyers switch brands" is easy. Collect one or two examples of your own for every definition.

**Dual coding** means combining words with visuals. Draw blood flow through the heart, a supply and demand diagram or a flowchart of protein synthesis, and label it from memory. Words and pictures give you two routes to the same idea.

## Worked examples, then fade them

When you are a beginner, solving problems alone can overload you, so study a **worked example** first. Then remove the support step by step:

| Stage | What you do |
|---|---|
| 1. Study | Read a full worked example. Explain each step to yourself: why this step, why now? |
| 2. Complete | Try a similar example where the last steps are hidden. Finish it. |
| 3. Solo | Do a similar question with no help, then check against a full solution. |
| 4. Mixed | Do the same type of question inside a mixed set, under time pressure. |

Once you reach stage 4 easily, stop studying examples for that topic and spend your time on questions.

## The Feynman technique

The physicist Richard Feynman was famous for explaining hard ideas in simple words. This technique finds exactly where your understanding breaks.

:::steps
1. Choose one concept, for example "moles" or "opportunity cost".
2. Explain it out loud or on paper as if teaching a younger student. Use plain words and an example.
3. Notice where you hesitate, use jargon you cannot explain, or say "it just is". Those are your gaps.
4. Go back to your notes or the chapter and fix those gaps only.
5. Explain it again, more simply. Finish with the exact syllabus definition.
:::

:::tip If English is your second language
Explain the idea first in your own language to check you understand it. Then explain it again in English, using the precise syllabus terms. Examiners reward the correct technical words, so collect them in a personal word list with a short meaning next to each.
:::

## Spaced-repetition flashcards

Flashcards combine retrieval and spacing. A **spaced-repetition** app such as **Anki** (free on computers, Android and the web) shows each card just before you are likely to forget it: cards you know appear rarely, and cards you miss come back soon.

- **One fact per card.** "State the units of the Planck constant" is a good card; "quantum physics" is not.
- **Make your own cards.** Writing them is learning, and shared decks often do not match your syllabus.
- **Use cards for exact facts:** definitions, equations, units, reagents and conditions. Use diagrams and questions for processes and problem-solving.
- **Review every day,** and add only about 10 to 20 new cards a day so reviews do not pile up.

On paper, use the **Leitner system**: review box 1 daily, box 2 every three days and box 3 weekly. A correct card moves up a box; a wrong card goes back to box 1.

## Focus: your phone is the biggest problem

Every time you check a message, it takes minutes to regain full concentration, and a phone on the desk distracts you even when it is silent.

- Put the phone in another room, or use a focus mode that blocks apps while you study.
- Choose one specific task before you start: "Questions 1 to 6 on the discriminant", not "do maths".
- Study at the same place and time when you can. Routine makes starting easier.

The **Pomodoro technique** makes this easy: 25 minutes of full focus, then a 5-minute break; after four rounds, a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes. ## Maths and science versus essay subjects

The same principles apply everywhere, but what you practise is different.

| Subject type | Main thing to practise | Best retrieval tool | Typical trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics | Many questions, in mixed sets | Questions with worked answers | Reading solutions before trying |
| Physics and Chemistry | Definitions, equations, calculations, practical skills | Flashcards; past-paper questions | Learning calculations but not explanations |
| Biology | Precise terms, processes, data analysis | Diagrams from memory; long answers | Vague words such as "it affects" |
| Business and Economics | Definitions, diagrams, evaluation | Timed paragraph plans; case studies | Describing the case instead of analysing it |

For essay subjects, write full answers regularly and compare them with the mark scheme's levels; see [Writing top-band business answers](#bus.writing).

## Studying with friends

Group study works when everyone tests and explains, and fails when it turns into chatting. Keep groups to two to four people, agree a topic and a finishing time, and use the time to quiz each other, take turns teaching a topic (the Feynman technique with a real audience), mark each other's answers with the official mark scheme, or compare essay plans. If you study alone as a private candidate, an online study partner gives the same benefits.

## Using AI tools responsibly

AI chat tools can be excellent tutors or terrible ones. The rule: **use AI to explain and to check, never to do the practice for you.** Learning happens when your own brain retrieves and works things out. If a tool does that work, you get none of the benefit, and you still meet the exam alone.

| Good uses | Bad uses |
|---|---|
| "Explain the discriminant in a different way, with an example." | "Solve these ten homework questions for me." |
| "Here is my answer and the mark scheme. Which marks did I miss?" | Copying an AI answer into your notes without trying first |
| "Quiz me on Group 2 trends, one question at a time." | Trusting AI calculations or facts without checking |
| "Translate this sentence from the question into simpler English." | Submitting AI-written work in coursework or applications |

:::warn AI can be confidently wrong
AI tools sometimes give wrong facts, wrong numbers or answers for a different syllabus, in a confident tone. Check against your syllabus, a trusted textbook or the mark scheme. In coursework or non-exam assessment, presenting AI-generated work as your own is malpractice and can lead to disqualification.
:::

## A sample weekly schedule

Here is a realistic week for a student taking Mathematics, Physics and Chemistry, with about two hours of independent study on weekdays. Each block is two Pomodoros (about 50 minutes). Swap in your own subjects; the structure is what matters. For a plan built around your exam dates, use [Build your personal study plan](#road.planner).

| Day | Block 1 | Block 2 | Short review (15 min) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | Maths: learn a new topic | Physics: questions on last week's topic | Flashcards |
| Tuesday | Chemistry: learn a new topic | Maths: questions on Monday's topic | Flashcards + blank-page recall of Monday's maths |
| Wednesday | Physics: learn a new topic | Chemistry: questions on Tuesday's topic | Flashcards |
| Thursday | Maths: mixed question set | Physics: questions on Wednesday's topic | Flashcards + update error log |
| Friday | Chemistry: mixed question set | Maths: redo last week's wrong questions | Flashcards |
| Saturday | One timed past-paper section (rotate the subject each week) | Mark it with the mark scheme | Add mistakes to error log |
| Sunday | Rest | Plan next week (10 minutes) | Optional light flashcards |

Each subject returns several times (spacing), new content is tested on a later day (retrieval), Thursday and Friday mix topics (interleaving), and Saturday is a test. Sunday's rest protects your motivation; see [Motivation, sleep and stress](#road.wellbeing).

@summary Knowing the content is half the job; the other half is turning it into marks. This chapter covers reading questions, timing, working, accuracy and units, and how to answer every type of question, from multiple choice to essays and practical papers.

:::why
Examiners can only mark what is on the page. Every year, examiner reports repeat the same message: many candidates lose marks they could have earned by misreading the question, leaving out units, rounding too early or running out of time. These are skills, and skills can be practised. The habits in this chapter are worth a grade or more to many students.
:::

**Before you start**, it helps to know what each instruction word means: see [Command words decoded](#road.commands). Practise every habit here with real papers, as described in [Phase 4: Past papers and mark schemes](#road.pastpapers).

## Read the question, then read it again

Most lost marks start in the first ten seconds, when a student sees a familiar topic and starts writing the answer to a question they expected, not the one printed. Use the same routine for every question:

:::steps
1. Read the whole question, including any introduction, data or diagram. Information is there because you need it.
2. Underline the **command word** (describe, explain, calculate, evaluate…) and the **topic**.
3. Circle any **conditions**: "two reasons", "exact", "to 3 significant figures", "with reference to the data", "using your answer to (a)".
4. Look at the **marks** and plan that many separate points.
5. Answer, then reread the question to check you answered what was asked.
:::

## Marks tell you how much to write and how long to spend

As a rough guide, **one mark needs one correct, separate point**. A 3-mark "explain" question needs three linked steps of reasoning, not one long sentence. The number of answer lines is a second clue: if you have filled two lines for a 6-mark question, you have probably missed something.

Marks also set your time budget. Divide the time by the total marks:

| Cambridge paper | Time | Marks | Time per mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics 9709 Paper 1 or 3 | 1 h 50 min | 75 | about 1.5 min |
| Mathematics 9709 Paper 4, 5 or 6 | 1 h 15 min | 50 | 1.5 min |
| Sciences Paper 1 (multiple choice) | 1 h 15 min | 40 questions | about 1 min 50 s per question |
| Sciences Paper 2 (AS structured) | 1 h 15 min | 60 | 1.25 min |
| Sciences Paper 4 (A Level structured) | 2 h | 100 | 1.2 min |
| Business 9609 Paper 2 | 1 h 30 min | 60 | 1.5 min |
| Economics 9708 Paper 2 | 2 h | 60 | 2 min |

So an 8-mark maths question deserves about 12 minutes. If you have spent 20, move on and come back later.

:::tip Write checkpoints on the front page
At the start, write two or three checkpoint times, for example "halfway marks by 10:00". A quick glance at the clock then tells you whether you are on pace, without constant calculation.
:::

## Show your working

In mathematics and in science calculations, many marks are for **method**. In mark schemes, M marks are for a correct method, A marks are for accuracy and depend on the method mark, and B marks are independent. If your final answer is wrong but your method is visible, you can still earn many of the marks. A bare wrong answer earns nothing.

Examiners also use **error carried forward** (often called "follow through"): if you make a mistake in part (a) and then use your wrong answer correctly in part (b), you can usually still earn the part (b) marks. That only works if they can see what you did.

Use the same pattern every time: **formula, substitution, answer with unit**.

\[E_k = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.50 \times 4.0^2 = 4.0\,\mathrm{J}\]

:::exam "Show that" questions
When the answer is printed in the question, the marks are for the route, not the result. Every step must be visible, with no jumps. Then use the given result in the next part, even if you could not prove it: the later marks are still available.
:::

## Significant figures, units and rounding

- **Do not round in the middle of a calculation.** Keep the full value on your calculator (use the memory or ANS key) and round only the final answer.
- **Mathematics:** give answers to 3 significant figures unless told otherwise, and angles in degrees to 1 decimal place. If the question says **exact**, leave answers as surds, fractions or in terms of \(\pi\) or \(\ln\).
- **Sciences:** give the final answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data, usually 2 or 3. Never fewer.
- **Units:** write a unit on every final answer and check the prefix. A missing or wrong unit often costs a mark on its own.

:::warn Early rounding changes the answer
A ball of mass \(0.65\,\mathrm{kg}\) falls \(1.8\,\mathrm{m}\) from rest. Take \(g = 9.81\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-2}}\). Its speed is \(v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.81 \times 1.8} = 5.9427\ldots\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). If you round \(v\) to 5.9 and then calculate \(\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2\), you get \(11.3\,\mathrm{J}\). Keeping the full value gives \(11.5\,\mathrm{J}\), which matches \(mgh = 0.65 \times 9.81 \times 1.8\). One early rounding lost the accuracy mark.
:::

## Multiple choice

Cambridge science Paper 1 has **40 questions in 1 hour 15 minutes**: about 1 minute 50 seconds each. Economics Papers 1 and 3 are also multiple choice. Edexcel IAL and the UK boards mix multiple-choice questions into some written papers instead.

- **Answer before you look.** Cover the options, work out your answer, then find it. This protects you from attractive wrong options.
- **Eliminate.** Cross out options you know are wrong. With two options left, even a guess has a 50% chance.
- **Watch for NOT, FALSE and INCORRECT,** usually in bold. Underline them.
- **Check powers of ten and units.** Wrong options are often your answer with a factor of 10, 1000 or 2 missing.
- **Never leave a blank.** There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so guess any question you cannot do.
- **Transfer carefully.** If the paper uses a separate answer sheet, check every answer is on the correct line.

## Structured questions

Structured questions are split into parts: (a), (b)(i), (b)(ii). They usually build on each other, so an answer to (a) is often a hint for (b). Keep answers precise: use the technical term, give one idea per mark, and do not copy out the question. "With reference to the data" or "use the information" means quote figures from the question, with units, in your answer.

## Extended writing

For long answers in any subject, build each paragraph with **Point, Evidence, Explain**: make one clear point, support it with evidence or data, and explain why it matters to the question.

In Business and Economics, the mark scheme rewards four skills in order: **knowledge** (correct terms and theory), **application** (using the business or economy in the case), **analysis** (chains of reasoning: "this means that… so…") and **evaluation** (a supported judgement). The top levels require evaluation, so a long, accurate answer with no judgement cannot reach full marks. See [Writing top-band business answers](#bus.writing).

## Data, graphs and diagrams

When you **describe data**, give the overall trend, quote key values with units, describe any change in the rate of change, and point out anomalies. "Sales rose from 1200 to 1800 units, an increase of 50%" earns more than "sales went up".

When you **draw a graph**:

- label both axes with quantity and unit, for example "time / s";
- choose a scale so that the points cover at least half the grid in each direction;
- plot points with small, precise crosses;
- draw a single best-fit line or smooth curve, never dot-to-dot;
- find a gradient using a large triangle, and show the values you read.

In Business and Economics diagrams, label the axes (price, quantity), every curve (D₁, D₂, S), the equilibrium points, and show shifts with arrows. Then refer to the diagram in your writing.

## Practical and planning papers (Cambridge Papers 3 and 5)

Cambridge Physics, Chemistry and Biology each have two practical-skills papers.

| | Paper 3: Advanced Practical Skills (AS) | Paper 5: Planning, Analysis and Evaluation (A Level) |
|---|---|---|
| Format | 2 h, 40 marks, hands-on in a laboratory | 1 h 15 min, 30 marks, written |
| What you do | Follow instructions, take measurements, record, graph, calculate | Plan an experiment, analyse given data, evaluate conclusions |
| Key marks | Tables with "quantity / unit" headings; readings to the precision of the instrument; repeats; good graphs; specific improvements | Clear variables (independent, dependent, controlled); a workable method; safety; how to analyse (what to plot); uncertainties and error bars |

In Paper 3, improvements must be specific: "use a video camera with a timer to measure the oscillation time" earns marks, "be more careful" never does. Edexcel IAL tests practical skills in written papers instead (Units 3 and 6). The subject guides go further: [Physics practical skills](#phys.practical), [Chemistry practical skills](#chem.practical) and [Biology practical skills](#bio.practical).

## Checking and getting unstuck

Leave about 5 to 10 minutes at the end. Check that every question has an answer, every final answer has a unit and the right significant figures, and that each answer matches its command word. Ask "is this sensible?": a car is not travelling at \(3000\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\).

When you are stuck:

- Mark the question, move on, and come back with fresh eyes.
- Write what you know: the relevant formula, a definition, a labelled diagram. These can earn marks on their own.
- Use every piece of given information; if you have not used something, that is probably the key.
- Write inside the answer space. If you need more room, use the extra page provided and label the question number clearly.

## Common ways marks are lost

| Mistake | Fix |
|---|---|
| Describing when asked to explain | Underline the command word before writing |
| Missing or wrong units | Write the unit with every final answer |
| Rounding too early | Keep full values; round only at the end |
| Bare answers with no working | Formula, substitution, answer |
| Vague words such as "it affects" | State the direction and size of the change |
| Ignoring the context in a case study | Use the business's name and its data |
| One-sided evaluation | Weigh both sides, then make a justified judgement |
| Running out of time | Use your time per mark and checkpoints |

## Weak and strong answers compared

**A science "explain" question.** *Explain why the rate of a reaction increases when the temperature is increased.* [3 marks]

:::cols
:::col Weak answer (about 1 mark)
The particles move faster, so they collide more and the reaction goes faster.

This repeats the question ("goes faster") and never mentions activation energy or successful collisions.
:::
:::col Strong answer (3 marks)
At a higher temperature the particles have more kinetic energy. [B1] So a greater proportion of collisions have energy equal to or greater than the activation energy. [B1] Therefore the frequency of successful collisions increases. [B1]
:::
:::

**A business "evaluate" question.** *Leyla's Bakery made a net profit of $18,000 last year. Leyla needs $40,000 to open a second shop. Evaluate whether she should finance it with a bank loan.* [12 marks]

:::cols
:::col Weak paragraph
A bank loan is good because you get money quickly and do not lose control. But you have to pay interest. Overall she should take the loan.

Correct knowledge, but nothing about Leyla's bakery, no chain of reasoning, and a judgement with no reason.
:::
:::col Strong paragraph
A loan lets Leyla keep full ownership and control (**knowledge**). Her $18,000 profit suggests she could afford repayments from current earnings (**application**). However, a new shop may take months to build sales, while repayments are fixed, so cash flow could turn negative and put both shops at risk (**analysis**). A loan is the better choice only if her forecast for the new shop is realistic and she keeps a cash reserve; if sales are uncertain, a partner sharing the risk may be safer (**evaluation**).
:::
:::

The strong paragraph is not much longer. It earns more because every sentence does a job: it uses the case, builds a chain of reasoning and ends with a judgement that depends on clear conditions.

@level ref

@time 30 minutes to read; keep it open while you practise
@summary Every exam question starts with an instruction word such as describe, explain or evaluate, and each one asks for a different kind of answer. This reference decodes the Cambridge command words and the special phrases used in maths papers.

:::why
The command word tells you what kind of answer earns the marks. A perfect description scores almost nothing if the question said "explain", and a long, accurate essay cannot reach the top band if it was asked to "evaluate" and never makes a judgement. Learning these words is one of the fastest ways to raise your marks without learning any new content.
:::

Use this page alongside [Exam technique](#road.technique). Cambridge prints the official list of command words in each syllabus; check the one for your subject, because not every word is used in every subject.

## Three families of command words

It helps to sort command words by how much thinking they demand.

| Family | Words | What the answer looks like |
|---|---|---|
| **Recall** | define, state, give, identify, outline, summarise, describe | Facts, features or trends, with no reasons needed |
| **Apply and reason** | calculate, determine, deduce, predict, show (that), sketch, demonstrate, compare, contrast, examine, analyse, explain, suggest | Working, links and reasons; using knowledge in a new situation |
| **Judge** | assess, evaluate, discuss, justify, comment, consider, develop | Arguments weighed against each other and a supported conclusion |

## The Cambridge command words

| Command word | What it means | What examiners expect | Example stem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Analyse** | Break a topic into parts and show how they connect | Chains of reasoning (cause, effect, consequence) using the data or case | Analyse how a fall in interest rates could affect a car manufacturer. |
| **Assess** | Weigh something up and reach a reasoned judgement | Points on both sides, then a judgement about importance or extent | Assess the importance of cash flow forecasting for a new business. |
| **Calculate** | Work out a numerical answer | Working shown; final answer with unit and sensible significant figures | Calculate the resistance of the wire. |
| **Comment** | Give an informed opinion on the information | A judgement supported by reasons or data, not just a description | Comment on the reliability of these results. |
| **Compare** | Point out similarities and differences | Both things in every point, with words such as "whereas" and "both" | Compare the structures of DNA and RNA. |
| **Consider** | Think about the given information and respond to it | A response that uses the information, often weighing factors | Consider whether the firm should accept the new order. |
| **Contrast** | Point out differences only | Paired differences: "X is…, whereas Y is…" | Contrast the properties of simple molecular and giant covalent substances. |
| **Deduce** | Reach a conclusion from the information given | The conclusion plus the step of logic from the data | Use the data to deduce the order of reaction with respect to A. |
| **Define** | Give the exact meaning of a term | Precise syllabus wording; an example alone is not a definition | Define specific heat capacity. |
| **Demonstrate** | Show how something works, or give an example | A clear illustration, worked case or example | Demonstrate how a price cut could reduce total revenue. |
| **Describe** | Say what something is like or what happens | Main features, steps or trends, with figures from data; no reasons needed | Describe the trend shown in Fig. 2.1. |
| **Determine** | Find an answer using the information available | A value obtained from data, a graph or a calculation, with method | Use the graph to determine the gradient. |
| **Develop** | Take an idea further or build on given information | Extra depth: the next step, consequence or detail | Using the data, develop a plan to reduce staff turnover. |
| **Discuss** | Write about an issue in depth, in a structured way | Arguments for and against, analysed, usually with a conclusion | Discuss whether economic growth always raises living standards. |
| **Evaluate** | Judge the quality, importance or value of something | Strengths and weaknesses weighed, "it depends on…", a justified final judgement | Evaluate the usefulness of ratio analysis to an investor. |
| **Examine** | Look closely and in detail at an issue | Detailed investigation of causes, assumptions and effects | Examine the causes of the fall in exports. |
| **Explain** | Give reasons (why) and mechanisms (how) | Cause-and-effect links with "because", "so", "therefore" | Explain why the current in the circuit decreases. |
| **Give** | Produce an answer from the source or from memory | A short answer; no explanation | Give two examples of fixed costs. |
| **Identify** | Name, pick out or recognise | A word or phrase, often from a diagram or data | Identify the structure labelled X. |
| **Justify** | Support a choice or conclusion with evidence | Reasons and evidence for the answer you gave | Justify your choice of indicator for this titration. |
| **Outline** | Set out the main points briefly | Key points with little detail | Outline the stages of the product life cycle. |
| **Predict** | Say what is likely to happen, from information or a pattern | A specific outcome, sometimes with a brief reason | Predict the effect on the position of equilibrium of increasing the pressure. |
| **Show (that)** | Give structured evidence that leads to a given result | Every step visible, ending exactly at the given result | Show that the speed of the ball is about \(6\,\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\). |
| **Sketch** | Draw a simple diagram or graph of the key features | Correct shape, labelled axes and key points; not to scale | Sketch a graph to show how enzyme activity varies with pH. |
| **State** | Express clearly and briefly | A fact, value or name; no explanation | State the unit of electric charge. |
| **Suggest** | Apply knowledge to a new situation with several possible answers | A sensible, reasoned proposal; you are not expected to have learned it | Suggest why the two groups gave different results. |
| **Summarise** | Select and present the main points only | A short overview without detail or examples | Summarise the main findings shown in the table. |

:::warn "Explain" is not "describe"
Describing a graph when asked to explain it is the most common command-word error. A description says **what** happens ("the rate rises, then falls"). An explanation says **why** ("because the active site changes shape"). If your answer has no "because", "so" or "therefore", it is probably a description.
:::

:::tip "Suggest" means you can think, not just recall
"Suggest" questions put you in an unfamiliar situation on purpose. The mark scheme usually accepts several answers. Use your knowledge to propose something sensible, give a short reason, and move on. Do not leave it blank because "we did not learn this".
:::

## Phrases in mathematics papers

Maths papers use a small set of phrases with exact meanings. Misreading them costs marks even when the mathematics is right.

| Phrase | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Find**, **calculate**, **determine** | Work it out and show your method |
| **Write down**, **state** | The answer can be seen with little or no working; usually 1 mark |
| **Show that** | The answer is given: every step must be shown, ending exactly at the printed result |
| **Prove** | A complete general argument, every step justified, ending with a conclusion; examples are not proof |
| **Hence** | You must use the previous result; a different method may score nothing |
| **Hence or otherwise** | Using the previous result is the intended quick route, but any correct method earns full marks |
| **Exact** | No calculator decimals: leave surds, fractions, \(\pi\), \(e\) or \(\ln\) in the answer |
| **Sketch** | Correct shape with key features labelled (intercepts, turning points, asymptotes); not to scale |
| **Plot**, **draw accurately** | Calculate points and mark them precisely on a grid |
| **Verify** | Substitute the given values to confirm a result, then make a conclusion |
| **In the form…** | Match the form exactly, for example \(a(x + p)^2 + q\) |
| **In terms of** \(k\) | Your answer should contain \(k\) |

:::eg Hence versus hence or otherwise
(a) Show that \(x = 2\) is a root of \(x^3 - 7x + 6 = 0\). (b) **Hence** solve \(x^3 - 7x + 6 = 0\).

(a) \(2^3 - 7(2) + 6 = 8 - 14 + 6 = 0\), so \(x = 2\) is a root. [B1]

(b) "Hence" means use part (a): \((x - 2)\) is a factor. Dividing gives \(x^3 - 7x + 6 = (x - 2)(x^2 + 2x - 3)\). [M1]
\[(x - 2)(x - 1)(x + 3) = 0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x = 2,\; 1 \text{ or } -3\] [A1]

Writing the three roots straight from a calculator's equation solver does not use part (a), so it would not earn the method mark. If the question had said "hence or otherwise", a different valid method with full working would also be accepted.
:::

:::board
Most command words mean the same across boards, but each board publishes its own list and wording, so read the one for your specification.

- **Edexcel and AQA** often write "To what extent…?" or "Assess…" in Business and Economics. Treat both as evaluation: the top marks need a supported judgement.
- **AQA and OCR** also use "Name" (a word or phrase only) and "Complete" (fill in a table or diagram).
- **Edexcel Mathematics** papers sometimes print "Solutions relying on calculator technology are not acceptable": you must show algebraic working.
- **OCR Mathematics** uses "In this question you must show detailed reasoning": an answer without full working scores nothing.
- **AQA Mathematics** uses "Fully justify your answer": each step needs a reason.
:::

## Describe, explain and evaluate on one topic

The clearest way to see the difference is to answer three questions on the same data.

:::eg One set of data, three command words
A student measured the volume of oxygen released per minute by the enzyme catalase at different temperatures.

| Temperature / °C | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rate / cm³ min⁻¹ | 3.1 | 5.8 | 9.9 | 12.4 | 6.2 | 0.8 |

**Describe the results.** The rate increases from \(3.1\,\mathrm{cm^3\,min^{-1}}\) at 10 °C to a maximum of \(12.4\,\mathrm{cm^3\,min^{-1}}\) at 40 °C. Above 40 °C it falls steeply, to \(0.8\,\mathrm{cm^3\,min^{-1}}\) at 60 °C. (Trend, key values, units; no reasons.)

**Explain the results.** Up to 40 °C, enzyme and substrate molecules gain kinetic energy, so they collide more often and more enzyme–substrate complexes form per minute. Above the optimum, vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds in the enzyme, so its tertiary structure and the shape of the active site change. The substrate is no longer complementary to it, so fewer complexes form: the enzyme is denatured. (Reasons and mechanisms, linked with "so".)

**Evaluate the conclusion that the optimum temperature is 40 °C.** The data support an optimum near 40 °C, because that reading is the highest. However, readings were taken only every 10 °C, so the true optimum could lie anywhere between 30 °C and 50 °C, and with one reading at each temperature, an anomaly could not be spotted. Repeating each reading three times, at intervals of 2 °C between 30 °C and 50 °C, would make the conclusion more reliable. Overall, the conclusion is reasonable but only approximate. (Strengths, limitations, improvement, judgement.)
:::

:::quiz
? Which command word requires you to reach a supported judgement?
- Describe
- Outline
+ Evaluate
- State
> Evaluate means weighing strengths and weaknesses and reaching a justified conclusion. The others ask for facts or features only.
? A question says "Hence or otherwise, solve the equation." What does this mean?
- You must use the previous part, or you get no marks
+ The previous part gives the intended route, but any correct method with working earns full marks
- You must not use the previous part
- You only need to write down the answer
> "Hence" alone forces you to use the previous result; "hence or otherwise" allows any valid method.
? What does "sketch" require in a maths or science question?
+ The correct shape, with labelled axes and key features, not necessarily to scale
- An accurate plot of calculated points on graph paper
- A written description of the graph
- A graph drawn with a ruler through every point
> A sketch shows the shape and key features (intercepts, maxima, asymptotes). Plotting points accurately is "plot" or "draw".
? Which answer best fits "Suggest why the two groups gave different results"?
- "We did not study this."
- A word-for-word definition from the syllabus
+ A sensible reason based on your knowledge, briefly justified
- A description of both sets of results
> "Suggest" puts you in an unfamiliar situation. The mark scheme accepts a range of sensible, reasoned answers.
:::

:::recap
- The command word decides what kind of answer earns marks: recall, apply and reason, or judge.
- Describe says what happens; explain says why and how; evaluate weighs evidence and reaches a judgement.
- State, give, identify and write down need short answers with no explanation.
- Assess, evaluate, discuss and "to what extent" need both sides and a justified conclusion.
- Suggest means apply your knowledge to something unfamiliar: several answers can score.
- In maths, "hence" forces the previous result, "hence or otherwise" allows any method, "exact" means no decimals, and "show that" needs every step.
- Sketch means shape and key features; plot means accurate points on a grid.
- Each board publishes its own list: check the one in your syllabus or specification.
:::

@level guide

@time 30 minutes to read
@summary Two years of A Level study is a marathon, not a sprint. This chapter shows how to build habits that keep you going, beat procrastination, sleep and move for better memory, handle exam nerves and burnout, and where to get help.

:::why
Your brain is the tool you study with. Looking after it is part of studying, not a break from it. Students who sleep well, move every day and take real rest usually learn more in fewer hours than students who study until they are exhausted. None of this is about being "strong". It is about working with your body instead of against it.
:::

This chapter supports every phase of the road, from [Phase 2: Learn the content](#road.learn) to [Phase 5: Final revision](#road.final). Pair it with [Study methods that actually work](#road.methods).

## Motivation follows action

Many students wait to feel motivated before they start. But motivation is unreliable: it rises and falls with mood, sleep and the weather. The good news is that it usually **follows** action. Start a small task, make some progress, and the motivation to continue arrives.

So do not rely on motivation. Rely on **habits** and **process goals**.

:::def Process goal
A goal about an action you control, such as "do ten mixed maths questions every weekday", rather than about a result you cannot fully control, such as "get an A*" (an outcome goal).
:::

| Outcome goal (the destination) | Process goals (what you do each day) |
|---|---|
| A* in Mathematics | Ten mixed questions every weekday; one timed paper every Saturday |
| A in Chemistry | 15 minutes of flashcards daily; one past-paper question per topic learned |
| A place to study medicine | Read one article on health science each week; prepare for the admissions test 3 hours a week |

Outcome goals give direction. Process goals give you something to do today, and you can tick them off, which feels good and builds momentum.

## Building habits

A habit is something you do with little thought because it is attached to a regular cue. To build a study habit:

- **Link it to a cue.** Use a specific plan: "After dinner, at my desk, I will do 20 minutes of flashcards." Plans in the form "when X, I will do Y" are much more likely to happen than "I will study more".
- **Make it small.** Start with a version so easy you cannot say no, even five minutes. You can increase it once the habit is fixed.
- **Make it visible.** Mark each day you do it on a calendar. A growing chain of marks is motivating.
- **Never miss twice.** Missing one day is normal. Missing two starts a new habit of not doing it.

## Beating procrastination

Procrastination is not laziness. It is usually a way of avoiding a bad feeling: a task feels too big, too vague, too boring or too frightening. Fix the feeling and the task becomes easier to start.

| Why you are avoiding it | What to do |
|---|---|
| It feels too big ("revise organic chemistry") | Break it down; do only the first 25-minute piece |
| It is vague | Write the exact first action: "open the June paper, question 1" |
| You fear doing badly | Treat the first try as practice; mistakes show you what to fix |
| Something more fun is nearby | Phone in another room; block distracting sites |
| You are tired | Drink water and walk for 10 minutes, then start; if you are exhausted, rest properly and plan the morning |

:::tip The five-minute rule
Tell yourself you will work for just five minutes and then you are allowed to stop. Starting is the hardest part. Most of the time, once five minutes have passed, you will keep going.
:::

## Sleep: when memories are saved

Sleep is not wasted study time. While you sleep, your brain **consolidates** what you learned during the day: it strengthens new memories and links them to old ones. Students who cut sleep to study more often remember less of what they studied.

Teenagers need roughly **8 to 10 hours** of sleep a night. Teenage body clocks also run later than adults', so falling asleep early can be hard. The most useful habit is a **regular wake-up time**, kept within about an hour on weekends too.

- Stop using screens 30 to 60 minutes before bed, and charge your phone outside the bedroom.
- Avoid caffeine (coffee, strong tea, energy drinks) from the early afternoon: it stays in your body for many hours.
- Keep your room cool, dark and quiet.
- If you nap, keep it to about 20 minutes and not late in the day.

:::warn All-nighters do not work
Staying up all night before an exam damages exactly what the exam tests: recall, reasoning and concentration. The night before an exam, a full night's sleep usually does more for your marks than a few extra hours of revision.
:::

## Exercise and food

The World Health Organization recommends that young people aged 5 to 17 do an average of **60 minutes a day** of moderate to vigorous physical activity. Exercise improves mood, sleep and concentration. You do not need a gym: a brisk walk, a game of football or a bike ride all count. A 10 to 20 minute walk is also one of the best breaks between study blocks.

There are no magic "brain foods". What helps is ordinary good eating: regular meals including breakfast, plenty of water, fruit and vegetables, and not living on sugar and energy drinks, which give a short boost followed by a dip. On exam days, eat a familiar breakfast and take water into the exam if your centre allows it.

## Exam nerves: making them work for you

Some nervousness before an exam is normal and useful: it makes you alert. It becomes a problem when it blocks your thinking. These techniques are simple and backed by evidence.

- **Slow breathing.** Breathe in for a count of 4 and out for a count of 6 to 8. A longer out-breath calms your heart rate. Repeat five times.
- **Rename the feeling.** A racing heart feels the same whether you are anxious or excited. Telling yourself "this is energy that will help me focus" can improve performance.
- **Write the worry down.** In a well-known 2011 study, anxious students who spent ten minutes writing about their worries just before an exam scored better than those who did not. Write the worry, then put it away.
- **Practise under real conditions.** Timed past papers at a desk make the real exam feel familiar, and familiar situations are less frightening.

:::steps
1. If your mind goes blank in the exam, put your pen down.
2. Take three slow breaths, with a long out-breath.
3. Move to a question you know you can do, and do it.
4. Come back to the hard question and write anything relevant: a formula, a definition, a diagram.
:::

## Perfectionism

High standards are good. **Perfectionism** is different: it means feeling that anything less than perfect is failure. Warning signs include spending hours making notes beautiful, not starting until conditions are perfect, avoiding past papers in case you score badly, and feeling worthless after one poor mark.

Remember that top grades never require perfection. Grade thresholds are set below full marks, so you can drop marks on every paper and still reach an A*. Early past-paper scores are supposed to be low: they show you what to fix. Aim for progress, keep notes "good enough", and talk to yourself the way you would talk to a friend who made the same mistake.

## Burnout: know the signs

**Burnout** is exhaustion that comes from too much pressure for too long. Watch for:

- tiredness that a night's sleep does not fix;
- dreading study, or feeling "what is the point?";
- working longer hours but achieving less;
- irritability, headaches, stomach aches or getting ill often.

If you notice these, the answer is not to push harder. Reduce your workload for a week, protect your sleep, take a full day off, and talk to someone. Then adjust your plan to be sustainable.

:::analogy
Athletes do not get fitter during training; they get fitter while they recover from it. Training without rest leads to injury. Study is the same: the learning settles during rest and sleep.
:::

## Rest is part of the plan

Schedule rest the way you schedule study. Take at least one full day, or two half-days, off each week. Make breaks real: a walk, a meal with family, sport or a hobby, not an hour of scrolling. Keep seeing friends. During school holidays, lighten the load for a few days before building up again.

## When to get help

Everyone has bad days. But talk to someone if worry, low mood or poor sleep lasts more than about two weeks, if you have panic attacks, if you stop eating properly, or if stress stops you doing normal things.

- **A trusted adult:** a parent, relative or teacher you feel comfortable with.
- **Your school counsellor or guidance teacher**, if you are at a school. Private candidates can ask their exam centre or tutor who to contact.
- **A doctor:** your family doctor can help with sleep problems, anxiety and low mood, and can refer you to specialist support.

If your exam performance is affected by illness or a serious event, your exam centre can apply to the board for **special consideration**. If you have a diagnosed condition, **access arrangements** such as extra time must be applied for in advance through your centre. See [Phase 6: Exam day, results and resits](#road.examday).

:::warn If you are in danger
If you ever think about harming yourself, or feel unsafe, tell someone straight away. Call emergency services (112 in Turkey and across Europe; 999 or 112 in the UK) or go to the nearest hospital emergency department. In the UK and Ireland you can also call Samaritans free, at any time, on 116 123.
:::

:::recap
- Motivation follows action: start small and rely on habits and process goals, not feelings.
- Attach study to a cue ("when X, I will do Y"), make it small, track it, and never miss twice.
- Beat procrastination by making the first step tiny and specific, and use the five-minute rule.
- Teenagers need roughly 8 to 10 hours of sleep. Sleep is when memories are consolidated, so never sacrifice it before an exam.
- Aim for about 60 minutes of activity a day, eat regular meals and drink water.
- Calm exam nerves with slow breathing, renaming the feeling, writing worries down and practising under real conditions.
- Perfection is not required for an A*. Watch for burnout signs and schedule real rest every week.
- Talk to a trusted adult, counsellor or doctor if problems last; in an emergency, call 112 (Turkey and Europe) or 999 (UK).
:::

## Textbooks

One good textbook per subject is enough. On each syllabus page, Cambridge lists **endorsed** textbooks, which have been reviewed against that syllabus. For Edexcel IAL, Pearson publishes its own student books for each unit, and the UK boards list approved books for their specifications.

## How to judge a resource

Before you trust a website, video or book, ask:

- **Is it for my board, my syllabus code and the current years?**
- **Who made it?** An exam board, an experienced teacher or a university is more reliable than an anonymous upload.
- **Does it explain why, not just what?** Good resources show full working and reasoning.
- **Does it match the mark scheme?** Compare one of its answers with an official mark scheme.
- **Does it make me active?** Questions and quizzes beat passive watching.

:::tip A small, strong toolkit
For each subject, aim for: the syllabus, one textbook, past papers with mark schemes, one question website, and your own flashcards. That is enough for an A*. More resources usually means less depth.

## Avoid leaked and pirated material

Sometimes social media accounts claim to sell "leaked" papers before an exam. These are almost always scams. If a real paper were involved, using or sharing it would be **malpractice**, and boards can disqualify candidates from a subject or from all their exams. Never buy, download or share them. Equally, do not post or discuss questions online after you sit a paper: candidates in other time zones may not have taken it yet.

Pirated copies of textbooks and paid courses break copyright law, often carry malware, and are frequently old editions. Use the many free and legal resources above, your school or public library, and the official board websites instead.

@summary How to take A Levels without a school: choosing a board that works for private candidates (especially in the sciences), finding an exam centre, entry deadlines, predicted grades and references, a self-study plan, and exam-day logistics.

:::why
Many students take A Levels privately: home-educated students, students whose school does not offer A Levels or a particular subject, students resitting, and adults returning to study. It is entirely possible, and universities treat the results exactly like any others. But without a school, all the organisation is your job. The most common disaster is not a bad exam. It is a missed entry deadline, or a subject that no centre nearby can run.
:::

## What being a private candidate means

:::def Private candidate
A candidate who takes exams at an approved exam centre without being a student of that centre, and who is responsible for their own preparation and exam entries.
:::

:::def Exam centre
A school, college or organisation approved by an exam board to enter candidates for its exams and to run those exams under the board's rules.
:::

You prepare on your own, or with tutors or an online school. You then register with a centre that accepts private candidates. The centre enters you for the exams, supervises them, and passes on your results. You pay the centre's fees.

## Step 1: choose your board

For Mathematics, Business and Economics, every major board assesses you by written exams only, so choose by syllabus, resources and exam dates. Subjects with practical work or coursework need more care, because a centre must supervise that part. **The sciences are the main example**, because of how practical skills are assessed.

| | Cambridge International A Level | Pearson Edexcel International A Level (IAL) | UK A Levels (AQA, OCR, Edexcel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exam series | May/June and October/November | January, May/June and October/November | Usually May/June only |
| Science practical | **Paper 3** is a hands-on laboratory exam; Paper 5 is written | **Units 3 and 6** are written papers testing practical skills | Written papers, plus a separate **practical endorsement** assessed by teachers over the course |
| What a private candidate needs | A centre with a laboratory that will run Paper 3 for private candidates | Any centre that runs IAL exams | A centre willing to supervise the required practical activities |
| Structure | AS can be taken first and carried forward to the full A Level | Modular: units can be sat in different series and resat one at a time | Linear: all exams at the end |

Because the Cambridge science Paper 3 is a practical exam, you must find a centre that has a suitable laboratory and agrees to run it for private candidates, and many centres do not. Edexcel IAL tests practical skills in written papers (Units 3 and 6), so no laboratory session at the centre is needed. This is one reason many private candidates choose Edexcel IAL for the sciences. It is not a shortcut: the written practical papers test planning, measurement, data analysis and the core practicals in detail, so you still need to understand experiments well, ideally by doing some.

For UK A Levels in the sciences, the practical endorsement is reported separately from the grade. Private candidates often find it hard to arrange, and some university science and medicine courses expect a pass.

:::warn Do not mix boards within one subject
An AS result from one board cannot be combined with A Level papers from another board. Choose your board for each subject before you start studying, because syllabuses, textbooks and paper styles differ. You can take different subjects with different boards.
:::

Before you commit, check that the universities and courses you are interested in accept your combination, especially for medicine, where some universities have rules about resits and modular exams.

## Step 2: find a centre

- **In Turkey**, the British Council runs school exams for private candidates in **Istanbul and Ankara**. Some schools registered with Cambridge or Pearson also accept private candidates.
- **Anywhere**, use the centre search tools on the Cambridge International and Pearson websites, contact your local British Council office, and ask international schools near you directly.

Ask every centre the same questions, and keep the answers in writing:

- Do you accept private candidates for my board, subjects and series?
- (Cambridge sciences) Can you run the practical Paper 3 for private candidates?
- What is your entry deadline for the series I want, and is there a late-entry option?
- What are your fees, per subject or component, and are there administration charges?
- What identification do you need, and where exactly will the exams take place?
- Can you arrange access arrangements, such as extra time, if I am eligible?
- How and when will I receive my results and certificates?

## Step 3: entry deadlines and fees

**Centres set their own entry deadlines**, and they are usually much earlier than the board's own deadline, often months before the exams. Late entries, where accepted at all, cost considerably more. Put the deadline in your calendar the day you learn it, with a reminder a month before.

**Fees are set by each centre.** They usually combine the board's entry fee for each subject or component with the centre's own charge, so they vary a lot between centres and countries. Ask for a full written breakdown before you enter.

When you enter, check the exact **entry options**: for example AS only or full A Level, and the right components. With Cambridge, an AS result can be carried forward to the full A Level within 13 months, so you can sit AS in one year and complete the A Level the next.

## Predicted grades and references without a school

When you apply through UCAS, universities expect a **reference** and **predicted grades**. Without a school, you still need someone who knows your academic work.

- **Referee:** a tutor, a former teacher, or the head of an online school who has taught you. Family members and friends must not write it.
- **Predicted grades:** these must come from someone who has seen and marked your work. Sit timed past papers regularly and have a tutor mark them, and keep a dated record of scores. That record is the evidence behind a credible prediction.
- **Apply after your results:** many private candidates apply once their grades are known, often taking a gap year. Your offers are then based on real results, which removes the predicted-grade problem completely.

See [University applications and admissions tests](#road.university) for the full process.

## A self-study plan

Cambridge designs each A Level around roughly **360 guided learning hours** per subject (about 180 for AS). On your own, plan for at least that much, plus revision. Over two years, that means a steady weekly routine, not bursts.

| Stage | Roughly when | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Set up | First month | Choose subjects and boards, download syllabuses, buy textbooks, contact centres |
| AS content | Months 2 to 9 | Learn the AS topics in order, with questions after every chapter |
| AS consolidation | Months 10 to 12 | Timed AS past papers; sit AS exams now if you are taking them separately |
| A Level content | Months 13 to 19 | Learn the A Level topics and keep revisiting AS topics with mixed questions |
| Exam preparation | Months 20 to 24 | Full timed papers, error logs and final revision |

To turn this into a week-by-week plan around your exam dates, use [Build your personal study plan](#road.planner) and the methods in [Study methods that actually work](#road.methods).

## Tutors, online schools and study partners

**Tutors** are most valuable for the things you cannot do alone: marking long answers against the mark scheme, explaining a topic you are stuck on, setting mock exams, and writing a reference. Choose one with experience of your specific board, ask for a trial lesson, and avoid paying large sums in advance.

**Online schools** can provide a full course, regular feedback and a reference. Check what they actually include: most do not enter you for exams, so you still need a centre.

**Study partners** keep you accountable. An online partner taking the same subject can quiz you, compare answers and share the motivation.

## Exam-day logistics

- **Identification:** private candidates normally have to show valid photo identification, such as a passport or national identity card, at every exam. The name must match your entry exactly.
- **Statement of entry:** when the centre sends your list of entries and timetable, check every subject, component, date and the spelling of your name. Report any error immediately.
- **Venue and travel:** the exam may be held at a different address from the centre's office. Visit or plan the route in advance and aim to arrive at least 30 minutes early.
- **Equipment:** black pens, pencil, ruler, eraser and an approved calculator in a clear pencil case. Check the board's calculator rules; calculators with computer algebra are not allowed.
- **Results and certificates:** you receive results through your centre. Certificates arrive later; collect them and keep them safe, because universities and employers may ask for them.

## Your checklist

:::steps
1. Choose your subjects and check that they fit the university courses you are considering.
2. Choose a board for each subject, thinking carefully about the practical papers in the sciences.
3. Download each syllabus and specimen paper, and get one endorsed textbook per subject.
4. Find a centre that accepts private candidates for your subjects (and runs Paper 3 if you choose Cambridge sciences).
5. Write down the centre's entry deadline and fees, and set reminders.
6. Build a weekly study plan, and find a tutor or teacher who can mark your work and act as referee.
7. Enter before the deadline and check your statement of entry carefully.
8. Sit timed mock exams and keep the marked papers as evidence for predicted grades.
9. Prepare identification, travel and equipment for each exam day.
10. Collect your results and certificates, and plan any resits early.
:::

:::recap
- A private candidate studies independently and sits exams at an approved centre that accepts private candidates.
- Cambridge science Paper 3 is a hands-on laboratory exam, so you need a centre that can run it; Edexcel IAL Units 3 and 6 test practical skills in written papers, one reason many private candidates choose IAL for sciences.
- Do not mix boards within one subject; choose before you start.
- Centres set their own entry deadlines, often months ahead, and their own fees. Get everything in writing.
- For UCAS you need a referee who knows your work and evidence-based predicted grades; applying after results avoids predictions.
- Plan for about 360 guided learning hours per subject over the course, plus revision.
- Bring valid photo identification to every exam and check your statement of entry carefully.
:::

## How UCAS works

**UCAS** is the central admissions service for undergraduate degrees in the UK. You make one online application and send it to up to **five choices** (a course at a university). There are two important limits:

- For **medicine, dentistry and veterinary medicine or science**, you can use at most four of your five choices on these courses.
- You can usually apply to **Oxford or Cambridge, but not both**, in the same year.

| Part of the application | What it is |
|---|---|
| Choices | Up to five courses; every university sees the same application |
| Qualifications | Results you already have (for example IGCSE or AS) and those you are taking |
| Personal statement | Your answers to three questions about why and how you have prepared for the subject |
| Reference | Written by a teacher or tutor who knows your academic work |
| Predicted grades | Your referee's professional estimate of your final grades |

If English is not your first language, most universities also ask for an English test such as IELTS Academic, unless they accept another qualification you hold. Check each university's requirements early.

## Deadlines for 2027 entry

| Date | Deadline |
|---|---|
| **15 October 2026**, 6 pm UK time | Oxford, Cambridge, and most courses in medicine, dentistry and veterinary medicine or science |
| **13 January 2027**, 6 pm UK time | Equal-consideration deadline for most other courses |

After 13 January you can still apply until the end of June, but universities do not have to consider late applications equally, and popular courses may be full. After that, places are filled through Clearing. Schools often set an internal deadline weeks earlier so that teachers have time to write references, so ask your referee early.

## The personal statement

From 2026 entry onwards, the personal statement is no longer one free essay. You answer **three questions**:

1. Why do you want to study this course or subject?
2. How have your qualifications and studies helped you to prepare for this course or subject?
3. What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are these experiences useful?

The total limit is **4,000 characters including spaces**, with **at least 350 characters** for each answer. You can share the characters between the questions however suits you. All your choices see the same statement, so write about the subject, not about one university.

| Question | Strong answers include |
|---|---|
| 1. Why this subject? | A specific idea, problem or experience that interests you, and what you did to follow it up |
| 2. Qualifications and studies | Topics from your A Levels that you enjoyed or found challenging, and skills they gave you, linked to the degree |
| 3. Preparation outside education | Reading, competitions, online courses, projects, work or volunteering, and what each taught you |

:::tip Show, then reflect
"I am passionate about physics" tells the reader nothing. "After a lesson on the photoelectric effect I read about how solar cells are designed, and tried to estimate the efficiency of the panel on our roof" shows the interest. Then add one sentence on what you learned from it. Specific detail and reflection are what make a statement convincing.
:::

:::warn Write it yourself
UCAS checks statements for copied text. A statement written by an AI tool or by someone else is not your own work and can put your application at risk. Use feedback from teachers or tutors, but every sentence should be yours.
:::

## References and predicted grades

Your **reference** is written by a teacher or tutor who knows your academic work. It describes your abilities and potential and includes your **predicted grades**: your referee's professional judgement of the grades you are likely to achieve. Universities make offers based on these predictions, so strong, consistent work over the year before you apply matters. Private candidates should read the advice on referees in [Studying as a private candidate](#road.private).

## Offers and replying

Each university replies with one of these decisions:

- **Conditional offer:** a place if you achieve certain results, such as "A*AA including A* in Mathematics".
- **Unconditional offer:** a place with no conditions, usually because you already have your results.
- **Unsuccessful:** no offer.

When all your decisions have arrived, choose a **firm** choice (your first choice) and an **insurance** choice (a back-up with lower conditions), and decline the rest. If you meet your firm offer's conditions, you are committed to it, so only choose a firm offer you really want. Your reply deadline depends on when your last decision arrives, and is shown in your UCAS account.

If you have no offers, **UCAS Extra** lets you add further choices one at a time from late February, and **Clearing** fills remaining places in the summer, especially around results day.

## Admissions tests for 2027 entry

Many competitive courses require a test. Several tests are run by UAT-UK and taken on computer at Pearson VUE test centres, which exist in many countries.

| Test | Used for | Format | When (2027 entry) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ESAT** | Engineering and science courses at Cambridge, Oxford (replacing the PAT), Imperial and UCL | Mathematics 1 plus two modules chosen from Mathematics 2, Physics, Chemistry and Biology; each module 40 minutes, 27 multiple-choice questions | 12–16 October 2026 or 4–8 January 2027 |
| **TMUA** | Mathematics, computer science and economics courses at Cambridge, Oxford (replacing the MAT), Imperial, LSE, Warwick, Durham and UCL | Two 75-minute papers, each with 20 multiple-choice questions; no calculator | 12–16 October 2026 or 4–8 January 2027 |
| **TARA** | Several Oxford courses (replacing the TSA) and some UCL courses | Three 40-minute modules: critical thinking, problem solving and a writing task | 12–16 October 2026 or 4–8 January 2027 |
| **UCAT** | Most UK medical and dental schools, including Oxford and Cambridge (the BMAT no longer runs) | Four timed sections (verbal reasoning, decision making, quantitative reasoning, situational judgement), about 2 hours | Summer before you apply: 13 July to 24 September 2026 (now closed) |
| **LNAT** | Law at Oxford, Cambridge, UCL, KCL, LSE, Durham, Bristol, Glasgow, SOAS and others | 42 multiple-choice questions on reading passages (95 minutes), then one essay (40 minutes) | From 1 September 2026; by 15 October 2026 for Oxford and Cambridge; by 31 December 2026 for KCL, LSE and UCL; by 25 January 2027 for most others (13 January for Bristol and Durham) |
| **STEP** | Mathematics at Cambridge (part of almost all offers); some other universities, such as Warwick, may include it in offers | STEP 2 and STEP 3, long problem-solving papers, run by OCR | June 2027, after you receive an offer |

:::warn Book early, and know which sitting you need
Most Oxford and Cambridge applicants must take ESAT, TMUA or TARA in the **October** sitting; January is mainly for other universities. Booking closes weeks before each sitting (the October 2026 window closed in late September), and booking for January opens in late October 2026. Each test can be taken only once per application year. There is a fee for each test, and bursaries exist for some students; check the official websites for current details.
:::

Prepare with each test's official practice papers, timed and marked like A Level past papers.

## Interviews

Oxford interviews the applicants it shortlists, and Cambridge interviews most of its applicants, usually in **December**; many interviews take place online. They are academic conversations: you will be given unfamiliar problems or texts and asked to think aloud. Interviewers want to see how you reason, not how much you have memorised. Medical schools use panel interviews or **multiple mini interviews** (MMIs), short stations testing communication and judgement, usually between November and March.

To prepare, practise talking through hard problems out loud (Underground Mathematics and Isaac Science are excellent for this), reread your personal statement and be ready to discuss anything in it, and ask a teacher or tutor for a mock interview. For an online interview, test your camera, microphone and connection beforehand and have paper and a pen ready.

## Supercurricular activities and the EPQ

**Supercurricular** activities take your subject beyond the syllabus, unlike extracurricular hobbies. Universities, especially the most selective, value them because they show genuine interest. Examples:

- reading books, articles and popular science about your subject;
- free online courses and recorded university lectures;
- competitions such as the UKMT mathematics challenges, the British Physics Olympiad, or national science olympiads (in Turkey, run by TÜBİTAK);
- essay competitions, summer schools and small research projects;
- for medicine, work experience or volunteering in caring roles.

What matters is reflection: what you learned and how it changed your thinking, not a long list.

The **Extended Project Qualification (EPQ)**, offered by UK boards, is an independent project, usually a dissertation of about 5,000 words or an artefact with a report. It is worth up to 28 UCAS points (for an A*), and some universities make lower offers to applicants with a good EPQ. It needs a supervising centre, so ask your school or exam centre whether it offers the EPQ or a similar project qualification.

## Applying outside the UK

A Levels are recognised by universities around the world, but each country, and often each university, has its own rules. Always check the university's international admissions page.

- **United States:** you apply to each university directly, often through the Common App. Many universities require or recommend SAT or ACT scores, and policies have changed in recent years. Strong A Level results may earn credit for some first-year courses.
- **Europe:** many universities accept A Levels, often with requirements on the number of A Levels and specific subjects. English-taught degrees are common in countries such as the Netherlands and Germany.
- **Turkey:** your route depends on your citizenship and where you completed secondary school. Many Turkish universities accept A Level results in their international student admissions, often with minimum grades, while most Turkish citizens who finished school in Turkey enter through the national exam (YKS, run by ÖSYM). You may also need a diploma equivalence (denklik) from the Ministry of National Education. Check with the university and official sources before you rely on A Levels alone.
