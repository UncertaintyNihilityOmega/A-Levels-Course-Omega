:::why
Two years, or even one, is a long time, and long projects fail in a predictable way: weeks drift past, the hard topics get pushed back, and past papers start far too late. A road with clear phases turns one big, frightening goal into small weekly jobs. It also tells you what to do next on any given day, which is half the battle when you study alone.
:::

## The six phases

Every successful exam preparation, whatever the subject or board, passes through the same six phases. They are not strict boxes. Learning and practice run side by side, and past-paper work starts before the last topic is finished. But the order matters: you cannot practise what you have not learned, and timed papers only help once you know most of the content.

| Phase | What you do | Why it matters | How long |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. [Fix your foundations](#road.foundations) | Check and repair the GCSE/IGCSE skills each subject assumes: algebra, units, graphs, essay writing | Every A Level topic is built on them, and small gaps slow down everything later | 2–6 weeks |
| 2. [Learn the content](#road.learn) | Work through the syllabus topic by topic: read actively, make notes, follow worked examples | You cannot practise or revise what you never understood | About half of your total time |
| 3. [Practise and test yourself](#road.practice) | Retrieval practice, spaced review, flashcards, topic questions, an error log | Understanding fades within days unless you test it; testing makes it stick | Alongside phase 2, then 3–6 weeks of its own |
| 4. [Past papers](#road.pastpapers) | Real papers under timed conditions, marked honestly with the official mark scheme | Teaches the exam's style, its timing and the wording that earns marks | 8–12 weeks |
| 5. [Final revision](#road.final) | Condense notes, target weak topics, sit full mock exams | Turns what you know into marks you can rely on under pressure | The last 6–8 weeks |
| 6. [Exam day](#road.examday) | Prepare the logistics, manage your time in the paper, then deal with results, reviews and resits | Avoids losing marks to nerves, admin or a missing plan B | The exam series and results day |

## Three tracks: how long each phase takes

How long you spend in each phase depends on how much time you have. The three most common situations are below. Find yours, then use the [study planner](#road.planner) to turn it into dated weeks.

| | 2-year school route | 12-month intensive | 5–6-month resit or crash plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who it suits | Students in Years 12–13 at a school or college | Private candidates studying full-time, often straight after IGCSE | Students resitting, or who already know much of the content |
| Typical dates | September of Year 12 → May/June of Year 13 | June → June, or October → October/November of the next year | June → October/November, or December → May/June |
| 1. Foundations | 3–4 weeks (the summer before, plus September) | 3–4 weeks | 1 week: a diagnostic test only |
| 2. Learn | September of Year 12 to about February of Year 13 | 26–30 weeks | 4–8 weeks, weak topics only |
| 3. Practise | Every week, plus about 4 weeks of consolidation | Every week, plus about 3 weeks | Every week: the main activity |
| 4. Past papers | Topic questions from Year 12; full papers January–April of Year 13 | 8–10 weeks | 8–10 weeks |
| 5. Final revision | 8 weeks (April–May of Year 13) | 6–8 weeks | 3–4 weeks |
| 6. Exam day | May/June; results in August | Your chosen series | Your chosen series |
| Study outside lessons | 10–15 hours a week | 30–36 hours a week | 8–12 hours a week per subject |

:::board Staged, linear and modular
Cambridge International lets you take the AS papers at the end of Year 12 and the rest of the A Level a year later (**staged assessment**). An AS result can be carried forward for up to 13 months. If your school works this way, you run phases 4–6 twice: a short version for AS in Year 12, then the full version in Year 13. AQA and OCR A Levels are **linear**: every paper is sat in May/June at the end of the course. Edexcel International A Levels (IAL) are **modular**, with exams in January, May/June and October, so you can sit some units early and spread the load.
:::

## How many hours a week?

Cambridge designs each A Level around **360 guided learning hours** of teaching (about 180 for an AS Level). That is class time with a teacher; practice and revision come on top. As a rough guide:

- **At school:** lessons plus 3–5 hours of your own study per subject per week, so 10–15 hours a week for three subjects. Raise this to 20 or more in the final 8 weeks.
- **Private candidate, full-time:** 10–12 hours per subject per week, so 30–36 hours for three subjects. Treat it like a job: a fixed start time, fixed breaks, and one day a week off.
- **Resitting one or two subjects:** 8–12 hours per subject per week.

:::tip Hours are not the goal
Two hours of testing yourself beat five hours of rereading. Track your hours to make sure the time goes in, but judge your progress by your scores on questions you have not seen before.
:::

## Are you ready to move on?

Use these checks at the end of each phase. If you fail one, do not panic: spend another week or two on that phase, then check again.

| Phase | You are ready to move on when… |
|---|---|
| 1. Foundations | You score at least 8 out of 10 on each foundation self-check without looking anything up |
| 2. Learn | For every syllabus learning outcome, you can explain the idea aloud and answer a basic question without notes |
| 3. Practise | You score 70% or more on mixed topic questions a week after learning them, and the same mistakes have stopped appearing in your error log |
| 4. Past papers | Your last three full papers, timed and honestly marked, reach your target grade |
| 5. Final revision | Every topic in your tracker is green, and you can write out the key formulas and definitions from memory |

:::warn The commonest way to fall behind: living in phase 2
Many students spend months rereading notes and making them prettier, then reach the exam having answered almost no questions. Rereading feels productive because everything looks familiar, but recognising an idea is not the same as being able to use it. From the first week, end every study session with questions you answer without looking.
:::

:::exam Start from the syllabus, finish with the mark scheme
Two documents define what you need: the **syllabus** (what can be asked) and the **mark schemes** (what earns marks). Download the syllabus for your exam year before you study anything. Read your first mark scheme early, during phase 2, so you know what examiners look for from the start.
:::

:::tip Already part-way through?
You do not have to start at phase 1. If your exams are four months away, do one past paper per subject this week, mark it, and study the results. Then jump to the phase that fixes your biggest problem, and use the 5–6-month track for timing.
:::

## Build or check a plan by hand

The planner does the arithmetic, but you should understand it so that you can check the result, or redo it with a pen and a calendar.

:::steps
1. Count the whole weeks from next Monday to your first paper.
2. Keep about one week in ten free as a **buffer** for illness, holidays and family events.
3. Working back from the exam, mark the last 6–8 weeks as final revision (phase 5) and the 4–6 weeks before that as full past papers (phase 4).
4. Mark the first 2–4 weeks as foundations (phase 1).
5. The weeks left in the middle are for learning (phase 2), with practice (phase 3) built into every week. Share them between your subjects' topics.
6. Add the fixed dates: entry deadline, mocks, holidays and UCAS deadlines.
:::

Then do a **reality check**. Each chapter of this guide shows a study time at the top (for example, 6–8 hours for [Quadratics](#math.p1.quadratics)). Add about half as much again for practice, multiply by the number of topics, and compare the total with the hours your learning weeks give you. If it does not fit, you have three honest choices: study more hours a week, take fewer subjects in this series, or move to a later series. Never solve the problem by cutting phases 4 and 5, because that is where grades are made.

## How to read your plan

- **Phase dates are targets, not walls.** Phases overlap. Start past-paper questions on each topic as soon as you finish it, long before phase 4 officially begins.
- **The learning weeks are the tightest.** If your plan gives you fewer than about 10 weeks per subject to learn the content, you are on an intensive track, and your weekly hours matter more than anything else.
- **Phase 5 has a minimum.** Even a short plan needs at least 3–4 weeks of final revision.

## Adapting the plan

Every plan goes wrong at some point. What matters is how you respond.

| Situation | What to do |
|---|---|
| 1–2 weeks behind | Use a buffer week, or add 3–5 hours a week for a month. Do not push every later phase back |
| 3 or more weeks behind | Decide now: more hours, one subject moved to the next series, or an AS Level only this time (a Cambridge AS Level is a qualification in its own right) |
| Ahead of schedule | Do more mixed practice and past-paper questions; do not race ahead to new content |
| A topic takes twice as long as planned | Normal for hard topics. Borrow time from a buffer week, never from practice |
| Ill or away for a week | Restart with a weekly review; do not try to "catch up" by working through the night |

Run the planner again once a month: it shows whether you are on track while there is still time to act.

:::warn A plan with no spare time breaks in week three
If every hour of every week is filled, the first cold or family wedding pushes everything back, and the plan starts to feel like a list of failures. Leave buffer weeks, keep one day a week free, and plan only the next week in detail.
:::

@summary Every A Level assumes skills from GCSE or IGCSE. This phase checks those skills with a short self-test and repairs the gaps before they slow you down. It also covers the academic English that second-language learners need.

:::why
Most students who struggle at A Level do not struggle with the new ideas. They struggle because an old skill is shaky: rearranging a formula, handling a negative number, writing a clear paragraph. Every new topic then takes twice as long, and the student concludes "I'm not good at this subject" when the real problem is a two-hour gap from three years ago. ## What each A Level assumes

Cambridge writes its AS and A Level syllabuses for students who have studied the subject at IGCSE or GCSE (or an equivalent course). You do not need a top grade, but the core skills must be quick and reliable, because at A Level they are tools inside bigger problems, not the problem itself.

### Mathematics, and the maths inside the sciences

| Skill | You should be able to… | Repair it here |
|---|---|---|
| Number | Work with fractions, negative numbers, percentages and ratios without a calculator | [Numbers, fractions and percentages](#math.f.numbers) |
| Algebra | Expand and factorise; solve linear and simultaneous equations; rearrange formulas | [Algebra from scratch](#math.f.algebra) |
| Powers | Use the laws of indices, simplify surds, work in standard form | [Powers, roots, surds and standard form](#math.f.powers) |
| Graphs | Find a gradient, use \(y = mx + c\), plot points and read values from a graph | [Coordinates and straight lines](#math.f.graphs) |
| Geometry | Use Pythagoras' theorem, sine, cosine and tangent, angle facts, areas and volumes | [Geometry and basic trigonometry](#math.f.geometry) |

### Physics, Chemistry and Biology

The sciences use five maths skills constantly, so check these even if Mathematics is not one of your subjects.

- **Units and prefixes:** converting mm to m, kJ to J, \(\mathrm{cm^3}\) to \(\mathrm{dm^3}\), and knowing that \(1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3}\).
- **Rearranging:** making any letter the subject of a formula such as \(pV = nRT\) or \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\).
- **Standard form:** writing \(0.000\,045\,6\) as \(4.56 \times 10^{-5}\) and entering it correctly on a calculator.
- **Significant figures:** giving an answer to the same number of significant figures as the data, usually 2 or 3.
- **Graphs:** choosing scales, drawing a line of best fit, and finding a gradient from a large triangle.

Each science also has its own starting knowledge: atoms, ions and equations in Chemistry; forces, energy and circuits in Physics; cells, organs and basic genetics in Biology. Check and repair these with [Physics from zero](#phys.f.basics), [Chemistry from zero](#chem.f.basics) and [Biology from zero](#bio.f.basics).

### Business, Economics and other essay subjects

Essay subjects assume you can read a long case study quickly, pick out the useful facts and numbers, calculate a percentage change, and write a clear paragraph that makes one point and explains it. No prior Business or Economics course is needed, but if these skills feel slow, start with [Business from zero](#bus.f.basics) and [Writing top-band business answers](#bus.writing).

## The ten-minute self-check

Do these without a calculator or notes. Give yourself one mark for each fully correct answer. Score 8 or more and you can start phase 2; otherwise, repair the skills you missed first, using the links above.

:::try Foundation self-check (Mathematics and sciences)
Q: Work out \(\tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{3}{4}\).
A: Use a common denominator of 12: \(\tfrac{8}{12} + \tfrac{9}{12} = \tfrac{17}{12}\), which is \(1\tfrac{5}{12}\).
Q: Expand and simplify \((2x - 3)(x + 4)\).
A: \(2x^2 + 8x - 3x - 12 = 2x^2 + 5x - 12\).
Q: Factorise \(x^2 - 7x + 12\).
A: Two numbers that multiply to 12 and add to \(-7\) are \(-3\) and \(-4\), so \((x - 3)(x - 4)\).
Q: Make \(u\) the subject of \(v^2 = u^2 + 2as\).
A: \(u^2 = v^2 - 2as\), so \(u = \pm\sqrt{v^2 - 2as}\).
Q: Solve the simultaneous equations \(2x + y = 7\) and \(x - y = 2\).
A: Adding them gives \(3x = 9\), so \(x = 3\). Then \(y = 7 - 2(3) = 1\).
Q: Simplify \(\dfrac{x^5 \times x^{-2}}{x^2}\).
A: Add the powers when multiplying and subtract when dividing: \(x^{5 - 2 - 2} = x^1 = x\).
Q: Write \(0.000\,045\,6\) in standard form, and give \(0.004\,562\) to 2 significant figures.
A: \(4.56 \times 10^{-5}\) and \(0.0046\).
Q: Convert 250 mm to metres, 3.5 kW to watts, and \(250\,\mathrm{cm^3}\) to \(\mathrm{dm^3}\).
A: \(0.25\,\mathrm{m}\), \(3500\,\mathrm{W}\) and \(0.25\,\mathrm{dm^3}\) (because \(1\,\mathrm{dm^3} = 1000\,\mathrm{cm^3}\)).
Q: Find the gradient and the \(y\)-intercept of the line \(2y - 6x = 8\).
A: Rearrange to \(y = 3x + 4\): the gradient is 3 and the line crosses the \(y\)-axis at \((0, 4)\).
Q: A price rises from $40 to $46. Find the percentage increase.
A: The increase is $6, and \(\tfrac{6}{40} \times 100 = 15\%\).
:::

For essay subjects, the check is a list of "I can" statements. Be honest: tick one only if you could do it today, under time pressure.

- I can read a one-page article in English in under five minutes and summarise it in three sentences.
- I can write a paragraph that makes one point, explains why, and gives an example.
- I can calculate a percentage change and say what it means in words.
- I can read a simple table or chart and describe the trend with numbers.
- I can define ten basic terms in my subject (for Business: profit, revenue, costs, market, stakeholder and so on) in one sentence each.

## Academic English for second-language learners

Every paper is set and marked in English, and many students who know the science well lose marks to language. The good news: exam English is a small, learnable language of its own.

- **Learn the command words.** "Describe" (say what happens) and "explain" (say why it happens) earn different marks. See [Command words decoded](#road.commands).
- **Keep a vocabulary notebook.** Four columns: the English term, the word in your own language, the definition in English, and an example sentence. Learn definitions as fixed phrases, exactly as the syllabus words them.
- **Write short, complete sentences.** Examiners reward correct science and clear reasoning, not elegant style. One idea per sentence is safer than one long sentence with three ideas.
- **Read in English every day.** Fifteen minutes of your textbook or a good subject website builds reading speed, which matters in long case-study and data questions.
- **Use the [glossary](#tools.glossary)** in this guide: every key term from every chapter is collected there.

| English term | Turkish | Definition in English |
|---|---|---|
| gradient | eğim | the change in \(y\) divided by the change in \(x\) |
| dynamic equilibrium | dinamik denge | forward and reverse reactions happen at equal rates, so concentrations stay constant |
| demand | talep | the quantity buyers are willing and able to buy at each price |
| evaluate | değerlendirmek | judge the strengths and weaknesses, then reach a conclusion |

:::warn "I got an A at IGCSE, so I can skip this"
Skills fade, especially over a long summer, and A Level needs them to be fast as well as correct. Do the self-check anyway: it takes ten minutes, and it either confirms you are ready or finds a gap while it is still cheap to fix.
:::

:::exam Foundation skills carry marks in every paper
A correct method with a slip in rearranging, a wrong power of ten or a missing unit can still lose the final accuracy mark. In Cambridge Mathematics, a non-exact answer not given to 3 significant figures (unless the question asks otherwise) can also cost marks. Examiners usually accept small spelling mistakes, but not when the misspelt word could be another technical term, such as ethane and ethene, or mitosis and meiosis.
:::

## How long should phase 1 take?

| Your self-check result | Plan |
|---|---|
| 8–10, and quick | About 2 weeks: repair anything you missed and read the "from zero" chapter for each science, then start phase 2 |
| 6–7 | 3–4 weeks: work through the foundation chapters for every skill you missed, then redo the self-check |
| 5 or fewer, or no IGCSE in the subject | 5–6 weeks: work through all the foundation chapters for that subject in order |
| Any score, if English is your second language | Add 15 minutes of subject reading in English every day, all the way to the exam |

:::tip Fix the core now, the rest just in time
You do not need to repair everything before you start learning. Fix the core skills now (algebra, rearranging, units, paragraph writing), then repair smaller gaps as topics need them. Every chapter in this guide begins with a "Before you start" line that links to exactly what it assumes.
:::

:::recap
- Most A Level difficulty comes from shaky GCSE/IGCSE skills, not from the new ideas.
- Maths skills to check: number, algebra, powers and standard form, graphs, geometry and trigonometry.
- Science skills to check: units and prefixes, rearranging, standard form, significant figures, graphs.
- Essay subjects need fast reading, clear paragraphs and confident use of data.
- Score 8 out of 10 on the self-check before moving on; repair what you missed with the foundation chapters.
- Second-language learners: learn the command words, keep a four-column vocabulary notebook and read in English daily.
- Phase 1 takes 2–6 weeks, depending on your score.

## How to study one chapter actively

Passive study (reading, highlighting, copying) feels comfortable because the material looks familiar while it is in front of you. Active study makes your brain produce the ideas itself, which is harder in the moment and far more effective. Use this routine for every chapter in this guide or your textbook.

:::steps
1. **Preview (5 minutes).** Read the summary, the headings and the key-term cards. Ask yourself what the topic is for.
2. **Study one section at a time.** At the end of each section, look away and explain it aloud or on paper in your own words.
3. **Work through each worked example with the solution covered.** Attempt each step before you reveal it.
4. **Answer the practice questions without notes**, then check them against the full answers and mark honestly.
5. **Write your notes from memory**, then compare them with the chapter and fill the gaps in a different colour.
6. **Finish with the quick check**, and make flashcards from the key terms and formulas.
:::

Work in focused blocks of 25–50 minutes with a short break between them, and keep your phone in another room. The timer below helps.

## Turn the syllabus into a checklist

The syllabus for your exam year is the official list of everything that can be examined. Download it free from your board's website. In the subject content section, each topic is broken into numbered **learning outcomes**, short statements of what you must be able to do. Copy them into a tracker, in a notebook or a spreadsheet, and colour each one as you go.

| Learning outcome (Quadratics, Cambridge Paper 1, in our words) | Learned | Practised | Exam-ready |
|---|---|---|---|
| Complete the square and use it to find the vertex | Yes | Yes | Green |
| Use the discriminant to decide how many real roots there are | Yes | Yes | Amber |
| Solve quadratic inequalities | Yes | No | Red |
| Solve one linear and one quadratic equation simultaneously | No | No | Red |

- **Red:** not learned yet, or you cannot do it even with notes.
- **Amber:** you can do it with notes, or slowly.
- **Green:** you can do it without notes, under time pressure, at least a week after learning it.

The tracker becomes the backbone of your whole preparation. In phase 5 it tells you exactly which topics need work.

## Choosing a textbook

One good textbook per subject, this guide and the past papers are enough. Collecting more resources feels productive, but it is usually a way of avoiding practice.

- **Cambridge:** look for books that carry Cambridge's endorsement mark on the cover. Cambridge lists endorsed resources on its website, from publishers such as Cambridge University Press and Hodder Education.
- **Edexcel IAL:** Pearson publishes its own student books for the IAL specifications.
- **AQA and OCR:** each board approves or endorses textbooks from several publishers for its own specifications.
- **Check the syllabus years.** A book written for an older syllabus can miss new topics or include ones that were removed. Compare its contents with the current syllabus before you buy, especially second-hand.

## Pacing: how fast should you go?

Your plan from the [study planner](#road.planner) gives you a number of learning weeks. Divide the topics by the weeks to get your weekly target. For Cambridge, the number of topics in the syllabus is:

| Subject (Cambridge) | Topics or sections | Topics per week with 20 learning weeks |
|---|---|---|
| Mathematics 9709 (Pure 1, Pure 3, Mechanics, Probability & Statistics 1) | 8 + 9 + 5 + 5 = 27 | about 1.4 |
| Physics 9702 | 25 (1–11 AS, 12–25 A Level) | about 1.3 |
| Chemistry 9701 | 37 (1–22 AS, 23–37 A Level) | about 1.9 |
| Biology 9700 | 19 (1–11 AS, 12–19 A Level) | about 1 |

Topics differ in size, so treat these as averages. Study all your subjects every week rather than one subject per month: switching between subjects is tiring at first, but it gives each topic time to settle and keeps every subject fresh. If a topic takes more than one and a half times its budget, mark it amber, move on, and come back to it in your buffer week.

## Flashcards as you go

Make flashcards on the day you finish each chapter, while you still know what matters. Aim for 5–15 cards per chapter:

- **one fact per card**, written as a question: "What is the discriminant of \(ax^2 + bx + c\)?"
- **definitions** word for word on the back, exactly as the syllabus uses them;
- **"why" questions** as well as "what" questions: "Why does the rate of reaction increase with temperature?"

Paper cards in a box or a free flashcard app both work. How and when to review them is in [Phase 3](#road.practice).

## The weekly review

Once a week, for 30–45 minutes, step back from new content.

:::steps
1. Test yourself for 15 minutes, without notes, on this week's topics and one older topic.
2. Update the colours in your tracker.
3. Read your error log: is any mistake appearing again and again?
4. Compare where you are with your plan, and write next week's topics into your timetable.
:::

## When you are stuck

Being stuck is a normal part of learning, not a sign that you cannot do the subject. Work down this list.

1. **Slow down.** Reread the section, then copy out the worked example line by line, asking why each step follows from the one before.
2. **Go back one step.** Most stuck points are really an earlier gap. Follow the "Before you start" link at the top of the chapter.
3. **Find a different explanation:** your textbook, a video, or a friend who explains it in other words.
4. **Ask a precise question.** "I don't understand moles" is hard to answer; "I don't see why we divide by 2 in line 3" is easy. Teachers, study groups and online forums all respond better to precise questions.
5. **Set a time limit.** After 30 minutes stuck, mark the topic red, move on, and return in two days. Sleep often does the work for you.

:::tip Explain it to a younger student
If you can explain a topic simply to someone two years younger, without notes, you understand it. If you get stuck halfway, you have found exactly the part to study again.
:::

:::exam Learn definitions word for word
Definitions are quick marks, and mark schemes look for precise key words. "Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity" scores; "acceleration is how fast something speeds up" does not. Put every definition from the syllabus and from this guide's key-term cards onto flashcards, and practise writing them out exactly.
:::

- Learning means being able to explain and use an idea without looking, a week later.
- Study each chapter actively: preview, explain each section, attempt worked examples before reading them, answer questions without notes.
- Turn the syllabus learning outcomes into a red/amber/green tracker.
- Use Cornell notes, concept maps or one-page summaries; write notes from memory.
- Use one endorsed textbook per subject that matches your syllabus years.
- Pace yourself: divide topics by learning weeks, and study every subject every week.
- Make flashcards on the day you finish a chapter, and review your week every weekend.

## Retrieval practice: test, don't reread

:::def Retrieval practice
Learning by recalling information from memory, for example by answering questions or writing down everything you remember, instead of reading the material again.
:::

Every time you pull an idea out of memory, the memory becomes stronger and easier to find next time. Rereading does not do this, because the answer is already in front of you. A large 2013 review of ten common study techniques rated practice testing and spaced practice as the two most useful, and rereading and highlighting among the least useful.

Ways to practise retrieval:

- **Blank page:** write everything you remember about a topic on a blank sheet, then check it against your notes and add what you missed in a different colour.
- **Flashcards**, answered aloud or on paper before you turn the card over.
- **Quizzes:** the quick check at the end of each chapter, and the mixed [practice quizzes](#practice.quiz).
- **Exam-style questions**, from your textbook, this guide and past papers.

## Spaced repetition: when to review

:::def Spaced repetition
Reviewing material at increasing intervals (for example after 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days), so that each review happens just as the memory starts to fade.

| Review | Day | Date | What to do (5–15 minutes) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1st | 1 | Tuesday 13 October | Blank page, then check |

| 3rd | 7 | Monday 19 October | 3–4 exam-style questions |
| 4th | 14 | Monday 26 October | Quiz or mixed questions |
| 5th | 30 | Wednesday 11 November | A past-paper question, timed |

Every review is a test, never a reread. If a review goes badly, restart the cycle for that topic from day 1. Write the review dates in your diary on the day you learn the topic, so you never have to remember them.

## Flashcards that work

The **Leitner system** organises paper flashcards in five boxes. Box 1 is reviewed every day, box 2 every 3 days, box 3 every week, box 4 every 2 weeks and box 5 every month. A card you answer correctly moves up one box; a card you get wrong goes back to box 1. Flashcard apps such as Anki do the same scheduling automatically.

- **Answer before you flip.** Say or write the full answer first. A vague "I knew that" counts as wrong.
- **Be strict.** Half right is wrong for a definition, because the mark scheme wants the key words.
- **Mix card types:** definitions, formulas, "why" questions, and blank diagrams to label.

## Interleaving: mix your topics

:::def Interleaving
Practising a mixture of different topics or question types in one session, so that you must decide which method each question needs, instead of practising one method many times in a row.
:::

| | Blocked practice | Interleaved practice |
|---|---|---|
| What it looks like | 20 questions on completing the square | 12 questions mixing quadratics, functions, series and trigonometry |
| How it feels | Smooth and fast | Slower and harder |
| What it trains | Repeating a method you already know is needed | Choosing the right method, which is what the exam demands |
| When to use it | The first practice on a new topic | Everything after that |

Exam questions never tell you which chapter they come from. Once you have learned three or four topics, make most of your practice interleaved.

## Topic questions

After each topic, climb this ladder: textbook exercises, then the practice questions in this guide, then **past-paper questions on that topic**. Collections of Cambridge questions sorted by topic ("topical past papers") are widely available, and they let you practise a topic in exam style long before you sit full papers. Aim for at least 10–15 exam-style questions on each topic, more for big topics, before you call it green.

## The error log

The error log is the most valuable document you will make. Every mark you lose goes into it, with the reason and a rule to stop it happening again.

| Date | Question | Topic | Marks lost | What went wrong | Type | Rule for next time | Redo on |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 Oct | Maths practice Q4 | Quadratics | 2 | Used \(-b = -4\) when \(b = -4\) | Careless slip | Write \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) with their signs before substituting | 19 Oct |
| 14 Oct | Physics topic test Q2 | Kinematics | 2 | Did not convert \(\mathrm{km\,h^{-1}}\) to \(\mathrm{m\,s^{-1}}\) | Method | Convert every quantity to SI units first | 21 Oct |
| 15 Oct | Chemistry worksheet Q5 | Moles | 3 | Ignored the 1 : 2 ratio in the equation | Knowledge | Always use the mole ratio from the balanced equation | 22 Oct |
| 20 Oct | Business 12-mark question | Motivation | 4 | No judgement at the end | Exam technique | End with a justified conclusion that answers the question | 27 Oct |

Use five types: **knowledge** (you did not know it), **method** (you knew the topic but chose or applied the wrong method), **reading** (you misread the question), **careless slip**, and **exam technique** (timing, missing working, no evaluation). Once a week, read the whole log and count the types. If most are knowledge errors, go back to phase 2 for those topics. If most are careless slips, slow down and check every answer. Then redo each logged question on its redo date, without looking at your old answer.

## Calculation subjects and essay subjects

:::cols
:::col Calculations (Maths, Physics, Chemistry)
- Do many questions: volume matters.
- Write full working, as in the exam: method marks need visible steps.
- Check units and significant figures at the end of every answer.
- Redo wrong questions from a blank page, not by reading the solution.
- Time yourself from early on: about 1.5 minutes per mark in Cambridge Maths.
:::
:::col Essays (Business, Economics, long-answer Biology)
- Plan every answer for 3–5 minutes before writing.
- Build each paragraph: knowledge, application, analysis, evaluation.
- Write timed answers, then mark them against the mark scheme's levels.
- Collect strong chains of reasoning ("this leads to… which means…").
- See [Writing top-band business answers](#bus.writing).
:::
:::

## Measuring readiness

Test each topic with mixed questions a week or more after learning it, and record your score.

| Score | Meaning | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| 80% or more | Exam-ready | Mark it green; review it once a month |
| 60–79% | Nearly there | Mark it amber; work through its error-log entries and do more topic questions |
| Below 60% | Not secure yet | Mark it red; go back to the chapter (phase 2) |

You are ready to give past papers most of your time when your average across mixed sets is 70% or more, no topic is red, and the same mistakes have stopped appearing in your error log.

:::tip Start every session with retrieval
Spend the first 10 minutes of each study session on questions or flashcards from older topics, before any new work. It warms you up, and it builds your spaced reviews into every day without extra planning.
:::

:::warn Looking at the answer too soon
When a question gets hard, it is tempting to look at the solution after a minute. Struggle for at least 5–10 minutes first: the effort of trying is what makes the method stick. If you do look, close the solution and redo the whole question from a blank page.
:::

:::exam Practise in exam format from the start
Write practice answers exactly as you would in the exam: full working, units, sentences rather than notes, and diagrams labelled. Habits formed in practice are the ones you will use under pressure, so make them exam habits from the first week.
:::

:::recap
- Retrieval practice (testing yourself) beats rereading; use blank pages, flashcards, quizzes and exam-style questions.
- Review each topic about 1, 3, 7, 14 and 30 days after learning it, then monthly; every review is a test.
- Use a Leitner box or a flashcard app, and be strict with yourself.
- Interleave: once you know a few topics, mix them in every practice set.
- Keep an error log with a type and a rule for every lost mark, and redo each question a week later.
- Green at 80% or more on mixed questions a week after learning; move on to past papers at a 70% average with no red topics.
:::

:::why
Past papers are the closest thing to the real exam that exists. They show you the style of the questions, how long you really need, and the exact ideas that earn marks. Students who do many past papers, mark them honestly and learn from every mistake usually outscore students who simply know more content.
:::

## Where to find them

Use the official sources first. Each board publishes four kinds of document for each exam series: **question papers**, **mark schemes**, **examiner reports** (what candidates did well and badly) and **grade thresholds** or grade boundaries (the raw marks needed for each grade).

| Board | Where to look | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cambridge International | The syllabus page on [cambridgeinternational.org](https://www.cambridgeinternational.org) | Cambridge publishes some past papers on its website. The full archive is on the School Support Hub, which registered schools use; private candidates can ask their exam centre |
| Pearson Edexcel (IAL) | Your subject's page on [qualifications.pearson.com](https://qualifications.pearson.com) | Papers, mark schemes and examiner reports under the exam materials; grade boundaries on their own page |
| AQA | [aqa.org.uk](https://www.aqa.org.uk) | Past papers and mark schemes for each specification |
| OCR | [ocr.org.uk](https://www.ocr.org.uk) | Past papers in the assessment section of each qualification |

With every board, the newest series is sometimes available only to teachers for a while. Many other websites copy the official files. If you use one, make sure each paper comes with its matching mark scheme.

### Reading Cambridge file names

Cambridge files have names like **9709_s25_qp_12**. Once you can read them, finding the right paper takes seconds.

| Part | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 9709 | The syllabus code (Mathematics) |
| s25 | The series and year: s = May/June, w = October/November, m = February/March; 25 = 2025 |
| qp, ms, er, gt | Question paper, mark scheme, examiner report, grade thresholds |
| 12 | Paper 1, variant 2 |

Cambridge sets several versions (**variants**) of most papers in each series for different time zones. That is why there are so many papers to practise with: a few recent years give you 20 or more versions of some components.

## How many, and in what order?

Aim for **8–12 full papers per component** by exam day, plus topic questions, and more for components you find hard. Use them in this order:

1. **Older papers** (about 6–10 years old): cut into topic questions during phase 3.
2. **Middle years:** full timed papers in phase 4.
3. **The two or three most recent series:** saved for full mocks in phase 5.

Before you start, read the "changes to the syllabus" page at the front of your syllabus. Papers written before a syllabus change can include topics that are no longer examined, and miss new ones. Skip those questions, or replace them with questions from this guide.

## Sitting a paper under timed conditions

:::steps
1. Choose the same time of day as the real exam, a quiet desk, and only the permitted equipment: pens, pencil, ruler, an allowed calculator, and the formula list or data booklet your board provides (for Cambridge Mathematics, the list called MF19).
2. Put your phone in another room and set a timer for the real length of the paper.
3. Read the front cover as if it were the real exam.
4. Work to time. Cambridge Mathematics Paper 1 has 75 marks in 1 hour 50 minutes, so allow about 1.5 minutes per mark.
5. When time runs out, draw a line. You may finish in a different colour to see what more time would give, but count only the marks above the line.
:::

## Marking honestly

Mark the paper the same day, or the next, with the official mark scheme. Be strict: if your answer does not clearly contain the point the mark scheme describes, do not give yourself the mark. Generous self-marking only hides the problems you need to fix.

Mathematics mark schemes use a short code. Learn it, because it tells you exactly how marks are earned:

| Code | Meaning |
|---|---|
| M1 | A method mark, for a correct method applied to the problem |
| A1 | An accuracy mark: only possible if the related M mark is earned |
| B1 | An independent mark for a correct answer or statement |
| FT | Follow through: credit for correct work that uses your own earlier wrong answer |
| CAO | Correct answer only |
| OE | Or equivalent: another correct form of the answer is accepted |
| AWRT | Answer which rounds to the value given |
| AG | Answer given in the question: every step must be shown |

Science mark schemes list the acceptable answers for each mark point, and essay mark schemes describe **levels**: what an answer must show to reach each band of marks. Then turn your raw mark into a grade with the grade thresholds for that series and variant. Thresholds change from series to series because some papers are harder than others, so always use the matching one. Aim to be comfortably above your target grade, not just on it.

:::exam Why the method mark matters
An A mark depends on its M mark. A correct final answer from a wrong or invisible method can score nothing, while a wrong final answer from a correct method can still score most of the marks. In "show that" questions, the answer is printed (the mark scheme labels it AG), so all the marks are for the steps. Always write your working where the examiner can follow it.
:::

## Read the examiner report

For every paper you sit, read the examiner report's comments on it. Examiners describe the common errors, the misunderstandings they saw most often, and what the best answers did. Ten minutes with the report often explains a lost mark better than the mark scheme does. Copy anything that applies to you into your error log.

## Topic by topic, or full papers?

| | Topic-by-topic questions | Full papers |
|---|---|---|
| When | Phase 3 and the start of phase 4 | Most of phase 4, and all of phase 5 |
| What it trains | Depth in one topic; finding gaps quickly | Timing, stamina, switching between topics, choosing methods |
| The risk | The topic heading tells you which method to use | One paper cannot cover every topic |

Use both. Start phase 4 with one full paper per component per week, plus topic sets for your weakest areas. Build up to two or three full papers a week in the last month of the phase.

## The redo cycle

:::steps
1. **Sit** the paper, timed.
2. **Mark** it honestly, the same day.
3. **Log** every lost mark in your error log, with its type and a rule for next time.
4. **Relearn:** for a knowledge gap, go back to the chapter; for a method gap, do three to five similar questions.
5. **Redo** every question that lost marks, from a blank page, a week later.
6. **Track** your scores for each component, with the grade thresholds next to them, so you can see the trend.
:::

:::warn Do not memorise mark-scheme wording without understanding it
Mark schemes are written for examiners, not as model answers. Phrases learned by heart without understanding end up in the wrong context, where they earn nothing. Questions are reworded every year, and the mark is for the idea used correctly. Learn why each point earns its mark, then practise explaining it in your own words while keeping the key technical terms.
:::

:::tip Keep the newest papers for later
Do not use the two or three most recent series before phase 5. They are the best guide to your own exam, so save them for full mocks under real conditions.
:::

:::recap
- Use official sources: Cambridge (public website and the School Support Hub through your centre), Pearson, AQA and OCR, with mark schemes, examiner reports and grade thresholds.
- Read Cambridge file names: syllabus, series and year, document type, paper and variant (9709_s25_qp_12).
- Aim for 8–12 full papers per component: old papers as topic questions, middle years timed, newest saved for mocks.
- Sit papers in real conditions and to time: about 1.5 minutes per mark in Cambridge Maths Paper 1.
- Mark strictly; learn M, A, B, FT and AG; compare your mark with the thresholds for that exact paper.
- Read the examiner report for every paper, and follow the redo cycle: sit, mark, log, relearn, redo, track.
- Understand mark-scheme points; never memorise their wording blindly.
:::

## The last eight weeks, week by week

Count the weeks back from your **first** paper. If you have only six weeks, combine weeks 8 and 7, and weeks 6 and 5.

| Weeks to go | Main focus | Full papers per subject | Also this week |
|---|---|---|---|
| 8 | Audit: update your tracker, list every red and amber topic, plan the eight weeks | 1, as a baseline | Get the final exam timetable and mark every paper on your plan |

## The last two weeks

- **Follow the timetable.** The subject you sit first gets the first mocks and the most review time in the final days.
- **Sit papers at exam times.** If your papers start in the morning, do your mocks in the morning, so your brain is used to working hard at that hour.
- **Read your rules daily.** Read the "rule for next time" column of your error log every day. It is a personal list of the traps you fall into.
- **Make one sheet per paper:** the formulas, definitions and mistakes that matter most for that paper. Read it the evening before.
- **Plan the gaps between papers.** With three days between two papers, for example: one timed paper on day 1, weak topics on day 2, light review on day 3.

On a full revision day (study leave, weekends, or every day for a private candidate):

| 10:50–11:10 | Break, away from your desk |
| 11:10–12:30 | Mark the paper and log every lost mark |
| 12:30–13:30 | Lunch, away from screens |

## Running your own mocks

If your school runs mock exams, treat them as the real thing. If you study alone, run your own:

- the same length, the same time of day and the same rules as the real exam;
- a clear table, no notes, phone in another room, and if possible someone in the room acting as the invigilator;
- the whole set of papers over a few days, in the same order as your real timetable;
- marking within 24 hours, followed by the redo cycle from [Phase 4](#road.pastpapers).

A disappointing mock is useful: it shows you the problems while there is still time to fix them. Treat the score as information, not as a judgement of you.

## Condensing your notes

Condensing works because every step forces you to choose what matters and to recall it.

1. **Chapter notes** from phase 2.
2. **One page per topic:** write it from memory first, then check and fill the gaps.
3. **One page per paper:** only the formulas, definitions and traps for that paper.
4. **A final card per paper** to read on the evening before.

## Formulas, definitions and data

Get a clean copy of what your board provides in the exam: for Cambridge, the formula list MF19 for Mathematics, the data and formulae pages printed in each Physics paper, and the Data Booklet for Chemistry. Learn what is on it, and where, so you never waste time searching. Then **memorise everything else.** Each day in the last three weeks, write out every formula and definition for one paper on a blank page in five minutes, check it, and correct it. Our sheets help: [Maths](#tools.f.math), [Physics](#tools.f.phys), [Chemistry](#tools.f.chem), [Biology](#tools.f.bio) and [Business, economics and accounting](#tools.f.bus).

## Sleep, food and exercise

Your brain stores the day's learning while you sleep, so sleep is part of revision, not time taken away from it. Most teenagers need 8–10 hours a night. An all-nighter before a paper makes you slower and more error-prone the next day. Two weeks before the exams, move your bedtime and wake-up time so that you are alert at the time your papers start. A daily walk or other exercise helps you sleep and manage stress, and avoiding caffeine after mid-afternoon protects your sleep. There is more in [Motivation, sleep and stress](#road.wellbeing).

## What not to do

| Don't | Do instead |
|---|---|
| Start new textbooks, courses or apps | Stick to your notes, this guide and past papers |
| Reread your notes for hours | Test yourself: papers, flashcards, blank pages |
| Revise only your favourite topics | Work from your red and amber list |
| Sit papers without marking them | Mark every paper within 24 hours |
| Use the newest papers too early | Save them for the last three weeks |
| Stay up late to "finish" a topic | Keep the same bedtime every night |
| Compare hours with friends or on social media | Compare your scores with your own scores last week |

:::tip Revise by question, not by chapter
In the final weeks, start from questions rather than from your notes. Do a question; if you cannot, open the chapter, fix that one gap, and try a similar question. Your time goes exactly where your marks are being lost.
:::

:::warn The last-week panic switch
In the final week, many students abandon their plan and start rereading everything from the beginning. It feels safe but achieves little, and it leaves no time for the subject you sit first. Trust the plan you made when you were calm: light review, error-log rules, sleep.
:::

:::exam Turn your mock into a timing plan
After each mock, note how long each question took. Then write a timing plan for each paper: for Cambridge Mathematics Paper 1 (75 marks in 1 hour 50 minutes), about 1.5 minutes per mark, so roughly 45 minutes for the first 30 marks. Practise the plan in your final mocks so that pacing feels automatic on the day.
:::

:::recap
- Count back from your first paper; use the 8-week table, merging weeks if you have only six.
- Weeks 8–6 for red topics, week 5 a full mock, week 4 fixing the mock, weeks 3–2 timed papers and final mocks, week 1 light review.
- Sit papers at real exam times, mark within 24 hours, and read your error-log rules daily.
- Condense: chapter notes, one page per topic, one page per paper, one card per paper.
- Know what is on your formula list or data booklet, and memorise everything else.
- Sleep 8–10 hours; shift your bedtime to the exam timetable two weeks ahead.
- No new resources, no all-nighters, no last-week panic.
:::

:::why
By exam day the learning is done, but marks can still be lost to things that have nothing to do with knowledge: a forgotten calculator, a misread question, fifteen minutes stuck on one part. ## The night before

- **Check the details** on your **statement of entry**, the document from your centre that lists your candidate number and each paper's date and start time. Confirm the time and the room, and set two alarms.
- **Pack your bag:**
  - photo ID (a passport or national ID card): private candidates will not be allowed in without it;
  - your statement of entry;
  - a permitted calculator with fresh batteries;
  - at least three black pens, two pencils, an eraser, a sharpener and a ruler, plus a protractor and compasses for Mathematics;
  - a clear pencil case and a clear water bottle, if your centre allows them.
- **Check your calculator is allowed.** Rules differ by board and subject. For example, Cambridge Mathematics does not allow graphic calculators or calculators that can do algebra.
- **Read your one-page sheet** for the paper, then stop. Do not start anything new.
- **Plan your route**, with time to spare for traffic, and go to bed at your normal time.

:::warn Pens, pencils and correction fluid
Cambridge papers ask for black or dark blue pen, and do not allow erasable pens or correction fluid. Use pencil only for diagrams and graphs, and for multiple-choice answer sheets, which are marked by machine and need a soft pencil. If you make a mistake in pen, cross it out with a single neat line and write the correction next to it.
:::

## The morning

- Eat breakfast and drink some water, even if you are nervous.
- Arrive at least 30 minutes early. Private candidates should check the arrival time their centre gives.
- Take a last look at your one-page sheet, then put it away before you enter the room.
- Leave your phone, smartwatch and any other device at home or hand them in as instructed. Having one with you in the exam room can count as malpractice, even if it is switched off.
- Go to the toilet before the exam starts.

## In the exam

### Reading the paper

1. Write your name, centre number and candidate number exactly as they appear on your statement of entry.
2. Read the front cover: the number of questions, the total marks, and whether you must answer every question or choose some.
3. If you have time, look through the whole paper for a minute so that nothing surprises you later.
4. Read each question twice. Underline the command word and the key data.
5. Look at the marks for each part and the space given for the answer. Both tell you how much the examiner expects.

### Timing: minutes per mark

Divide the time by the total marks to get your pace. For example, Cambridge Mathematics Paper 1 has 75 marks in 1 hour 50 minutes, which is about 1.5 minutes per mark, so a 6-mark question deserves about 9 minutes.

| Cambridge paper | Marks | Time | Minutes per mark |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mathematics Paper 1 or 3 | 75 | 1 h 50 min | about 1.5 |
| Mathematics Paper 2, 4, 5 or 6 | 50 | 1 h 15 min | 1.5 |
| Physics, Chemistry or Biology Paper 1 (multiple choice) | 40 | 1 h 15 min | just under 2 per question |
| Physics, Chemistry or Biology Paper 2 (AS structured) | 60 | 1 h 15 min | 1.25 |
| Physics, Chemistry or Biology Paper 4 (A Level structured) | 100 | 2 h | 1.2 |
| Business Paper 2 | 60 | 1 h 30 min | 1.5 |
| Economics Paper 2 | 60 | 2 h | 2 |

To keep 10 minutes for checking in Mathematics Paper 1, work at about 1.3 minutes per mark. Edexcel IAL, AQA and OCR papers have different lengths, so work out your own pace from the front cover of a past paper.

:::exam Let the marks guide you
The marks for each part tell you what the examiner wants. A 2-mark "explain" question usually needs two separate points, or one point and its reason. A 1-mark "state" question needs a short answer, not a paragraph. For calculations, a part worth 4 or 5 marks needs several clear steps of working, because most of those marks are method marks.
:::

### When you are stuck

- **Move on** once you have spent about twice the time the marks deserve. Circle the question number and come back at the end.
- **Write what you know.** A formula, a labelled diagram or the first line of working can earn method marks.
- **Use answers given in the question.** If you cannot show that \(x = 4\) in part (a), use \(x = 4\) in part (b) anyway: the later marks are still available.
- **Never leave a multiple-choice question blank.** No marks are taken away for wrong answers, so eliminate what you can and choose.
- **If panic starts,** put your pen down, breathe slowly for thirty seconds, and read the next question. One hard question is only a few marks.
- **Need more space?** Use the extra page at the back of the booklet, and write the question number clearly.

### The last ten minutes

Check that you answered what was asked ("exact", "in terms of \(\pi\)", "to 3 significant figures"), that every answer has a unit where one is needed, that no part is left blank, and that your multiple-choice answer sheet is complete.

## After each paper

Leave the room and do not go through the answers with friends: it only creates worry about marks you can no longer change. Eat, rest, then spend thirty minutes planning for your next paper. If something affected your performance, such as illness, a family emergency or a disturbance in the room, tell your exams officer the same day. Your centre may be able to apply for **special consideration**, but only within a short time after the exam.

## Results day

- **When:** Cambridge results for the May/June series come out in August (the June 2026 results were released on 11 August 2026), and results for the October/November series in January. Each Edexcel IAL series has its own results day; check Pearson's key dates.
- **How:** your centre gives you your results, and some centres also give you a login to see them online.
- **University places:** if you applied through UCAS, check UCAS Hub on results day to see whether your place is confirmed. Before the exams, ask your centre whether it will send your results to UCAS.

Read your results calmly, and decide what to do before you phone anyone.

## Enquiries about results

If a grade is lower than you expected, your centre can ask the board to check it. Cambridge calls these **enquiries about results**; Pearson, AQA and OCR call them post-results services or reviews of results. The usual options are:

- a **clerical re-check** that all the marks were counted and added correctly;
- a **review of marking**, with a faster priority service when a university place depends on the result;
- a **copy of your marked script**, which shows exactly where you lost marks. This is very useful if you plan to resit.

Every service has a fee and a strict deadline, and it must be requested through your centre, including by private candidates. A review looks for marking errors; it is not a second marking, and **marks can go down as well as up**. It is usually worth it only if you believe you are close to a grade boundary.

## Resits

| Board | When you can resit | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Cambridge International | October/November (results in January), or the next May/June | You normally sit all the papers of the AS or A Level again; ask your centre whether anything can be carried forward, such as an AS result within 13 months |
| Pearson Edexcel IAL | January, May/June or October | Modular: you can resit only the units you choose, and your best result for each unit counts |
| AQA and OCR (UK A Levels) | May/June only | Linear: you sit all the papers again, a year later |

Resit when a university offer or a career depends on the grade, and when you know exactly what went wrong. If your university has already accepted you, the time is usually better spent preparing for the course. If you decide to resit, read the [study planner](#road.planner) example for a resit student, and start straight away.

:::tip Write your plan B before results day
In July, while you are calm, write down what you will do if you miss your offer by one grade, and by two. On results day you will then follow a plan instead of making big decisions in a panic.
:::

## UCAS Clearing and plan B

If you miss the conditions of your offer, do not assume the worst. Universities sometimes accept students who just miss their grades, so check UCAS Hub first, and phone the admissions office if your status is unclear (have your UCAS Personal ID ready). If you have no place, **Clearing** lets you apply for university courses that still have places. You phone universities, and if one makes you an offer, you add it as your Clearing choice in UCAS Hub. Clearing is busiest on A Level results day and in the days after.

Other good plan B routes include:

- a course with a **foundation year**, which has lower entry requirements;
- a **gap year** with resits, then a new application with real grades instead of predicted ones;
- universities in Turkey or other countries that accept A Level results;
- apprenticeships and work-based routes.

There is more on applications in [University applications and admissions tests](#road.university).

:::warn Do not grab the first Clearing offer
An offer on the phone can feel like relief, but you will spend three or four years on that course. Ask about the course content, the city and the costs, compare two or three options, and talk it through with someone you trust before you accept.
:::

- Stuck? Move on, write what you know, use given answers, never leave a multiple-choice question blank.
- Report illness or disruption to your exams officer the same day.
- Cambridge June results come out in August, November results in January. Enquiries about results have fees and short deadlines, and marks can go down.
- Resits: Cambridge in November or June, Edexcel IAL in January, June or October (best unit result counts), AQA and OCR in June only.
